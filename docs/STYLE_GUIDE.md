# Guidelines for Revising LLM-Generated Prose

Adapted from [Wikipedia: Signs of AI writing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Signs_of_AI_writing).

## Purpose

Revise prose to reduce the generic, repetitive, and formulaic patterns commonly found in LLM-generated writing, while preserving accuracy, clarity, substance, and effective expression.

The patterns described below are **warning signs, not prohibited constructions**. They also occur in good human writing. Their presence does not establish that a passage was written by AI or that it needs correction.

Judge each instance by whether it serves a purpose, whether its claims are supported, and whether similar constructions have become repetitive across the text.

The goal is better writing, not the elimination of every recognizable AI-associated expression.

## 1. Preserve specificity instead of inflating significance

LLMs often replace specific information with broad claims about importance, influence, legacy, or historical trends.

Watch for repeated assertions that something:

- Marks a pivotal moment or significant shift.
- Plays a crucial role.
- Leaves an enduring legacy.
- Reflects a broader trend.
- Underscores the importance of something.
- Contributes to a wider conversation or debate.

These claims can be legitimate, but they should be supported by the material.

Prefer explaining what happened, what changed, and why it mattered over simply declaring something important.

**Example:**

Before:
> The institute's founding in 1989 marked a pivotal moment in the evolution of regional statistics.

After:
> The institute was founded in 1989.

The original significance claim would require additional evidence. If the founding changed how regional statistics were collected or administered, explain that change instead of merely claiming a pivotal moment.

Do not remove genuine historical significance when the evidence establishes it.

## 2. Remove superficial analysis

LLMs frequently attach generic interpretations to otherwise factual statements, often using participial phrases beginning with words such as *highlighting*, *reflecting*, *underscoring*, *showcasing*, or *contributing*.

For example:

> The museum opened in 1998, highlighting the city's commitment to preserving its cultural heritage.

The opening date is factual. The interpretation about the city's commitment may be unsupported or unnecessary.

Consider whether the added phrase communicates a meaningful, substantiated relationship. If it does, retain or develop it. Otherwise, remove it.

Pay particular attention to commentary that could be attached to almost any subject without changing its meaning.

## 3. State relationships and attribution precisely

LLMs often describe relationships indirectly, using phrases such as *associated with*, *connected to*, or *in connection with*, even when a more specific relationship is known.

For example:

> She was associated with mathematics education at the university.

If the evidence establishes that she taught mathematics there, say so. If it establishes only a weaker association, retain that distinction.

Similarly, watch for vague attributions:

- Experts argue that...
- Researchers have noted...
- Critics widely believe...
- Several publications have recognized...

These can conceal unsupported generalizations or exaggerate a limited number of sources into an apparent consensus.

Identify who made a claim and what the evidence actually supports. Do not turn one person's opinion into the position of an entire field.

If the relationship or attribution cannot be established, flag the uncertainty rather than inventing a more specific claim.

## 4. Describe the subject, not merely its coverage

LLMs sometimes devote excessive attention to whether a subject has appeared in publications, databases, professional profiles, or other sources.

They may repeatedly state that sources "identify," "list," "document," or "recognize" a person or organization instead of directly describing the facts established by those sources.

They also tend to convert the existence of media coverage or citations into claims of importance or lasting influence.

Where appropriate, use sources to establish facts about the subject rather than making the sources themselves the focus of the prose.

Source attribution remains important when discussing disputed claims, interpretations, or the history of a subject's reception. Do not remove attribution that the reader needs.

## 5. Avoid formulaic conclusions and manufactured outlooks

LLMs frequently finish a discussion with generic comments about challenges, opportunities, continued progress, future prospects, or lasting relevance.

A common pattern acknowledges difficulties before concluding that the subject remains promising or important.

Such conclusions often add little information.

Prefer ending with a specific finding, consequence, limitation, or unresolved question supported by the preceding discussion.

Do not manufacture a positive outlook, a future research agenda, or a final statement of significance simply because the document seems to need a conclusion.

An actual discussion of limitations or future directions is valuable when the material warrants one.

## 6. Watch for recurring language and grammatical patterns

Several constructions occur disproportionately in LLM-generated writing. Consider their frequency across the whole passage rather than treating individual occurrences as errors.

**Characteristic vocabulary.** Words such as *pivotal, intricate, robust, vibrant, tapestry, landscape, delve, foster, leverage, showcase,* and *underscore* can accumulate into a recognizable style, particularly when used abstractly or without specific meaning.

These are not forbidden words. Retain them when they are accurate and appropriate.

**Elaborate substitutes for simple verbs.** LLMs sometimes write *serves as*, *stands as*, *functions as*, *boasts*, or *features* where *is* or *has* would be more direct. Prefer the simpler construction when nothing is gained by the alternative.

**Negative parallelisms.** Constructions such as "not just X, but Y," "not X, but Y," and "Y rather than X" are particularly common.

They are effective when establishing a genuine distinction or correcting a misconception. They become distracting when used repeatedly to manufacture rhetorical tension.

**Groups of three.** LLMs frequently arrange adjectives, examples, and claims into triples, sometimes adding redundant or weak items to complete the pattern.

Keep meaningful groups of three. Remove items that contribute nothing, and avoid repeatedly imposing the same structure.

The concern is cumulative repetition and inappropriate usage, not the mere presence of these constructions.

## 7. Avoid unnecessary disclaimers and source commentary

LLMs may introduce qualifications about what information is available, what sources do or do not establish, or how claims should be interpreted.

Some such statements are necessary. Others are generic cautionary language inserted without a genuine ambiguity to resolve.

Distinguish between:

- A meaningful limitation of the evidence.
- An important distinction between fact, inference, and interpretation.
- A generic disclaimer that adds no useful information.

Preserve the first two. Remove or simplify the third.

Do not replace cautious but accurate language with unjustified certainty.

## 8. Remove generated-text artifacts

Check for material that belongs to the writing process rather than the finished document.

Examples include conversational introductions, offers to help the user, editorial instructions accidentally left in the text, unresolved placeholders, and statements about how the document was prepared.

Also check for excessive boldface, mechanical heading structures, unnecessary lists, and formatting inappropriate to the intended publication.

These conventions are not inherently wrong. Markdown, for example, is entirely appropriate in a Markdown document. The problem is using a format that does not belong in the finished artifact.

Where citations are present, verify that they exist and actually support the associated claims. A plausible-looking citation is not sufficient evidence.

Problems with sources, references, or factual accuracy must be corrected substantively; changing the surrounding prose does not resolve them.

## 9. Apply the guidelines judiciously

When reviewing a passage, follow this procedure:

1. **Read the passage in context.** Identify its intended meaning, factual claims, and rhetorical purpose.
2. **Look for the patterns above.** Pay particular attention to recurring constructions, generic interpretations, inflated claims, and vague relationships.
3. **Determine whether each occurrence is actually a problem.** Ask whether it communicates something useful, whether it is supported, and whether repetition weakens its effect.
4. **Make targeted revisions.** Remove empty commentary, simplify needlessly elaborate expressions, and replace vague language with precise statements when the evidence supports doing so.
5. **Preserve effective constructions.** Do not rewrite a useful contrast, well-supported statement of significance, or meaningful qualification merely because it resembles an LLM pattern.
6. **Review the result as a whole.** Check that the revision has improved clarity and reduced mechanical repetition without losing information, nuance, or the author's intended style.

An important mathematical distinction may deserve an explicit contrast. An important historical achievement may deserve emphatic language. A difficult argument may need careful qualifications. Those are appropriate uses of devices that LLMs also frequently overuse.

Do not apply fixed word bans, numerical frequency limits, or automatic substitutions. Do not introduce artificial variation merely to avoid repetition.

## 10. Preserve substance and accuracy

The purpose of this editing pass is to improve expression while preserving content.

Do not:

- Invent facts, citations, examples, or explanations to make the text seem more specific.
- Remove necessary uncertainty or attribution.
- Reduce a substantive explanation to a brief assertion.
- Introduce unsupported claims of significance.
- Rewrite effective prose solely to make a visible change.
- Optimize the text for an AI-detection score.

When a passage lacks the evidence necessary to make a precise or meaningful statement, flag that limitation rather than fabricating supporting detail.

**The governing principle is editorial selectivity.** LLM prose often suffers from applying otherwise useful rhetorical devices too frequently, too generically, or without enough supporting substance. Improve the writing by identifying where those habits cause problems, while leaving effective uses intact.

If an edit does not improve the passage, do not make it.
