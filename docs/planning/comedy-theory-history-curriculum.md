# Comedy: Theory, History, and Craft — deep-dive lecture curriculum

**Status, 14 November 2026:** 001000–002000 authored. Subsequent titles are proposals. **Goal:** Explain not merely how to write a joke, but why comic mechanisms function, who has historically been able to use them, how different traditions evolved, and how technologies and audience norms changed what could be performed. Long-form, researched lectures. History and theory are complementary strands, not a simplistic chronological progression toward present-day stand-up.

## Research principles

- Distinguish **humor theory** (explanations of amusement, laughter, play) from **comedy tradition** (genre, performance practice, institutions, text transmission) and from **writerly craft** (timing, setup, escalation, persona, callbacks).
- Avoid making ancient Greece the claimed origin of global comedy. Greek, Indian, Chinese, Japanese, African, Middle Eastern, and other histories have independent trajectories and must be studied through their own surviving sources and scholarship. Shared techniques do not prove direct influence.
- Identify the survival problem: written scripts, elite patronage, recordings, oral traditions, and colonial archives all select what historians can know. Preserve original-language terminology and contextual meanings.
- For schools of comic thought, separate what each theorist actually argued from later retrospective labels. Use at least one counterexample for each purported general theory.
- For historically exploitative traditions, examine how they worked, who benefited, who was harmed, and how later artists resisted or repurposed their machinery. No reproducing long offensive routines as entertainment.
- A deep dive should ordinarily have enough room for a significant historical case, detailed analysis of its mechanism, opposing scholarly interpretations, and performance context. A 3-minute catalogue of names is not a lecture.

## Analytical framework for each historical deep dive

1. **Who performed and for whom?** Venue, community, patron, financial model, political access.
2. **What survives?** Plays, fragments, period recollections, scores, photographs, scripts, recordings; named silences.
3. **What was the mechanism?** Status reversal, incongruity, ritual inversion, persona, collective recognition, tension, linguistic form.
4. **What changed from adjacent contexts?** Media, institutions, authorship, audience composition, censorship, preservation.
5. **Competing interpretations:** Avoid single-author origin stories and unsupported claims of universal influence.
6. **A writerly laboratory:** Analyze an original short comic scene informed by mechanisms, then identify what cannot be transplanted intact across cultures.
