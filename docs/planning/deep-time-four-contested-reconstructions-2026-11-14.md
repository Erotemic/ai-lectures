# Deep Time extension: four contested reconstructions (033000–036000)

**Editorial status (14 November 2026):** All four manuscripts are delivered. This is a follow-on case-study arc after the completed 32-lecture initial curriculum, not a reclassification or replacement of the methodological conclusion. Preserve stable IDs.

## Why these case studies

The main chronological track explains the most widely accepted histories and their evidence. These four independent, standalone investigations take particular reconstructions apart, showing in detail why alternative explanations survive or fail. They span planetary formation, extreme climate, extinction mechanisms and anthropogenic ecological change. Their chronological order runs from ancient Solar System origins to the late Quaternary. Each has at least one live explanatory dispute and at least one historically important weak or rejected idea.

## Key recent research and updating policy

- **Moon:** Schneider and Kleine (2025) infer a lunar formation age near 4.502 ± 0.020 Ga in most of their modeled impact cases, but not in the canonical model; never present their date as an unconditioned direct measurement. Davies et al. (published late 2025 for a 2026 volume) explore impact chains as a simulation, not a recovered sequence of events.
- **Snowball:** Minsky et al. (2026) model repeated snowball–hothouse cycles within the Sturtian. This demonstrates a viable mechanism conditional on their model; it does not settle the extent or frequency of glacial breaks. Song et al. (2026) review evidentiary challenges to the simplest hard-snowball picture.
- **End-Permian:** Burgess, Bowring and Shen (2014) date a specific stratigraphic sequence, not all global biological losses. Burgess and Black (2025) summarize Siberian Traps mechanisms. A 2025 feedback study proposes internal Earth-system oscillations during recovery.
- **Megafauna:** Svenning and colleagues (2024) advance a strong human-pressure synthesis. A 2025 systematic literature survey documents continuing differences among researchers; tallying papers does not produce a causal probability. Holliday and colleagues' 2024 rebuttal argues that Younger Dryas impact indicators and claimed synchronous biological losses are not adequately supported. Neither the minority view nor its critics should be caricatured; evaluate observable predictions.

**Cross-cutting discipline:** A measured isotope ratio is not a directly observed impact date, a preserved glacial deposit is not a global photograph of sea ice, a carbon excursion is not a measurement of one gas source, and a last fossil occurrence is not necessarily the last living individual. Date each paper's result, preserve the distinction between an instrument value and the mechanism used to interpret it, and report qualitative confidence with specific scope.
