The first lecture distinguishes Esslin's critical category from the work and self-understanding of playwrights grouped by it. The second is explicitly historical about Dada and the 1924 surrealist movement; it does not say that later popular sketch programs belong to those movements. The third compares but does not identify Camus' and Nagel's responses. The fourth treats contemporary reality-based comedy as a set of production strategies with associated consent and ethical questions rather than as a uniform school.

The cumulative track remains under `content/comedy-history`; no other agent's source or global reader code is touched. ## Editorial cautions and provenance

- All opening comic premises, exemplary dialogue, scenarios and suggested rewrite exercises are original instructional material, not quotes from copyrighted performers.
- Dates of Esslin's formulation, Beckett's premiere, Ionesco's play, Jarry's production, Cabaret Voltaire and Breton's publications are supported by publisher, museum, or library records.
- Scholarly arguments are attributed as arguments. An abstract is not represented as a full-text audit of a scholarly work.
- No universal assertion is made that all theater grouped under the absurdist label is nihilistic, apolitical or European.
- Descriptions of what a fictional scene might make viewers feel are analytical possibilities, not claims of measured empirical effects.
- **No lecture duration policy applies.** Content determines length; word counts are reports of completed narration only.
