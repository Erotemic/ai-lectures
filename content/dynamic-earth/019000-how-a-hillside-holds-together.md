# How a Hillside Holds Together: Friction, Water, and Sudden Failure

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

A mountainside can hold itself at an angle for thousands of years and then move in less than a minute. That sounds like a contradiction until we recognize that a slope is not an inert pile whose stability was decided once when the mountain formed. It is a changing mechanical system. Rain enters pores and cracks, streams remove material at the foot, freezing and weathering widen fractures, roots grow and die, roads change drainage, and earthquakes add brief forces. The slope we see is the temporary result of those competing influences.

Imagine a block of rock on an inclined surface. Gravity pulls the block downward. Part of its weight presses it against the surface, and part tries to move it along the surface. The block resists by friction and, if the material has cohesive strength, by bonds between grains or intact pieces of rock. The steeper the surface, other things equal, the stronger the downhill component of gravity. This is the simplest possible picture of a landslide, and it gives us a vocabulary: driving stress, resisting strength, and a surface along which movement might occur.

Real slopes rarely provide a smooth plane. A rock face is cut by joints, bedding planes, and faults. Some fractures dip out toward a valley, making a rock slab more likely to slide. Other fractures lean into the mountain and may lock a block in place. Two slopes of equal steepness can therefore behave quite differently. In one, intact rock bridges across the fractures carry the load. In another, clay smeared along an old fault supplies a weak layer. A geologist mapping a dangerous cliff does not merely measure its angle. They measure which way its discontinuities point, whether those discontinuities continue into the slope, and how water could enter them.

There is a useful, deliberately simplified calculation. Suppose a layer of dry, cohesionless soil sits on a planar slope thirty degrees above horizontal. Let its angle of internal friction be thirty-five degrees. In a basic infinite-slope model, with no cohesion and no pore pressure, the factor of safety is the tangent of thirty-five degrees divided by the tangent of thirty degrees. That is about one point two. The resisting shear strength modestly exceeds the driving shear stress. A factor of safety above one suggests resistance exceeds driving stress in that particular model; below one suggests instability. It is not a measured probability that the hillside will survive a storm, and it is not a substitute for mapping the material.

Now allow the soil to become wet. The weight can increase as water fills pores, but an even more consequential effect is the pressure of water within those pores. Grain-to-grain contacts carry only part of the total stress. Water pressure carries the rest. The resistance due to friction depends largely on the stress transmitted through the grain skeleton, which geotechnical engineers call effective stress. If pore pressure rises, effective stress falls. It is entirely possible to weaken a soil without changing the friction angle of its grains. In the simplified example, if pore pressure reduces effective normal stress by half, the frictional factor of safety also falls by roughly half, from about one point two to about zero point six. Under those assumptions, the slope is no longer in equilibrium.

That numerical example is not a prediction for any real hill. Soil cohesion, root reinforcement, irregular slip geometry, drainage paths, transient saturation, and three-dimensional effects all matter. Its purpose is to reveal why water changes a slope's strength in a way that is different from simply adding weight. A soil can be visibly damp without having positive pore pressure at the possible slip surface. Conversely, water trapped above a low-permeability layer can build pressure where the surface looks dry. The measurement that matters may lie a meter beneath our feet.

Time also matters. A short intense storm may run over the surface of one hillside but infiltrate quickly into another. Weeks of steady rain may raise a deep groundwater level even without spectacular hourly intensity. Snowmelt can prolong recharge. Where fractures conduct water rapidly to a buried shear plane, pressure may respond quickly to a storm. A clay-rich slope with slow drainage may respond much later. Rainfall thresholds are useful for warnings only when matched to regional geology and the kinds of movement being anticipated. One rainfall number cannot specify the stability of every hillside.

The word landslide covers several distinct movements. Rockfall begins when a block detaches and moves by falling, bouncing, or rolling. A rockslide translates along a comparatively planar surface. A rotational slide follows a curved failure surface and may leave a head scarp and backward-tilted blocks. A slow earthflow can move over months or years. A deep-seated landslide can creep, crack roads, and repeatedly reactivate long before a dramatic failure. Debris flows behave more like rapidly moving mixtures; we will treat their distinct dynamics separately. The names are not bookkeeping trivia. They tell us what evidence to seek and which mathematical description might be appropriate.

Look carefully at a hypothetical road cut through layered rock. The excavation removes material at the base of a natural hillside. A bedding plane inclined toward the road daylighting in the new cut has lost its former support. Even without more rainfall, the slope's geometry and resisting forces have changed. An engineer might consider reducing the angle, adding drainage, using rock bolts, installing a retaining structure, or relocating the road. Each intervention addresses a different part of the problem. A bolt does little to prevent a boulder from rolling off a higher, unattached face unless it anchors the actual unstable block. A drainage system can be effective only if it reaches the water pathways controlling pressure.

There is another slow actor: erosion at the toe. Rivers undercut a bluff, waves remove beach cliffs, and glaciers once carved steep valley sides. The mass above may seem untouched while support below is progressively removed. A river can shift against the foot of a slope after a flood. Human alterations can create a similar effect by excavating, loading an embankment, redirecting runoff, or allowing a leaking pipe to recharge a weak layer. Human and natural causes are often intertwined. Labeling the final rainstorm as the cause may conceal the decades of geological and land-use changes that prepared the failure.

On March twenty-second, twenty fourteen, a large landslide near Oso, Washington, swept across the North Fork Stillaguamish River floodplain. It killed forty-three people. The affected bluff included glacially deposited sediment and evidence of earlier landslides. Research documented prolonged wet conditions and investigated how water-saturated sediment could contribute to extraordinary mobility. The event is a serious warning about two separate questions: why material becomes unstable in the first place, and how far it can travel afterward. A slope may not look exceptionally steep and may still produce a destructive, long-traveling movement. The absence of a recent disaster is not proof of safety.

How would we investigate a hillside today? First, use topography and field observations to identify old scarps, hummocky deposits, bulging toes, cracks, tilted trees, seepage, and displaced infrastructure. Lidar, which measures the ground surface from reflected light, may reveal ancient landslide shapes beneath forest cover. Next, map the materials: intact rock, weathered rock, soils, clay seams, and their orientation. Boreholes and laboratory tests can estimate strengths, and instruments such as inclinometers can locate zones of movement. Piezometers record water pressure. Repeat satellite radar measurements may detect slow deformation over broad areas, though vegetation, geometry, and rapid failure impose limitations. No one instrument delivers a universal answer.

A striking feature of slope monitoring is that movement need not mean immediate collapse. Some deep landslides creep for years under changing water levels. Others accelerate toward failure. In certain settings, changing displacement rates, pore pressures, and rainfall can inform short-term decisions. Yet forecasting the precise instant of a rapid collapse remains difficult because the subsurface geometry and strength are incompletely known. A responsible assessment distinguishes known movement, plausible mechanisms, and the parts of the slope that have never been observed. The difference between a warning and a prediction matters to the people living below.

An exposed cliff can also be stable in bulk while small rockfalls remain possible. A factor of safety calculated for a broad slip surface does not rule out a detached block falling from a crack. Protective structures—catchment fences, barriers, ditches, and rock sheds—are designed around possible block trajectories and impact energies, not only the average slope angle. Geology is translated into engineering through specific failure modes. If a map shows only that a hillside is steep, it has not yet explained whether the hazard is sliding, toppling, flowing, or falling.

The fundamental insight is that a hillside has a balance sheet, not an expiration date. Gravity supplies the driving force. Friction, cohesion, rock bridges, and sometimes roots supply resistance. Water pressure alters the load carried by grains. Erosion, excavation, and weathering change geometry and strength. The science begins with this balance, but it becomes useful only when we discover where the actual weak surfaces lie, how their pressures change, and what happens if they move. A quiet mountain is not motionless. It is a mechanical structure being continually renegotiated.

## Scientific companion: slope mechanics

A Coulomb-type strength model is \(\tau_f=c'+(\sigma_n-u)\tan\phi'\), where \(c'\) is effective cohesion, \(\phi'\) effective friction angle, \(\sigma_n\) total normal stress and \(u\) pore-water pressure. Effective normal stress is \(\sigma_n'=\sigma_n-u\). A factor of safety is model resistance divided by model driving stress; it is **not** a failure probability.

For a dry, cohesionless, infinite slope with a slip surface parallel to the ground, \(FS=\tan\phi'/\tan\beta\). With \(\phi'=35^\circ\) and \(\beta=30^\circ\), \(FS\approx1.21\). If pore pressure halves effective normal stress, with other stresses fixed in this simplified example, \(FS\approx0.61\). Real slopes require proper unit weight, drainage, thickness, geometry, cohesion and loading conditions.

**Distinguish:** failure initiation; motion type; mobility and runout; local susceptibility; and consequence. One successful slope-stability calculation cannot resolve all five.
