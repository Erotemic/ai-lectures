# A Cliff Is an Order of Events: Relative Time, Faults, and Missing Chapters

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

Imagine standing below a sea cliff where pale, almost horizontal sandstone rests on steeply tilted dark beds. The boundary between them is irregular. Some of the lower beds end abruptly at the contact; pebbles from those dark rocks occur in the sandstone above. Without measuring an isotope or identifying a fossil, you can already deduce an entire sequence. The dark beds were deposited. They became rock. They were tilted and eroded. Only then did the sand above accumulate. What looks like a dividing line on the cliff is a substantial episode in Earth's history.

This is the power of relative geological time. Before geologists could assign numerical ages measured in millions of years, they could establish which events preceded others. The reasoning is still indispensable. Radiometric dates supply numerical anchors, but they do not replace the observed order of beds, faults, intrusions, erosional surfaces, and mineral growth. To read the rocks, first learn how to ask what could not have happened before what.

Start with the principle of superposition. In an undisturbed succession of sedimentary layers, beds deposited on the bottom are generally older than those laid on top. The rule depends on knowing which way was originally up and whether the sequence has been overturned. Ripple shapes, graded beds, mud cracks, and other sedimentary structures can help determine facing direction. An overturned fold can leave older rock above younger rock in a geometric sense while the original depositional order remains entirely consistent.

The related principle of original horizontality is often summarized as saying that sedimentary beds begin flat. That is a useful first approximation, but a strict literal version is false. Dunes have sloping internal layers. River bars build inclined surfaces. Deltas can deposit sloping foresets. Many sedimentary surfaces are approximately level over a local scale, and large regions of later-tilted strata often reveal subsequent deformation. We need to distinguish a tilted package of originally near-horizontal beds from a sloping structure produced during deposition itself.

Cross-cutting relationships are a second kind of clock. If a fault breaks a rock unit, the faulting happened after that unit existed. If a magma-filled fracture cuts across several layers and cools into a dike, the intrusion is younger than the layers it crosses. A fragment enclosed within a rock is generally older than the rock that incorporated it. These rules are powerful because they use geometry as evidence. They can also be misread if a later event reactivated an older fault or if a complicated contact only appears to cut in two dimensions.

Let's work through a fictitious outcrop. A lower shale lies beneath a sandstone. Both are folded into a broad arch. A vertical dike cuts through the folded beds. A normal fault offsets the dike as well as the layers. Finally, an erosion surface truncates the fault and is covered by an undeformed conglomerate. The minimum event order is: deposition of shale, then sandstone; folding; dike intrusion; fault displacement; erosion; and conglomerate deposition. The exact timing between deformation and intrusion can be more complex if some folding continued during intrusion, but the stated cross-cutting relationships constrain the basic order.

Now change just one observation. Suppose the normal fault ends at the dike, and the dike crosses the fault without any displacement. The dike must then postdate the observed fault movement. A single new contact reverses the inferred relationship between two events. That is why a geological cross section is not a decorative drawing. It is a set of proposed chronological and spatial constraints, open to being revised when mapping reveals a new intersection.

The biggest clues sometimes lie in the absence of rock. An unconformity is a surface or relationship representing a break in deposition, erosion, or both. At an angular unconformity, older tilted or folded strata are truncated and overlain by younger beds of a different orientation. At a disconformity, younger and older sedimentary layers may remain approximately parallel, with the missing interval recorded by an erosional or nondepositional surface. Where sedimentary strata lie directly on crystalline basement, geologists often use the term nonconformity. The names describe relationships, not three distinct physical forces.

The famous exposure at Siccar Point on the Scottish coast made the stakes of this reasoning clear to James Hutton and his companions in 1788. Nearly vertical Silurian strata lie beneath gently inclined Devonian sandstones. Hutton recognized that this arrangement required an earlier episode of sediment deposition, lithification, deformation, erosion and renewed deposition. The significance was not that he could attach a precise age to either unit. It was that the visible contact demanded a history far longer and more cyclic than a simple, single deposition of Earth's rocks could accommodate.

An unconformity can be easy to identify at a cliff and difficult to map across a buried basin. Geologists look for truncated beds in seismic images, missing fossil zones, erosional relief, changes in weathering, or a sharp break in ages. But a sharp change in rock type is not always an unconformity. A normal transition from sand to mud may occur during continuous deposition. Nor does an unconformity automatically tell us how much time is missing. That must come from age constraints, fossil correlations, and regional reconstruction.

How do we read a fold? A fold bends rock layers; an anticline is defined by older rocks toward its core when stratigraphic younging is known, and a syncline by younger rocks toward its core. The purely geometric terms antiform and synform avoid that age implication when the original order is uncertain. Measurements of strike and dip record the orientation of a planar surface. A collection of these measurements can reveal a fold axis, a tilted block, or a fault-bounded structure even where only short segments of rock are exposed.

The surface geological map is an intersection between three-dimensional layers and an irregular land surface. A single layer can appear as two separate bands on a map because a valley cuts through a fold. A dipping sheet may appear wider where the ground surface nearly parallels it. The color pattern of a map therefore does not by itself reveal the thickness or geometry of a unit. Geologists build cross sections by combining the map with orientations, topography, boreholes and geological knowledge, then test whether the interpretation is geometrically plausible.

Faults make reconstruction more demanding. A fault can remove part of a sequence from a vertical section or repeat it. Some faults cut upward to the surface; others terminate underground. Deformation can occur in more than one episode, so a newly mapped fault may reactivate a much older structure. The oldest fault surface and the latest motion along it are not necessarily the same age. Sometimes the most scientifically responsible cross section includes two plausible subsurface alternatives instead of one confident line extending through unobserved rock.

Fossils add another ordering method. Assemblages of organisms change through geological time; where their distributions are sufficiently known, they can help correlate sedimentary rocks between distant exposures. They can also indicate environment. A marine fossil in a sandstone tells us something different from a radiometric clock in a volcanic ash bed interleaved with the sediments. The fossil may constrain the time interval and setting, while the ash can provide a numerical age for a near-synchronous deposition event. Together they can substantially improve the chronology.

These relative-time tools are most powerful when they are treated as claims that can be falsified. If a proposed older fault is offset by a supposedly younger one, look for the intersection. If an intrusion is alleged to predate a sedimentary unit, search for eroded fragments of the intrusion in the overlying deposit or contact metamorphism where it cuts the sediment. If a hill is interpreted as a folded layer, trace its rock identity and orientation across valleys. Geological interpretation improves when it predicts what the next exposure should show.

A practical exercise makes the map-to-history transition clear. Suppose a geological map shows two long, parallel outcrop bands of the same limestone separated by sandstone. There are at least two explanations. The rock could have been folded, bringing the limestone to the surface twice; or a thrust fault could have repeated the sequence. Dip directions, the facing of sedimentary structures, and the presence or absence of a fault contact help distinguish the alternatives. Finding limestone in two bands is an observation. Assigning a fold or fault is an interpretation that needs further observations.

Now imagine the map includes a steep dike crossing both bands. If the dike remains continuous while the limestone bands curve, we learn something about their relative histories; if the dike itself is bent or offset, the deformation may be later. Looking along the dike for thermal alteration of adjoining sedimentary rock can establish that magma intruded the older layers. An additional photograph may be less useful than a carefully mapped contact, because the contact establishes which physical process had to come first. The discipline is to seek observations that separate alternatives rather than accumulate dramatic-looking images.

The cliff is not a linear diary whose pages were all preserved. It is a collage assembled by deposition, deformation, intrusion, erosion, burial, and exposure. Some pages repeat because a fault moved them. Some are upside down. Some were destroyed before later pages were added. The craft of relative dating is to establish the parts of the order that evidence compels, and to separate them from the missing episodes we can only reconstruct provisionally. A small stretch of exposed rock can contain a large amount of history, precisely because its relationships record changes that no single layer could tell us alone.

## Scientific companion: solve a relative-age puzzle

**Fictitious sequence A:** shale \(S\) deposited; sandstone \(T\) deposited above \(S\); both folded \(F\); dike \(D\) intrudes; normal fault \(N\) offsets \(D\); erosion surface \(E\) truncates \(N\); conglomerate \(C\) deposited. Minimum relation: \(S<T<F<D<N<E<C\). The symbols indicate ordered events, not duration or absolute age; depositional episodes may overlap and additional unrecorded processes are possible.

**Sequence B change:** If \(D\) cuts an already displaced fault \(N\) and itself remains undisplaced, the mapped episode of \(N\) predates \(D\). Beware a fault reactivated later, for which there may be multiple event ages.

**Relative-age evidence:** superposition with original facing; cross cutting; inclusions; unconformities; fossil succession; structural orientation. Numerical ages complement rather than replace this ordering.

**Geometry caution:** sedimentary beds can be deposited on slopes; 'original horizontality' is a working approximation for many but not all depositional environments.
