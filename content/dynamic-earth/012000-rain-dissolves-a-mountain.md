# Rain Dissolves a Mountain: Weathering, Soils, and the Long Carbon Cycle

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

A mountain can be carried away in a landslide, grain by grain down a river, or even molecule by molecule in water so clear that it looks empty. The first two are easy to picture. The third may be more consequential than it appears. Rain, soil organisms, minerals, streams, and the ocean participate in reactions that slowly move elements out of rock and, over geological time, help regulate the amount of carbon dioxide in the atmosphere.

Begin with the difference between breaking rock and changing rock. Physical weathering breaks a boulder into smaller pieces without necessarily changing the chemical identity of the material. A rock expands and contracts as temperatures change; ice growth in fractures can help pry material apart under favorable conditions; roots may exploit joints; salt crystals and stress release can contribute. Chemical weathering changes minerals through reaction. Water can dissolve ions, transform feldspars into clay minerals, oxidize iron-bearing components, or transport dissolved silica. In real soils the two processes interact: fractures expose fresh surfaces, while chemical changes may weaken the rock and make it easier to break.

Water is not simply a neutral solvent washing grit downhill. Rainwater takes up carbon dioxide from air, and soil water often contains even more dissolved carbon dioxide because roots and microbes respire. The resulting water is weakly acidic. When it moves through rock, it can react with minerals and export dissolved products. A cave cut through limestone is one visible example of dissolution, but invisible weathering is happening through soils and fractured silicate rocks as well.

There is a useful distinction between limestone weathering and silicate weathering. Dissolving carbonate rock with carbonic acid involves carbon in the rock and carbon from water. When that dissolved material ultimately reprecipitates as carbonate in the ocean, the net long-term atmospheric carbon effect of the ideal carbonate-weathering-and-reprecipitation sequence is approximately neutral. Silicate weathering can have a different longer-term balance. Calcium or magnesium-bearing silicate minerals react with carbon dioxide-rich water; their cations and dissolved bicarbonate can travel to the sea; when marine carbonates are buried, the combined cycle can remove carbon dioxide from the atmosphere-ocean reservoir. The distinction depends on which reactions and eventual sinks are included. Simply noticing that water carries bicarbonate does not by itself prove a permanent carbon sink.

Take a simplified silicate mineral, calcium silicate. Let it react with carbon dioxide and water to form dissolved calcium and bicarbonate. Later, precipitation of calcium carbonate in the ocean releases some carbon dioxide back. Combine the two reactions and, in the standard idealization, one net molecule of atmospheric or oceanic carbon dioxide is consumed per calcium silicate converted and carbonate buried. Real mineral weathering involves multicomponent rocks, secondary clays, ion exchange, variable transport and biological processes. But the bookkeeping explains how a landscape can participate in climate regulation without anything resembling a leaf or a smokestack.

Weathering rates depend on more than temperature. They depend on water supply, flow paths, acidity, organisms, available fresh mineral surface, and how rapidly reaction products are removed. A hot, wet slope may weather quickly if it contains reactive minerals and water can reach them. A warm site with almost no runoff can behave quite differently. An old, deeply weathered soil may have exhausted much of its easily weathered primary mineral supply. A newly uplifted mountain or glacially ground landscape can continually expose fresh grains. Thus tectonic uplift and erosion may maintain the chemical opportunity for weathering, while climate controls much of the water and reaction environment.

The word soil deserves attention. Soil is not merely powdered rock. It contains mineral grains, organic matter, water, gases, roots, microbes, and networks of pores. Water entering a soil may flow quickly through cracks or more slowly through fine pores. Roots alter chemical conditions. Microorganisms catalyze reactions and recycle organic compounds. Clay minerals provide charged surfaces that exchange ions. The chemical composition of water at the bottom of a soil profile may therefore differ markedly from the rain that entered at the top.

Suppose you collect two stream samples. The first comes from a high mountain catchment after a storm. It is cloudy with suspended sediment. The second comes from a lowland spring and looks perfectly clear. It would be wrong to infer that the cloudy sample necessarily carries more total rock-derived material. The spring water might contain a substantial dissolved load of calcium, sodium, bicarbonate, and silica invisible to the eye. Researchers distinguish suspended sediment flux from dissolved solute flux, then measure water discharge to calculate the mass transported. Concentration alone is insufficient: a modest concentration in a large river can represent an enormous export of material.

The geological carbon-cycle feedback is often described in a sentence: more atmospheric carbon dioxide warms the climate, warming and runoff can accelerate silicate weathering, and weathering draws down carbon dioxide. This is a useful general negative-feedback mechanism, but it operates on long timescales and is not a quick thermostat. The direction and strength of the response depend on the landscape's fresh-rock supply and hydrology. It does not erase the consequences of human carbon emissions over the next several decades. Models and geochemical evidence are needed to estimate its pace, and those estimates are sensitive to assumptions about erosion, exposed minerals and how runoff changes.

You can see the coupling in a collision mountain range. Tectonics creates steep slopes, fractures rock, and raises terrain into strong precipitation gradients. Erosion exports sediment and exposes new bedrock. Chemical weathering mobilizes dissolved ions. Rivers carry both solid and dissolved loads to downstream basins and ultimately to the sea. Meanwhile the changing mountain's influence on weather can affect vegetation and water flow. Calling weathering a chemical process is correct, but treating it as isolated chemistry misses why its rates can change as landscapes evolve.

One prominent example is the weathering of feldspars. Feldspar is a major component of many rocks. In water containing acidity, chemical reaction can release dissolved cations and silica while forming secondary clay minerals. The new mineral has a different crystal structure and different interaction with water. Alteration may increase porosity in one setting, fill pores in another, or leave a weakened rind around a stronger core. This helps explain why rock faces often crumble along zones that have been exposed to fluids, even when they look coherent from a distance.

Not all chemical weathering removes carbon dioxide. The oxidation of sulfide minerals, for example, can generate acid; the acid can dissolve carbonate minerals and in some settings produce carbon dioxide. Sulfuric-acid-driven weathering therefore does not have the same carbon accounting as pure carbonic-acid-driven silicate weathering. The geological carbon budget must account for actual acids, minerals, transport and burial, not rely on a slogan that all rock dissolution cools the planet.

Weathering also controls hazards and resources. It can weaken slopes, alter fracture surfaces, and change how water moves through an aquifer. It creates soil minerals that retain nutrients, but it can also mobilize potentially harmful elements under particular conditions. The same chemical principles help interpret mining contamination, drinking-water composition, and the mineral availability that supports ecosystems. Geological weathering is one bridge between a mountain's deep history and its present biological environment.

What would it take to measure the loss of a mountain by chemical weathering? You would need samples representing water throughout the drainage network and across seasons, not one bottle collected on a sunny afternoon. You would measure discharge, dissolved ions, perhaps isotopic signatures that help distinguish sources, and inputs from rain and human activities. Then you would calculate exports and compare them with rock composition and the transport of solid sediment. You would still face questions about groundwater travel times and material stored temporarily within the basin. Measuring the disappearance of rock turns into a careful mass-balance problem.

A comparison of two catchments makes the measurement problem more concrete. One drains fresh basalt recently exposed by an eruption, while another drains an older granite landscape with deep soils. Suppose the basalt stream has twice the measured dissolved calcium concentration. It does not follow that it is removing twice as much rock. We need the volume of water flowing through each catchment, the contribution of precipitation and groundwater, and other dissolved sources. We also need to know whether calcium came from primary silicates, carbonate veins, or human additions. To estimate carbon dioxide consumption, geochemists may combine ion ratios and isotopic measurements with models of the possible mineral reactions. An apparently simple water sample becomes a testable account of its chemical origins.

A second caution concerns the phrase 'negative feedback.' Feedback does not mean the climate stays at one fixed temperature. It means that a disturbance can change a process in a direction that opposes part of the original disturbance. If weathering is limited by the supply of fresh silicate minerals or by a lack of runoff, that stabilizing response may be weak for a time. Even if it eventually becomes important, its geological timescale can be far longer than the lifetime of a human community. Deep-time regulation is an explanation of Earth's long history, not a reason to ignore rapidly changing conditions now.

A mountain disappears in several languages of physics. Its cliffs fracture. Its rivers move sediment. Its soils turn minerals into other minerals. Its streams carry invisible dissolved matter toward the ocean. Some of those reactions participate in a carbon cycle extending from atmosphere to rock and back through burial and volcanism. The lesson is not that mountains are fragile. It is that even Earth's hardest landscapes are chemically open systems, exchanging matter with water, air, life, and the deep planet for millions of years.

## Scientific companion: weathering and long-term carbon balance

**Idealized silicate reaction:** \(\mathrm{CaSiO_3 + 2CO_2 + 3H_2O \to Ca^{2+} + 2HCO_3^- + H_4SiO_4}\), followed by \(\mathrm{Ca^{2+} + 2HCO_3^- \to CaCO_3 + CO_2 + H_2O}\). Net: \(\mathrm{CaSiO_3+CO_2+2H_2O\to CaCO_3+H_4SiO_4}\). This is bookkeeping for a specific idealized pathway, not a literal universal mineral reaction for every catchment.

**Carbonate contrast:** dissolution \(\mathrm{CaCO_3+CO_2+H_2O\to Ca^{2+}+2HCO_3^-}\); marine precipitation reverses this net reaction under the simplified closed cycle. Externally supplied acid and organic carbon complicate real budgets.

**Flux calculation:** for a dissolved ion of concentration \(C\) kg m\(^{-3}\) and volume discharge \(Q\) m\(^3\) s\(^{-1}\), mass export \(F=CQ\) kg s\(^{-1}\). An observed concentration should not be mistaken for an export rate without discharge data.

**Timescale caution:** silicate weathering feedback is long-term; it does not stabilize the climate over a single heatwave or offset present-day emissions promptly.
