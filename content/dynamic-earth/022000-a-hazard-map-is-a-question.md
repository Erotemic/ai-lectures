# A Hazard Map Is a Question: Probability, Exposure, and Geological Risk

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

A colored map can make an uncertain world appear settled. A mountain valley appears in red, a broad plain in yellow, and a cluster of homes in green. A resident wants to know: Is my house safe? A planner wants to know: Where should the new road go? The map seems to answer both questions at a glance. But before reading its colors, we need to know what its maker actually mapped. The map may show the locations of past landslides, the terrain most susceptible to future ones, the chance of movement under a specified storm, or the possible losses to people and property. Those are four different questions.

Begin with an inventory. Geologists identify old and recent landslides from field observations, historical reports, aerial photographs, satellite imagery, and high-resolution topography. A landslide often leaves a head scarp, a displaced mass, and a disturbed toe. Large failures can remain visible for centuries. Repeated lidar surveys can expose changes that are hard to see on a forested hillside. An inventory tells us where recognizable landslides occurred or were mapped, but it is not a complete record of every past event. Some deposits are eroded or buried. Some remote slopes are never surveyed. Some damaging failures have detailed reports while countless harmless ones go unrecorded.

That bias creates a trap. Suppose a machine-learning model is trained on a map of known landslides. Roads and towns may be overrepresented because people inspected them and reported failures. Forested inaccessible valleys may be underrepresented. The model can learn the pattern of observation as well as the pattern of geology. A region with fewer reported landslides is not necessarily more stable. Good inventory work must therefore track the origin and confidence of records, and good model evaluation must consider whether the test data share the same mapping biases as the training data.

A susceptibility map asks which places have conditions associated with failures. Slope angle, terrain curvature, rock and soil type, drainage, vegetation, and evidence of prior movement are common ingredients. The USGS's continental-scale landslide susceptibility mapping uses high-resolution elevation and inventories to improve broad coverage. But its cells do not measure the precise stability of every garden or foundation. An old slide scar may be smaller than a grid cell, and a steep source area may threaten a building far downslope. Susceptibility is a spatial ranking or model output, not necessarily a dated prediction that movement will happen tomorrow or within ten years.

To ask about *when*, we need triggers. A rainfall-induced shallow landslide might depend on storm intensity, accumulated rain, soil moisture, and pore pressure. An earthquake-induced failure depends on shaking and material strength. A coastal cliff might fail after persistent wave undercutting. When a model estimates the probability of a landslide conditional on a particular storm, we should say exactly that. It does not automatically give the chance that the storm itself will occur in a year. To build an annual hazard estimate, we need information about the frequencies of different triggers and how the landscape responds to them.

Consider a made-up map that says a hillside has a twenty percent chance of failing *if* a storm delivers a specified rainfall intensity. Suppose a storm of that intensity has a five percent chance of occurring in a year. If only that trigger mattered, the simplest annualized product would be one percent. But actual calculations may need to consider other storm intensities, multiple events, changing vegetation, correlated conditions, and uncertainty about the probabilities themselves. The example teaches the difference between a conditional probability and an unconditional one. It is not a practical method for certifying any real hillside.

Even an annual probability is easy to misread. A one-percent annual event is sometimes called a hundred-year event. That shorthand does not mean one such event happens every hundred years like a scheduled appointment. Under the simplifying assumptions that yearly events have independent, identical one-percent probabilities, the chance of at least one occurrence over thirty years is one minus zero point nine nine raised to the thirtieth power—approximately twenty-six percent. If the climate, drainage, or slope condition changes during the period, the assumption of a fixed yearly probability may fail. Geological hazards are not always stationary.

Now consider travel distance. A hillside may fail, but the movement can stop quickly or cross a valley. To estimate where material travels, scientists model runout using the initiating volume, topography, material behavior, water content, and possible entrainment. For a fast rockfall, the relevant question may be the energy and bounce trajectory of blocks. For a debris flow, it may be the inundation depth across an alluvial fan. For a deep landslide, a road above its head scarp may be threatened by ground cracking even though it lies outside the downslope deposit. Risk maps that simply color the source slopes can miss these distinct exposure pathways.

Here is an instructive distinction from post-wildfire studies. An assessment may estimate the likelihood and volume of debris flows exiting burned basins under a standardized rainstorm. That is useful for prioritizing attention. But it should not be mistaken for a complete inundation map of neighborhoods downhill. Source-basin volume, path selection, bridges, embankments, buildings, and flow spreading determine local impacts. One map can be correct for the calculation it performs and still be the wrong tool for deciding whether a particular house will be struck.

A hazard becomes a risk when something exposed can be harmed. A rocky slope that falls into an unoccupied canyon can pose a severe geological hazard but little immediate human loss. A smaller failure above a busy highway can pose substantial risk. Exposure includes roads, homes, pipelines, electrical lines, schools, and ecosystems. Vulnerability asks how likely those things are to be damaged under the relevant intensity, duration, or displacement. A reinforced structure may respond differently from an older building. A bridge can fail from scour or debris blockage even when its foundations are outside the main flow path.

One formal way to think about expected loss is to combine probabilities of different events, the amounts exposed, and their vulnerability. But adding all these numbers into a single risk score can hide important distinctions. A low-frequency catastrophic event and frequent smaller disruptions might share the same average annual dollar loss, yet require different preparedness. Mortality, service interruption, cultural sites, and ecological effects cannot always be reduced to one financial currency. The mapmaker should tell users what the score measures, which consequences it omits, and at what spatial scale it applies.

A case like the twenty-fourteen Oso landslide shows how historical and topographic evidence can matter. Pre-event lidar identified forms of earlier landslides in the same valley. That history offered clues to terrain susceptibility, but it did not specify exactly how the twenty-fourteen event would unfold or how far the highly mobile mass would travel. Retrospective studies can improve understanding without implying that a complete, precise warning was technically available beforehand. The appropriate lesson is to examine repeat failures, runout, and uncertainties together, not to treat earlier mapping as an infallible clock.

Maps are also products of decisions. A modeler chooses which geological variables to include and how to handle gaps. They set the spatial grid size, select the training and testing periods, define an event class, and pick a color scale. A map of past ground motion will not automatically capture future landslides. A national screening map cannot replace a geotechnical investigation at a proposed dam. High resolution helps, but it does not create information about deep rock strength where no boreholes exist. More detailed colors can convey a false sense of precision if the measurements underneath remain sparse.

What should a responsible decision-maker do with such maps? Use them to ask sharper questions. Does a proposed road cross a mapped old slide? Is the main danger from a source slope or its runout? What rain or earthquake scenario is being considered? Are monitoring instruments available? Can the alignment avoid the hazard, or would drainage, slope stabilization, or protective works be needed? What would trigger a temporary closure? Does the map include neighboring terrain capable of sending rock or water into the site? The value of a map is often that it tells us where investigation should be concentrated.

Monitoring adds a time dimension. Rain gauges, soil-water instruments, extensometers, ground-based radar, repeated lidar, and satellite measurements can detect conditions or movement. But there is no universal rule that a particular displacement speed guarantees failure within a fixed number of hours. An early-warning system has to account for false alarms, missed events, communication time, and the actions people can take. A warning that arrives after the only safe road is blocked is not a functioning protection system, however accurate the scientific diagnosis might eventually become.

Asking whether a map is right is less productive than asking what claim it supports. A good geological map is an argument drawn on a landscape, with observations, assumptions, and uncertainty behind every color. The legend is part of the science. When we distinguish susceptibility from probability, probability from runout, and hazard from risk, the map becomes much more powerful. It no longer promises to remove uncertainty. It gives us a disciplined way to make decisions while knowing which uncertainty remains.

## Scientific companion: conditional probability and loss

For an idealized fixed, independent annual probability \(p\), the chance of at least one event in \(n\) years is \(1-(1-p)^n\). For \(p=0.01\), \(n=30\), the value is about \(0.260\). This is **not** valid without assumptions about stationarity and year-to-year independence.

The schematic \(P(\mathrm{failure\ in\ year})=P(\mathrm{trigger\ in\ year})P(\mathrm{failure}\mid\mathrm{trigger})\) applies only to a carefully defined single trigger and event class. Actual multitrigger models require summation or integration over triggering scenarios and dependencies.

**Hazard taxonomy:** landslide inventory (observed events); susceptibility (spatial predisposition); conditional hazard (event probability given scenario); runout/intensity (movement and impact footprint); exposure/vulnerability (consequences); risk (combination of likelihood and consequences).
