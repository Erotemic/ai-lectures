# How Rock Becomes Magma: Melting Without an Underground Ocean

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

Why does a volcano exist in one place and not in another? The tempting answer is that Earth is hot inside, and some of that heat leaks out through holes. But most of Earth's mantle is solid even though it is far hotter than an oven. A volcano requires particular conditions under which part of a rock melts, the resulting liquid separates from surrounding solid, and enough of it can reach the surface. That process involves temperature, pressure, water, chemistry, and pathways through the crust. There is no single global layer of molten rock waiting beneath every mountain.

Start with a piece of mantle rock, usually represented as peridotite, a rock rich in minerals such as olivine and pyroxene. At a given pressure, it begins to melt when the temperature exceeds the appropriate solidus for that material. Unlike a pure substance that melts at one temperature under specified pressure, a rock is a mixture of minerals. Partial melting begins with some components entering the melt while much of the original rock remains solid. The melt and the residue can therefore have different compositions.

The first major way to make magma is decompression melting. Beneath a spreading ridge, mantle rises as the plates separate. As pressure decreases, the rock may cross its solidus even if its temperature has not increased substantially during ascent. It begins to melt. Basaltic melt can form oceanic crust at a ridge and, under other circumstances, in some intraplate volcanic regions. The explanation is not that friction between separating plates heats everything until it liquefies. It is that pressure changes the conditions under which an already hot material can remain solid.

The second major route is flux melting, especially important in many subduction-related volcanic arcs. Descending oceanic lithosphere includes altered crust, hydrated mantle minerals, and sediments that contain water or other volatile-bearing components. As those materials change under pressure and temperature, fluids can enter the mantle wedge. Water alters the melting behavior of the mantle and can enable partial melting at temperatures that would not melt the same dry rock. In the Andes and the Japanese arcs, this is part of the explanation for abundant volcanism above descending slabs.

The third broad route is heating rock toward its melting conditions. Hot magma entering cooler crust can transfer energy and, in suitable circumstances, cause partial melting of crustal rocks. Magma can also mix with other magmas. This process matters particularly where prolonged intrusion, thick crust, and differences in chemical composition lead to complex magmatic systems. But heating alone should not become the universal explanation. It is one route among several, with different consequences for the products.

What comes out of the melting process is not necessarily the rock that goes in. Partial melts often have different concentrations of chemical components than their source. Some elements enter a liquid more readily than others. A basaltic melt produced from peridotite is not simply melted peridotite in a different physical state. The remaining solid changes composition as melt is extracted. Geochemists use the chemical patterns of lavas and minerals to infer source conditions and the extent and history of melting, while recognizing that later processes can modify those patterns.

Once melt forms, it must move. The melt may percolate through connected pores, collect in channels, or exploit fractures and faults. As melt accumulates it can form magma reservoirs, dikes, and sills. The familiar picture of a single enormous cavern filled with perfectly mixed liquid is often misleading. A subsurface magmatic system can include crystal-rich mush, interconnected melt bodies, and repeated intrusions that evolve through time. A volcano may receive several magma batches rather than one coherent tank of liquid.

As magma cools, crystals form. If those crystals are separated from the remaining melt, the chemistry of the liquid changes. This is fractional crystallization. For example, when certain magnesium- and iron-rich minerals crystallize early from a basaltic magma, the remaining liquid can become relatively enriched in components such as silica. Crustal assimilation, magma mixing, and different source compositions can further complicate the outcome. It is therefore unsafe to infer a volcano's source depth or tectonic setting from one silica percentage alone.

Temperature, pressure, dissolved water, and composition govern a magma's physical properties. Some magmas flow relatively easily; others are resistant to flow. Silica-rich melts often have more polymerized structures and greater viscosity, all else being equal, but the exact behavior also depends on temperature, crystals, and gas. A magma with abundant dissolved volatiles may change drastically during ascent as pressure falls. Gases that remained dissolved at depth can exsolve into bubbles, and the fate of those bubbles is central to the difference between a lava flow and an explosive eruption.

Consider a bottle of sparkling water opened after being kept under pressure. The carbon dioxide was more soluble at the higher pressure, and bubbles form as pressure falls. Magma is not a beverage, but decompression can also lead to volatile exsolution. Water vapor, carbon dioxide, and sulfur-bearing species can form gas phases as magma rises. If gas escapes readily, pressure can be relieved. If bubbles are trapped in a viscous or rapidly ascending magma, pressure and fragmentation can produce violent eruptions. The analogy explains the direction of the change; actual magmas involve much higher temperatures, multiple volatiles, moving crystals, and complex conduits.

A location such as Hawaii helps separate volcanoes from plate boundaries. Hawaiian volcanism occurs within the Pacific Plate rather than at a present plate margin. Theories involving mantle upwelling and melting explain much of the hotspot system, but the precise structure and origin of each hotspot must be investigated rather than assumed from a cartoon. The trail of older Hawaiian-Emperor volcanic islands and seamounts records relative motion between the plate and the region of volcanic generation, with complications from changes in both plate and hotspot motion. The chain gives a record of a moving plate, not a permanently identical subterranean pipe.

Another instructive comparison is Iceland, where a mid-ocean ridge comes above sea level and interacts with an anomalous supply of heat and magma. Iceland is not merely a volcano placed on a ridge; its crustal thickness, rifting, glacial history, and mantle processes make it an especially rich natural laboratory. At subduction arcs, by contrast, the addition of slab-derived fluids, different crustal thicknesses, and longer chemical evolution can produce a wide range of magma compositions and styles. The tectonic setting constrains possibilities without determining every eruption in advance.

How do scientists test these explanations? They analyze major and trace elements, isotope ratios, mineral compositions, crystal zoning, melt inclusions, temperature and pressure indicators, and seismic or deformation observations. They compare lavas to experimental melting of likely source rocks. Each measurement has ambiguities. A change in lava chemistry could reflect a different source, a different amount of melting, later crystallization, or assimilation of crustal rock. Combining evidence narrows the choices.

The central insight is that magma is a product of conditions and history, not a default underground fluid. Rocks melt partly when pressure falls, water changes the solidus, or sufficient heat is added. Melts rise, react, crystallize, mix, and may pause in reservoirs. Their compositions and gas inventories change along the way. A volcanic eruption at the surface is the visible end of a process that began with solid rock and continued through a chain of physical and chemical transformations.

## Scientific companion: three melting routes

**Decompression melting:** a hot parcel ascends; pressure decreases and the mantle solidus may be crossed. **Flux melting:** water or other volatiles alter melting conditions (especially mantle wedge above subducting slab). **Heating:** intrusion of hotter magma may trigger crustal partial melting. These mechanisms may coexist.

**Partial melting is fractionation:** melt differs from source and residue; degree of melting, partition coefficients and mineral assemblage govern trace-element patterns. Subsequent differentiation by fractional crystallization, mixing and contamination can overwrite parts of a primary signal.

**Gas expansion during ascent:** dissolved volatile solubility often decreases as pressure drops. Bubble nucleation, permeability, conduit ascent and viscosity affect whether gases escape or fragment the melt.

**Illustrative energy distinction:** a parcel can begin to melt on ascent because its solidus temperature drops with pressure, even when an approximately adiabatic path involves little temperature change. This is not heat created from reduced pressure.
