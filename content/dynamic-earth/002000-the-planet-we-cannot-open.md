# The Planet We Cannot Open: How Seismic Waves Reveal the Interior

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

We can drill into Earth's crust, but not through the mantle to the core. Even the deepest boreholes barely scratch the outer shell of a planet whose radius is more than six thousand kilometers. If we want to know what lies below, we need evidence that travels through rock without anyone bringing the rock to the surface. Earthquakes provide such evidence. Every large earthquake sends pulses through the body of the planet. A worldwide network of seismometers listens, and the timing, strength, and type of waves begin to reveal the planet's hidden structure.

To see how this works, imagine tapping one end of a steel beam and listening at the other. The pulse arrives after a delay set by the beam's length and by the way elastic disturbances travel through steel. Replace the beam with a planet, the tap with an earthquake, and the listener with thousands of sensitive instruments. The analogy is imperfect: Earth is layered, curved, hot, under pressure, and made of different materials. But the central inference is the same. A wave carries information about the material it passed through.

Seismologists distinguish several families of waves. A compressional, or P, wave alternately squeezes and releases material along its direction of travel. In a simple elastic solid, it is the faster of the two main body-wave types. A shear, or S, wave moves the material sideways relative to the direction the wave travels. This is the crucial distinction: liquids sustain pressure but not a static shear stress, so an ordinary bulk S wave cannot travel through a liquid the way it travels through a solid. Surface waves, by contrast, are guided by Earth's exterior and can produce large motions near the surface, especially at periods that matter to buildings.

If the entire planet were made of one uniform material, the map of arrival times would be relatively simple. But travel times change with distance in ways that demand a layered Earth. Waves refract as their velocities change with depth, and some are reflected at boundaries. There are distances where a direct wave fails to arrive, creating a shadow zone. The pattern is not a defect in the instruments. It is evidence for a region through which certain waves cannot pass and in which other waves bend sharply.

One of the major historical discoveries came from Richard Dixon Oldham's interpretation of earthquake records in 1906. He distinguished the arrival of different wave types and inferred a core whose seismic behavior differed strongly from that of the mantle. Later work sharpened the evidence: the outer core behaves as a liquid, while the inner core is solid under immense pressure. The word solid here is not just a guess based on temperature. It comes from what seismic phases can do, and from mineral physics describing materials at high pressure.

Before looking deeper, we should separate two different ways of slicing the Earth. One is by composition: crust, mantle, and core. The crust consists largely of silicate rocks, with different average compositions under oceans and continents. The mantle is also mostly silicate rock, but its mineral assemblages and physical properties differ from those of the crust. The core is dominated by iron with nickel and lighter components. Another way is mechanical: lithosphere and asthenosphere describe how strongly rock resists deformation. The lithosphere includes crust and uppermost mantle. Confusing these categories is as misleading as treating the wooden skin of a table as the same thing as its structural frame.

The boundary between crust and mantle is often called the Moho, after Andrija Mohorovičić. Seismic velocities increase across it because mantle rocks are physically and compositionally different from typical crustal rocks. The Moho is not the bottom of a rigid plate everywhere. A strong tectonic plate can extend well beneath it, through part of the upper mantle. The plate's lower boundary is a transition in mechanical behavior, while the Moho is a compositional and seismic boundary.

There is another apparent paradox. We say that the mantle is solid, but we also say it flows and carries moving plates. How can both be true? The answer depends on timescale and stress. On the time scale of a seismic wave, the mantle transmits shear, so it behaves elastically like a solid. Over millions of years, at high temperature and sustained stress, minerals deform through mechanisms including crystal creep. Rock can flow without becoming an ocean of liquid. A glacier is a more familiar example of solid ice that slowly changes shape; the mantle is far hotter and subject to very different conditions, but the distinction between instantaneous elasticity and long-term creep is similar.

Seismic tomography extends the inference. It uses many earthquake-to-station paths to construct maps of relative wave-speed variation in three dimensions. In some places, relatively fast anomalies align with cold slabs descending beneath subduction zones. Elsewhere, slower anomalies may indicate hotter material, differing composition, partial melt, or other changes. A colorful tomographic image is not a photograph of temperature or magma. Wave speed depends on several properties, so interpreting the colors requires mineral physics, measurements, and assumptions. Nevertheless, these observations can test whether a proposed deep structure is compatible with the paths the waves have taken.

What heats the interior? Earth inherited energy from accretion and early differentiation, and radioactive decay in rocky material continues to supply heat. Cooling and the growth of the inner core also transfer energy through the deep interior. The thermal balance is not a static furnace. Heat leaves the planet by conduction through the lithosphere and by the transport associated with mantle flow and magma. The mantle is convecting rock, not a permanently molten reservoir. The outer core, however, is a conducting liquid whose motion is central to generating the geomagnetic field.

We can supplement the seismic argument with gravity and density. Earth's mean density exceeds that of most surface rocks. Unless Newtonian gravity or the measured size and mass are badly mistaken, the interior must include substantially denser material. The planet's response to rotation, its moment of inertia, high-pressure experiments, and meteorite chemistry all add constraints. None of these methods provides a direct sample from the inner core. Their strength comes from constraining the same hidden object from several independent directions.

Let us calculate a travel-time effect with deliberately simplified numbers. A seismic path through a uniform material travels six hundred kilometers. At six kilometers per second it arrives in one hundred seconds. If the relevant average wave speed on the path were eight kilometers per second, it would arrive in seventy-five seconds. A difference of twenty-five seconds would be easy to detect. Real earthquakes and paths require careful geometry, refraction, source timing, and station corrections; the simple calculation only shows why travel times can be sensitive to the deep material.

We should also ask what earthquakes cannot reveal. They sample paths unevenly because earthquakes and instruments are not distributed uniformly. A tomographic feature may be blurry at depth or dependent on a modeling choice. A slow wave does not uniquely diagnose high temperature. Some fine-scale boundaries remain under debate. The scientific response is not to ignore these issues but to combine more wave types, better instruments, mineral experiments, and alternative models.

The interior matters to everything visible on the surface. A plate can be strong enough to store stress yet weak enough to move over geological time. A descending oceanic slab can be cold enough to remain a high-velocity seismic feature deep within a hot mantle. Partial melting may occur locally while surrounding mantle remains solid. These apparent contradictions disappear once we distinguish material, pressure, temperature, and timescale.

The striking achievement is that our map of the deep Earth is not a fantasy cutaway. It is an inference from travel times, wave types, gravity, laboratory physics, and planetary heat. A planet we cannot open has nevertheless yielded a detailed interior. And that interior sets the conditions under which rocks on the surface can drift, deform, break, and melt.

## Scientific companion: two kinds of Earth layers

**Compositional:** crust (silicate, composition differs by setting); mantle (predominantly solid silicate, pressure-dependent minerals); core (iron-rich; liquid outer, solid inner). **Mechanical:** lithosphere (strong, elastic plate on geological time scales relative to asthenosphere), asthenosphere (solid but weak and creeping over long times). The Moho is not identical to the lithosphere-asthenosphere boundary.

**Elastic-wave relation in isotropic medium:** \(v_P=\sqrt{(K+4\mu/3)/\rho}\) and \(v_S=\sqrt{\mu/\rho}\), with bulk modulus \(K\), shear modulus \(\mu\), density \(\rho\). In a liquid \(\mu\approx0\), so no ordinary bulk S-wave travels through it. This equation is an idealized local material model, not the entire planetary ray-tracing calculation.

**Illustrative travel time:** \(t=L/v\), hence 600 km at 6 km/s gives 100 s; at 8 km/s it gives 75 s. More realistic paths curve and change velocities with depth.

**Inference caution:** seismic tomography maps seismic speed; temperature is inferred only by combining mineralogical, compositional, and physical constraints.

### Questions to reason through

1. Why can a solid mantle deform over millions of years while transmitting shear waves over seconds?
2. Why does a wave-speed anomaly alone fail to prove a local pocket of magma?
3. What is the difference between the Moho and the bottom of a lithospheric plate?
4. Why is the missing direct S-wave passage through the outer core evidence about physical state rather than merely composition?
