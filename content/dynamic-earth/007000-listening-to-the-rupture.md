# Listening to the Rupture: Seismic Waves, Shaking, and Tsunamis

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

A seismogram is a trace of motion through time. On its own it may look like a nervous pencil line: a small disturbance, then a larger wavering arrival, then minutes of complicated shaking that gradually fades. For a seismologist, that line holds information about where an earthquake began, how large a region of fault moved, how waves traveled through the planet, and why shaking differed between two neighboring towns. But a single seismogram does not tell the whole story. The difficult and rewarding work is to combine many records into a physical reconstruction.

Imagine an earthquake at a distance from two instruments. The first wave to arrive is commonly a compressional P wave. The stronger shear and surface-wave arrivals may follow later. The difference between the times helps constrain the distance to the event because the wave types travel at different speeds. If several stations each estimate a distance, we can find candidate locations where the corresponding distance surfaces intersect. In reality the Earth is layered, waves follow bent paths, and stations can be far apart, so modern location methods use velocity models, many arrivals, and uncertainty estimates rather than drawing ideal circles on a flat map.

Let's take simplified speeds to see what information is present. Suppose a P wave travels at six kilometers per second and an S wave at three and a half kilometers per second through an idealized uniform region. If the source is one hundred and twenty kilometers away, the P wave would take twenty seconds. The S wave would take a little over thirty-four seconds. The gap would be roughly fourteen seconds. With other distances and speeds, the numbers change, but the principle remains: the arrival gap is a clock whose calibration depends on Earth's material.

The hypocenter, sometimes called the focus, is the location at which rupture begins. The epicenter is the point directly above it on Earth's surface. These are useful labels, but large earthquakes are not point events. A rupture can spread hundreds of kilometers from its initiation, with different patches releasing stress at different times. Some of the strongest shaking may originate far from the epicenter. A point on a news map is an address for the beginning of a process, not a complete picture of the fault that moved.

Seismologists can use patterns of P-wave first motion to infer fault orientation and slip style. At some stations the first pulse compresses the ground; at others it dilates it. For a simplified double-couple earthquake source, the polarity pattern separates space into regions of opposite first motion. A focal mechanism, sometimes drawn as a black-and-white beach ball, represents this geometry. It usually offers two candidate fault planes: the actual fault and an auxiliary plane. Independent geology, aftershock locations, or surface deformation may be needed to decide which plane slipped. The diagram is a mechanical inference, not a photograph of a hole opening in the ground.

Why does shaking vary so much? First, distance matters: seismic waves spread their energy through larger volumes, and some energy is lost to absorption and scattering. Second, different earthquakes radiate different mixtures of frequencies and direct seismic energy in different directions. A rupture progressing toward a city may produce stronger, shorter-duration motion at certain frequencies than one moving away, a phenomenon related to rupture directivity. Third, the material below the observer matters. Thick sedimentary basins can amplify or prolong some motions; soft near-surface soils may respond differently from hard rock.

Magnitude and intensity should therefore never be confused. Magnitude characterizes the earthquake as a source. Intensity describes shaking and its consequences at particular places. A magnitude seven earthquake has one reported magnitude, perhaps revised as more data arrive, but it can have a wide range of intensities. A town on hard rock farther from the rupture may fare differently from a city on thick sediments closer to the fault. Building design and vulnerability then determine how much of that shaking turns into injury or destruction.

A particularly vivid example comes from large sedimentary basins. Imagine two identical instruments at equal distances from an earthquake: one stands on competent rock, the other above a deep package of sediments. Seismic waves can slow in the sediments and undergo reflections and resonance. At particular frequencies, the surface motion can be amplified or last longer. The buildings above are mechanical oscillators too. If their natural periods align with prominent shaking periods, the damage potential can rise. The same source event is therefore experienced differently because geology changes the signal on its way to the surface.

The ocean introduces another mechanism. Certain large offshore earthquakes abruptly displace broad regions of sea floor. The water above is shifted, and gravity drives long waves outward across the ocean. The resulting tsunami can travel at great speed in deep water while having a relatively small wave height there. As the water becomes shallower, the wave slows and its height and flow can grow under the influence of nearshore topography. It does not behave like the steep, short-period wind waves familiar to surfers; it can act more like a powerful, rapidly changing inundation and retreat of water across low ground.

Not every strong earthquake generates a tsunami. The geometry of movement is critical. A large vertical displacement of sea floor across a broad area often poses more tsunami potential than a predominantly sideways, deep rupture with little vertical sea-floor deformation. Submarine landslides and some volcanic processes can generate tsunamis too. The 2004 Indian Ocean and 2011 Tohoku earthquakes demonstrated the reach of major subduction-zone tsunamis, but the particular pattern of flooding at any coast depends on source detail, propagation, local bathymetry, and coastline shape. Magnitude alone is not a coastal flood prediction.

What can monitoring do when an earthquake begins? Earthquake early warning is possible in some regions because P waves travel faster than the most damaging later arrivals, and electronic messages can outrun the seismic waves. Instruments close to the source detect an event that has already started. Computers estimate the location and likely shaking, and warnings may reach more distant places seconds before strong motion arrives. This is not a prediction of an earthquake before its initiation. Near the epicenter, there may be little or no time to warn, especially if the damaging waves arrive before a reliable alert can be issued.

Long-term seismic hazard maps answer a different question. They combine mapped faults, historical and geological activity, rupture models, and estimates of ground motion to assign probabilities to levels of shaking over specified time windows. Engineers can use these probabilities when designing buildings and bridges. Hazard is not identical to risk: hazard concerns expected physical shaking or inundation, while risk also depends on who and what is exposed and how vulnerable those things are. A remote fault can pose high geological hazard and limited immediate human risk; a densely populated region can have substantial risk from events of more modest magnitude.

The chain of inference is worth appreciating. Instruments record acceleration or velocity at particular sites. Analysts identify wave phases and estimate where the rupture began. Arrays of observations constrain source geometry and fault slip. Material models explain how waves refract, scatter, and amplify. Coastal models predict how water displaced by certain sources might propagate. Engineering models estimate structural response. Each link has uncertainties, and they differ. A beautiful-looking shake map or flood map should not be treated as a direct measurement of every point on the map.

The most responsible lesson is neither that earthquakes are unknowable nor that modern instruments make them predictable on demand. We can measure, locate, interpret, and model seismic processes with great sophistication, while remaining unable to announce an exact future earthquake time. The seismogram is not a mystical heartbeat from the Earth. It is a physical recording whose details we can read, provided we remember that the recorded waves have traveled through a complicated planet and that an earthquake is an extended event, not just a dot on a screen.

## Scientific companion: an arrival-time example

**Illustrative uniform-path calculation:** distance \(r=120\) km, \(v_P=6\) km/s, \(v_S=3.5\) km/s. Then \(t_P=20\) s, \(t_S\approx34.29\) s, \(t_S-t_P\approx14.29\) s. Actual event localization requires path-dependent speeds and multiple stations.

**Source versus site:** moment magnitude describes an earthquake source; Modified Mercalli intensity and measured motion vary geographically. Site effects include sediment resonance, basin reverberation, soil nonlinearity and amplification, as well as possible liquefaction in saturated susceptible sediments.

**Tsunami speed in shallow-water approximation:** \(c\approx\sqrt{gH}\), valid when water depth \(H\) is small compared with wavelength. At \(H=4000\) m, \(c\approx198\) m/s, about 710 km/h; at 100 m, about 31 m/s. Actual coastal run-up requires source and nearshore hydrodynamics.

**Terminology:** earthquake *forecast* or *probability* is not the same as exact *prediction*, and *early warning* starts after rupture has begun.
