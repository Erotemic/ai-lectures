# Why a Fault Lets Go: Elastic Strain, Friction, and Earthquake Rupture

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

A fault is not simply an open crack waiting for the ground to fall into it. It is a surface or zone where rocks on opposite sides have moved relative to one another. Some faults creep slowly. Others remain effectively locked for long periods, while the surrounding rock deforms and stores elastic strain. When a locked fault slips abruptly, part of that stored deformation is released as an earthquake. Understanding the earthquake begins before the shaking: with forces accumulating invisibly in rock that, on a human timescale, looks immovable.

Imagine two blocks of rough material pressed together and pulled in opposite directions through springs. At first the springs stretch while the blocks stick. The resisting friction is sufficient to prevent movement. When the force reaches a threshold, the contact begins to slide; the springs relax; and the blocks move abruptly. Real faults are much more complicated than laboratory blocks. Rock surfaces are rough, fluids alter effective pressure, friction can change with slip speed, and the deforming region may extend kilometers into surrounding rock. But the spring-block thought experiment captures why continuous plate motion can produce intermittent earthquakes.

The elastic-rebound explanation emerged after the 1906 San Francisco earthquake, when Harry Fielding Reid examined measurements of deformation along the San Andreas system. The essential idea is that the rock on either side of a locked fault can deform elastically while the fault itself barely slips. During rupture, part of the accumulated offset is expressed suddenly as permanent displacement near the fault. The surface may appear to spring back, even though it need not return to the position it occupied before many decades of tectonic loading.

It is helpful to name three kinds of stress. Compression pushes material together. Tension pulls it apart. Shear tries to move adjacent material sideways relative to itself. Fault types are partly classified by the motion accommodated. A normal fault generally involves extension and downward movement of the hanging-wall side relative to the footwall. A reverse or thrust fault generally accommodates shortening. A strike-slip fault has predominantly horizontal relative motion. These labels tell us about geometry and displacement; they do not mean that every stress state produces only one tidy fault or that a fault maintains the same motion throughout its history.

What keeps a fault locked? Rock surfaces press against each other, and friction resists sliding. A simple friction model says resistance rises with effective normal stress: the pressure clamping the surfaces together after fluid pressure is accounted for. If fluid pressure in a fault increases, it may reduce effective normal stress and make slip easier. But not every fluid injection triggers a large earthquake, and pore-pressure diffusion, fault orientation, rock properties, and stress conditions matter. Some faults creep stably because their frictional behavior tends to strengthen with faster sliding or because deformation occurs over many small structures.

A rupture is also a propagating process. It may begin on a relatively small patch of fault, the hypocenter, then spread across a larger area as stress concentrations at the rupture front help neighboring patches fail. The rupture does not have to propagate in every direction equally. It can stop at bends, material transitions, or regions that lack sufficient driving stress; sometimes it jumps between segments. The final size of an earthquake depends on the area that slips and the average displacement over that area, among other properties.

That brings us to seismic moment. The moment is approximately the shear rigidity of the rock multiplied by the area of the fault that slipped and the average slip. This quantity has units of force times distance. A fault of one hundred square kilometers slipping by one meter releases a different mechanical deformation than a fault of ten thousand square kilometers slipping by one meter, even if local shaking near the first event is striking. Moment magnitude puts seismic moment on a logarithmic scale suitable for earthquakes from small to very large, avoiding some of the saturation problems of older amplitude-based magnitude measures.

Work a simplified example. Let the shear modulus be thirty billion pascals. Suppose a rupture covers an area of one hundred square kilometers, which is one hundred million square meters, with average slip of one meter. Multiply thirty billion by one hundred million and by one. The seismic moment is about three times ten to the eighteenth newton meters. That does not mean all of this numerical quantity is radiated as seismic-wave energy; seismic moment and radiated energy are different measurements. Fault displacement also leaves permanent deformation and heat, and their balance depends on the rupture process.

Magnitude has a surprising consequence. An increase of one unit in moment magnitude corresponds to about thirty-two times greater seismic moment, to the extent the relation is expressed through moment magnitude, and broadly the familiar energy comparison is also about thirty-two for common approximations. An increase of two units corresponds to about a thousand times. This is why a magnitude eight earthquake is not just moderately bigger than a magnitude six. Yet damage is not set by magnitude alone. Distance, frequency content, rupture direction, soil conditions, building vulnerability, and duration all affect what people experience.

Earthquakes also change the stress field around a fault. Aftershocks can occur on or near the rupture as the crust adjusts, sometimes on neighboring structures. Aftershocks are not merely smaller pieces left over in a fixed sequence, nor is every later earthquake an aftershock of the previous one. Statistically, aftershock rates often decay with time, making probabilistic forecasts useful. A nearby fault may also be loaded or unloaded by the redistribution of stress. These facts matter for hazard assessment, but they do not identify a precise date for the next large rupture.

Why do some faults creep instead of building a catastrophic event? Their rocks, temperatures, pressures, and sliding properties differ. Rate-and-state friction laws describe, in simplified form, how resistance evolves with sliding speed and the history of contact. Some fault patches are velocity strengthening and tend toward stable slip. Others are velocity weakening under relevant conditions and can support unstable rupture. A single fault may contain both kinds of patch, sometimes producing slow-slip events that do not generate the intense shaking of an ordinary earthquake. Modern instruments detect movements that would have been invisible to a surveyor a century ago.

A great practical distinction is between predicting an individual earthquake and estimating hazard. Scientists can map active faults, infer long-term slip rates, identify soil deposits prone to amplification, estimate how frequently certain types of earthquakes occur, and calculate possible shaking under different rupture scenarios. They cannot currently tell us exactly when, where, and how large the next damaging earthquake will be. The absence of exact prediction does not make hazard modeling useless. It means structural design, land planning, and preparedness must work under uncertainty rather than waiting for a deterministic announcement.

Return to the spring and rough blocks. The steady pull represented the plate-motion loading. The frictional interface determined whether motion was smooth or jerky. The elastic spring stored recoverable strain while the blocks stuck. But the real Earth adds interacting faults, fluid pressures, three-dimensional stresses, and ruptures that can propagate for hundreds of kilometers. A magnitude on a news report is a summary of an event, not a complete description of its physics. The earthquake is the moment at which a deforming geological system suddenly changes how it carries its load.

## Scientific companion: a rupture estimate

**Scalar seismic moment:** \(M_0=\mu A\bar D\), where shear modulus \(\mu\) is in Pa, fault area \(A\) in m², and average slip \(\bar D\) in m. Example: \(\mu=3\times10^{10}\) Pa, \(A=100\,\mathrm{km}^2=10^8\,\mathrm{m}^2\), \(\bar D=1\) m gives \(M_0=3\times10^{18}\,\mathrm{N\,m}\).

**Moment magnitude approximation:** \(M_w=(2/3)(\log_{10} M_0 - 9.1)\) with \(M_0\) in N m (constant convention varies slightly). Example above is about Mw 6.3. Tenfold moment corresponds to about 0.67 magnitude unit; one full Mw unit corresponds to about \(10^{1.5}\approx31.6\) times moment. Radiated seismic energy is not numerically identical to moment.

**Effective normal stress:** a simplified friction model has \(\tau_f\approx\mu_f(\sigma_n-p)\). Pore pressure \(p\) changes the effective normal stress. This is not a complete predictive failure criterion for complex faults.
