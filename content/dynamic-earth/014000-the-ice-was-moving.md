# The Ice Was Moving: Glaciers, Valley Carving, and the Rebounding Earth

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

A glacier can look like a frozen lake draped over a mountain. From a distance, the white surface seems motionless. Walk along the same ice margin over several years and the crevasses, debris bands, and ice edge have changed. Track markers placed in the ice and they move. A glacier is a body of ice that flows under its own weight. To understand the landscapes it leaves behind, we need to picture not a permanent snowbank but a moving, deforming conveyor of water and rock.

Glacial ice forms when snow persists through many seasons, becomes compacted, loses much of its original air space, and recrystallizes. Once sufficiently thick and favorably positioned, the mass begins to deform in response to gravity. Ice is crystalline and can fracture near the surface, producing crevasses, while deeper ice can undergo slow internal deformation. Depending on the temperature and drainage conditions at its base, the glacier may also slide over bedrock or deform the sediment beneath it. Different glaciers use these mechanisms in different proportions. Ice can be strongly attached to a cold bed in one region and slide rapidly over a water-influenced bed in another.

The distinction between ice motion and glacier length matters. A glacier can flow downhill even while its terminus retreats uphill. Imagine a moving walkway delivering ice toward the valley floor. If melting and ice loss near the lower end exceed the supply arriving there, the visible end shifts back despite continued motion of ice toward it. The position of the terminus reflects an integrated balance between accumulation, transport, melting and sometimes calving, not the direction in which individual ice crystals move.

A glacier reshapes its bed using rock fragments as tools. Debris frozen into basal ice or dragged beneath it can abrade bedrock, scratching and polishing surfaces. Fractured blocks can be detached through processes collectively called quarrying or plucking. Where water enters cracks and changes pressure or freezes, it may help loosen blocks. Glacial erosion is therefore neither simple scraping nor simple freezing. Its effectiveness depends on sliding, bed roughness, rock fracture geometry, water conditions and the availability of debris.

Consider a V-shaped river valley carved by flowing water and hillslope movement. If a thick glacier occupies it for a sufficiently long time, the ice can erode its sides and floor, widening and deepening it into a broad trough. After the ice disappears, the valley may display a U-shaped cross section. Smaller tributary glaciers often erode less deeply than a large trunk glacier, leaving tributary valleys high above the main trough. Waterfalls may plunge from these hanging valleys. Ice operating from several directions can cut steep cirques and sharpen the ridges between them into arêtes; a peak eroded from several sides may become a horn.

None of these shapes is a perfect diagnostic without context. A valley can have inherited tectonic structure, rock of varying resistance, and later river incision. A sharp peak might owe some of its geometry to faults or different erosion processes. Geologists combine landform shape with striated bedrock, sediment deposits, distribution of erratic boulders, and dated surfaces to infer where ice once flowed. The form alone is a hypothesis; the assemblage of evidence is a reconstruction.

A glacier is also a sediment transporter. It can carry a chaotic mixture of clay-sized particles, sand, gravel, and huge boulders. When ice deposits this material directly, it often produces poorly sorted till. A ridge of such material near a former glacier margin is a moraine. Water issuing from melting ice can sort sediment into outwash plains, channels, terraces and deltas. These deposits can appear beside each other but record different agents: direct deposition by ice versus transport by running water. The distinction matters when reconstructing ice-margin positions.

Imagine finding a granite boulder resting on limestone where no granite bedrock occurs nearby. If its mineral composition matches distant terrain up-ice and it lies among other glacial features, it may be an erratic carried by glacier flow. But a boulder's presence alone is not proof of a particular ice route. It might have been transported by a river, an iceberg, or a landslide under different conditions. A credible ice-flow reconstruction uses multiple boulders, striation directions, landforms, and geological provenance.

We should connect glaciers to the deeper planet. An ice sheet several kilometers thick adds enormous weight to the crust. The lithosphere bends and the underlying solid mantle adjusts over time. When the ice melts, unloading can produce postglacial uplift as the mantle and crust respond. Parts of northern North America and Scandinavia continue to rise long after the great ice sheets disappeared. Nearby regions can subside as mantle mass redistributes. These patterns are called glacial isostatic adjustment. They can be measured by precise satellite positioning and inferred from past shorelines.

Here is a numerical thought experiment. A kilometer of ice has a mass per unit area of approximately nine hundred thousand kilograms per square meter, taking ice density near nine hundred kilograms per cubic meter. That is a very large distributed load. It can depress the solid Earth measurably, but the amount, pattern, and timing of that depression are not simply the height of the ice multiplied by a constant. The response depends on the lithosphere's flexural strength and the viscosity structure of the mantle, as well as the spatial history of ice growth and loss. Scientists use observations of present uplift and geological sea-level markers to constrain those properties.

There is a potentially confusing effect near a deglaciated coast. Global mean sea level can rise as melting land ice adds water to the ocean, while the land itself can rise as weight is removed. In a place where uplift exceeds nearby sea-level rise, the ocean may appear to retreat relative to the land. Elsewhere, land subsidence or different ocean responses can produce rapid relative sea-level rise. A raised beach can therefore record a moving shoreline without implying that the total volume of seawater was shrinking at that moment. Relative sea level requires both land motion and sea-surface change.

Glacial erosion can also expose deep rock and alter mountain mechanics. Removing rock from valleys changes loads, and mass redistribution can contribute to local isostatic response. In high mountain belts, glaciers interact with river incision, landsliding, and tectonic uplift. A deep glacial valley can steepen neighboring slopes, which then fail and supply more debris to the ice. After deglaciation, unsupported slopes and water-filled deposits may remain prone to change. A glacier's hazard legacy can last far beyond its visible lifetime.

What can we learn from old glacial landscapes? A terminal moraine gives evidence of a former ice margin, not by itself an exact date. Cosmogenic nuclides accumulating in exposed boulders may help constrain when the surface became exposed to cosmic rays, provided inheritance, erosion, and shielding are considered. Lake sediments behind moraines may record subsequent changes. Marine fossils on raised beaches can constrain former relative shorelines. Each measurement has a clock or environmental interpretation, and the best reconstruction uses more than one.

There is no need to turn every glacial landform into an idealized textbook icon. Glacier National Park, Yosemite, the fjords of Norway, and many valleys in Alaska preserve mixtures of glacial excavation, structural inheritance, postglacial landslides, and renewed stream erosion. Their beauty is connected to a long history of mass transport by ice, but the history is not visible all at once. It must be inferred from rocks, sediments, surfaces and measurements.

One striking difference between a glacier and a river is the material supporting the flow. Liquid water remains a liquid under ordinary stream conditions, while the ice itself is the flowing solid. That means frictional resistance at the bed, meltwater pressure, internal crystal deformation, and the supply of new snow all influence its motion. During some periods, a glacier's speed can change abruptly if its subglacial drainage network reorganizes. A large short-lived increase in speed does not necessarily mean the glacier has gained mass. Motion and mass balance have to be measured separately.

The deposits left behind after melting can create a new landscape of water. A moraine may temporarily dam a lake, while fine glacial sediment can make downstream water turbid. In other settings the ice erodes a bedrock basin that fills with water after retreat. These lakes may continue to change as surrounding slopes collapse or sediment arrives. The end of glaciation therefore begins another set of geomorphic processes rather than leaving a frozen museum of glacial forms. Studying those changes links ice mechanics to modern river hazards and the long reconstruction of ancient climate.

The glacier's remarkable ability is to make a solid behave as a river over long times. Ice accumulates from snowfall, flows under gravity, abrades and plucks rock, sorts it only when meltwater takes over, and leaves behind a landscape that may still be rebounding thousands of years later. What looks like an empty valley after the ice has vanished is not the absence of geological activity. It is a physical record of an immense load, its motion, its erosion, and its eventual removal.

## Scientific companion: ice mechanics and land response

**Ice load:** mass per unit horizontal area \(m/A=\rho_i H\). With \(H=1000\) m and \(\rho_i=900\) kg m\(^{-3}\), \(m/A=9.0\times 10^5\) kg m\(^{-2}\); vertical pressure at the bed in a simple flat-surface column is about \(\rho_i g H \simeq 8.8\) MPa.

**Slope-driven stress (simple approximation):** basal driving stress \(\tau_d\approx \rho_i g H\sin\alpha\), where \(\alpha\) is surface slope. For one kilometer thick ice at two degrees, \(\tau_d\) is of order 0.31 MPa. This is not the actual local basal traction on a complex glacier.

**Mass-balance distinction:** local change in ice thickness depends on surface input and loss plus horizontal ice-flux convergence. Terminus retreat does not imply upstream ice has stopped flowing.

**Relative sea level:** change in relative water height = change in sea-surface elevation minus change in land elevation (with both referenced consistently). A rising land surface can leave beaches stranded even while global sea level rises.
