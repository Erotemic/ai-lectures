# Inspect corrosion, welds, and durable finishes

## Corrosion and Outdoor Fabrication

A steel bracket on an outdoor gate may remain strong for years and then lose its fastener at a damp joint. Rain does not attack every millimeter equally. Moisture gets trapped beneath a washer, coating is scratched along an edge, and water containing salts remains in a crevice. The exposed metal can look acceptable from the front while a small hidden area deteriorates. Durable outdoor fabrication therefore depends as much on geometry and exposure as on the nominal strength of the metal chosen.

Corrosion is a family of chemical and electrochemical processes, not simply a synonym for rust. Iron-based materials may develop iron oxides and other corrosion products. Aluminum and stainless steels can form protective surface films in many environments, yet particular chemicals, crevices, and chloride exposure can destabilize protection. Choosing 'stainless' does not make an assembly immune to all corrosion.

### A corrosion cell needs conditions

Galvanic corrosion occurs when dissimilar electrically connected materials experience a conductive electrolyte, with one becoming the preferential anode. Not every contact between different metals produces an important problem: the environment, relative electrode behavior, contact area, and water access matter. Outdoor assemblies can create the necessary conditions when rainwater bridges a metal bracket and a different fastener.

Area ratio deserves attention. A small anodic region coupled to a much larger cathodic surface can face severe localized attack because the corrosion current is concentrated on relatively little anodic area. That is why a tiny scratched area or small incompatible fastener may deteriorate rapidly in a larger assembly. It is also why painting only one member of a galvanic couple, without understanding which metal remains exposed, can sometimes make the local problem worse.

Electrical isolation can interrupt the metal-to-metal path, while drainage and sealing can limit the electrolyte. Isolation pieces themselves need durability and load-capacity consideration; a soft washer inserted beneath a highly loaded connection can alter joint behavior. Design constraints must be satisfied together rather than optimizing corrosion in isolation from strength.

### Crevices and pockets

A horizontal shelf in a fabricated frame catches rain and dirt. Two closely pressed plates retain water where drying is slow. An unsealed tube admits water through a hole and traps it at the bottom. A welded joint may create a recess that is hard to clean or coat. All of these are design choices that change corrosion exposure even though none changes the alloy certificate.

A practical outdoor detail should shed water, avoid unnecessary pockets, and allow cleaning or inspection. If water can enter a hollow section, ask where it exits and whether the intended fabrication method requires ventilation or drain holes. For hot-dip galvanizing, hollow fabricated articles can present pressure and process hazards if improperly vented; correct design requires the galvanizer's specifications. A hole placed casually can also weaken an important member, so drainage and structural design must be coordinated.

Coating performance depends on preparation and continuity. Paint over loose rust, salt, and oily residue has a poor foundation. Sharp corners and weld spatter can leave thin or defective film. A protective coating may provide a barrier, but chips and edge damage become entry points. Metallic coatings such as zinc can offer a different protective mechanism in suitable systems. Service environment and maintenance determine whether a particular coating system is appropriate.

### Materials expand, wear, and move

Outdoor metal is heated by sun, cooled by weather, and wetted repeatedly. Thermal expansion can make a long railing change length enough to stress fixed attachments. A sliding or slotted connection may be required, but slots change load transfer and require proper detailing. The base material also matters: an embedded metal connection into masonry can be damaged by freeze-thaw movement or water collecting around the anchor.

Corrosion and fatigue can interact. A small pit may become a local stress concentration; repeated loading can then initiate a fatigue crack. A welded frame may fail at a poorly detailed joint despite the plate material having adequate nominal strength. Conversely, a welded connection that can carry load in a dry indoor shop may corrode prematurely outdoors if its geometry traps water and its surface cannot be inspected.

### A fabrication walkthrough

Suppose you need to mount a camera on an exterior steel post. Draw the load path from the camera through the arm, fasteners, and post to the support. Then draw the rain path. Will water accumulate beneath the bracket? Is there a sealed or drainable interface? Are dissimilar metals used? Can a future worker inspect and replace the fasteners? Is there enough access to coat all critical edges? Will wind vibration load the connection cyclically?

A well-designed bracket may need more than strong bolts. It may need washers that spread loads, a corrosion-compatible fastener choice, a coating system appropriate to local exposure, and a geometry that sheds water. Coastal salt spray, road deicing salts, industrial fumes, and ordinary sheltered rain present different environments. There is no one material label that replaces exposure assessment.

Failure investigation follows the same order. Photograph the location before disassembly, note which surface corroded first, examine trapped moisture and coating failures, and ask whether the load path changed as metal was lost. Replacing a rusty bolt with the same specification without fixing the water trap may reset the timer rather than solve the problem.

The broad design lesson is to think of the environment as part of the assembly. A durable fabrication must be strong when installed, resistant to the exposures it actually receives, and accessible enough that deterioration can be detected before it becomes failure.

## Inspecting and Repairing Corroded Assemblies

An exposure mechanism gives context to the visible evidence on an already corroded assembly.

A steel bracket outdoors develops orange rust. Another bracket looks smooth but has corrosion concealed beneath its mounting plate. Which one is in worse condition? Surface color alone cannot answer. Corrosion is a chemical and electrochemical process, while the engineering consequence depends on where material has been lost, how much remains, and how forces travel through the assembly.

In a simple corrosion cell, metal oxidation occurs at anodic regions, electrons move through conductive material, and ionic reactions occur through an electrolyte such as water containing dissolved salts. Oxygen availability, surface coatings, wet-dry cycles, and contact with different metals can change the reaction. Rust on steel is a visible family of corrosion products, not a calibrated depth gauge. A thin film can look dramatic while leaving substantial sound metal. Local pitting, especially at a highly stressed edge or fastener hole, can be mechanically serious even if the total mass lost is small.

### Trace the forces before the discoloration

Imagine a steel handrail post bolted to a concrete step. Its base plate is partly hidden, and the bolts enter a damp, salt-exposed area. When someone leans on the rail, the post develops a bending moment. The base and anchors must transmit that moment to the concrete. Material loss near an anchor hole is more consequential than a similar patch of rust at an unstressed decorative surface. Corrosion at a welded root, an undersized section, or a fatigue crack also requires special attention.

Inspection therefore begins by identifying the structural job. What loads does the assembly resist? Where are forces concentrated? Which surfaces collect water, where do joints trap moisture, and which parts cannot be seen without disassembly? Sounding, visual measurement, thickness measurement, and non-destructive inspection can provide evidence when matched to the material and accessibility. A qualified assessment is required when load-bearing capacity is uncertain.

### Crevices and mixed metals

A sealed-looking overlap can be a corrosion trap. Water enters through a narrow seam, remains there, and creates an environment that differs from the open surface. Paint applied only to what is visible may improve appearance while leaving the most aggressive wet region untouched. The repair may need to change geometry or drainage, not just coating chemistry.

Galvanic corrosion occurs when dissimilar electrically connected metals share an electrolyte. Relative electrochemical behavior can increase corrosion of the more anodic member, but the outcome also depends on area ratios, oxygen, electrolyte, and protective films. Saying that 'stainless steel never corrodes' or that one metal combination is always safe oversimplifies the problem. Stainless alloys may be vulnerable to localized attack, notably in certain chloride environments; the actual alloy and exposure matter.

The practical design question is whether a joint can stay dry, drain, be inspected, and avoid problematic material combinations. Isolating washers or barriers may help in some assemblies, but they must themselves withstand load and environment. An insulating layer that creeps under bolt preload can introduce a mechanical failure while solving an electrochemical one.

### Repair changes the structure

A repair may remove corrosion products, restore protective coating, replace a deteriorated component, reinforce a joint, or redesign the water path. Each choice changes the assembly differently. Grinding away pitting removes additional metal and can create a notch; welding introduces heat and can alter material properties and residual stress; a doubler plate can create a new moisture crevice; sealants can trap water if drainage is unresolved. Simply covering corrosion with a paint film may leave active contamination beneath the coating.

Coating performance depends on substrate preparation, environmental conditions, product compatibility, film thickness, and curing. Surface preparation needs to remove unstable corrosion and contamination to the level specified by the coating system. The new coating should protect the entire exposure path, including edges and fasteners where practical. In a load-bearing repair, a coating is not a substitute for verifying the remaining section and connections.

### Look for progression, not a single photograph

Photographs with scale and date, thickness readings at repeatable locations, notes about standing water, and records of past repairs make deterioration measurable. A fast-growing pit, loose fastener, or widening crack deserves different attention from stable superficial oxidation. A recurring rust streak below a concealed joint can be an important pointer to a source that has not been exposed yet.

A useful repair plan ends with evidence of performance: corrected drainage, sound remaining material, verified joint function, appropriate protective system, and an inspection interval matched to exposure. The goal is not to remove every orange mark at all costs. It is to preserve the required load path and service life while controlling the mechanism that caused damage.

## Choosing Metals and Fasteners for Corrosive Environments

Two shiny bolts hold an exterior bracket. Five years later, the bolts still look bright, but the aluminum around one has turned powdery and the bracket is loose. The obvious question is why a corrosion-resistant bolt did not protect the entire assembly. The answer lies in the difference between choosing a metal in isolation and designing an electrochemical system.

Metals do not corrode only because they are weak or because water touches them. Many corrosion processes involve reactions at surfaces coupled by an electrolyte, such as rainwater containing dissolved salts. Some regions behave more anodically and dissolve; other regions support reduction reactions. When dissimilar metals are electrically connected and wetted by a conducting liquid, the coupling may accelerate attack on the more anodic material under those conditions. Galvanic corrosion therefore depends on the metal pair, environment, exposure geometry, and electrical continuity.

### Why the fastener matters to the entire joint

Consider a large stainless-steel plate attached to a small aluminum bracket with a thin wetted joint. The relative exposed areas of cathodic and anodic regions can affect corrosion current density at the vulnerable material. A small anodic area connected to a large cathodic area can be a poor arrangement. Reversing the area ratio may change the severity. But an area rule alone does not predict service life: films, coatings, oxygen, water retention, and alloy composition alter behavior.

Stainless steel gains much of its corrosion resistance from a thin passive surface film. It is not immune to every environment. Chloride-rich water, stagnant crevices, poor drainage, and some temperatures can cause localized pitting or crevice corrosion. A fastener labeled stainless is not automatically suitable for a dock, a coastal railing, or a chemical washdown installation. The alloy grade and the details around the joint matter.

Galvanized steel uses a zinc coating that can provide a barrier and a sacrificial electrochemical function in some conditions. The coating's protection is finite: scratches, cut edges, abrasion, and prolonged wetting can consume it. Hot-dip galvanizing and thin electroplated coatings are not interchangeable just because both products look silvery. Coating thickness, fabrication sequence, and the actual exposure must be part of selection.

### Manage water before choosing stronger metal

A horizontal joint that traps salty water can defeat an otherwise reasonable material choice. Design drainage so water does not sit in blind pockets. Avoid crevices that stay wet through repeated weather cycles. Keep dissimilar surfaces separated where appropriate with compatible insulating washers, sleeves, or coatings, but remember that a conductive path elsewhere may still complete the circuit. An insulating washer that is crushed, worn, or bypassed by bare metal may not provide the intended separation.

Imagine an outdoor steel railing attached to concrete. Water can reach anchor holes, corrosion products can expand, and local cracking of the surrounding material can occur. An anchor has to satisfy both structural loading and corrosion exposure. Selecting a rust-resistant head does not establish the condition of the embedded shank. Repairs should inspect the concealed connection and identify whether water can drain away from the mounting point.

Fastener selection is multidimensional. Tensile and shear strength, temperature, fatigue, preload, galling, expansion, tool access, and replacement requirements all matter. Two fasteners with similar diameter may have different mechanical classes and coating systems. The most corrosion-resistant available alloy may have unsuitable strength, compatibility, or installation behavior for a particular design. A specification should identify the required properties instead of substituting appearance or a broad word like marine grade.

### Coatings create maintenance obligations

Protective coatings can isolate metal from an electrolyte, but they need sound surface preparation, compatible primers, proper film thickness, and coverage at edges and welds. Edges are vulnerable because film build may be lower there. A coating that is breached at one crevice can create a concentrated deterioration site. Overpainting rust without diagnosing wetting, salt contamination, and adhesion problems may hide continued attack.

For a repair, document where corrosion is located, whether it is uniform or localized, and which material is being lost. White powder on aluminum, reddish iron oxides on steel, or a dark stained stainless crevice are observations to investigate rather than complete chemical diagnoses. Measure loss of section where capacity matters, inspect threads and bearing surfaces, and identify interacting materials. Replacing the visible fastener while leaving the supporting member compromised does not restore the assembly.

A good outdoor joint is not one made from the most impressive alloy name. It is a joint whose materials, water paths, load demands, and inspection access were chosen together. The first defense against corrosion is often geometry that keeps water from lingering. The next is an appropriate material system. The last is a maintenance plan that catches deterioration before it becomes structural.

## Reading a Weld: Fit-Up, Surface Evidence, and Hidden Defects

Weld inspection similarly has to distinguish visible surface evidence from what remains unknown inside the joint.

A weld can be smooth, shiny and weak. Another can look rough while remaining adequate for its intended service. Appearance is evidence, but its meaning depends on the joint design, loading, material, process and acceptance criteria. The first skill in weld inspection is to stop asking whether a bead is pretty and start asking whether the required joint was actually made.

Picture a steel gate hinge welded to a square-tube post. The gate carries its own weight, and every opening and closing cycle applies forces through the hinge. A weld might be large enough to look reassuring but attached to a thin wall with poor fit-up. It might have cracks at a sharp transition, lack fusion along part of the edge, or leave an unprotected pocket where water collects. If the joint fails, replacing a bead with a bigger bead may not repair the underlying load path.

### Inspection begins before the arc

Start with the drawing or the intended design. Which members carry the load? Is the joint a butt joint, lap, corner or tee? What weld size, length, location and process are specified? Does the design require continuous welding, intermittent welds or a connection that accommodates movement? If there is no clear design basis for a consequential structural assembly, an inspector cannot invent one by looking at the finished metal.

Next comes fit-up. Gaps, misalignment, dirty surfaces, incorrect bevels and inaccessible root regions affect what the welding process can achieve. Heat changes the shape of metal; tacks and fixtures control some distortion, but can also create new stresses. Inspecting the joint while it is still accessible is often more informative than examining a sealed enclosure afterward.

For a practice assembly, measure the fit before welding and after cooling. Record changes in squareness, flatness and position. The movement itself teaches why weld sequence and fixturing matter. A tiny angular change at one hinge may translate into visible misalignment at the opposite edge of a long gate.

### Surface discontinuities tell different stories

A crack is not simply an unattractive line. It may indicate shrinkage stresses, hydrogen-related cracking, fatigue, or another mechanism. A crack-like indication needs serious interpretation because crack propagation can undermine a joint under repeated loads. Porosity appears as gas-related voids; surface pores may be visible, while internal porosity may not. Undercut is a groove at the weld toe that reduces local section and can concentrate stress. Overlap is deposited metal rolling over a base surface without proper fusion. Excessive convexity can add material without proportionately improving the load path.

Lack of fusion is particularly important because the weld may appear to cover a surface while not bonding soundly to it. Some lack of fusion is visible at an accessible edge; internal lack of fusion requires other examination methods. The backside of a joint may be inaccessible after assembly. That is one reason a photograph of a smooth top bead cannot certify penetration through the joint.

A quick practical comparison: imagine a fillet weld on two plates forming a right angle. The relevant geometry includes leg sizes, effective throat and actual continuity. The face can be wide while the root is not properly joined. The shape also affects where stress concentrates at the toes. Measuring nominal bead width alone does not reveal the whole effective connection.

### Visual inspection is powerful but limited

Visual examination can find surface cracks, excessive undercut, visible porosity, poor profile, missing weld length, misalignment, arc strikes and some accessibility problems. It can also document whether the specified joint was assembled correctly. But it cannot see a hidden root or verify internal soundness. Other nondestructive examination methods—such as liquid penetrant, magnetic particle, ultrasonic or radiographic methods—address different flaw types and materials. Each has applicability limits, access requirements, trained operators and interpretation standards.

A structural job can have an acceptance procedure tied to a welding code, qualified procedure, material and service conditions. A household repair may have no formal code dossier, but that does not make all defects harmless. The more severe the consequences of failure, the stronger the need for an explicit design and appropriate inspection. A trailer hitch, pressure boundary and decorative plant stand are not the same application.

### Corrosion and fatigue finish the story

Return to the gate hinge. The weld may initially carry the load, but the tube's exposed interior can collect water. A bead may create a crevice where paint coverage is poor. Dissimilar hardware may accelerate corrosion in certain environments. Repeated gate motion can impose alternating stress at a weld toe. The repair should therefore address drainage and protection, alignment, member thickness and cyclic loading as well as the visible crack.

Suppose the hinge broke along the base metal next to the bead. That does not necessarily mean the weld was strong enough for service. It may mean the heat-affected zone, nearby section or stress concentration became the weak link. If the fracture lies in the weld, that too does not identify a unique cause without examining the joint and service history. A failed connection is a system failure, not just an appearance score.

### An evidence-based repair sequence

For a noncritical practice joint, establish the intended geometry and acceptance criteria first. Photograph the fit-up, record the material and process, then measure alignment after welding. Inspect both sides where accessible. Compare what you see with the specified criteria and, when relevant, obtain appropriate additional examination. Mark which regions were not inspectable. Record environmental protection and how moisture will drain.

For a failed service joint, preserve evidence before grinding it away. Look for fatigue marks, corrosion, repeated misalignment and hidden load paths. Repairing the immediate fracture without correcting the geometry or service environment invites recurrence. Do not use a successful-looking practice bead as proof that a load-bearing joint is ready for service.

Welding inspection is a lesson in epistemic limits. You can learn a great deal by looking carefully, measuring and following the forces. You also need to know what you cannot see. The confidence in a weld should come from its design, process control, accessible evidence and required examinations, not from how easily its surface catches the light.

## Surface Preparation, Coatings, and Durable Metal Finishes

Two steel brackets are fabricated from the same metal and painted with the same coating. One lasts for years outdoors; the other blisters after its first winter. The difference may be hidden underneath the paint: oil, mill scale, salt, rust, incompatible coatings, sharp edges or moisture left at a joint before application.

A protective coating is a system, not a colored skin. It needs to adhere to an appropriate surface, form a continuous barrier and survive the movement, temperature, abrasion and water exposure expected in service. Some coating systems also provide sacrificial corrosion protection. Good application starts with understanding what the substrate is and what the coating is supposed to do.

### Corrosion needs a path

Rusting of carbon steel is an electrochemical process involving metal oxidation and a compatible environment that usually includes moisture and oxygen. Chlorides can make conditions more aggressive. Painting can slow access of corrosive agents to the surface, but damage at an edge, scratch, bolt hole or crevice may admit water. If that water becomes trapped under a poorly adhered film, corrosion products can lift the film and spread deterioration.

This is why edge design matters. A sharp laser-cut edge may receive a thinner coating than a rounded one, depending on material and application. A welded lap that retains moisture can corrode inside even when the external coating looks sound. A continuously sealed joint may prevent entry, while a deliberately drained detail may tolerate some water that does enter. Choosing which strategy is appropriate is a design decision before painting begins.

### Surface preparation is a sequence

First identify the actual metal. Bare carbon steel, stainless steel, aluminum and hot-dip galvanized steel have different surface chemistries and preparation requirements. Next determine what is present on the surface. Oil and grease interfere with adhesion. Rust and loose scale may prevent a durable bond. Salts can remain even after visible dirt has been removed. Surface profile affects mechanical anchoring of certain coatings.

Cleaning and abrasion are different steps. Abrading an oily surface can smear contamination into scratches rather than remove it. After cleaning, a specified abrasive treatment may establish the profile a coating requires. The surface must then stay clean and meet temperature, humidity and dew-point restrictions at application. A freshly blasted steel surface exposed to damp air can flash-rust before a primer is applied.

Imagine a simple gate repair. Someone wire-brushes a rusty patch, paints directly over it and calls it finished. A second worker cleans contaminants, assesses the remaining metal section, removes unsound material by an appropriate method, prepares the surface to the coating specification, treats edges, verifies the condition and applies a compatible primer and topcoat at the required dry-film thickness. The second process has more variables to control, but those variables directly explain the difference in durability.

### Galvanizing is not paint

Hot-dip galvanizing applies a zinc coating that protects steel partly as a physical barrier and partly through zinc's sacrificial electrochemical behavior. Painting galvanized steel can extend protection, sometimes called a duplex system. But freshly galvanized, partially weathered and fully weathered surfaces differ in their chemistry and preparation needs. Applying a generic solvent and paint without consulting the specific system may produce adhesion problems.

Stainless steel is another distinct case. Its corrosion resistance is associated with a passive surface film, but it is not immune to chloride attack or crevice corrosion. Using stainless fasteners through a coated carbon-steel panel introduces galvanic and crevice questions. Aluminum likewise demands a compatible pretreatment and coating. The label 'rustproof' does not remove the need to consider the operating environment.

### Measuring and verifying a finish

Visual appearance is not enough. An accepted coating specification may control surface cleanliness, profile, primer choice, number of coats, application window, wet-film or dry-film thickness, curing and holiday detection. The coating on a complex bracket may be thinner in deep corners or on edges; thickness needs enough sampling and suitable instruments.

Paint thickness can be measured by different techniques depending on the substrate. An instrument suitable for a nonmagnetic coating on steel may not work the same way on aluminum. Measuring wet-film thickness gives process feedback during application; measuring dry-film thickness helps verify what remains after solvent or water evaporates. Neither tells you alone whether the film adheres well to a contaminated surface.

### Coatings are maintenance decisions

A durable coating strategy anticipates access for inspection, water drainage, repairability and replacement. The most elaborate multilayer system may be unsuitable if its surface cannot be maintained. A simpler finish with better geometry and accessible touch-up points may last longer in the actual use case.

Start with the environment, the metal and the failure mode. Select a coating system rated for those conditions. Prepare the surface it requires and verify the process rather than guessing by color. Corrosion control is an exercise in chemistry, geometry and workmanship at once.

**Track:** Metalworking and Fabrication. **Background:** Metallurgy, joints, fatigue, and forming. **Purpose:** design outdoor assemblies for real exposure, drainage, inspection, and material compatibility.

#### Technical companion (non-spoken)

##### Design review grid

For each connection: materials and galvanic compatibility; electrolyte exposure; area ratio; crevice and drainage; coating preparation and application; fatigue and thermal movement; fastener load path; access for inspection/replacement.

##### Counterexample

A small uncoated anodic fastener in electrical contact with a large, more noble component in salty water may lose material rapidly even though only a small fraction of the total assembly is visibly exposed. Isolation and moisture control are alternatives worth investigating; choose them based on the actual structural and environmental constraints.

**Review boundary:** Structural weld design, galvanizing details, coating specifications, and coastal or industrial exposures require standards and specialist assessment. In particular, do not improvise vent/drain details for sealed hollow sections intended for hot-dip galvanizing.

**Track:** Metalworking and Fabrication. **Background:** metallurgy, welding, fatigue, galvanic corrosion, and coatings. **Purpose:** separate cosmetic corrosion from section loss and hidden joint failure.

#### Technical companion (non-spoken)

##### Inspection record

Record alloy or best available material identification, coating condition, exposed environment, dissimilar contacts, fastener-hole condition, joint geometry, load role, visible corrosion morphology, and repeatable photo scale. Distinguish **uniform surface loss**, **pitting**, **crevice attack**, **galvanic attack**, and **cracking/fatigue**; different mechanisms require different inspection and repair methods.

##### Decision example

A rail base plate with localized section loss adjacent to anchors must be assessed in the connection load path. An intact decorative cover with broad superficial oxidation may be a surface-preparation task. Do not infer acceptable remaining thickness or fastener capacity from the apparent amount of orange coloration.

**Review boundary:** Welding on installed load-bearing assemblies, grinding suspected cracked sections, and altering anchorage require qualified evaluation of structural capacity and work hazards. Follow the relevant coating manufacturer's substrate preparation and environmental limits; check repair compatibility and galvanic exposure.

### Choosing Metals and Fasteners for Corrosive Environments

### 013000 — Choosing Metals and Fasteners for Corrosive Environments

#### Technical companion (non-spoken)

##### Material-selection record

Specify: load and temperature; principal base metals; proposed fastener alloy/coating and strength class; known chloride or chemical exposure; persistent wetting/crevices; exposed cathodic/anodic area relationship; electrical separation details; coating preparation; inspection access. Do not infer compatibility from color.

##### Failure comparison

- **Uniform rusting:** Consider barrier failure, standing water, and coating coverage.
- **Pitting/crevice attack:** Examine local chemistry, stagnant gaps, and alloy passivity limits.
- **Galvanic attack:** Confirm metal contact and conductive wetting; inspect both materials.
- **Galling:** Thread seizure can be an installation failure even without long-term corrosion.

**Editorial boundary:** Specific alloy grades, bolt classes, chemical resistance, and anchor capacity need published material data and project design review. Avoid broad statements such as “stainless never rusts” or “zinc works in every marine environment.”

### Reading a Weld: Fit-Up, Surface Evidence, and Hidden Defects

### 019000 — Reading a Weld: Fit-Up, Surface Evidence, and Hidden Defects

#### Technical companion (non-spoken)

**Joint worksheet.** Record base material and thickness, joint geometry, fit-up, access to root, weld specification, process/consumable, tack sequence, final dimensions, surface indications, weather exposure and inspection limitations. For critical loads, require design/acceptance criteria before evaluation.

**Discontinuities versus defects.** A discontinuity is an interruption in typical structure; whether it is rejectable depends on type, size, location and the governing criteria. **Practical exercise.** Compare mock or documented photographs of undercut, visible porosity, overlap and a crack. Describe evidence and uncertainty without assigning pass/fail where no acceptance standard has been specified.

### Surface Preparation, Coatings, and Durable Metal Finishes

### 020000 — Surface Preparation, Coatings, and Durable Metal Finishes

#### Non-spoken technical companion

**Workflow.** Identify alloy/coating state -> assess structural loss -> remove oil/salts/loose corrosion with specified process -> prepare surface profile -> document substrate temperature, dew point and environmental conditions -> apply compatible system -> verify cure, dry-film thickness and defects according to coating specification.

**Special cases.** Newly hot-dip galvanized steel needs different cleaning/profiling from fully weathered galvanized steel. Zinc-rich primers and barrier coatings are not interchangeable. Painting does not restore metal lost to corrosion. For existing lead-based coatings, abrasive removal may create a regulated exposure hazard; test and plan accordingly.

**References.** American Galvanizers Association, *Painting Galvanized Steel—Surface Preparation*; ASTM D6386 (source issuer ASTM International, current edition and access to be checked); AMPP/SSPC surface-preparation guidance; relevant coating manufacturer application specification. Technical design and hazardous coating removal require competent review.
