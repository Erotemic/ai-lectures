# Burials beside the Lualaba: Work, Wealth, and the Upemba Wetlands

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

A burial can tell us how a person was laid to rest. It may also tell us what objects others chose to place beside them. But a burial does not show an entire society. In the Upemba Depression, a great wetland in what is now the southeastern Democratic Republic of the Congo, archaeologists have excavated an exceptional series of graves spanning more than a thousand years. Pots, iron tools, copper ornaments, fishing gear, and later cross-shaped ingots make it possible to study changing forms of work, exchange, and social distinction. The challenge is to remember that these are records of burial decisions, not a census of every living household.

The depression lies within the upper Congo drainage, crossed by the river also known as the Lualaba. Lakes, marshes, higher ground, and channels create a patchwork of habitats. Fishing would offer one set of opportunities, cultivation on suitable ground another. Much of the oldest archaeological sequence is known through excavations at places such as Sanga, Kamilamba, Kikulu, Malemba-Nkulu, Katongo, and Katoto. Nicolas Nikis's 2021 synthesis describes roughly fifty identified sites and almost three hundred excavated graves. Those are substantial collections, yet associated settlements are poorly understood. Long occupation and disturbance have made domestic layers difficult to expose over large areas.

In the earliest clearly identified iron-using archaeological phase, called Kamilambian, sites in the northern part of the depression show occupation around the sixth through eighth centuries of the common era. Iron tools include implements that could be used in cultivation and fishing; a grinding-stone fragment and certain pottery features add to the picture. These traces suggest people who exploited water and land, but the remains do not provide a complete farming calendar. Nor is it certain whether the Late Stone Age practices visible in older layers ended abruptly. A ceramic phase name gives us a starting classification, not a complete set of social and economic habits.

The following Early Kisalian phase, roughly the eighth and ninth centuries, is associated with different burial goods. Nikis describes objects such as iron tools and, in some graves, items interpreted as symbols of authority, including a ceremonial axe or an anvil. An anvil could be a practical metalworking tool in one setting and an emblem in another. The burial context may suggest high standing, but it does not supply the deceased person's name or office. It does, however, provide an important counterweight to histories that search only for stone capitals: social difference and organized specialist work can become archaeologically visible without monumental buildings or a surviving royal chronicle.

Copper then becomes increasingly significant. It is not the chief mineral resource of the Upemba wetlands themselves; much of the metal appears to have come through links to the Copperbelt farther south. When archaeologists find copper among graves along the upper Congo, they are looking at one section of a much longer network of mining, casting, exchange, and use. Similarities in pottery with other regions and the appearance of imported materials can suggest connections, but a graveside bracelet does not disclose which intermediary delivered it. Some other goods, especially foods and textiles, would survive less reliably than metal and ceramics.

The Classic Kisalian phase, approximately the tenth through thirteenth centuries in Nikis's broad chronology, produced some elaborate burials. Graves can contain many vessels, iron implements, copper ornaments, hooks and harpoons, and objects that required specialized manufacture. Those goods show that the lives represented were connected to both watery environments and wider material exchange. They also suggest differences in access to valued things. Still, a rich grave is not a household account book. The amount placed with a dead person depends on ritual decisions and relationships among survivors, not necessarily on an exact proportion of wealth possessed during life.

There is a striking aquatic dimension to this history. The Upemba wetlands supplied fish, and some graves contain equipment used in fishing or objects that specialists connect with boat-based food preparation. Dried fish may have been exchanged for goods from farther away. That possibility fits the ecology and later histories of exchange, but preserved dried fish is not available in the relevant graves to certify the full route. It is reasonable to reconstruct a strongly water-based economy from multiple traces. It would be unreasonable to treat every hook as evidence of a state monopoly on fishing or every vessel as a receipt for a market transaction.

Burials have also made it possible to ask about diet in a more direct, though still partial, way. In 2016, Nonhlanhla Dlamini, Alan Morris, and Judith Sealy published measurements of carbon isotopes in human tooth enamel, together with evidence of dental cavities. Individuals buried at Sanga showed stronger signatures of foods associated with a category of plants called C-four, while the Katoto sample indicated more C-three resources. One possible explanation is variation in the consumption of cereals or other vegetation, but an isotope value does not identify a specific crop by itself. Dental decay also reflects many aspects of diet and oral environment. These measurements demonstrate regional differences rather than one uniform Upemba menu.

The comparison between Sanga and Katoto is especially useful because the two are not interchangeable descriptions of the same community. Researchers give the southern Katoto burial tradition a separate archaeological label, Katotian, overlapping some of the centuries of the Classic Kisalian but less fully understood. A region with many lakes and settlements can contain more than one material and dietary tradition at a time. If we collapse those records into a single people with a single food system, we erase precisely the differences the archaeological evidence preserves.

A further transition is marked by what archaeologists call Kabambian A, approximately the thirteenth to mid-fifteenth centuries in the northern Upemba sequence. The range and quantity of some metal grave goods changed, while particularly rich graves contained substantial collections of copper crosses and other objects. The pattern has been interpreted as a reorganization of authority and exchange networks. Yet the appearance of a more sharply distinguished set of wealthy graves does not determine whether a ruler imposed new taxes, a local lineage gained prominence, or ritual priorities changed. These are hypotheses to test alongside settlement and trade evidence, not meanings permanently stamped into copper.

Later Kabambian phases and the eventual integration of parts of the region into the Luba kingdom belong largely to centuries after the fifteenth. Archaeologists have traced some continuity in vessel-making across these later transformations, but we must not read the structure of a much later kingdom backward into every eighth- or eleventh-century grave. The political organizations of earlier Upemba communities may have differed substantially. Later traditions of Luba power can enrich the questions we ask, but they cannot serve as eyewitness testimony for the people buried centuries before those traditions were recorded.

The history of the excavations is also part of the history of knowledge. Research campaigns in the twentieth century assembled collections now held both in the Democratic Republic of the Congo, including at the museum in Lubumbashi, and in Belgian institutions. The archaeological categories familiar from books grew from those particular projects and their available sites. Future excavation of homes, workshops, fields, or harbors could change our picture of the people who produced the goods discovered in graves. A burial record is powerful evidence of action around death, but everyday labor often remains just outside its frame.

What, then, does Upemba contribute to the wider story of pre-1500 Africa? It shows long-term occupation and changing communities in wetland landscapes that were neither remote from history nor simply minor outposts of coastal states. Fishing, food production, metalworking, copper exchange, and ritual distinctions developed over many centuries. Archaeologists can trace some of those changes through pottery, tools, grave goods, and chemical signatures in human remains. They cannot transform those objects into a complete list of offices, languages, or laws. The task is to build an account that is both substantial and proportional to what survives.

## Evidence boundary

Almost three hundred excavated graves provide a strong burial sequence but a weak direct census of homes and farming. Iron tools, fish remains, pottery, and copper goods support several economic inferences; grave wealth does not establish named offices or particular redistribution laws. Sanga–Katoto isotope differences do not identify individual cereals. The chronology of later Luba political formation must not be projected onto Early or Classic Kisalian phases.
