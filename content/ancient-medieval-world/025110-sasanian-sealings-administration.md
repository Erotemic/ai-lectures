# Five Marks in Clay: Seals and Offices in Sasanian Iran

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

A small piece of clay can outlive a written document by fourteen centuries. At Qasr-e Abu Nasr near Shiraz, archaeologists excavated hundreds of such pieces from a fortress occupied in late Sasanian times. One surviving object bears five impressions: birds, a camel, writing, and a plant-like design. The writing or packet once sealed by the clay is gone. Fire destroyed the perishable material and hardened the clay, which is why this fragment survives. We face a strange historical reversal. What the administrators tried to protect has vanished, while the little object they used to close it tells us that the administrative act happened.

A sealing of this sort is often called a bulla. Wet clay was pressed against a cord or fastening and stamped with one or more carved seals. A broken cord, a disturbed clay lump, or a damaged impression could make interference visible. People also used impressions to identify a sender, a witness, an official, or a responsible party. A seal was therefore not just decoration. It could certify a relationship or a chain of custody. Yet the same technology served several different purposes. An impression does not always announce whether it closed an official letter, secured an expensive parcel, or recorded a private transaction.

The Metropolitan Museum describes more than five hundred sealings excavated from the Qasr-e Abu Nasr fortress. Their preservation owes much to a fire. In the excavation they appeared in an administrative setting, but what survives is a fragment of a once larger archive. We cannot reconstruct the original order of every action by laying seals into a new sequence of our own. Even whether a group of clay impressions was deliberately stored after use or simply preserved where a disaster occurred can be disputed. The secure observation is that seal-impression practice was extensive enough to leave a substantial archaeological concentration.

Some seals belonged to named individuals. Others represented an office. This distinction matters because an office could continue after the person holding it died or moved away. A clay impression might bear a personal name, a title, or a geographical designation. If specialists can read the inscription, they begin to reconstruct layers of administration that royal victory reliefs leave invisible. Yet seals can move with their owners, and objects may be deposited far from where they were first impressed. Location of discovery and location of authority are separate questions. An administrator's seal discovered in one place is not proof that the administrator ruled the whole surrounding countryside.

At Tappe Bardnakoon in the central Zagros, excavations yielded 559 clay bullae. A 2020 study identified twenty-two administrative sealings among the material and used their inscriptions to connect the site with named Sasanian districts and neighboring provinces. Those are unusually useful traces: they provide the vocabulary of a geographical bureaucracy in the hands of people who actually used its documents. But one should distinguish the excavated count from the specialists' classification and then distinguish that classification from a complete map of government. Some offices and districts are identified confidently; the reach of each one cannot be read from a lump of clay.

A different problem arises with collections assembled without controlled archaeological excavation. Parsa Ghasemi studied seventy-three sealings returned to Iran in 2015. In that collection, the author counted administrative and personal impressions and identified several titles associated with Sasanian government and military ranks. Such a study can recover precious names and writing. It cannot recover a secure findspot merely by recognizing the title on a seal. Because commercial circulation and collecting remove objects from their original context, a specialist can identify an office more confidently than the exact route by which its bulla reached a museum.

The uneven mix of personal and office impressions is important in another way. Administration required interaction with people who were not kings. A merchant closing a package, an official receiving stores, or a temple representative recording a transfer all needed some way to make responsibility visible. A particular surviving impression does not prove that one of these imagined actors made it. It tells us why an authenticated mark would be useful across different settings. The technique connects private ownership, institutional authority, and exchange without making them the same activity.

The seals also complicate the idea of a Sasanian administration with one unchanging shape. Different centuries brought different offices, military pressures, and regional centers. The articles on administrative sealing emphasize ranks in civil and military government and the problem of interpreting who had jurisdiction over what. A name on a seal can offer a snapshot of a title in use. It cannot tell us whether its bearer collected a tax efficiently, enforced an order far away, or maintained authority through rebellion. Institutions existed as relationships among people and places, not simply as categories printed in modern tables.

Sometimes a clay imprint carries an image that could be mistaken for a religious label. Birds, animals, crescents, human figures, or other designs may convey identity and prestige. Their interpretation changes with context. One person might use imagery that another associated with a deity; an image of a camel is not a census of camels or a tariff record for camel caravans. In the same way, the presence of Zoroastrian priestly titles among seal impressions demonstrates priestly participation in legal and administrative practices. It does not prove that every document passed through a fire temple or that all inhabitants shared the same religion.

There is a material lesson here about what historians are likely to find. Clay fired by accident survives where paper, parchment, cord, and textiles disappear. A damaged archive becomes an archaeological treasure precisely because of the destruction that ended its ordinary use. Other offices that never burned may have left little of the same kind of evidence. If we compare the number of preserved impressions between two sites without accounting for this bias, we might mistake a fire for a larger bureaucracy. Excavation context, dates, and the recovery methods of archaeologists matter as much as the inscriptions.

These impressions can be studied alongside the Sasanian lawbook. The lawbook tells us that jurists argued about authority, obligations, claims, and written instruments. The bullae reveal a material technique through which an identity or office could be attached to a document or parcel. Neither body of evidence fills the other's gaps automatically. We cannot match an orphaned sealing to an imagined lawsuit or turn an abstract legal rule into a dated order from a provincial official. Used together, however, they show that governance required both recognized procedures and physical mechanisms for making actions verifiable.

Return to the clay from Qasr-e Abu Nasr. The five impressions survived because the bundle they once protected was lost. What remains is enough to show that people stamped, recognized, and stored marks of authority in a landscape of forts, roads, estates, and officials. It is not enough to recreate every letter, tax payment, or person who passed through the fortress. The discipline is in learning what an object can prove, and then resisting the temptation to make it speak more clearly than it does.

## Evidence boundary

Clay bullae survive disproportionately where fire baked them. A sealing may identify a person or office without revealing the missing document or merchandise to which it was originally attached. Unprovenanced collections should not be used to locate a specific historical office or transaction. Published count totals refer to individual excavations or studied collections, not the total Sasanian bureaucracy.
