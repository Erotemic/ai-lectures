# A Grant at Kollam: Traders, Churches, and the Work of a Port

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

A port is not only a line of ships at a beach. Goods must be unloaded, inspected, weighed, stored and carried onward. People must resolve disputes about a broken measure, a debt and a cargo whose owner is somewhere across the sea. On India's southwestern coast, the medieval port of Kollam stood within routes connecting the Arabian Sea, Persian Gulf and farther eastern markets. We can recover a part of its working life from an object much less spectacular than a ship: a set of inscribed copper plates that granted rights to a religious community and its commercial associates.

The Tarisappalli or Tharisapalli plates are generally dated to 849 or 850 CE. They place an order in the fifth regnal year of the Chera ruler Sthanu Ravi and name the local ruler Ayyan Adigal Tiruvadi, associated with Venad. They concern a Christian establishment linked to Maruvan Sapir Isho, whose name has several spellings in modern scholarship. The copper is precious evidence because people at the time wanted the terms of an arrangement to remain legible after the original negotiation. The grant was not composed for a future historian; it was meant to have practical force.

The church stands at the center of the grant, but that does not mean the event was only religious. The documents set out land, tax concessions and relationships among people and commercial groups. The grants also connect rights to families of workers, not only to prominent merchants. We should hear that carefully. An agreement that makes a church wealthy or secure might also affect who owes labor to whom, whose dues can be collected and who has standing to settle a dispute. Privilege for one institution is not an automatic statement of freedom for everyone living around it.

Two names appear in discussions of the plates: Manigramam and Anjuvannam. These were organized merchant groupings, though their membership, development and precise legal character cannot be assumed identical from one port to another. The Kollam grant connects them to protection of church property and to the political economy of the marketplace. The presence of collective commercial bodies is significant. A long-distance trader needed more than personal bravery; a merchant association could provide recognized relationships, pooled influence and a way to coordinate demands with rulers and local authorities.

Weights and measures were political matters. A port where one buyer's measure differed from another's could generate profit through unfairness, but also conflict and loss of confidence. Scholars discussing the Kollam plates identify Maruvan Sapir Isho as a major merchant associated with an official responsibility concerning weights and measures. Whether an official personally inspected every transaction is not the point. A formal title links commerce to recognized authority. The same person might belong to a religious network and have a role in governing exchange. Medieval ports could combine offices that modern textbooks place in separate compartments.

The plates have also drawn attention because the witnesses are connected to several scripts and religious communities. Scholars have discussed signatures associated with West Asian traditions, including Jewish, Christian, Muslim and Zoroastrian participants. This does not authorize a portrait of a perfectly harmonious cosmopolitan city. Participation in a legal document is evidence that different networks met within a commercial and political arrangement; it tells us far less about how each group was treated in a quarrel, or how poor people lived outside the circles of those signing.

Christianity along the Malabar coast has origin narratives that place it in apostolic times. Such traditions have a history of their own and were important to later identities. But the copper plates from the ninth century cannot establish that every detail of those narratives happened in the first century. What they securely demonstrate is more limited and more useful for our purpose: by the middle of the ninth century, a Christian institution and named participants were important enough to receive a royal grant at an Indian Ocean port. It is possible to respect the tradition without asking this document to prove what it does not say.

The act of granting an exemption was also a form of statecraft. A ruler surrendered or redirected particular dues, but might gain loyal associates, functioning facilities and a more attractive marketplace. That calculation could involve rivalry with other coastal powers, security along routes or the need to attract merchants whose connections stretched beyond a single court. Pius Malekandathil emphasizes how commercial and religious relationships reinforced one another. We need not suppose that the ruler knew precisely which ship would arrive next to see why a church with merchants behind it was politically valuable.

The grant speaks from the viewpoint of the people authorized to issue, witness and preserve it. Those who rowed boats, made ropes, cooked food or hauled grain mostly remain unnamed. This silence is not evidence that such workers were marginal to the economy. It reminds us what legal documents were designed to commemorate: durable boundaries of rights, not a description of every task that kept trade moving. The named worker families appear mainly as categories in an arrangement. Their presence should sharpen our questions about dependence, mobility and hierarchy rather than disappear into a cheerful story of successful merchants.

The records were written on copper rather than disposable writing material for a reason. Metal plates could be stored, displayed or produced when someone questioned a right. But survival is selective. The grant has been copied, translated and interpreted under later political and religious conditions, and even the best modern edition does not bring back every spoken negotiation. A historian therefore asks which words are clear on the plates, which names depend on transliteration and which arguments come from comparison with other records. Reading a document responsibly means keeping its material history in view.

The ninth-century Kollam grant is a small archive for a much bigger ocean. It shows a coastal kingdom working with commercial organizations and a Christian foundation to define privileges. Its signatures and associations place the port within networks that crossed linguistic and religious boundaries. And its clauses reveal that trade depended on land, law and labor as much as on monsoon winds. The merchant sailing toward Kollam and the worker assigned to a local institution were part of the same system, although the plate lets us hear the powerful much more clearly than the rest.

## Evidence boundary

The surviving Kollam plates date to 849–850 CE and document particular grants and privileges, not the origins of Christianity in India or an exhaustive census of Kollam. Traditions about the apostle Thomas are treated as later traditions, not verified from this ninth-century grant. Interpretations of merchant roles and witness identities follow published specialists rather than assumptions based only on scripts.
