# Power That Moved: Pastoral Worlds and the Empires of the Eurasian Steppe

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

On a map, an empire is often a colored shape. Its borders enclose fields and cities, and its capital is marked with a dot. For the peoples of the Inner Asian grasslands, this familiar picture is misleading. Control could depend on which herding communities followed a leader, which valleys they used in different seasons, and which routes connected people and animals across great distances. A political center might be a mobile court rather than a fixed palace. To understand the early steppe empires, we have to ask what it means to govern people whose movements are both a livelihood and a source of power.

The Eurasian steppe is not a single endless pasture. From the forests of Mongolia and southern Siberia to drier grasslands and desert margins farther west, rainfall, snow, water, and the availability of winter grazing change sharply. Herding families needed detailed knowledge of those differences. Horses, sheep, goats, cattle, and sometimes camels provided transport, food, fiber, and goods for exchange; the composition of herds varied by place. Seasonal movements were choices made within ecological and political constraints. They required household work: tending animals, preparing food, producing felt and clothing, repairing gear, teaching children routes and skills, and negotiating access to pasture. This was a productive way of life, not an absence of settled civilization.

Our written evidence is uneven. Chinese court historians recorded northern peoples because rulers needed information about rivals, allies, and frontier problems. Their texts preserve names, negotiations, and military events, but they also organize foreign societies through the concerns of an imperial court. Archaeology supplies another view: burial grounds, camps, pottery, traces of cultivation, animal bones, and objects made or obtained far away. More recently, research on ancient DNA and isotopes has added evidence about mobility and biological relationships. None of these sources can be substituted for another. A burial can show that an object traveled; it cannot explain every political agreement behind the journey.

The Xiongnu confederation became a formidable power from the late third century before the Common Era. Chinese texts associate its consolidation with a leader called Modu, or Maodun, around 209 BCE. The imperial formation included communities across parts of present-day Mongolia and neighboring regions, and endured in changing forms for roughly three centuries. Its leaders could mobilize armed forces, negotiate with the Han court, and establish relationships among groups with different local histories. The label Xiongnu therefore names a political formation more reliably than it identifies one language, ancestry, or uniform culture. Its scale should not persuade us that every community within it lived in the same way.

From the perspective of Han officials, frontier conflict often took the form of raids, campaigns, gifts, hostages, marriage diplomacy, and arguments over access to markets. Peace agreements could authorize flows of silk or grain; conflict could interrupt them. From the steppe, however, these exchanges were not merely payments extracted from a wealthy neighbor. Alliances and access to goods could help leaders maintain relationships with subordinate communities. A ruler who redistributed coveted materials might strengthen loyalty. The balance changed repeatedly, and the difference between a diplomatic gift and tribute depended in part on whose account survived. The people who bore the costs of campaigns and frontier fortification rarely wrote the records we possess.

Pastoral mobility did not preclude cultivation. Archaeological surveys of the Egiin Gol valley in Mongolia have identified Xiongnu-period settlements, ceramics, and evidence for farming alongside herding. That evidence challenges a picture in which all steppe people lived permanently on horseback and never worked the soil. It does not mean the same combination existed everywhere. A winter settlement, a trading station, and a mobile camp could each be part of one wider regional economy. We learn more by asking where activities occurred and how they were connected than by trying to decide whether an entire population should be classified as nomadic or settled.

Graves reveal both inequality and cultural variety. Some Xiongnu-period burials contain elaborate structures and imported objects, while other burials are modest. Archaeological and genomic research has found considerable diversity among people associated with the empire. An object from far away may indicate trade, diplomacy, inheritance, or movement of people; its presence does not by itself identify the deceased person's language. Biological ancestry is likewise not equivalent to political membership. A confederation could incorporate people from different backgrounds, and a wealthy grave reflects one person's funerary treatment rather than a direct census of living social classes.

Consider the work of supporting a traveling political elite. Horses had to be raised and supplied; carts, saddles, weapons, and textiles had to be made or obtained; messengers had to carry information; winter feeding and water were constant concerns. Imperial politics therefore depended on craftspeople, herders, and intermediaries as well as warriors. Some material goods entered the steppe from China or farther across Eurasia. They could arrive by negotiation, commerce, seizure, or through several stages of exchange. Discovering imported glass or silk does not prove a single merchant traveled from its manufacturing center to the place where archaeologists found it.

The steppe was inhabited by many formations besides the Xiongnu. The Yuezhi, Saka, Xianbei, and later Turkic-speaking groups each appear in different sources and periods. Their histories intersected, sometimes through warfare and displacement, but no single migration story connects them all into a continuous people. It is particularly important not to treat the Xiongnu as simply identical with the much later Huns who entered European records. Scholars debate the possible relationships, but similarity in names or the presence of riding armies does not prove uninterrupted ethnic or political descent across centuries.

In the sixth century CE, rulers identified as Turks, or Türks, organized a powerful khaganate across extensive parts of Inner Asia. They negotiated and fought with Chinese states and interacted with Sogdian cities and other neighbors. Power could shift between eastern and western courts, leaders, and allied communities. The title khagan expressed a claim to leadership over more than a local tribe. A network of mobile people could support expansive authority, yet the same dependence on alliances made political unity fragile. The lands visible on a modern map do not show all the loyalties that had to be sustained to hold the khaganate together.

By the eighth century, some Turkic rulers spoke to posterity in their own written monuments. The Orkhon inscriptions associated with Köl Tegin, Bilge Khagan, and Tonyukuk celebrate leadership, campaigns, and the relationship between rulers and their people. These are among the earliest surviving substantial texts in Old Turkic. They provide a perspective different from a Chinese historian's account of frontier affairs. But they, too, are selective. An inscription erected for a ruler has reasons to praise victories, warn against disloyalty, and present past events as a lesson. It lets us examine political argument, not read a verbatim diary of every person under the khagan's authority.

The Orkhon valley also contains evidence for the Uighur polity that followed the earlier Turkic khaganate. The eighth- and ninth-century center at Khar Balgas shows that rulers with deep pastoral traditions could support substantial built settlements and administrative or ceremonial centers. It is wrong to imagine that urbanism belonged only to one side of a permanent wall between farmers and nomads. Political authorities could use different settlement forms for different purposes. A capital, a seasonal camp, and a distant oasis community might each contribute to a single imperial system without becoming identical places.

Women appear in the archaeological record and sometimes in written sources, but reconstructing their full range of work and authority remains difficult. Burials can offer evidence for status and individual lifeways; they do not tell us every task performed by women in ordinary households. The production of clothing, food, shelter materials, and animal products was indispensable to mobility, whatever formal titles royal histories chose to record. A history written only through male war leaders would leave out much of the labor that made steppe power possible. Yet imposing one universal model of steppe gender relations across a thousand years would be another way of replacing evidence with an expectation.

Environmental change mattered, but it cannot explain political change alone. Snow conditions could make winter pastures difficult, drought could alter migration strategies, and disease could affect herds. Conflict or a failed alliance might deny a community access to needed land at precisely the wrong moment. These forces interacted rather than operating as a single environmental switch. When an empire fragmented, people did not simply disappear from the grasslands. They formed new coalitions, moved into different relationships with neighboring states, retained cultural practices, and adapted economic routines. The breakup of a court is not the end of the society around it.

There is a larger lesson here. Writing from cities often made the steppe visible mainly at moments of war, when mounted forces threatened a frontier. Archaeology and self-authored monuments make a more complicated history possible: herding households, cultivated patches, local settlements, diverse communities, imperial diplomacy, and arguments about legitimate leadership. The governments of Inner Asia were not incomplete copies of agrarian states. They drew on a different set of skills and relationships, and their people helped build the networks connecting Eurasia. The next time a map leaves the grasslands blank, remember that a blank space on paper is not an absence of history.
