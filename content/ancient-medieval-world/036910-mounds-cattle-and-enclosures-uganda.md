# Mounds, Cattle, and Enclosures: Ntusi, Bigo, and Political Change in Uganda

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

In the grasslands of western and southwestern Uganda, some places remain visible because people piled up the debris of daily life; others because they cut great trenches into the earth. The settlement at Ntusi has substantial mounds of ancient refuse, bones, ash, and broken pottery. Not far away, at Bigo bya Mugenyi, earthworks define large spaces with banks and ditches. Both have been drawn into stories about the legendary Cwezi rulers and the beginnings of Great Lakes kingdoms. But a legend and a trench answer different historical questions. The challenge is to give proper attention to both without turning one into an automatic proof of the other.

Ntusi was occupied over several centuries, roughly from the eleventh through the fifteenth century of the common era in the chronology proposed by archaeologist J. E. G. Sutton. The site lies amid the Bwera grasslands and has deposits showing substantial activity, including the remains of cattle and the preparation or use of grain. An archaeological mound is not necessarily an enormous tomb or an earthen palace. Repeated disposal of food remains, ash, and broken objects can build layers that preserve a record of ordinary practices. The excavation of such a mound gives evidence of activity over time, even if it does not tell us how many households were present in every year.

The Ugandan heritage submission for Ntusi describes two major mounds, sometimes called the male and female mounds, and a broad basin known as Bwogero. Excavations have revealed deep deposits of pottery, cattle bone, ash, and stones. Large pots found in one area have been interpreted as evidence for storing food, potentially below ground. These finds invite questions about the quantity of food brought to the site, the animals consumed there, and relations with smaller settlements in the surrounding country. None of these questions requires us first to identify a king. A settlement could become important through work, concentration of people, exchange, and control of resources before anyone left a royal genealogy.

Cattle are particularly visible in Ntusi's faunal record. Large accumulations of their bones indicate repeated consumption and perhaps a wider system for supplying animals from surrounding pasture. Grain evidence points to cultivation and preparation alongside herding. We should resist imposing a later social division in which one separate class only kept cattle while another only worked the fields. Some of the archaeological evidence suggests that grain and animals belonged to interrelated households and economies, although exactly who owned herds, decided slaughter, and distributed food remains open to debate. Consumption at a central place is not the same as a surviving record of tax collection.

Bwogero gives another perspective. The term is applied to a striking basin or depression created or reshaped through human activity. Sutton interpreted this landscape feature in relation to the need for water for people and cattle. The physical scale suggests repeated labor and attention to local conditions. But it does not provide a schedule of who dug, how obligations were assigned, or what ceremonial meanings particular workers attached to the place. To move from the feature to institutions, historians have to compare settlement evidence, environment, and later traditions without assuming that all of those records describe the same generation.

Bigo bya Mugenyi has a different archaeological signature. The Ugandan nomination to UNESCO's tentative World Heritage list describes an extensive system of trenches and enclosures, including outer works connected to the landscape around the Katonga River and nested interior spaces. The tentative listing places them broadly in the fourteenth to sixteenth centuries. This is not the same as claiming that all trenches were dug at once. Dating such features is difficult, because a charcoal sample may date a deposit above or below construction rather than the exact day a ditch was cut. Earthworks can also be repaired and reused over many years.

What were the ditches for? Earlier interpretations often called them forts. Peter Robertshaw challenged treating that answer as self-evident. He suggested that functions could have overlapped: excluding elephants from cultivation, protecting a ruler or valuable property, regulating the movement of people and goods, enclosing animals, and demonstrating collective power. Some possibilities fit the shape of particular features better than others. Without a written account from the builders, the relative importance of defense, farming, ceremony, and political display remains contested. The scale of the work, however, establishes that substantial labor was mobilized, and that fact itself demands historical explanation.

Munsa offers a third comparison. This western Ugandan site, centered around the hill of Bikegete, has evidence for ironworking, cattle consumption, probably grain storage, burial, and other activities. Excavations indicate a long history of use, with some activity beginning well before the major encircling ditches. Robertshaw's dating work proposed that the earthworks at Munsa and Kibengo were probably made in the fifteenth or sixteenth centuries, with Bigo possibly comparable or somewhat earlier. A site occupied in the tenth or eleventh century need not have had its later trenches then. That distinction prevents us from assigning every find inside an enclosure to the century in which the enclosure was built.

Now consider the Cwezi traditions. In several later Great Lakes societies, oral histories tell of powerful rulers, ancestral figures, and the realm often called Kitara. These traditions matter as historical practices: they transmit accounts of legitimacy, descent, place, and the past, and they have been reinterpreted in different political circumstances. Some earlier scholarship tried to assign named Cwezi rulers to the earthworks as if the trenches were surviving royal signatures. Robertshaw argued that the archaeology did not establish a single ancient Cwezi empire, and proposed the possibility of distinct contemporary polities. Sutton likewise warned against literally matching legendary dynasties with excavated places. Neither position makes the oral histories meaningless; it changes the questions historians ask of them.

The evidence also allows us to ask why some settlements grew large. Grain cultivation, cattle herds, access to water, and connections to exchange networks could help support places like Ntusi. A central site might accumulate wealth by receiving animals, food, craft products, or visitors from smaller settlements. But the shape of a landscape does not tell us whether a ruler collected tribute, hosted feasts, organized seasonal work, or exercised coercion in exactly the same way as later kingdoms. Those possibilities need comparison with the distributions of materials, settlement sizes, and dated occupation phases. The existence of political complexity does not mean that one modern constitutional label fits every excavated community.

Environmental evidence adds another complication. Pollen, charcoal, and sediments from western Uganda record periods of changing vegetation and moisture. Researchers such as David Taylor, Peter Robertshaw, and colleagues have asked how agriculture, cattle, settlement concentration, and climate might have interacted. Forest reduction may reflect clearing as well as drying; apparent settlement decline could result from a movement toward dispersed households rather than a demographic catastrophe. An earthwork left without its earlier occupants need not mark a single dramatic conquest. The archaeological and environmental sequences can help constrain such explanations, but cannot make each one certain.

Our history ends at a chronological seam. Ntusi's earlier occupation and the beginning of some major earthworks lie before 1500, but much of the excavated evidence at Munsa and Bigo reaches into the sixteenth century and beyond. We should not squeeze those later phases backward simply to fit a course that leads toward the early modern world. Instead, the sites show processes that crossed that boundary: changing landscapes of cattle and grain, expanding capacity for collective labor, and political institutions whose later histories became part of powerful stories about origins. In the mounds and ditches, we have traces of those developments. In the traditions, we have histories people continued to tell about them. Responsible historical reconstruction keeps the two kinds of evidence in conversation without requiring them to say the same thing.

## Evidence boundary

The excavated chronology of a settlement must not be equated with the age of its later ditches. Ntusi's earlier occupation and the fifteenth- to sixteenth-century earthworks overlap the course boundary at 1500. The UNESCO records are Uganda's tentative nominations, not World Heritage inscriptions. Cwezi/Kitara traditions are historically consequential, but no excavation cited here establishes a single empire ruled by the named legendary figures. Proposed earthwork functions remain interpretations.
