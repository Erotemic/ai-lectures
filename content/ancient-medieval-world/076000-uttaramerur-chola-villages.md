# A Name Drawn from a Pot: Village Government under the Cholas

In a village of southern India, the names of eligible men were written on slips and placed in a pot. A boy would draw a slip, and the selected name would be read before an assembly. This procedure is described in inscriptions at Uttaramerur, in what is now Tamil Nadu, dating to the early tenth century. The scene is often offered as proof that ancient India possessed democracy. The surviving records support a more instructive history. They show an organized local assembly, detailed qualifications for office, restrictions on who could serve, and a king's involvement in revising the rules. They also show why a procedure for selection is not the same as political equality.

The Chola state grew in power across parts of southern India from the ninth century onward and reached a wider imperial scale in later centuries. Armies, temples, tax systems, irrigated farms, ports, and competing regional rulers all shaped its reach. The Cauvery delta supplied important agricultural resources, but it should not stand in for the entire peninsula. A useful history of Chola government must move between the court's campaigns and the local institutions that managed land, water, obligations, and disputes. Royal inscriptions tell us what rulers wished to claim; the records of village corporations reveal a second set of political actors.

Uttaramerur was a brahmadeya settlement, meaning a village or tract associated with land granted to Brahmins. Its assembly, often called a sabha, was not simply a gathering of every adult resident. The rules recorded under Parantaka the First concerned committees of qualified male landholders. Eligibility required property and residence, an age within a specified range, and recognized learning, among other conditions. Certain kinds of misconduct and conflicts of interest could exclude candidates. The inscription thus reveals both administrative expectations and social boundaries. Tenants, landless laborers, and many people whose work sustained the settlement were not represented on equal terms in this scheme.

The celebrated selection method was called kudavolai, referring to the pot and written slips. The regulations divided the village into wards and created committees with responsibilities that could include tanks, gardens, and other common resources. A person eligible for one committee might be barred from immediate return after service; the rules addressed the problem of repeated officeholding. These details matter because they make the inscription a record of institutional design, not merely a vague statement that local people governed themselves. Procedure, membership, accountability, and disqualification are distinct problems, even when they appear on the same stone.

The surviving rules were not timeless custom. Two inscriptions under Parantaka, commonly dated to 919 and 921, set out and amend procedures. The later revision suggests that existing arrangements were capable of generating disputes or failing in practice. The king's order helped authorize changes that the village assembly recorded. Historians should therefore avoid both extremes: imagining a completely autonomous republic unaffected by royal institutions, or a village whose decisions were simply dictated from a distant capital. The very fact that the rules needed to be written and revised suggests negotiation among local authority, social standing, and royal power.

Another distinction is among the types of settlements. Brahmadeya villages with sabhas were not identical to other villages, frequently described through the ur and its assembly. Regional and occupational differences mattered. Scholars of Chola administration, including Noboru Karashima and others who worked systematically with inscriptions, stress how landholding, service relations, revenue, and corporate bodies varied. It is dangerous to take an unusually detailed record from one Brahmin settlement and call it the constitution of every Chola village. It can be a valuable source precisely because its limits are named.

The tank committee invites us to look away from the names of officeholders and toward water. In much of southern India, irrigation depended on channels, embankments, storage tanks, and seasonal cooperation. A village could have a formal responsibility for maintaining a tank, but a committee's title does not tell us exactly who removed silt, repaired damage, or bore the cost. Landowners had an interest in reliable water because crops and revenue depended on it. Laborers and cultivators had a stake because a failed harvest threatened daily subsistence. The inscription allows us to study decision-making, but the practical work of keeping fields productive needs other forms of evidence as well.

Temples joined this system in several ways. They were places of worship and ceremony, but inscriptions also record their land, revenue rights, endowments, and employees. A donor could assign a field or income stream to maintain a lamp, feed a religious community, or support ritual activities. Such gifts created obligations that could persist after the original donor's death. They also generated documents. Stone inscriptions were a way to make rights and promises public, to discourage later denial, and to display the donor's status. That publicity favored some claims over others: a temple wall tells us more readily about an endowment than about a worker's unsuccessful request for wages.

Royal expansion and village life were linked but not identical. Chola armies fought in Sri Lanka, parts of peninsular India, and across maritime routes. Ships and port markets offered forms of exchange to merchants and officials, while wars could impose taxes, service demands, and displacement. A royal claim to possess distant places does not by itself prove sustained control of all of them. Local assemblies could endure through changes at court, just as royal revenue officers could intrude into village matters. A history focused only on conquest would miss how the state was reproduced in recurring acts of assessment, arbitration, irrigation, and worship.

Women are present in temple donations and in the wider social history of southern India, even if the Uttaramerur committee qualifications exclude them from the particular offices described. Some women controlled resources, commissioned gifts, or worked in the institutionally complex life of temples. Other women performed agricultural and domestic work that records rarely name. We should not confuse exclusion from one assembly with absolute absence from economic and religious agency. Nor should we romanticize named female donors as evidence that all women had similar opportunities. Different documents reveal different capacities, and none represents every household.

Writing itself shaped who appears. Chola inscriptions were often engraved in Tamil, sometimes alongside Sanskrit or other forms associated with courtly and religious claims. An inscription might contain a precise formula for witnesses, a boundary description, or an order about revenue. The precision is useful, but it is precision within a selected transaction. An omitted tenant does not become historically irrelevant. A rule written on a temple wall records what authorities prescribed; it does not guarantee that every rule was obeyed every year. We need to distinguish a legal aspiration, an actual selection event, and the long-term performance of an institution.

Historians have read these inscriptions differently. Older narratives often celebrated village self-government as an enduring national institution. More recent scholarship has examined who held the land, how caste and status structured access, and how royal power interacted with local corporate bodies. Those questions do not diminish the administrative sophistication of the inscriptions. They make it possible to explain it accurately. Procedures for eligibility, auditing, and committee rotation are meaningful historical achievements; describing them does not require importing modern universal suffrage into a tenth-century Brahmin settlement.

Imagine the pot again. A name is drawn, read, and recognized by those present. What does that moment establish? There was an acknowledged process for filling an office, sustained by people able to write, assess qualifications, and meet in an assembly. What does it leave unanswered? How were ordinary cultivators affected by decisions? Who could challenge an assessment? How did the village handle scarcity, debt, or conflict beyond the concerns the inscription names? The answers require wider social history, not a slogan about democracy or monarchy. Uttaramerur is valuable because its stone records let us see a working political technology: rules created by people, amended when circumstances demanded, and embedded in a society of unequal rights and obligations.
