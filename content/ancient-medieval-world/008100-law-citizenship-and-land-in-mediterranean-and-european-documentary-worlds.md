# Law, citizenship, and land in Mediterranean and European documentary worlds

What can a written claim establish, and whose lives remain outside it? Inscriptions, civic rules, charters, and land records offer several answers. We will cross large gaps in time and move among societies with different institutions; a shared question about evidence is the thread that connects them.

## Letters across the Water: Phoenician Cities and the Making of a Mediterranean

A small inscription on stone may be easier to carry through time than the ship that brought a merchant across the sea. The timbers decay. The sail disappears. A few scratches naming a person, a gift, or a god may survive. Along the coast of the eastern Mediterranean, communities made both kinds of things: ships that connected distant shores, and inscriptions that preserved speech in a compact writing system. We often call these communities Phoenician. Their history is larger than an alphabet, but the alphabet helps explain how the history of many other places became entangled with theirs.

The name Phoenician comes to us through Greek writers. It was not the official name of one unified kingdom. Tyre, Sidon, Byblos, and Arwad occupied a narrow and often politically divided coastal zone in what is now principally Lebanon and neighboring parts of the Levant. Their fortunes changed with those of Egypt, Assyria, Babylon, Persia, and many smaller neighbors. A sailor from Tyre did not necessarily serve the same ruler or have the same interests as a craftsman in Byblos. Treating all these cities as a single nation hides the rivalries that made their political history.

Their cities were not created out of nowhere after the great disruptions around 1200 before the Common Era. Byblos had an extensive Bronze Age past, and the eastern Mediterranean had traded wood, metals, oils, textiles, and other goods for centuries. What changed across the early Iron Age was the organization of power and exchange. Some palace networks contracted; other towns and ports expanded their opportunities. Coastal traders adapted to unsettled conditions, sometimes working under powerful inland empires and sometimes beyond those empires' immediate supervision. The word revival can be useful here, but only if we remember the older skills and relationships on which that revival depended.

A coastal city could have access to one resource that mattered far inland: timber. The mountains behind the ports supplied trees sought for construction and shipbuilding. Craftspeople also worked ivory, metals, wood, glass, and cloth. Purple dye, extracted from particular marine mollusks through labor-intensive processing, became associated with luxury production. Neither purple cloth nor cedar explains every voyage. The important point is that maritime commerce joined land, forests, workshops, harbors, and political demands. An object sold in one city might embody work performed in several others.

Now consider writing. The Phoenician script was a compact way of recording a Semitic language with signs used chiefly for consonants. It was not the world's first writing, nor did Phoenician merchants single-handedly invent the alphabet. Early alphabetic experiments had developed centuries before in Egyptian and Levantine contact zones, and scholars continue to argue about their precise routes of development. What the Phoenician cities did was sustain and circulate a durable branch of that alphabetic tradition. Inscriptions from places such as Byblos let us examine the actual letter forms, rather than assuming the story from later accounts of a miraculous invention.

A sign system can travel while changing its function. Speakers of Greek adapted an alphabet related to Phoenician writing and employed some signs to represent vowel sounds. That was not simply a copy, and it did not make the older script defective. Each writing system served languages with different structures. Through later Greek and Italian developments, these experiments contributed to the alphabets familiar in much of Europe. Other branches of the wider Northwest Semitic writing world, including Aramaic, followed their own consequential trajectories. The history is one of borrowing and modification, not a single people handing finished literacy to everyone else.

What did the alphabet change for the people who used it? It made possible relatively short inscriptions on objects, monuments, offerings, and tombs. It did not remove the need to learn the language, understand legal and religious conventions, or gain access to materials. A society can have an alphabet and remain deeply unequal in whose words are preserved. Most surviving Phoenician texts do not give us a private journal of ordinary port workers. We know substantially more about public dedications and elite transactions than about the conversations heard on a dock at dawn.

The sea routes moved westward across Cyprus, Sicily, Sardinia, North Africa, and the Iberian Peninsula. These were not empty lands into which newcomers simply arrived. Local communities already possessed ways of farming, exchanging, worshiping, and governing. At some places newcomers joined existing settlements; at others they established new centers and entered relationships of collaboration, competition, or coercion. Archaeology has to distinguish an imported vessel from a resident merchant and a resident merchant from a conquering government. Goods can move far without their makers moving at all.

Carthage, on the coast of what is now Tunisia, became one of the most consequential western centers. A traditional foundation date of 814 before the Common Era belongs to later historical memory; it is not a perfectly observed founding day. Over time, Carthage developed its own political institutions and influence among ports and inland communities of North Africa. In the western Mediterranean, the term Punic is often used for its related language and culture. By the first millennium before the Common Era, people in the west were not merely distant extensions of Tyre. Their histories had become rooted in African places and relationships.

An empire could be close to a Phoenician port without controlling its entire commercial horizon. Assyrian kings demanded tribute and made military interventions. Later political systems placed other demands on coastal cities. Tribute, taxation, and naval service mattered to rulers; fishermen, dock workers, metalworkers, and merchant households still had to negotiate the ordinary risks of weather, debt, illness, and uncertain prices. Our evidence rarely lets us hear each group equally. Accounts written by outside empires describe the coast as a source of supplies or a target of policy. Their purposes were not those of a household in Sidon.

The material record reveals a world that could borrow without becoming identical. A carved ivory might use a motif associated with Egypt, a metal vessel a technique shared around the eastern Mediterranean, and a dedication an alphabetic script used in several cities. This mixture does not prove that the artist or owner possessed a modern cosmopolitan identity. It does show that older labels such as Egyptian, Greek, and Phoenician can obscure the circulation of skilled labor and visual ideas. The most persuasive historical explanation begins with the object, its dating, and where it was found.

Later Greek and Roman writers left dramatic accounts of Phoenician cleverness and Carthaginian power. Their texts are valuable, but many were written amid rivalry, war, or retrospective explanation. Carthaginian dedicatory stelae, including examples studied in museum catalogues, give a different sort of access: named dedications, languages, and religious practices. Even these stones leave difficult questions. A votive object can establish that a dedication was made. It cannot automatically explain every practice associated with a sanctuary or validate every accusation made by an enemy.

The story also has an ending that is too often told too neatly. In 146 before the Common Era, Roman armies destroyed Carthage during the Third Punic War. A major political center was devastated, and lives and institutions were changed by conquest. Yet Punic language and cultural practices did not vanish on that day. Evidence for later Punic and Latin-letter Punic writing reminds us that the end of a state is not the immediate disappearance of a population. The western Mediterranean after Rome's conquest retained histories older than Roman rule.

If we ask what the Phoenicians gave the world, an alphabet is the familiar answer. A better historical question is how a set of politically distinct coastal cities became participants in networks that others also built. Letters, timber, dye, ships, workshops, imperial demands, and negotiations with local communities all mattered. None alone explains the Mediterranean. The enduring transformation was a multiplication of routes through which objects, words, and institutions could change hands, take new forms, and persist long after the circumstances of their making had passed.

## Between the Story and the Stone: Israel, Judah, and the Empires Around Them

Remaining in the eastern Mediterranean, we can set inscriptions beside archaeology and much later textual transmission.

A king tells the story of his victory by carving it into stone. Another king commissions pictures of a siege on the walls of a palace. A community, centuries later, preserves histories, poems, laws, and arguments about what the catastrophe meant. These records can describe overlapping events without being the same kind of witness. To study ancient Israel and Judah, we need all three: the evidence of neighboring kingdoms, the archaeology of settlements and war, and the long history of texts that became the Hebrew Bible. We also need to ask what each source was made to accomplish.

In the late Bronze Age, the southern Levant lay within networks of Egyptian influence and regional city-states. An Egyptian royal inscription associated with Merneptah, around the end of the thirteenth century before the Common Era, contains the earliest generally recognized reference to a people called Israel outside the biblical tradition. The pharaoh claims to have defeated them. A boast on a victory monument cannot be treated as a reliable census, and the name does not supply a constitution or a map. But it matters that a contemporary Egyptian court thought this named group was worth mentioning.

The hill country changed over subsequent centuries. Archaeologists study small settlements, storage practices, pottery, and changing agricultural landscapes, asking how communities organized themselves as older regional powers weakened or adapted. The emergence of kingdoms was gradual and contested, not an instant transformation from scattered tribes into a unified modern state. Biblical narratives speak of Saul, David, and Solomon, but those narratives were shaped by processes of writing, editing, and transmission much later than the reigns they describe. What can be reconstructed independently about the extent of early monarchy remains debated.

By the ninth century before the Common Era, inscriptions from neighboring rulers offer firmer points of comparison. The Mesha Stele, made for a king of Moab, boasts of recovering territory from Israel and speaks of the house of Omri. It is a Moabite victory claim, not a neutral history of Moab's neighbors. At Tel Dan, an Aramaic inscription probably commissioned after battles in the region refers to a ruler of Israel and, in the widely accepted reading, the house of David. That dynastic phrase does not verify every episode in the biblical stories about David. It tells us something narrower and historically important: a southern royal house could be identified by that name.

The northern kingdom of Israel and the southern kingdom of Judah were not interchangeable. Israel's political and economic center included Samaria and a fertile northern landscape; Judah's centers included Jerusalem and its surrounding highlands. Their rulers could compete, ally, and face outside threats in different ways. The written sources that survived are uneven. Royal and religious institutions often preserved their own interests, while agricultural work, local customary practices, and the voices of enslaved or displaced people are less directly recorded. Any account confined to named kings mistakes the archive's bias for the shape of society.

Assyria transformed the region's options in the eighth century. Assyrian campaigns demanded submission, tribute, and sometimes the transfer of populations. The conquest of Samaria, generally placed in 722 or 720 before the Common Era depending on the event and chronology being described, ended the northern kingdom as an independent monarchy. Deportations and provincial reorganization changed communities, but they did not mean that every inhabitant simply vanished. The later history of people identifying with Israel includes continuity, migration, and new forms of collective memory. To say that a state ended is not to say that everyone who lived there ceased to have a history.

Judah faced Assyrian power under King Hezekiah. In 701 before the Common Era, Sennacherib's armies campaigned in Judah. Here three evidence streams meet. An Assyrian prism, an account prepared for royal commemoration years after the campaign, celebrates cities taken and tribute secured. Relief panels from Sennacherib's palace portray the siege of Lachish, showing siege machinery, captives, violence, and royal triumph. Excavations at Lachish uncover destruction and the material consequences of attack. These sources agree on a grave military event, even though each has a different purpose and none tells every civilian's story.

Jerusalem creates a further problem of interpretation. Sennacherib's inscription describes Hezekiah as confined in his city, but it does not claim the capture of Jerusalem. Biblical accounts explain the city's survival in religious terms. Historians can compare what these accounts assert and what archaeology suggests, but a royal omission is not a transcript of battlefield decisions. Nor does the city surviving the campaign mean that Judah's villages were safe. An imperial victory can be real for a countryside even when its capital remains standing. That distinction matters for understanding displacement and tribute.

A century later, the balance of power changed again. The Neo-Babylonian kingdom inherited influence in the region after Assyria's decline. A Babylonian chronicle records that Nebuchadnezzar took Jerusalem in 597 before the Common Era, seized its king, appointed another, and carried away wealth. The short cuneiform report is particularly useful because it was not written as a later interpretation of biblical prophecy. It also has limits. It does not list the experiences of every person deported or left behind. It is an entry in an imperial chronicle, written from the conqueror's side.

The later destruction of Jerusalem and its temple in 587 or 586 before the Common Era became a defining catastrophe in Judahite and later Jewish memory. Differences in modern chronological conventions account for the familiar alternative dates. Deportation did not involve every resident, and settlement continued in the land while communities developed in Babylonia and elsewhere. Families could be dispersed, property reorganized, institutions damaged, and religious practice transformed. The word exile names a historical experience, but it can also hide the unequal circumstances of those who were moved and those who remained.

What became the Hebrew Bible preserves multiple voices addressing kingship, justice, covenant, worship, social conflict, and disaster. Some passages interpret events as divine judgment; others mourn loss, dispute power, preserve law, or envision restoration. Historical scholarship studies their composition and transmission without pretending that every passage was written at the time it depicts. To investigate when a text took a particular form is not to decide whether its religious meaning matters to those who read it. It is to ask a different question. A prophetic warning and a palace chronicle cannot be treated as identical evidence just because both speak about a king.

Persian conquest of Babylon in 539 before the Common Era brought another change of imperial setting. Judean communities, including people in Babylonia, encountered opportunities and constraints under Achaemenid rule. Temple rebuilding in Jerusalem, often placed toward the end of the sixth century, was a significant institutional development, but not a universal return of everyone who had been displaced. Law, worship, scribal activity, and communal identity continued to develop across several locations. It would be misleading to imagine a single moment in which all the practices of later Judaism were created at once.

The ancient evidence does not authorize easy claims about a direct, uninterrupted identity between an Iron Age kingdom and any modern population or state. Political boundaries, languages, institutions, and collective memories changed repeatedly. The history of Israel and Judah is indispensable to understanding the religious traditions that later developed, and it is also the history of people living within an eastern Mediterranean world of competing powers. Neither significance excuses a careless leap from a damaged inscription to a complete story.

Return to the three witnesses with which we began. The Moabite king celebrates victory. The Assyrian king displays a city under siege. Judahite and later Jewish writers preserve arguments about kings, obligation, suffering, and survival. The records disagree at points and answer different questions, but they allow us to establish real political changes and to hear how those changes were remembered. The historian's task is not to choose between stone and story. It is to explain what each can tell us, who was left outside its frame, and why the distinction still matters.

## Who Counted as the People? Democracy, Law, and Labor in Athens

In the Greek world, the question shifts from the claims of kings to the boundaries of a citizen body.

In ancient Athens, citizens assembled to decide what their city should do. Speakers make proposals. Hands rise to vote. Some of the participants will later sit on a jury; others may serve on a council selected by lot. The scene contains recognizable elements of democracy, but before describing it as government by the people we must ask who counted as the people. The answer was written into institutions of citizenship, marriage, property, work, and law. Athenian democracy was a real redistribution of political authority within a bounded community, not equal participation by everyone who lived there.

The system developed through conflicts over power during the sixth and fifth centuries before the Common Era. Athenians later attributed important reforms to Solon and Cleisthenes, among others. The surviving account conventionally called Aristotle's Constitution of the Athenians was composed much later, in the fourth century. That text is valuable, but it looks backward through traditions, documents, and political arguments. We should distinguish a later explanation of reform from a verbatim record of what a sixth-century farmer believed he was doing. Institutions changed over time; the democracy of one generation was not identical to the democracy of another.

One crucial institution was the deme, a local civic unit. The Constitution of the Athenians describes the enrollment of young male citizens at eighteen, with fellow demesmen judging age and qualifying parentage. By the late fifth and fourth centuries, descent from two citizen parents was a condition of citizenship. A family relationship thus became a public qualification. An Athenian-born person could live among neighbors, speak Greek, worship local gods, and still be excluded from the assembly. The boundaries of a political community were enforced by people who knew and judged one another.

Women born into citizen families mattered deeply to the reproduction of citizenship and to religion, household management, and property arrangements, but they did not possess the political rights of adult male citizens. Resident foreigners, conventionally called metics, contributed labor, crafts, commerce, and taxes without automatically gaining a vote. Enslaved people worked in homes, workshops, fields, mines, and public services without freedom of civic participation. An institution can be participatory for its recognized members and coercive toward people excluded from membership. Both descriptions are required if we want an accurate political history.

A surviving passage from Xenophon's Ways and Means treats resident foreigners as an important source of revenue. It proposes policies to encourage their presence and reduce burdens that made their work less attractive. This is not an abolitionist argument for equal rights. Xenophon is calculating what the city could gain from a population whose economic importance he recognized. The passage tells us something that an assembly diagram does not: the operation of citizen politics depended on people without a vote. Their labor and taxes helped support the conditions in which democratic participation was possible.

Within the citizen body, participation took multiple forms. The assembly was not the whole state. A council helped prepare business, public officials carried out tasks, and large citizen juries decided disputes. Selection by lot for many offices expressed mistrust of permanent monopolies by wealthy or distinguished families, although some military and other positions were elected. Officials served limited terms and could face scrutiny. These arrangements gave political activity a regular shape. They also relied on scribes, heralds, slaves employed in public duties, and the physical infrastructure of meeting places and recordkeeping.

Payment for civic service changed who could afford to take part. The Constitution of the Athenians recounts arguments and changes over compensation for assembly attendance. Jury service also came to be paid. Such measures mattered because a farmer or artisan lost time from earning and producing when attending a meeting. Payment could widen participation among citizens of modest means, yet it did not erase unequal wealth, distance from the city, or differences in social influence. Having a legal vote and having equal capacity to speak, organize, and persuade are not the same thing.

Political speeches reveal a society that argued intensely over justice, honor, public need, and private advantage. But speeches surviving in literary collections were selected, transmitted, and often edited through long histories. They disproportionately preserve the words of trained male speakers. Women could bring actions through male representatives in many circumstances, and enslaved people appear in legal narratives largely through the claims of owners and litigants. The courtroom record is therefore informative and slanted at once. It shows how Athenians represented disputes, not a complete record of what every participant thought.

It is tempting to divide Athens into two stories: admirable democratic citizens and regrettable excluded groups. The division can make the excluded seem like an afterthought. Instead consider how household organization, military service, imperial tribute, and urban production were bound to civic authority. The citizen who spent a day at a jury might return to a household where women, children, hired workers, or enslaved people managed essential tasks. Political equality among citizens did not require equality in the home. The contradiction was built into how this democracy defined who belonged to the ruling community.

Athens was also one city among many Greek-speaking communities. Sparta used a different political and social organization, and Greek cities elsewhere had democracies, oligarchies, tyrannies, and changing combinations of these forms. Even Athens alternated between democratic and oligarchic regimes during crises, including the late fifth century. Its political vocabulary was not a single permanent achievement that settled all later conflict. The same citizens who prized debate could support war, repression, and imperial demands on allied cities. Self-government at home did not guarantee respect for self-government abroad.

The treatises of Aristotle are useful precisely because they do not simply celebrate one regime. He compares constitutions and asks what a citizen is. Yet his discussions of women and people enslaved by others also show the assumptions of an elite Greek philosopher. His claim that some forms of subordination are natural is a philosophical justification, not evidence that subordination was fair or biologically inevitable. We learn about ancient politics by reading arguments critically, including arguments made by people close to its institutions. A text can document an ideology without establishing that the ideology was true.

No simple population percentage can measure Athenian democracy without specifying a period, a place, and whether the count includes children, enslaved people, foreigners, and citizens living away from the city. Estimates vary, and the evidence is incomplete. The more secure point is institutional: a restricted adult male citizen body held direct decision-making powers, while much of the population remained outside it. That is enough to ask precise questions about representation. When a law was passed, who could propose it, who could challenge it, who had to obey it, and who had no voice in its adoption?

The answer also changed across time. Restrictions on citizenship, compensation for political participation, procedures for registering citizens, and responses to civil conflict were historical choices, not timeless properties of being Greek. Surviving writing usually describes those choices from the perspective of people who were already citizens or closely connected to civic institutions. Archaeology, inscriptions, and the study of household and labor systems widen the frame. They do not give every excluded person a surviving speech, but they prevent the most prominent public speeches from becoming the only history.

Athenian democracy mattered because it demonstrated that political power could be organized through ordinary members of a defined citizen community, with public argument, voting, courts, and scrutiny of officeholders. It also demonstrates that the definition of the citizen can carry the deepest inequality of a political system. Calling Athens either a modern democracy in ancient clothing or an empty fraud loses something important. A sound history can describe its participatory institutions without borrowing their exclusions. The question of who counts as the people belongs at the center of the story, not at its edge.

## A Charter under Pressure: Magna Carta and the Claims of Law

We now cross many centuries to medieval England. Magna Carta belongs to a different political world from Athens; the connection is our question about whom a legal claim includes.

In June 1215, King John of England met rebellious barons near the River Thames at Runnymede. The agreement that emerged was later called Magna Carta, the Great Charter. In popular memory it is often the beginning of constitutional freedom, a moment when a king accepted the rule of law for everyone. The document itself tells a more specific story. Its clauses respond to disputes about land, inheritance payments, justice, forests, towns, and royal interference. It was a political settlement negotiated under pressure, and understanding its limits is essential to understanding its later importance.

John had fought costly wars, lost territories in France, and demanded resources from elites he needed to govern. Barons complained about arbitrary financial demands and abuses of royal authority. Their rebellion was not a general democratic uprising. Many of the negotiators were powerful landholders seeking to protect their own rights and the customary arrangements on which their influence rested. Their anger could still produce rules with consequences beyond their personal interests. That is one reason Magna Carta matters: a conflict among unequal political actors could create language later used by people outside the original bargain.

A charter was a familiar type of medieval document. Kings and lords issued authenticated records of grants, privileges, and obligations. Magna Carta's form was not the invention of written law in England. What made the 1215 agreement politically significant was the scale and subjects of the constraints demanded from the king, including provisions for enforcement. The written text turned grievances into claims that officials could be instructed to recognize. Yet a written promise only mattered if people had the power to insist on it. At Runnymede, the immediate balance of military and political pressure gave the document force.

A famous clause protects a free man against imprisonment or dispossession except through lawful judgment or the law of the land. We should read those words in their thirteenth-century setting. The phrase free man did not include the whole population, and customary legal processes could differ by status and locality. Many people were unfree tenants or otherwise subordinated. The charter did not establish modern equal citizenship or universal voting rights. It did express an influential demand that royal officials follow recognized procedures rather than simply treating power as sufficient justification.

The 1215 charter also dealt with practical obligations: the sums owed when lands passed to heirs, wardship, the treatment of widows, and the protections claimed by merchants and towns. These subjects reveal the daily material stakes of royal government. A fine or inheritance payment could alter a family's economic future. A dispute over wardship could determine who controlled land and marriage arrangements. The text records the concerns that made powerful people resist John, but it also shows how law shaped families, trading communities, and property across the kingdom.

One provision has received much attention because it called for a group of twenty-five barons to help enforce the agreement against the king. That was a dramatic concession in a royal charter. It was also part of the 1215 settlement's immediate failure. John soon rejected the terms, with papal support, and the conflict continued into civil war. A document whose language would become enduring was not a successful peace treaty in its first form. This is an important distinction between the history of a text and the later history of its reputation.

John died in 1216. Those governing for his young successor, Henry the Third, reissued a revised charter, seeking political support. Further versions followed in 1217 and 1225. The differences matter. Some original clauses disappeared, and in 1217 the forest provisions were treated in a separate Charter of the Forest. The version of 1225 became especially influential in later law. A reader who quotes a clause without identifying which issue it belongs to may mix together different political settlements. Magna Carta is a family of related texts, not one unchanging object.

Royal forest law shows why this matters to people beyond the highest aristocracy. Forest designation could affect the use of woodlands, grazing, hunting, and access to resources. A forest in medieval legal language was a zone of special royal regulation, not necessarily a landscape covered entirely with trees. The Charter of the Forest addressed rights and restrictions around such areas. Its existence reminds us that constitutional history can concern access to land and livelihood as much as the procedure of courts. The authority to call an area forest had material consequences for communities living within it.

By 1297 a version of Magna Carta was confirmed and entered the English statute tradition. The charter's later status depended on repeated reissuance, political contest, legal citation, and changing interpretations. Historians and lawyers did not simply preserve the intentions of the barons of 1215. They reread the text in disputes shaped by their own periods. In later centuries, advocates of rights and limits on government selected aspects of Magna Carta that could serve new purposes. A document can acquire influence far beyond what its first negotiators envisaged without having contained those later ideas fully formed.

The surviving copies themselves are historical evidence. Four exemplars of the 1215 issue remain, along with later copies and confirmations. Their parchment, seals, handwriting, and differences of transmission matter to scholars of law and manuscripts. Museums may display a document as a symbol of liberty, but its material history records the mundane task of producing texts that officials and communities could consult. Someone prepared the parchment, wrote the clauses, sealed the charter, carried it, and preserved it. A legal rule entered public life through practical communications as well as political theory.

The charter's exclusions are not a footnote. Women appear in the text in relation to specific property and family concerns, but they were not recognized as a general political constituency. Unfree peasants and many others were not represented in the negotiations. Free townspeople and merchants could benefit from particular provisions without receiving equal control of government. We should neither pretend the charter was a declaration of universal human rights nor conclude that it was insignificant because it was narrow. The historical question is what its specific limits did and how later groups worked to change their meaning.

Many people whose lives were touched by these rules left no written account of Runnymede. A tenant facing a disputed forest fine, a widow negotiating her rights, or a trader crossing a town boundary might experience royal authority quite differently from a baron. Medieval records can reveal aspects of those disputes, but they rarely let us reconstruct the feelings of every participant. Studying the charter alongside administrative orders and later legal cases gives us a fuller view than treating its most famous clauses as a self-contained moral lesson.

Law is more than words, and Magna Carta demonstrates this point repeatedly. First, its bargaining depended on military pressure. Second, its earliest form failed to secure lasting peace. Third, successors renewed and changed it for their own political reasons. Fourth, later judges, rulers, and political movements argued about what its language authorized. The changing power of the charter was not stored inside a single piece of parchment, waiting to be discovered. It developed through institutions, memories, disputes, and the uneven capacity of people to bring claims.

Magna Carta remains historically consequential because it supplied durable words for the idea that rulers could be bound by law. The path from the 1215 agreement to later constitutional traditions was neither direct nor inevitable. It was made by successive generations, each with different conflicts and different exclusions. Begin with John and the barons, but do not end there; read the revised versions, the forest charter, the courts, and the people whose daily lives were regulated. A charter can be famous for principles later readers found within it, while still requiring us to understand the narrower bargain that first produced it.

## The Village as a Taxpayer: Farming and Property in Byzantium

To compare another form of obligation, return to the eastern Mediterranean slightly earlier, in the twelfth-century Byzantine world.

In the year 1103, a record associated with the monastery of Iviron on Mount Athos concerned a village called Radolibos. The documents studied by historian Kostis Smyrlis identify one hundred and twenty-two peasant families living there. It is a precise number, and for that reason it feels intimate. We can begin to imagine a village with many households, fields requiring work, and obligations that reached beyond its boundaries. But precision in a fiscal record is not the same thing as a census of every human life. We must ask why a monastery wanted this number and how its relationship to land made the residents legible to people far away.

The eastern Roman, or Byzantine, Empire ruled changing territories around the eastern Mediterranean for more than a thousand years. Its inhabitants did not all live in Constantinople, and their daily institutions cannot be described only through emperors, bishops, and wars. Agriculture sustained cities, armies, markets, religious foundations, and local communities. A village might have a recognized collective identity for tax purposes, even while residents differed in wealth, status, or occupation. Alan Harvey's account of the Byzantine village emphasizes its role as both a place of social organization and a unit the state could assess. That double role brought advantages and dangers.

What does it mean for a village to pay tax? It does not imply that a village was a single person with a purse. Rather, officers and landholders could attach assessments and obligations to identifiable holdings or communities. Neighbors depended on one another for irrigation, paths, shared resources, and possibly collective action in disputes. The same proximity made it easier for authorities to identify a place as a fiscal unit. When a farmer left land idle or a plot was abandoned, the consequences might reach other taxpayers and the wider revenue system. Yet we should not project one unchanging Byzantine tax formula across hundreds of years. The evidence changes with empire, region, and century.

One source of extraordinary interest is a text usually called the Farmer's Law. It addresses disputes concerning fields, animals, fences, damage, theft, and cooperation among cultivators. Historians have debated its date and how to classify it: was it an official enactment, a compilation of practice, or a text assembled from several legal traditions? Walter Ashburner traced the disagreement among earlier specialists, some of whom dated it to the eighth or ninth century and others to the reigns of earlier emperors. The uncertain date does not make the text useless. It changes what kind of claim we are entitled to make about the communities described in it.

Suppose an animal damages a neighbor's crop. There must be some answer to a practical question: who bears the loss? A legal compilation can specify what should happen when boundaries are crossed or duties neglected. But it does not tell us the frequency with which livestock escaped, how every village handled compensation, or whether poorer households could obtain the same remedy as influential ones. Norms are evidence for the problems jurists and lawmakers thought required regulation. They are not recordings of every quarrel. If we study the Farmer's Law as though it were a journalist's report from one particular village, we will ask it to do work it was not designed to do.

By the tenth century, another conflict occupies imperial legal texts: powerful landowners acquiring property from less powerful cultivators. Emperors from Romanos the First to Basil the Second issued measures intended to defend small property holders and protect the taxable village community. Some rules gave relatives and neighbors a preferential opportunity to buy land before an outsider did. Historians refer to this as a right of pre-emption. It mattered because control of a plot could change not just one family's livelihood but the social balance of the village and the revenue that the state expected from it.

The language of these laws often contrasts the powerful with the poor. That is politically useful language, but not every landowner fits into one of two neat groups. A peasant might hold some property while renting another plot, or a family might prosper enough to become an important local actor. Smyrlis points out that monastic archives reveal mid-level proprietors and town residents with land, though the preserved records most readily show people when their properties reached the monasteries. Modern history must look beyond a simple story in which giant estates swallowed all villagers without opposition. It was a struggle involving institutions, court decisions, purchases, donations, and local relationships.

Monasteries belong centrally in this story. A religious foundation might receive land as a donation, buy a holding, or litigate over its boundaries. Monastic archives survived in some places where the records of nearby families disappeared. Their charters can therefore illuminate local land use with striking clarity while also skewing our perspective toward the rights of institutions that preserved them. To say a monastery owned or administered land does not mean every resident who worked there had taken religious vows. Many were ordinary cultivators whose labor maintained households and generated resources used by the monastery.

Radolibos is valuable precisely because the records give a village-scale view. The number of recorded families makes it possible to think about settlement and estate management without treating a royal capital as the only arena of power. Yet we do not know the complete biographies of those families. A document concerned with rights and liabilities may preserve names or categories relevant to the institution, leaving out childhood, illness, neighborly assistance, or resentment at an official demand. The 1103 record anchors a question about a real community. It should not license us to invent the name of a shepherd or the words of a widow in that community.

The broader countryside also changed during the eleventh and twelfth centuries. Some large properties expanded, while circumstances after war or political disruption could cause changes of ownership or a return to local control. In Smyrlis's analysis, the evidence of villages in monastic archives does not support one straight line from independent peasants to a landless population. There were purchases and donations, contested rights, and differences between localities. We should be especially careful about comparing a village in Greece, a settlement in Asia Minor, and a rural community in southern Italy as though all occupied the same administrative position.

What could villagers do when property was threatened? The laws reveal a conception of rights that families and neighbors might invoke. Monastic archives show that groups of people could appear in land agreements and disputes. But whether a rule was enforced depended on the capacities of courts, officials, and local interests. James Howard-Johnston argues that the tenth-century imperial legislative campaign was more effective than an older pessimistic view allowed. Other historians highlight the continued growth of large holdings. It is useful to recognize this disagreement. A ruler's declared aim, an individual court outcome, and the total transformation of landholding are three different kinds of historical evidence.

The village as a taxpayer is therefore a village seen from an administrative angle. From that angle it is a place with assessments, recognized property, and obligations that can be transferred or disputed. From the angle of residents it was also a cluster of homes, cultivated ground, households with unequal resources, and relationships that no surviving register lists in full. Both histories belong together. The law shows what government and jurists tried to protect or regulate; the archive shows some transactions made in actual places; neither makes the village into a mere entry in a ledger.

One hundred and twenty-two families at Radolibos. We can keep the number without pretending that those families are all equally known. It marks the moment a local community entered a surviving administrative record. Behind it stood fields and paths, decisions about land, and people who would not necessarily have described their home primarily as a taxpayer. Medieval history changes when a village becomes a subject in its own right. The records are still incomplete, but the question we ask of them has shifted from how an emperor ruled to how a community endured the demands and opportunities of that rule.

## Boundaries in Three Languages: Land and Labor in Norman Sicily

Across the Mediterranean in Norman Sicily, records of land and labor also passed among languages and institutions.

Imagine a boundary between two fields on an island where an administrator can describe it in Greek, another can write it in Arabic, and a third may record a later grant in Latin. The fields have not moved. Their owners and cultivators may know the same stream, stone, or old path. But the order in which the clerk names the boundaries can differ. In twelfth-century Sicily, this was not an abstract problem in translation. It was part of the practical machinery of government, landholding, and rural work. The documents of the Norman kingdom reveal a society where several writing traditions met within the same administration.

Norman forces conquered Sicily during the eleventh century. The island had long been connected to Mediterranean Islamic, Byzantine, and Latin Christian worlds. Its inhabitants included Arabic- and Greek-speaking communities, Muslims, Christians of different traditions, Jews, and people whose identities cannot be captured by one of these labels alone. Roger the Second became king in 1130. A conquest changed political authority, but it did not instantly erase the languages, legal habits, or knowledge of landscapes on which government depended. New rulers needed people who could identify lands, collect revenue, draft grants, and recognize the names that farmers used for their own settlements.

The royal administration is often called the diwan, a term associated with Arabic writing and state offices. Jeremy Johns's study reconstructs registers of people and land from a small surviving set of charters and references to lost administrative books. Many of the working registers themselves have not survived. This is a crucial limitation. We possess documents that refer to a larger bureaucracy, but that does not mean we can reconstruct the entire archive or assume every local procedure looked the same. What survives, however, shows rulers directing resources through registers whose forms reflected several administrative traditions.

Among the most revealing records are six documents relating to the Greek monastery of Saint George of Tròccoli near Caltabellotta, published and studied by Vera von Falkenhausen, Nadia Jamil, and Jeremy Johns. Three documents are bilingual in Greek and Arabic, and three are Arabic. They deal with the monastery's lands and with Muslim peasants connected to them. A Christian religious foundation appears in paperwork written in languages associated with several communities and administrative traditions. This is not a paradox. A monastery could own land cultivated by people of another faith, while officials employed whatever written practices were recognized and operationally useful.

An 1141 register connected to the monastery records 115 peasant households on the specified properties. It makes a region of rural Sicily suddenly countable in a way that a royal chronicle cannot. Yet even this number needs a label. It is a record of households within the scope of the grant, not a count of every person or of all the landowners in the district. The scholars who edited the documents use it to examine settlement density and the practical concerns of the royal administration. For us the most important lesson may be how the administrative record identifies which families had claims, duties, and relationships recognized by the monastery and the crown.

The texts also contain names that help scholars discuss the backgrounds of some peasant families. There are signs that people from North Africa reached Sicily and entered relationships of dependence with the monastery. It would be easy to turn this into a confident tale of desperate refugees fleeing one famous famine. But the chronology complicates that account. The editors point out that some people already appear before the famine of the early 1140s usually cited as an explanation. Hard conditions in North Africa and opportunities in Sicily are plausible contexts, yet each recorded family is not accompanied by a testimony stating why it moved.

A register of peasants is also a record of unequal power. The people listed were described through categories of labor and dependence meaningful to the crown and monastery. Calling them villeins does not give us a modern description of their wishes or explain all the legal distinctions involved. Some had crossed the sea, perhaps seeking better conditions; their decision to enter a dependent relationship would not make that relationship free of constraint. Their names survive because an institution needed to know who had obligations tied to its estates. A charter can preserve evidence of migration and limited choices at the same time.

Land descriptions reveal another kind of interaction. Alex Metcalfe compared boundary clauses in medieval Mediterranean documents written in Latin, Greek, and Arabic. In many Romano-Byzantine examples, descriptions began with the eastern boundary; many Arabic legal traditions favored the southern boundary. The difference was not an innate property of a language. It was a convention maintained through schooling, scribal forms, and learned practice. When one government used several documentary traditions, its boundary formulas could retain traces of those different institutional histories.

The Sicilian evidence becomes even more revealing when we distinguish who was issuing the document. Metcalfe found that private Arabic documents often followed a south-first convention, while Arabic descriptions produced for royal landholding could follow an eastern-first pattern closer to Romano-Byzantine practice. An Arabic sentence might thus participate in a particular state procedure shaped by other scribal traditions. This undermines the convenient equation of language with an administration's ethnic or religious origin. A change in who commissioned the document could matter as much as the language in which it was written.

How did officials know where an estate began? They depended on people with local knowledge, on landmarks, and on ways of validating a grant. A formula was not a substitute for walking or recognizing a boundary. Legal words gave a description an authority that could be cited if claims later conflicted. But geographical knowledge was still distributed among villagers, estate managers, religious foundations, and officials. The multilingual charter is therefore not just an accomplishment of translators in a royal office. It is an instrument joining the ruler's command to the locality where someone would have to recognize or enforce it.

These sources also expose the danger of celebrating medieval Sicily only as a perfectly harmonious meeting of cultures. The same documents that show several languages functioning together show dependency and unequal ownership. A Greek monastery held estates with Muslim cultivators; royal authorities confirmed rights; a person on the register was part of a labor system managed by others. Political power shaped which names were written and which transactions survived. Cultural exchange was real, but it did not abolish coercion or erase the conquest that had placed new rulers above older communities.

One further caution concerns the fate of the archive. Documents survived because monasteries and later owners had reasons to keep proof of property. Lost charters could radically change our sense of how widespread a practice was. We therefore cannot count the handful of bilingual records and turn that count into the percentage of Sicily that used a particular language. Nor can we assume that an individual peasant was able to read the document that recorded his family's obligations. Written administration was influential because it translated local relationships into durable claims held by powerful institutions.

Return to the boundary between the fields. There is an eastern edge, a southern edge, and a person whose livelihood may depend on how the grant is interpreted. Greek and Arabic may describe the same place in different orders, while the people who work there live with the consequences of decisions made in a distant office. This is a history of a Mediterranean state built not only by soldiers or kings but through lists, translations, measurements, and negotiated obligations. The paperwork contains an extraordinary amount of life, provided we remember how much of that life it leaves unwritten.
