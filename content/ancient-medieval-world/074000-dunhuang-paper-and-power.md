# The Price of Paper: Work, Debt, and Community at Dunhuang

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

In a ninth-century register from Dunhuang, an official kept track of paper owed by scribes. Blank sheets were valuable; copyists had to account for them. The document preserved in the International Dunhuang Programme as IOL Tib J 1359 describes liability for missing paper and threats of corporal punishment. At first glance this looks like a small administrative problem. But it opens a history of labor, religious sponsorship, imperial authority, and a town whose inhabitants worked in several languages. The great libraries of the medieval world were made not only by scholars and patrons, but by people expected to deliver a certain number of written pages.

Dunhuang was an oasis in the corridor between northwestern China and the wider Inner Asian worlds. Its fields depended on water managed in an arid landscape; its roads connected neighboring settlements and distant political centers. Famous Buddhist caves at nearby Mogao preserve images and manuscripts, but the people who lived nearby also planted grain, paid dues, borrowed goods, negotiated marriages, and organized ceremonies. Their lives were not merely episodes in caravans passing through. The International Dunhuang Programme's records and the specialist studies built on them make it possible to ask about local work as well as long-distance exchange.

A critical chronological distinction is the period of Tibetan imperial control. The Tibetan empire took Dunhuang in the late eighth century, around 781, and held it until the middle of the ninth century. Chinese and Tibetan administrative practices interacted during these decades. After 848, local rulers associated with the Guiyijun regime governed the region through changing relationships with neighboring powers. The manuscripts preserve traces of both eras. A contract in Chinese, a Tibetan command, and a Buddhist scripture may belong to different years and institutional settings even if they eventually lay together in one cache.

The cache is usually called the Library Cave, Mogao Cave 17. It contained tens of thousands of manuscripts and other objects when it was opened around 1900, after having been sealed near the beginning of the eleventh century. The reasons for depositing and sealing the collection are still debated. A sealed cave is not a snapshot of one year. Papers may have been copied decades or centuries apart, used, discarded, and gathered before deposition. Their later dispersal through foreign expeditions and collecting removed many from their original context. Modern catalogs and digital reconstructions reconnect materials now held in different institutions.

The texts are multilingual. The International Dunhuang Programme identifies Chinese and Tibetan as major languages of the collection, together with Sanskrit, Khotanese, Sogdian, Uyghur, and others. Language choice can reveal schooling, religious affiliation, official practice, and connections between regions. But it is not a direct census of ancestry. A scribe might use a Tibetan personal name and a Chinese surname; a resident might hear more than one language in the marketplace. The manuscripts show how people operated across administrative and cultural settings. They do not justify putting everyone into one modern national category.

The paper register belongs to a much larger copying enterprise. Under Tibetan imperial sponsorship, Buddhist texts were repeatedly transcribed, and Dunhuang's paper-making and scribal resources were mobilized for the work. The British Library's account of the register describes sheets allocated to particular copyists and the punishment threatened for shortages. This is evidence that an administration tried to control materials and labor. We cannot infer from the written threat exactly how many copyists were beaten. A register records a rule or demand; it needs other evidence to establish how often the rule was enforced.

Scribes were not interchangeable hands. They maintained brushes, made errors, practiced characters, negotiated supplies, and sometimes borrowed from one another. The International Dunhuang Programme describes a paper wrapper associated with a scribe named Ser Dzinkheng, who used it to record an agreement involving cloth ties, paper, and a knife. This tiny record is especially revealing because the objects belong to a working person's daily needs. A manuscript wrapper can show where laborers made arrangements with peers, even while their work supported institutions much larger than themselves.

Paper was reused. A Buddhist scripture might be copied on one side of a sheet while the other carried a contract or a note. What later appears as a disorderly heap of fragments can therefore preserve relationships among religious copying, administration, household demands, and resource shortages. Reused paper is evidence of value and of practical habits. It also complicates dating: two texts on one sheet need not have been written at the same moment, and the presence of a copied scripture does not necessarily tell us what the reverse-side contract was used for.

Local associations organized another layer of society. The International Dunhuang Programme highlights a circular dated to 886 that summoned members to a gathering and feast at Jiejia Hermitage. The message moved from one member to another for acknowledgment and warned of fines for absence or lateness. Other association notices concerned funerals, irrigation work, and women's groups. These records offer a glimpse of organization that was neither an imperial command nor a purely private conversation. Members collectively recognized obligations, and some groups maintained procedures for enforcing them.

The irrigation notices are especially important. In an oasis, a channel that fails can harm many fields, so water management requires coordinated labor and decisions about access. Associations could mobilize people for work or communicate duties. Officials also took censuses, assessed taxes, managed diplomacy, and resolved disputes. Such arrangements were not necessarily harmonious. A surviving circular can show the penalties announced but not whether all members thought them fair. The presence of a collective organization tells us that people had institutions between the household and the state.

One surviving contract, dated to 916, records the sale of a seven-year-old child named Guangde by a widowed mother, A Wu. It appears on a reused scroll described by the International Dunhuang Programme. This is painful evidence of coercion and vulnerability, and it should not be softened into a neutral sale of labor. At the same time, the contract alone cannot reveal the full circumstances leading to the transfer or the child's subsequent life. The mother's identity and the written act survive; her private thoughts and the child's future do not. A historical lecture should resist both sensational invention and the temptation to omit hard facts.

Specialist scholarship by Gertraud Taenzer compares Chinese and Tibetan secular manuscripts from the period of Tibetan rule. Tax lists, land records, labor obligations, and correspondence reveal administrative arrangements in more detail than a court chronicle. Chinese-language accounts may preserve the bookkeeping of lower officials, while Tibetan texts record communications at other levels. The contrast is useful: one government could be visible through different written procedures, and the surviving archive may favor the activities of those required to keep accounts. The evidence remains selective even when it is unusually abundant.

Another selection bias comes from religious preservation. Many manuscripts in Cave 17 are Buddhist texts, including works produced under large copying programs. That does not mean all Dunhuang residents were monks or that other religions and household practices did not exist. Nonreligious documents survive partly because they were written on paper subsequently reused for religious purposes. Modern researchers must therefore consider the history of the material itself: why a sheet was produced, who handled it, how it was reused, and how it entered the sealed room.

Dunhuang's modern history is inseparable from this research. Expeditions removed a great deal of the cave's contents in the early twentieth century, and objects now reside in libraries far from China. Digitization permits comparison and study, but it does not undo the history of removal. Catalogues identify collections, languages, physical formats, and editors; these are not ornamental citations. A historian using a translated Tibetan register or Chinese contract should acknowledge the scholars who transcribed it and the limits of their readings. The more specific the claim, the more important the source's identity becomes.

The history of Dunhuang is often introduced with an image of Buddhist painting or a map of the Silk Roads. Both are valuable, but a register of paper debts changes the scale. It shows a worker accountable for sheets, an administration concerned with production, and religious writing embedded in systems of reward and punishment. A society circular shows neighbors agreeing to meet and organizing obligations; a child's sale contract exposes the vulnerability such systems could contain. The oasis was a place of art, empire, and trade, but also of work and unequal power. That fuller history survives because apparently ordinary scraps of paper crossed a thousand years.
