# The Pacific coast: land, water, management, and collective relations

Along the Pacific coast and inland river systems, people shaped harvests through knowledge, work, and relationships. Sea gardens, oak groves, salmon fisheries, and camas grounds are distinct cases. Comparing them will make the scale of environmental management clearer without erasing the peoples whose homelands we enter.

## Working with the Tides: Sea Gardens and Communities of the Northwest Coast

When the tide withdraws on the Northwest Coast of North America, it reveals a landscape shaped by more than waves. Low stone walls can hold sediment along a beach. Wooden stakes can mark an old fish trap. Shells accumulate where generations harvested and prepared food. These traces tell us that coastal families did not merely take whatever nature happened to provide. They worked with the tides, sometimes changing places in ways that improved future harvests. The region extends across many Indigenous homelands, from the Salish Sea through the central British Columbia coast and northward. Its peoples were never one uniform society, and their technologies, languages, laws, and histories must not be collapsed into a single coastal custom.

The sea gave access to fish, shellfish, sea mammals, and routes between communities. Rivers and forests supplied additional foods and materials, including cedar in many areas. Yet abundance alone does not explain settlement. Harvesting salmon depended on understanding the timing of runs and the behavior of each stream. Shellfish beds differed in productivity. Storage involved processing food so that it lasted beyond the season of capture. Boats, nets, houses, and wooden tools required skilled work. A community's food system therefore combined ecology, technology, social cooperation, and rules about access. To call it a gift of a rich environment would hide much of the labor that made it reliable.

One example is a clam garden. In parts of the coast, Indigenous people built or maintained rock walls low in the intertidal zone. The walls helped form beaches at useful elevations and expanded places where clams could grow. This was not modern aquaculture imposed backward into ancient history; it was a set of locally developed practices documented by Indigenous knowledge and physical research. The ethnobiological study of Kwakwaka'wakw clam gardening draws on the knowledge of Chief Kwaxistalla Adam Dick, who learned practices transmitted by earlier generations. His contribution is not decoration around an archaeological finding. It is evidence about the choices and expertise that archaeological surveys might otherwise fail to recognize.

Archaeological investigations in parts of northern Quadra Island have identified long histories of clam gardens and associated settlements. A 2022 study brings together radiocarbon dating, beach mapping, and evidence of human occupation to argue that people tended clam-growing places over at least thirty-eight hundred years in the studied area. That result is important precisely because it is geographically specific. It does not establish the construction date of every stone wall between Alaska and Washington. Different gardens were created, rebuilt, and used at different times. Dating a place requires attention to which part of the garden was sampled and what a dated shell or sediment layer actually represents.

There is a technical difficulty here worth understanding. A rock wall contains stone that may be much older than the wall. A shell found nearby may have lived before it was moved. Radiocarbon dating can establish the age of organic material, but not automatically the moment when people placed particular stones. Researchers have therefore experimented with optical dating, which can estimate when sediment grains last saw sunlight before burial. Using several techniques can narrow a history of construction and use. A coastline can deposit material naturally, so the method must distinguish human activity from those processes. A plausible human-made structure must be studied, not simply declared ancient because it looks old.

Fish traps offer another window into coordinated labor. A wooden barrier or funnel in a tidal channel required material, planning, and repair. A research synthesis published in 2026 assembled hundreds of radiocarbon dates from more than two hundred wooden fish traps across the coast. Its authors found patterns connecting investment in large traps with substantial settlements and other changes in social organization. Those associations do not give us the names of the organizers or prove that the same institution governed every fishery. They do show that repeated collective work can be studied through many small dates rather than only through the largest surviving buildings.

The decision to build a fish trap raises questions of fairness and power. Who could decide when to harvest? Who maintained the structure, and how was the catch divided? Archaeology may show that people cooperated and sometimes invested heavily in one location, but it cannot recover every agreement. Indigenous law and continuing knowledge of fisheries provide a different kind of testimony about rights and responsibilities, including the relationships between families, specific places, and living resources. It is tempting to call these systems communal or private by modern categories; both labels can miss the obligations attached to access. A fishery was a social institution as well as a tool.

On parts of the Northwest Coast, large wooden houses sheltered extended groups. Cedar planks could be arranged into substantial structures, with styles differing among peoples and places. The Burke Museum's educational materials contrast coastal plank houses with tule-mat houses of other Northwest communities, a useful reminder that even one broad region contains distinct building traditions. A large plank house required access to timber and woodworking knowledge. It also made decisions visible: who maintained the building, how families shared interior space, and how valuables and stored foods were protected. Excavated house patterns can help reconstruct settlement histories, but ethnographic descriptions from later centuries must be used cautiously for much earlier periods.

Archaeologists also study the remains of meals. Shell middens contain discarded shells, bones, charcoal, tools, and sometimes carefully separated occupation layers. A midden is not mere rubbish; it is a record of repeated decisions. Species composition can suggest whether a place was used seasonally or year-round, but preservation, processing practices, and sampling complicate the calculation. Cedar and other organic materials survive poorly in some settings and remarkably well in waterlogged deposits. What we can count is never a neutral sample of everything people ate, built, or considered important. Missing remains may reflect decay, not a lack of skill.

Coastal networks involved travel by water, which linked villages and distant trading partners. Canoes could carry people, food, and objects through routes that appear obstructed to someone studying only a road map. But mobility did not erase the importance of particular territories. An estuary, clam beach, river mouth, or forest could be associated with family histories and responsibilities. Coastal Indigenous accounts often explain such relationships through named places and inherited knowledge. Modern scholarship can examine those accounts and compare them with dated evidence, but should not treat them simply as illustrations for a theory of trade. Places were meaningful before outsiders charted their shores.

Social distinctions also mattered. Later historical and ethnographic records describe ranked relationships, inherited privileges, and forms of slavery in several Northwest Coast societies. Those facts are part of the region's history, not an exception to be concealed behind a romantic image of harmony with nature. Yet it is unsafe to project the precise political institutions recorded in the nineteenth century unchanged into every community a thousand years earlier. Archaeological differences in houses, burials, and access to resources can suggest inequality, but their interpretation is debated. A long-lived fishery need not have had one unchanging owner, and a large house is not automatically the palace of a hereditary ruler.

Food technology and ceremonial life were linked through the work of making and maintaining relationships. Feasting, redistribution, kin ties, and exchange appear in later Indigenous historical records in locally distinct forms. Ancient archaeological evidence for gathering places and stored food can raise questions about such institutions, but it rarely permits a direct identification of a later named ceremony. The most careful studies separate evidence for collective investment, evidence for social differentiation, and the explanations given by descendant communities. They also allow traditions to change. Continuity does not mean frozen repetition.

Colonialism transformed these systems. Dispossession, disease, laws limiting Indigenous management, commercial fishing, and pressure on coastlines damaged relationships between communities and food places. The 2022 sea-garden study explicitly relates the decline in some archaeological indicators of gardens and nearby settlements to the colonial period. That history is not outside the lecture merely because it occurred after fifteen hundred. It explains why some practices became difficult to observe and why restoring Indigenous management is connected to recovering historical knowledge. Researchers increasingly work with First Nations rather than treating the coast as an unoccupied laboratory.

The Northwest Coast teaches an important lesson about historical evidence. A stone wall can be both an engineered landscape and a relationship maintained by generations. A fish trap can reveal patterns of cooperation without disclosing every rule of governance. A shell midden can hold centuries of meals and still leave us uncertain about a family's stories. Indigenous expertise, archaeological dating, and environmental science each illuminate different parts of the account. Before European colonization, these coasts were already thoroughly inhabited and managed. Their history was shaped by people making decisions about a living sea, not simply by people living beside it.

## Oak Groves and Ocean Routes: Indigenous California before 1500

Moving south along the coast to California changes both the environment and the communities whose knowledge shaped it.

A basket filled with acorns and a canoe crossing a channel may seem to belong to separate histories. One begins under the trees; the other moves across open water. In what is now California, both depended on accumulated environmental knowledge, skilled labor, and social relationships. We cannot compress the region's many peoples into a single way of life. Languages, climates, political institutions, fishing traditions, and methods of managing land differed across valleys, mountains, deserts, and coasts. But the examples of oak landscapes and the Santa Barbara Channel show something essential: the absence of an enormous stone city does not mean an absence of organized economy or engineering.

Acorns were an important food in many California communities, and turning them into a reliable meal was labor. People harvested, dried, stored, shelled, ground, and leached bitter compounds from particular kinds of acorns. Basket making and other technologies formed part of the food system, not a decorative craft placed outside it. Seasonal variation made knowledge of different groves and other foods valuable. An acorn crop depended on weather and local conditions; people used combinations of resources rather than betting on a single harvest. The details of who had rights to particular trees, gathering places, or stores varied by community and period, and later ethnography does not supply one universal ancient law.

The landscape was not simply a wild pantry that people visited. Indigenous land managers used fire for many purposes, including improving access to plant resources, supporting useful growth, producing materials for basketry, moving through terrain, and reducing hazardous fuels in some places. The US Forest Service describes this as a major part of Native California history, not a modern invention. Yet the use of fire was not uniform across all California ecologies or seasons. Burning under an oak and burning vegetation for a different purpose can have different effects; neither can be assumed at a particular archaeological site unless there is specific evidence.

Modern research on oak woodland fire history draws on historical accounts, ecological patterns, and Indigenous knowledge. These bodies of evidence can explain why repeated managed burns might make acorns easier to collect or improve certain habitats. They cannot automatically date an individual prescribed burn to, say, the eleventh century at a named village. Recognizing a long tradition of land stewardship does not require imagining an unchanging set of practices. Methods shifted with population, environment, neighboring communities, and later colonial restrictions. The larger lesson is to look for historical agency in habitat itself, even when the archive of written decrees is absent.

Along the Santa Barbara Channel, ocean movement offers a different kind of technological evidence. The Chumash developed and maintained plank canoes known as tomols. Building one required carefully prepared boards, joining and sealing materials, tools, and knowledge of water conditions. The National Park Service describes redwood planks, binding, and sealing substances in its account of these vessels. A tomol did not make the sea safe. Its construction meant that people could undertake deliberate crossings between islands and mainland, moving goods, people, stories, and social obligations across a space that might appear to a distant observer as an obstacle.

The Channel Islands and nearby mainland supported varied settlements and resources. Some island communities did not have direct access to all the materials available inland. Exchange could connect marine foods, tools, and ornaments to plant foods and manufactured items. Such relationships were not automatically equivalent to a merchant market with prices written on every transaction. They included community ties and the unequal value that participants placed on different materials. Archaeological sites preserve shell debris, stone tools, and other traces of work. They can establish where production happened more securely than they can reconstruct every conversation between island and mainland households.

Shell beads are an especially instructive form of evidence. Chumash communities made beads from shells, and some were used in exchange. Archaeologist Lynn Gamble has argued that shell bead money in parts of California may have a considerably longer history than earlier estimates allowed. But the argument depends on identifying which beads were used as currency, rather than assuming that every perforated shell was a coin. Some beads served as ornaments, markers of status, or gifts. A thousand-year-old bead tells us about manufacture and circulation; using it to assert a uniform monetary economy over the entire state would ignore local variations that the research itself emphasizes.

Tools for cutting, drilling, polishing, and stringing beads demanded practice, raw materials, and time. A finished string could condense many hours of work into something portable. If beads moved between regions, their paths can be inferred through materials and archaeological distributions, but not all movement equals anonymous buying and selling. Marriage, alliance, ceremonial exchange, debt, and reciprocal obligation can also move valuables. The label money can be useful when supported by several lines of evidence. It should not erase the social setting in which an object changed hands, nor turn California into a rehearsal for a modern cash economy.

We also need to be precise about the word Chumash. It names living peoples and related traditions, not a single unchanging identity we can assign to every artifact in a museum drawer. Neighboring communities spoke different languages and practiced different systems of exchange. The people responsible for early archaeological layers are known through research, living community histories, and sometimes imperfect attempts to connect archaeological sites with later recorded names. Archaeological categories may simplify those histories for classification. The descendants of the region's peoples are not merely witnesses to a vanished past; they continue to preserve and revitalize knowledge, including the use of the tomol.

Imagine standing at a coastal village and seeing a canoe depart. Its passengers may know the weather and the route. They also know people on the opposite shore: where to arrive, what is expected, and whether a visit is welcome. An outsider watching from a cliff might record the movement of the boat and miss the relationship that made the journey possible. The same is true of an oak grove. A survey could map tree locations yet miss the knowledge of gathering and stewardship embedded in social practice. In both cases, history requires more than observing a resource. It requires asking how people organized claims and responsibilities around it.

Colonial documents created later are not a transparent record of ancient California. Missions, violence, displacement, disease, and new restrictions on land use altered communities and the evidence available to later observers. Accounts produced under those conditions must be situated in their own time. A tool described in the nineteenth century may have a much longer history, but a historian needs archaeology and Indigenous testimony to evaluate continuity. A practice lost or transformed after colonization should not be retrospectively treated as insignificant before it. Conversely, a surviving practice must not be projected unchanged across two millennia.

These examples complicate a common assumption about human achievement. Food production does not always mean plowed fields. Complex exchange does not always mean a centralized market. Engineering includes preserving useful plants, designing tools and boats, and coordinating seasonal harvests. These activities can support communities of considerable scale without producing the same buildings as agrarian courts. Their evidence is often perishable, scattered, or entangled with living traditions. That makes studying them more demanding, not less worthwhile. The narrative should begin with what the evidence actually shows and stop before it reaches a confident story the evidence cannot support.

Return to the oak and the canoe. The first asks us to see managed habitats and the work of preparing food; the second asks us to see navigation, craftsmanship, and relationships across water. Both were sustained by knowledge shared among people over generations. Neither can stand for every Indigenous nation in California, and neither survives to us without losses and reinterpretations. Together they show why a history of California before 1500 belongs in a world history curriculum as a history of its own institutions and technologies, not merely as the scene in which outsiders would eventually arrive.

## A River with Rules: Salmon, Weirs, and Klamath Communities

The Klamath and Trinity rivers narrow the view to salmon, fisheries, and the rules linking villages to a river.

When salmon travel upstream, the river briefly gathers together lives that usually occupy different places. Fish return from the ocean. People prepare nets and wooden structures, watch currents, and organize the work of harvesting and preservation. Along the Klamath and Trinity rivers in northern California, Indigenous communities developed systems in which taking fish was tied to relationships among villages, responsibilities to the river, and ceremonial time. This is a history of food production and environmental knowledge, but it is also a history of law: who may fish, when a harvest should begin, and what must be left for others.

The Klamath basin has never been the home of only one people. Yurok communities live along the lower river and coast; Karuk communities maintain profound relationships with stretches farther upstream; Hupa communities have their own history along the Trinity River. These names identify living peoples with distinct languages, institutions, and histories, not interchangeable branches of one prehistoric culture. Their own accounts describe long attachments to specific places, ancestral responsibilities, and continuing relationships with salmon. When speaking about life before 1500, we must not casually copy every practice documented in the nineteenth century backward without question. But we must not treat tribal historical knowledge as irrelevant simply because it was not written in a European archive.

The Yurok Tribe's account of its history identifies fishing, eeling, canoe making, weaving, healing, and storytelling as connected forms of life, and the Klamath-Trinity river system as central to its food supply. The breadth of the evidence makes a single, simple explanation harder to sustain. Salmon could be a major seasonal resource without being the only item people ate or traded. Gathering, hunting, planting or tending useful species, craftwork, and exchange extended across different environments. Coastal and inland communities had access to different resources and maintained relationships through travel and obligation. The river was both a place to harvest and a route among settlements.

A wooden weir is a form of engineering. Stakes and woven or fitted materials can alter the movement of fish while allowing water to continue downstream. Workers must read river depth, current, seasonal flow, and the behavior of fish. They must also coordinate construction, maintenance, and removal. An ethnographic description of a weir tells us something about the technology used when it was recorded. Archaeological remains and ecological studies can help historians investigate earlier fishing practices, although perishable wood seldom survives as well as stone. A technical reconstruction cannot establish the exact rules governing every old fishery on its own.

Research on Indigenous salmon management brings governance into that picture. A scholarly synthesis published in 2021 examines systems across the Pacific coast, including Klamath examples in which fishing structures and opening practices were part of regulating access and allowing fish to pass upstream. Some historical descriptions of a Yurok weir at Kepel emphasize limited periods of use and deliberate openings. We should not turn one reported arrangement into a universal Indigenous conservation code. Practices differed with place and circumstance. But neither should we assume that effective fisheries management only began when modern agencies introduced permits and quotas.

For Karuk communities, ceremonial timing is central to the relationship with salmon. The Karuk Tribe's fisheries account explains the Spring Salmon Ceremony at Ameekyáaraam and the limits on fishing that traditionally accompanied it. A first fish, a period of restraint, or a signal that harvesting may begin can carry religious meaning and practical consequences together. It would be reductive to describe such a ceremony as merely a biological conservation technique. It also expresses a world of obligations and authority. At the same time, ecological effects can be part of why the timing of a fishery mattered to communities responsible for long-term returns.

A fishing regulation is never just an idea in someone's head. It requires people to know the rules, recognize who has authority to announce or enforce them, and coordinate when the river connects groups with different locations and needs. A weir near the lower river affects fish moving to upstream communities. Decisions about opening or removing it therefore have consequences beyond one fishing place. Intercommunity relationships could involve negotiation, ceremonial responsibilities, trade, and conflict. Historical sources do not give us a complete record of every disagreement before 1500, but the geography of a migrating fish makes shared interests and competing claims understandable.

Archaeological evidence requires special care. Fish bones in an old deposit can show a species that people caught or consumed. The abundance of bones may reflect food preparation, disposal practices, and preservation rather than the total scale of harvest. Stable isotopes or other methods can sometimes shed light on diets, but results vary with context. Ancient salmon remains can also inform the historical distribution of species. Studies of California's archaeological fisheries show why such evidence may be important for reconstructing baselines that later dams, mining, diversions, and habitat changes altered. A historical baseline is not necessarily a fixed untouched natural state.

The living testimony of communities is another source with its own strengths and interpretive demands. Oral narratives and ceremonial practices convey relationships and knowledge that may have been transmitted for generations. A contemporary tribal account tells us how a community understands its responsibilities today and how it connects them to its past. It need not be treated as a literal transcript of events in a particular prehistoric year. Archaeological evidence and transmitted knowledge can support one another while addressing different questions. Historians should be clear whose voice is being reported, especially when older ethnographic accounts were recorded under conditions of coercion and dispossession.

The period after European settlement changed the river histories dramatically. In the nineteenth century, mining, violence, and forced changes to land access disrupted many communities. Later dams and other infrastructure transformed salmon migration and habitat. Those events lie outside the pre-1500 focus of this lecture, but they explain why the historic fishery is not merely an archaeological curiosity. Yurok, Karuk, and other Indigenous governments remain active participants in the river's management and restoration. A story that ends by declaring their ancestors to have disappeared would contradict the continuing communities who preserve and develop this knowledge.

Another problem is the phrase sustainable harvesting. We can study evidence that particular rules limited takes, coordinated passages, or protected future runs. We cannot calculate an exact long-term population effect from a ceremonial account alone. Salmon numbers are affected by ocean conditions, climate, predators, river flows, and habitat change as well as fishing. Historical practice should be evaluated on the evidence available, without assuming that earlier communities either perfectly controlled nature or had no means of adapting to it. Long experience with a river is expertise, not omniscience.

Why call this a history of law? Because it shows that institutions need not take the form of a stone building, written code, or royal official. They can live in shared knowledge of a river, public ceremonies, recognized places, and responsibilities among communities. The consequences are material: whether a weir is built, whether a run passes upstream, who receives fish, and which families can preserve food for later months. Without the institutions, the engineering would tell only half the story. Without the ecological knowledge, the institutions could not be understood in their setting.

Imagine the start of a salmon season from the riverbank. Some people are watching the fish; others prepare wood, nets, fires, and places for drying or storage. Their decisions are shaped by a history of relations upstream and down, and by responsibilities that reach beyond the day's catch. Much of what they knew will never be recoverable through an excavation alone. But archaeology, historical records, and the knowledge of living nations together show that the river was governed as well as used. The Klamath was never an empty natural resource waiting for rules. It was part of a homeland whose people had already spent generations making obligations visible through the way they fished.

## Where Salmon and Camas Meet: Life on the Columbia Plateau

Across the mountain divide, the Columbia Plateau brings river fisheries together with the seasonal use of inland plants.

At a constriction in the Columbia River, the water once broke into rapids and falls. Salmon attempting to move upstream were concentrated in a narrow, dangerous stretch. People knew the place, its seasons, and the work needed to fish there. Celilo Falls was not simply a natural feeding station. It was a gathering place where fishers, traders, families, and visitors formed relationships over many generations. The water that made travel difficult also made certain fishing technologies effective. To understand the Columbia Plateau, we can begin with that paradox: a barrier for boats could be a center of movement and exchange for people.

The Columbia watershed reaches across what are now multiple states and into Canada. Its peoples did not form one polity or speak one language. The plateau and adjacent regions supported distinct communities, including peoples identified today with the Yakama, Umatilla, Warm Springs, Nez Perce, Colville, and others. Salmon was a central food and cultural relation for many, but not all the plateau's resources could be reduced to it. Native Knowledge 360, produced by the Smithsonian National Museum of the American Indian, describes continuing First Salmon ceremonies and the way communities define themselves through responsibilities to fish. Such living testimony conveys values and relationships that archaeological bones alone cannot record.

Archaeology shows very long human occupation along the river, including evidence for activity near the falls thousands of years ago. It does not show that every fishing platform, village, or ownership rule recorded in recent centuries existed in the same form during the early medieval period. The Oregon Encyclopedia reports occupation of sites along Celilo stretching to at least about eleven thousand years ago. Dates like that reveal the depth of settlement history, not the continuous identity of a particular wooden scaffold. Riverbanks shifted, populations moved, technologies changed, and accounts were transmitted in forms other than an excavator's notebook.

Fishing was an active technology. Nets, traps, spears, and later well-documented platforms depended on knowledge of current, season, and fish behavior. Harvesting was followed by cleaning, drying, storing, and distributing food. The Northwest Power and Conservation Council cautions that archaeological evidence of very ancient fishing does not automatically demonstrate how much of the catch was dried for winter consumption at that time. That is an important limit: the presence of a fish bone establishes less than a complete calendar of meals. The labor of preservation made fish available beyond a single run, but its scale and timing need evidence from particular places.

Fish moved people as well as feeding them. Gatherings at places such as Celilo created opportunities to exchange goods, arrange visits, maintain kinship, and share news. In its regional account, the Smithsonian records the continuing importance of Celilo in Indigenous knowledge and notes that people came from considerable distances. The economic value of such a gathering was inseparable from its social and ceremonial value. A traveler might bring food from another ecological zone or a crafted object, but also join relatives and renew obligations. The word trade is accurate, yet incomplete, if it imagines only strangers bargaining for anonymous goods.

Beyond the river's main fishing sites lay landscapes shaped by roots, grasses, game, and seasonal travel. Camas was an important plant food in much of the wider region. Its bulbs required knowledge of timing, harvesting, and preparation, especially because similar-looking plants could be dangerous. Research on the Columbia Plateau describes access to root grounds, localized resources, and patterns of winter villages and smaller seasonal camps. That picture must not be copied onto every valley, altitude, or century. It instead asks how people maintained a portfolio of places, so that a community's life could follow several seasonal rhythms rather than a fixed year-round settlement.

Rights to useful locations mattered. A fishing place could be associated with particular families and social rules; root grounds, camps, and paths could have established caretakers or understood responsibilities. Those arrangements were not simply the absence of property because they were not written in European-style deeds. Nor should historical descriptions from the nineteenth century be presented as direct observations of the twelfth. Oral histories, landscape features, comparative archaeology, and colonial documents must be read with their different dates and purposes in view. The existence of regulation and stewardship is clear; their exact form changed among peoples and through time.

Environmental knowledge was also work. Fishing runs fluctuate; roots grow differently according to water, fire, and harvest; storage is vulnerable to decay and animals. Successful communities planned for variation, often by maintaining knowledge of several resources and places. Historian and archaeologist Ruthann Knudson described a pattern of localized but reliable foods and shifting seasonal tasks that complemented larger gathering events. That framework helps us ask better questions of the past, but it is a scholarly reconstruction, not a timetable that every ancestral household left behind. It points toward flexibility rather than a single fixed agricultural formula.

The plateau also belonged to broader exchange systems. It connected riverine travel, inland paths, and the resources of different ecological zones. Some materials can be tracked archaeologically through stone sources or other distinctive properties. Others, especially woven containers, dried foods, and oral agreements, leave much weaker traces. If an excavated object came from outside the immediate region, it shows some movement across distance; it does not prove the owner personally walked the entire route. The broader story is of chains of exchange and hospitality, often assembled through many local relationships rather than a single central market.

Celilo is also a case in why historical boundaries matter. The falls were inundated in 1957 after construction of The Dalles Dam. That event belongs to the modern history of river engineering, treaty rights, and Indigenous struggles to retain fishing practices. It cannot be moved backward into 1200, yet leaving it out would imply that the landscape we see today is the same one precolonial people used. Some of the physical evidence is now underwater. Historical photography, oral testimony, archaeology, and records made before flooding help describe features no longer visible. This modern rupture affects both communities and the historian's ability to inspect the past.

Treaty-era descriptions of fishing platforms and family rights are particularly informative but must be handled with chronology in mind. They show institutions in a region already profoundly transformed by contact, trade, disease, settlement, and coercive government policy. Continuities matter and are affirmed by living nations; so do changes. A careful lecture does not replace Indigenous history with the moment a federal dam was built, but it recognizes that modern dispossession shaped which practices could continue openly and which sites remain accessible. The historical task includes explaining why certain records exist and what pressures produced them.

What would a good historical map of the plateau show? Not just a river line and a dot labeled Celilo. It would show seasonal resources, paths to root-gathering places, locations of exchange, and relations among neighboring communities. Much of this map would be tentative because archaeological sites do not preserve every name and entitlement. Such uncertainty is no excuse for simplifying the region into unoccupied wilderness. There were rules, skills, ceremonies, repeated work, and long-distance connections. Political history can be found in who could fish, who organized a gathering, and how communities cared for the places on which their food depended.

At the end, return to the current and the fish. A salmon run was a natural event, but a fishery was a human relationship with that event, made durable by technology, knowledge, and institutions. Camas grounds and seasonal camps tell a parallel story about planning and stewardship beyond the banks. The people of the plateau were not waiting for agriculture, towns, or outsiders to make their history begin. Their histories were already being made in the choices of fishers, gatherers, hosts, elders, and travelers. The surviving evidence gives only fragments, but those fragments are enough to demand a fuller account of the place and its people.
