# Remembering political origins: Luba, Lunda, and the lukasa

A story of political origins is an action in the present as well as an account of the past. Luba narratives, the lukasa memory board, and Lunda traditions show different ways of preserving and interpreting authority. Comparing them will not turn remembered founders into securely dated eyewitness history.

## A King in the Story: Luba Origins, Authority, and Historical Evidence

The Luba tell of a ruler called Nkongolo and of the figures who would overturn his order. In different tellings, the visitor Mbidi Kiluwe brings a different ideal of kingship, and his son Kalala Ilunga becomes the figure who defeats Nkongolo and establishes a new legitimacy. This is a powerful political story. It asks what makes rule rightful, why a dynasty should be remembered, and how authority should be recognized. But the story was transmitted through oral and ritual traditions, not preserved as a dated eyewitness report of an event in a particular century.

The landscape in which these traditions mattered lies in the south-central Democratic Republic of the Congo, near river systems that eventually feed the Congo. The name Luba has been used for peoples, languages, and multiple political communities; these senses should not be collapsed into one timeless kingdom. Archaeology in the Upemba wetlands shows material and social developments over the first and second millennia. Yet the royal state described by historians is much clearer in later testimony. Keeping those different histories in view is more useful than choosing a mythical founder and assigning all earlier artifacts to his government.

Thomas Reefe, writing about Luba genesis traditions in the nineteen seventies, explained an important difficulty. For many societies of west-central Africa, historians can test some oral accounts against Portuguese documents from earlier centuries. For the states of the more distant interior, comparable written records are scarce before the nineteenth century. Origin narratives may preserve real relationships and conflicts, but matching one heroic name to another across versions cannot by itself produce a reliable chronology. Historians also need to ask who told each version, for whom, and what the telling accomplished.

The archaeologist begins from a different direction. At Upemba, ceremonial iron axes, anvil-associated burials, and the accumulation of copper show that some families and specialists acquired distinction long before modern written histories of Luba kingship. Material continuities can also be followed through pottery into the centuries when Luba political structures are better known. But neither an axe nor a pot names Kalala Ilunga. When a later story attributes the beginning of iron forging or sacred rule to a culture hero, it may express the value that later communities placed on that practice, rather than the first moment the technology existed.

In the later Luba kingdom, the king was more than the head of a household. The political ideal involved sacred authority, councils, relationships with neighboring leaders, tribute, and inherited positions. The Metropolitan Museum of Art describes a model flexible enough to draw other communities into the authority of prestigious royal ancestors. Giacomo Macola also emphasizes challenges posed by dispersed settlements and competing descent groups. These accounts illuminate real institutions of a later period, especially around and after the seventeenth century. They should not be dropped intact into an eleventh-century Upemba village.

Why would a community give such importance to an origin story? A claim of descent can establish who is entitled to act as a chief. A story about a defeated tyrant can distinguish proper conduct from an unacceptable order. A genealogy can connect a newly affiliated local ruler with an older, prestigious dynastic line. These are historically meaningful actions in the present of the teller. They do not make the past freely inventable. Listeners may know places, predecessors, obligations, and counterclaims that limit what a person can credibly say. Historical interpretation must respect both the continuity of remembered knowledge and its political use.

Royal sites also carried memory. Later Luba histories connected past rulers to particular villages and landscapes, which could become shrines associated with ancestors. Members of the Mbudye association were recognized as specialists who sustained and interpreted historical knowledge. Authority was therefore remembered through people, places, ritual performances, and objects. That is a different archive from written chancery rolls, but it was not for that reason devoid of rules or expertise. The difficulty for modern historians is to reconstruct the history of these practices when many surviving descriptions and artifacts are relatively late.

Visual objects make the stakes of royal memory tangible. A carved seat, an iron emblem, or a ceremonial staff could communicate a relationship to authority. Some later Luba works depict idealized women associated with ancestral rulers, often called mwadi, connecting kingship to feminine representation. The surviving museum pieces cited in contemporary catalogs are generally dated to the nineteenth or early twentieth century. They show the symbolism and art of the documented kingdom. They cannot certify that exactly the same objects and interpretations existed a thousand years before their collection.

Scholars sometimes talk about the formation of a state as though one day someone declared it and the story began. In the Luba case, the archaeological record shows earlier differences of wealth and craft production, while subsequent historical traditions describe strategies for binding leaders and clans together. These could be related without being identical. The crucial question is which practices can actually be followed across the centuries: metalworking symbols, styles of pottery, claims about ancestors, rules for inheriting a title. Each has its own source and different degree of chronological security.

The Luba origin narratives are therefore not footnotes to an allegedly real story found somewhere else. They are historical evidence for how people understood power, remembered relationships, and argued about legitimacy. Archaeology is historical evidence for earlier lives, labor, and material distinctions. These records can be brought into dialogue, but neither should swallow the other. The productive outcome is a history of central African political thought and material change that explains what is remembered, what is measured, and what still cannot be dated.

## A History in the Hand: Luba Lukasa and the Work of Remembering

The next case changes the medium of memory from a narrated origin to an object used by trained custodians of historical knowledge.

Imagine a wooden board small enough to hold in two hands. Its surface has beads, pegs, shells, or carved shapes, arranged in ways an untrained visitor may not understand. This is a lukasa, an object connected with the historical knowledge of the Luba peoples in central Africa. To someone who knows the relevant traditions, its features can help call forth stories of predecessors, relationships, sacred places, and royal authority. A lukasa is not a book whose written words can be sounded out by every reader. It is a material partner in an expert performance of memory.

The surviving boards often date to the nineteenth or twentieth century. That point matters. The Luba political world had developed over earlier centuries, and the Mbudye association helped preserve and interpret knowledge associated with its rulers. But a later dated wooden board cannot demonstrate the age of every rule and story performed with it. The date of an object, the date of a narrated event, and the time when a method of narration was developed are three different questions. An archive made for the living can have more than one historical clock.

A specialist who has learned the stories can touch the surface and use the placement of its features to guide a narrative. The performance may concern the origins of rulers, the relationships among named offices, or landscapes associated with past political events. It depends on memory that was taught, corrected, and interpreted in social settings. The board does not replace the knowledgeable person. Without that training, a modern museum visitor sees things that are visible but not their full historical significance. The difference is not between a society with a history and a society without one; it is between different ways of practicing historical authority.

One surviving object in the Smithsonian collections reveals both the richness and the limits of museum knowledge. It was accessioned in nineteen twenty-one and described as a wooden memory board associated with Luba communities. A later curatorial note reports a particularly elaborate form, including carved heads and a reptile-like figure. The record also preserves a frank admission: some informants could not, or would not, explain the symbolism to a researcher. This is not a failure of the object. It is evidence that access to specialist knowledge was itself part of the social world in which the object functioned.

In the museum account, the board features could mark ancestral kings, royal places, or rules of conduct, while the exact narration varied with the historian. This is a key feature of mnemonic practice. A carved form or a cluster of beads can remind a specialist of an episode and its relations, much as a place may prompt a remembered story. It is not necessary for the entire spoken account to be inscribed on the wood. The knowledge lives partly in a community of trained interpreters. Historians studying the object must investigate the people and institutions around it, not just count its beads.

Why did political communities value these methods? Luba dynastic histories mattered to the distribution of recognition, titles, and rights. The Mbudye association is described in scholarly and museum writing as a body of specialists who preserved and interpreted historical knowledge connected to kingship. Remembering could offer precedent, challenge a claim, or reinforce one. The board did not mechanically decide every dispute, and we should not imagine it as a secret constitution printed in code. Its significance depended on who was entitled to interpret it, who listened, and how a remembered past was made relevant in the present.

The objects of Luba memory extended beyond the lukasa. Museum collections preserve carved seats, bow stands, staffs, headrests, and iron emblems connected with royalty and ancestral authority. Many incorporate images of women and references to earlier rulers. The Metropolitan Museum of Art has objects dated to the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries that bear these forms. Artistic representation is not simply decoration added after the political work was done. It could be part of how claims to authority were visible and persuasive. But stylistic resemblance to medieval metalwork cannot by itself prove that every earlier object served the same purpose.

The study of the lukasa also has a history. Mary Nooter Roberts, Allen F. Roberts, and colleagues assembled the 1996 exhibition catalog Memory: Luba Art and the Making of History. Its chapters brought archaeology, historical scholarship, performance, and the study of objects into conversation. That collaboration offered a way to describe memory as something actively made and interpreted, not merely a container of fixed dates. In our own source records we must also be careful: a publicly available catalog description tells us the topic and authors, while a museum object record documents a particular accession. Neither should be passed off as a transcript of an initiation ceremony.

There is a broader historical lesson here. Written archives preserve some things exceptionally well but also contain gaps, official preferences, and deliberate omissions. Oral performances require living transmission, which can preserve relationships while adapting to political change. Objects can anchor that transmission without carrying everything on their surface. These differences shape which questions historians can ask. A date on a museum accession card is not a date for the origin of a kingdom; a remembered genealogy is not a radiocarbon measurement; a copper axe in a medieval grave is not itself a lukasa.

A lukasa helps us see why historical evidence cannot be reduced to documents on paper. People made the past present through trained recall, performance, crafted materials, and authority shared or contested within a community. The work for modern historians is to take those practices seriously while remaining honest about date and access. We can describe the collected board, the association that used objects of this kind, and the later institutions they served. We cannot turn them into an eyewitness account of the earliest Upemba settlers. The board is a record of the work of remembering, and that work has its own history.

## A Marriage Told Many Ways: Luba, Lunda, and Political Histories

Lunda traditions bring related stories into another political setting, where a marriage can make an argument about legitimate rule.

A princess receives a bracelet that gives her the right to rule. A hunter arrives from outside her court. Their marriage becomes a story about the origins of a kingdom. In several Central African traditions, the princess is called Lueji or Ruwej and the hunter Chibinda Ilunga. The famous story links Lunda, or Ruund, political history with Luba origins. It has often been told as though one marriage could explain the appearance of an entire state. Yet historians ask another question as well: who was telling the story, and what kind of political relationship did it help establish at that moment?

The geographical setting includes the broad savannas between the upper Congo rivers and the Kasai system, extending into what are today the Democratic Republic of the Congo, Angola, and Zambia. The Luba dynastic world lay to the east of Ruund centers. In the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries both developed influential political networks, but they did not have identical institutions. A tradition of a traveler from one court bringing an idea to another may describe prestige and affinity. It can also exaggerate the role of a single outside founder, especially if the receiving society had institutions of its own.

Giacomo Macola points to features of Ruund political organization that should not be reduced to an imported Luba model. One is positional succession. A successor to an office holder could take up not merely the title but the social relationships attached to it. Another is perpetual kinship: relations among titled positions endure even when the actual biological kinship of their occupants changes over generations. In this way political alliances could be expressed as lasting relations among parent and child positions. Those institutional ideas help explain how networks of authority could expand while local groups remained important in their own places.

Luba traditions, for their part, made sacred royal ancestry, affiliation with prestigious forebears, and relations among chiefs central to political legitimacy. Museum and scholarly accounts emphasize councils and a flexible pattern of incorporating other communities. There may have been mutual learning between neighboring polities, but the influence could travel in more than one direction, across long periods. The story of a marriage alone cannot tell us when each institution developed. It is more useful to compare the ways that authority was organized than to force every practice into a tale in which a single person carried civilization across a border.

Research published by David Gordon in 2023 turns to the late nineteenth century, when different groups were telling versions of the Lunda love story for identifiable reasons. His evidence comes from documented encounters, political conflicts, and the writings of Henrique Dias de Carvalho, a Portuguese explorer and diplomat. Carvalho gathered accounts from several regions and assembled them into a larger story that could appear to bind many rulers and traders to one political center. This is not simply a question of whether he wrote the narrative down accurately. It is a question of how his project and his informants helped shape the version that later readers inherited.

The accounts implicated Chokwe, Imbangala, Luba, Lunda, and other people participating in caravan commerce and negotiations. Chiefs and traders could connect themselves to legendary kin relations in order to assert precedence, rights to movement, or entitlement to cooperate and bargain. A story about cousins and in-laws was therefore useful in a world of mobile commercial partnerships. Different versions did not necessarily preserve an untouched central script. Gordon suggests that the widely recognizable, unified love story developed through the interaction of regional accounts and late nineteenth-century political needs.

Some of the most interesting evidence is in the details that fail to fit a tidy family tree. Gordon investigates the figure Kinguri, whose connection to the Lunda dynasty was narrated in ways that made sense for Imbangala and related political positions. He also examines Chokwe accounts that relate their leading families to Lueji. These were not merely passive copies of what happened at a distant royal capital. People at different edges of the political world could actively make and defend their place within its stories. It is possible that some familiar characters entered a wider, assembled account relatively late.

Carvalho imagined that a common history could support peace under Portuguese influence. Gordon shows how this ambition belonged to a moment of conflict and shifting power among central African trading groups. The attempt to frame rival claims as one grand family history also reveals the limits of the proposed settlement: the resulting story could not simply command peace. We should not mistake a European compiler for the sole creator of the traditions. Earlier motifs and locally meaningful claims existed, while his expedition and book gave them a distinctive shared shape and new political uses.

The distinction between a founding legend and a history of telling matters well beyond Lunda. Oral traditions can preserve knowledge, including memories of places and political relationships. But the surviving version of a story has a history of its own. Historians ask when it was recorded, which speakers were consulted, which interests were competing, what was translated, and what was omitted. Such questions do not automatically discredit oral knowledge. They make it possible to study political reasoning in which remembered kinship shaped practical negotiations. That is richer than calling a heroic marriage either literal fact or a fiction without historical meaning.

The deeper connection to the medieval history of the region is one of method, not an unbroken cast of named rulers. Archaeology in Upemba finds earlier social distinction, copper exchange, and cultural change. Much later, Luba and Ruund historical practices reveal elaborate ways to organize authority and relate communities across distance. To move between these periods we need separate dates and multiple kinds of evidence. The marriage of Lueji and Chibinda Ilunga teaches us how a remembered past could serve statecraft, while the earlier graves teach a different history. Keeping both records distinct lets central African societies appear as makers of institutions rather than characters in a timeless legend.
