# Remote Oceanic settlement and maritime centers

Oceanic voyaging led to settlements with different histories of household life and political authority. We will move from Lapita evidence in the western Pacific to later maritime centers in Tonga, Samoa, and Pohnpei, without mistaking that route for one empire expanding across the ocean.

## Before the Distant Islands: Lapita Households and the First Remote Ocean Voyages

At Teouma on Efate in Vanuatu, archaeologists uncovered broken pottery, food refuse, and a cemetery. These remains belong to the first period of settlement of islands that earlier generations of people had not occupied. The pots are especially recognizable: artisans pressed small toothed tools into clay, building complex designs from many impressions. Yet the living community was more than the fragments it left behind. A family needed a place to cook, water to drink, materials for tools, and relations with others who knew how to cross the sea. The earliest Remote Oceanian story is both a story of voyaging and one of the ordinary decisions that turned a landing into a home.

Archaeologists use Near Oceania for regions whose settlement histories stretch back many thousands of years, including New Guinea and neighboring islands. Remote Oceania describes island groups separated by more demanding stretches of open water. The distinction is geographic, not an index of the intelligence or daring of their inhabitants. Much earlier maritime knowledge already existed in the western Pacific. Around the middle and later second millennium before the common era, communities associated with Lapita pottery began spreading into what is now Vanuatu, Fiji, Tonga, and neighboring archipelagos. The dates differ by island, and the expansion was neither an instant jump across a map nor the creation of a single government.

The pottery matters because decorative techniques and vessel forms can be compared among distant sites. A stamped motif recovered from a settlement is a clue about knowledge shared among makers, not a signature of a particular boat captain. Archaeologists also compare stone tools, shell ornaments, ovens, animal bones, and the locations of settlements. Differences in these materials help reveal local adaptations. People in several archipelagos could share a repertoire without maintaining identical households, ceremonies, or languages. A single surviving design can outlast the circumstances in which it was learned. We must therefore treat the term Lapita as the name of a material tradition, rather than a passport carried by one people unchanged through centuries.

Dating Teouma required more care than placing a charcoal fragment beside a pot and reading a year. Wood can have been old before it was burned. Marine foods and shells may take up carbon with a different history from the contemporary atmosphere. Later disturbance can move objects into earlier-looking deposits. A reanalysis used the observed archaeological contexts and a Bayesian chronological model. It places the most likely start of the Lapita deposits at roughly twenty-nine hundred years before the present, around the beginning of the first millennium before the common era. This is a modeled range, not the date of the first canoe to touch Vanuatu.

Tonga offers a related but separate chronology. David Burley and Sean Connaughton examined sites in the Vavaʻu islands, using radiocarbon dates to trace the northern part of Tonga's early settlement sequence. Their results suggest that the movement through the Tongan islands happened rapidly after initial entry into the archipelago. Decorated Lapita pottery also seems to have given way to plainer forms relatively quickly. Neither change requires a sudden loss of practical knowledge. Potteries respond to use, local preferences, and the movement of craftspeople. The absence of abundant decorated fragments on an island may reflect more than a delay before the island was occupied.

A settlement required work that was not recorded on the face of its most decorated pot. People had to procure stone from suitable sources, shape blades, collect shellfish, catch fish, prepare food, and maintain the ground around dwellings. Some brought domesticated animals or crops, and the mix varied by place and period. The excavated Teouma material includes animal remains and food deposits, but an animal bone does not tell us who fed it or whether its presence was welcome. Archaeologists reconstruct diets by comparing multiple classes of remains and by asking how deposits formed. The work of households sits behind every long-distance voyage, even when names cannot be retrieved.

The cemetery at Teouma provides a further record, but a delicate one. Human remains offer evidence about lives and funerary treatment that pottery cannot supply. Their interpretation carries responsibilities toward the communities whose ancestors are being studied. Researchers have analyzed ancient genomes from several of these early individuals. In a 2020 study led by Mark Lipson, the earliest sampled people from Teouma had ancestry closely related to a source in eastern and southeastern Asia. That conclusion describes relationships in the available genetic comparison set. It does not establish the spoken language of every Teouma resident, or tell us which relatives commanded a canoe, made a pot, or raised a child.

The ancestry history did not stop at the first settlement. Later ancient individuals in Vanuatu show substantial ancestry connected to populations farther west in the Pacific, including the Bismarck Archipelago. Researchers distinguish more than one period of population movement and mixture. That sequence is difficult to express with a simple picture of a founding party whose descendants remain unchanged forever. It also warns against treating a single genetic label as a culture. Material practices can move through teaching and marriage; people can migrate while adopting local skills; a pottery style can end without the people who used it disappearing. Different kinds of evidence illuminate different parts of this history.

Later Polynesian languages and societies developed out of long histories in the western Pacific. Tonga and Samoa became important centers for subsequent movement into more remote parts of the ocean, but their people did not simply wait in place for a thousand years until another expedition was launched. Settlements grew and changed. Methods of cooking and storing, patterns of obligation, and traditions of making and navigating vessels all developed over generations. Radiocarbon chronologies for the much later settlement of East Polynesia should therefore not be glued onto Lapita chronology as though one uninterrupted voyage crossed the entire ocean. Different voyages and communities belonged to different historical moments.

Comparing Teouma and Tonga shows the value of being exact about the scale of a claim. A group of dates from one cemetery can help place the lives and burials there. A set of Tongan excavation dates can test how rapidly several islands were occupied. A comparison of genomes can reveal relatives and deeper ancestry relationships across time. None of these observations alone reconstructs the motives, political loyalties, or memories of the travelers. The achievement of crossing the sea is real, but the settlement afterward is no less important: the work of building kitchens, raising children, sharing skilled labor, and learning what an unfamiliar island could provide.

The fragments at Teouma can therefore be read without imagining that they explain every aspect of a people. The stamped designs tell us about craft transmission and links among islands. Food remains point to the practical business of making a life. Burials and genomic data introduce personal and population histories, each with its own limits. The Pacific did not become inhabited in a single dramatic stroke. It was transformed through repeated voyages and settlements, followed by generations of local experimentation. That is the scale at which Lapita history becomes a human history instead of a parade of named archaeological cultures.

## Stone from Faraway Shores: Tonga, Samoa, and Maritime Power

Later developments in Tonga and Samoa let us compare early household evidence with more concentrated forms of maritime political power.

On the island of Tongatapu, a platform faced with carefully fitted stone can mark a chiefly burial. Some of the stone had to be quarried, moved, and shaped through organized effort. But other rocks found in these elite places tell a story about an even larger landscape. Geochemical analyses show that tools were brought to Tonga from distant islands, including Samoa and Fiji. The finds turn a Pacific political center into evidence for an ocean of sustained relationships. A powerful chief could draw significance not only from fields at home but from marriages, visitors, tribute, craft specialists, and voyages that linked households across many days of sailing.

Tongatapu lies within a long archipelago, where islands differ in their geology and local resources. A limestone island does not supply the same stone needed for tools as a volcanic one. Movement between islands was therefore part of the material economy well before a centralized Tongan ruling order emerged. Archaeologists found nonlocal worked stone in earlier contexts, as well as in the better-known monumental centers. The important historical question is not whether Tonga first invented inter-island exchange, because it did not. It is how chiefly institutions changed the destinations, intensity, and political use of connections that already existed.

A sequence of elite centers helps us locate this transformation in time. Heketa on eastern Tongatapu developed monumental architecture around the thirteenth century. Later, the center of paramount activity moved to Lapaha beside the Fanga Uta Lagoon, around the fourteenth century. At Lapaha, stepped stone-faced tombs of the Tuʻi Tonga line became prominent features of the landscape. Chronologies combine radiocarbon dates, architectural ordering, and later genealogical accounts. They give broad historical phases without turning every ancestral name into an independently dated reign. The buildings establish organized labor and ceremonial centrality even where the identities of particular builders remain unknown.

Consider the work behind a large stone burial platform. Workers extracted stone, loaded it, moved it by water or land, prepared foundations, and raised the structure. Others grew and delivered food for gatherings. Canoes and landing places mattered because stone was only one part of what had to arrive at the center. Archaeologists have identified wharf-related works, ditches, and reclaimed shore areas at Lapaha. That concentration of construction does not mean the entire population lived at a royal court. It means a political center could command or attract work and resources from people whose daily homes were dispersed across the islands.

In a 2014 study, Geoffrey Clark and colleagues analyzed stones from Heketa and Lapaha and compared their geochemical signatures to reference samples from multiple islands. Among the worked adze flakes in the elite-center sample, about two thirds came from outside the Tongan archipelago. The proportion is striking, but it is a proportion of a small group of artifacts recovered largely near chiefly tombs. It does not mean two thirds of every person's tools in Tonga were imports. Sampling context is essential. A result from a royal foundation trench cannot be turned into a national trade statistic without further evidence.

The chemical signatures point especially to Samoa and Fiji. One artifact was traced most plausibly to the Society Islands, roughly twenty-five hundred kilometers away. This is powerful evidence for objects crossing a vast ocean, though it cannot tell us how many hands transferred that particular piece or whether its journey involved a visiting crew, an intermediary, or a marriage exchange. A chief's possession of something distant could be valuable because of the relationships needed to obtain it. The stone itself did not have to be rare everywhere to matter at Lapaha. Its social history could make it important.

The comparison with Samoa prevents a simple imperial map from replacing a history of interaction. The researchers examined Samoan archaeological stone as well as the Tongan elite sites. Their sampled Samoan stone tools were local, even though stone from Samoa had traveled to Tongan centers. This is an asymmetry in the excavated materials, not proof that Samoans had no voyaging knowledge or that their societies were ruled in every detail by Tongatapu. Samoa contained several chiefdoms with their own histories. Political influence, gift exchange, ceremonial links, and coercion could overlap without becoming one permanent uniform administration.

Later historical traditions remember powerful Tuʻi Tonga leaders and relationships involving other island groups. Such histories are important sources for the political meaning of kinship, precedence, and chiefly authority. They require a separate question from stone sourcing: when was each account transmitted, and which historical moment does it describe? A named king cannot be assigned to a particular imported adze simply because both are connected with the same center. Where archaeology gives us an age for a tomb and a chemical origin for a stone, it establishes a material sequence. The later narrative can add meaning, but it should not silently substitute for an inscription that does not exist.

Elite exchange depended on people who rarely appear on monuments. Farmers supplied ceremonial feasts and ordinary meals. Canoe builders and navigators made voyages possible. Stoneworkers understood materials and the handling of heavy blocks. Relatives visiting from another island could carry ideas as well as objects. Research on chiefly funerals and tribute suggests that ritual gatherings helped concentrate and redistribute resources. The model is convincing as an explanation for the central places, but it does not provide a payroll, a list of workers, or precise instructions given to each village. This is a history of organized obligations reconstructed from their durable traces.

The Lapaha tombs were not all completed before 1500. Some belong to later centuries, and Tongan political institutions kept changing. That matters for a medieval course: later buildings can illustrate the continuing importance of the site, but they cannot be used without qualification to describe a fourteenth-century ceremony. By the fifteenth century, powerful chiefly centers and inter-island connections were already established. Their later intensification was part of another historical phase. Distinguishing stages allows us to see state formation as a process, not an event that appeared suddenly, fully formed, with the first monumental stone.

The imported stones at Tonga challenge two misleading pictures. The first would treat Pacific islands as isolated worlds cut off by water. The second would paint every link between islands as a uniform empire whose rulers possessed every shore they could reach. The evidence supports neither. It shows a maritime political center whose leaders worked within far-reaching networks and helped redirect some of those networks toward elite institutions. Tonga's history belongs to the study of how power grows from geography, skills, and relationships. Its ocean crossings were not marginal to politics: they were part of what made politics possible.

## Islands Made by Hand: Nan Madol and the Histories of Pohnpei

Moving northwest to Pohnpei brings us to another distinctive center, where artificial islets preserve a different relationship among construction, water, and authority.

Along the edge of Pohnpei, in the Federated States of Micronesia, stone walls rise from shallow water. They divide a complex of artificial islets, passages, courtyards, and buildings known as Nan Madol. In some places long columns of dark basalt lie one above another, carried from elsewhere on the island and fitted into walls. There are more than a hundred islets in the surviving complex. A visitor sees a monumental center, but the labor behind it is more difficult to see: people transporting stone, placing coral rubble, preparing food for workers, and tending the canoes that moved through its channels. Those activities connect Nan Madol to the broader social history of Pohnpei.

Pohnpei was not empty until its walls appeared. The island had earlier settlement, gardening, exchange, and community life. Nan Madol emerged within an inhabited landscape, rather than establishing island society from nothing. Its buildings included ceremonial spaces, burial places, and residential areas; their concentration speaks to the organization of people and resources. Archaeologists connect the monumental center with the chiefly order conventionally described as the Saudeleur dynasty. That label is useful for studying a period of island-scale authority, yet it is not a document listing every office, tax, or person's duties. The built environment must be considered alongside histories preserved in Pohnpeian communities.

The age of the structures has been a difficult problem. Wood charcoal from a disturbed deposit can be older or younger than a particular wall. Coral offers a different opportunity: living coral takes up uranium, and after the coral dies its radioactive changes can be used to estimate age. A 2016 study led by Mark McCoy applied this method to coral associated with the monumental Nandowas tomb. It obtained dates around 1180 to 1200 of the common era. Such a result places a major building episode in history more precisely than an undated genealogy can. It still dates the sampled coral and its archaeological context, not every piece of basalt on Pohnpei.

More recent research complicated that picture in a productive way. A 2024 investigation combined more than one hundred seventy uranium-series measurements from coral fragments with radiocarbon evidence from excavations. Its authors proposed two major phases of construction from approximately the tenth through the fifteenth centuries. That suggests activity at the complex earlier than the best-known late-twelfth-century tomb, and it changes how the site might be related to the development of chiefly power. The two studies need not be forced into a contradiction: a carefully dated tomb can belong to a long history of construction, rebuilding, and changing use. The precision comes from comparing contexts rather than choosing one memorable date.

The materials posed practical problems. Columnar basalt does not occur in the shape of a ready-made wall beside every islet. Workers had to acquire and move heavy pieces from sources elsewhere on Pohnpei. Coral and other material filled foundations and helped make usable platforms in shallow water. Water passages connected built islands, so movement through the center depended on boats and the shape of the shoreline. The effort required organized cooperation over time. Yet archaeology alone does not tell us whether every household was compelled to work, whether some volunteered for ceremonial projects, or how obligations differed between neighborhoods. The stones demonstrate labor more securely than they document its exact political terms.

Nan Madol is often called a city, but that familiar word can conceal its specialized character. Its preserved islets contain ceremonial, mortuary, and residential domains rather than a street plan resembling a modern town. The island's population also lived beyond the monumental complex. The work of food production, exchange, and kinship could not have happened only inside walls. An elaborate center requires connections to gardens, fisheries, shore landings, and ordinary houses. The evidence from Nan Madol thus concerns both concentrated authority and the wider landscape that sustained it. We should not equate the number of islets with a census of residents.

Changing water conditions are part of the research debate. Pohnpei has experienced relative sea-level change associated with land movement and larger ocean-climate patterns. The 2024 study proposed that shifts in sea level and the El Niño–Southern Oscillation affected construction opportunities and maintenance demands. The suggestion is historically consequential: walls, canals, and foundations can be defenses against water as well as displays of power. But the authors present an environmental hypothesis, not a demonstration that climate alone ended a dynasty. Changes in political relationships, labor, and ritual can occur even without a single natural cause. Physical chronology and political causation are distinct tasks.

Pohnpeian traditions remember the Saudeleur and the political order that followed. The World Heritage account connects the site with continuing ceremonial institutions, including the Nahnmwarki system of chiefly authority and stewardship. Oral history does not operate like a laboratory measurement, and a coral age does not translate a narrative. They address related but different questions. Traditions can convey the legitimacy and obligations of offices, relationships among places, and moral judgments about rulers. Archaeological chronology can test when certain material activities occurred. Treating either kind of knowledge as irrelevant would leave the history less complete.

The site is also a place in the present. UNESCO lists threats to its physical survival, including vegetation growth, siltation of waterways, and damage associated with changing water conditions. Conservation work has to contend with the fact that the walls are neither isolated objects nor anonymous ruins. They belong to a living landscape with people who maintain cultural and historical relationships to the place. That makes the modern history of archaeology and heritage management part of the story. We can measure a stone, record a coral date, or map a wall, while still needing to ask who has authority to care for the site and interpret its meaning.

Nan Madol changes how we picture the medieval Pacific. It was not a small imitation of a Eurasian palace or a mysterious work produced beyond history. It was a distinctive center built and rebuilt by island communities with skilled knowledge of stone, shore, water, and social obligation. Research now identifies a more extended construction sequence than older summaries suggested, while leaving important arguments about political reorganization open. The practical history of moving rock and maintaining passages is inseparable from the political history of the Saudeleur and the continuing traditions of Pohnpei. The walls matter because generations of people made them meaningful.
