# Coins beneath the House: Kilwa and the Meaning of Swahili Wealth

More than three hundred small copper coins were found together beneath the floor of a stone house at Songo Mnara, off the Tanzanian coast. Some carried names associated with rulers of nearby Kilwa. Their presence invites an obvious question: had someone hidden savings? Archaeologists offer a more specific possibility. Because the deposit was built into a foundation together with other valued objects, the coins may have marked a new house and tied prosperity to a place. They were both objects with economic uses and objects that could have social meaning. This small discovery leads beyond the great harbors and palaces of medieval East Africa into the rooms where people lived.

Kilwa Kisiwani and Songo Mnara were towns of the Swahili coast, a long belt of African communities connected by sea travel, farming, fishing, and trade. Their prosperity increased especially between the thirteenth and fifteenth centuries. Kilwa's merchants handled goods that moved between inland Africa and the Indian Ocean, including gold and ivory, while imported ceramics, beads, and other objects arrived from distant ports. These towns were not foreign colonies set down on an empty shore. Their language and culture developed in African coastal societies that interacted with Arabian and Asian worlds over many centuries.

The standing buildings show what wealth could make possible. Kilwa had an important congregational mosque and extensive stone architecture. The palace complex known as Husuni Kubwa was built in the early fourteenth century. Its scale advertised the power of the rulers who could command materials and workers. Yet a stone ruin hides most of the labor necessary to keep a town operating. Food had to arrive at markets, boats had to be repaired, timber obtained, stone cut, and households provisioned. Imported porcelain does not mean that every resident ate from imported vessels. Archaeology must examine where objects were found and who could use them.

The coins offer a route into these questions. Kilwa minted copper pieces bearing Arabic inscriptions, and a number survive with names associated with different rulers. Numismatists can compare inscriptions and styles to propose sequences. But the age of a coin is not always the age of its final use. The Songo Mnara deposit contains types apparently produced at different times, some much older than the house in which they were placed. This matters because the same coin could circulate, be kept, and gain new meaning for generations. Its original inscription was only one part of its later history.

Stephanie Wynne-Jones and Jeffrey Fleisher ask us not to divide coins neatly into either currency or symbols. Archaeological context shows multiple kinds of value. A copper piece might help in a transaction, but it could also be placed in a foundation or associated with an event worth commemorating. Without the context, a museum's isolated coin cannot tell us which use it had. This does not mean monetary exchange was absent. It means that a familiar modern category—money—does not cover everything an object did. The same problem appears in many societies, including those with sophisticated commercial systems.

The geographical networks behind the coins were extensive. Ocean navigation was seasonal, shaped by monsoon winds, currents, and the knowledge of pilots. Ships could move between East African ports, Arabia, western India, and beyond through relays of exchange. Not every foreign ceramic traveled on a ship that sailed directly from its production site to Kilwa. Goods could pass through multiple ports, each adding merchants, costs, and relationships. The idea of a single long-distance trade route can conceal this relay economy. It can also conceal the African inland producers whose labor supplied high-value exports.

At Songo Mnara, an excavated house is more informative than a reconstructed story of a merchant prince. The foundation deposit is associated with a particular building event. Its coins and accompanying material suggest that the people constructing or occupying the building placed value deliberately into its fabric. Researchers can describe the deposit's location and the range of coin types. They cannot identify the precise person who chose it or reconstruct a spoken ceremony from the objects alone. The distinction protects an important historical insight from being buried beneath an appealing but unsupported anecdote.

The towns also contained religious and social institutions. Mosques were sites of worship and gathering, and Arabic inscriptions linked local rulers and communities to broader Muslim traditions. Yet speaking of Islamic Swahili towns should not erase their African languages and local institutions. Muslim communities on the coast developed through particular histories of trade, settlement, and marriage, not through a uniform replacement of existing life. The meanings of stone houses, mosques, pottery, and coins were made locally. Indian Ocean connections expanded the possibilities available to residents; they did not define every aspect of their identities.

There is also a difference between trade volume and control. A ruler might collect revenue at a harbor, sponsor buildings, issue coins, or claim precedence among nearby settlements. None of these alone proves that every boat, cargo, or village belonged to the ruler. Archaeologists reconstruct political power through the distribution of goods, settlement patterns, monumental investment, and written accounts when they survive. Some powerful centers may have depended as much on partnership with merchants as on direct command. A city's skyline could proclaim authority, while its everyday economy required continual cooperation across households and ports.

We should be equally careful about decline. Portuguese attacks and commercial intervention in the early sixteenth century changed coastal politics, but the history of Swahili communities did not stop there. The monuments most visible to visitors today also underwent centuries of damage, rebuilding, and conservation. Songo Mnara's ruined houses are not evidence that the town's inhabitants had no descendants or that an entire culture disappeared. Many Swahili towns remained active, while networks shifted and political centers changed. The archaeological period of a particular complex is only one chapter of a longer coastal history.

Why did people place coins in a house? We can propose meanings because the pattern is documented, yet the exact intention is unknowable. Perhaps the deposit marked a foundation and an enduring investment; perhaps other customary expectations were involved. The coins would have been recognizable within a world of markets and political names, but they entered a new setting under the floor. This is the advantage of excavation over collecting objects without provenance. It reveals an action, not just an artifact. A copper piece can tell a different story when we know the room in which it was left.

Kilwa's rulers, the traders who crossed the sea, and the people who worked in stone houses belonged to the same coastal economy. Their resources and influence were not equal. The palace reflects one concentration of power. The foundation deposit reflects a choice made within a household, even if we cannot name that household. Together they make the medieval Swahili coast more than a map of imported luxuries. Its history was made by African communities deciding what to build, what to trade, what to keep, and how to connect a home to a world extending far beyond the horizon.
