# Fuel and Iron in Buhaya: Technology, Forests, and Change beside Lake Victoria

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

On the western shore of Lake Victoria lies Buhaya, in what is now northwestern Tanzania. For archaeologists, the history of this landscape can be read partly in the remains of iron furnaces, charcoal, and altered vegetation. For people who worked there, iron was not only an object. It was a process that required fuel, air, skill, and a reliable supply of materials. The story of Buhaya shows why a technological achievement cannot be separated from the forest that fed its fires, or from the successive communities that changed the way those fires were made.

Iron was valuable because a forged implement could be made strong, edged, repaired, and reshaped. But ore did not simply turn into an axe when placed on a fire. Smelting demanded a controlled furnace. Charcoal had to burn hot enough, with the right supply of air, for iron compounds in the ore to be chemically reduced. The bloom that came out still had to be consolidated and worked. When archaeologists find a furnace or a slag deposit, they are finding the residue of organized tasks that began long before the firing and continued after it ended.

Peter Schmidt's research in Buhaya placed ironworking within a long and uneven chronology. In a 1997 study, he described evidence for production along the western lake shore beginning roughly twenty-five hundred years ago, with an especially marked expansion of smelting and settlement in the first half of the first millennium of the common era. Sites spread through parts of the landscape toward Lake Ikimba. The dates concern particular archaeological contexts, not a guarantee of uninterrupted occupation or the same workshop practices at every place. Buhaya is a region, and its history includes change over centuries.

Schmidt and metallurgist Donald Avery also conducted influential investigations of Haya iron-smelting techniques, combining archaeological inquiry, observation, and technological reconstruction. In 1978 they reported a sophisticated process in which incoming air was preheated, helping the smelting furnace reach conditions capable of producing high-carbon iron or steel. Their work challenged the idea that advanced metallurgical knowledge must be measured by resemblance to familiar European installations. At the same time, this is a published reconstruction and argument: it is not proof that every excavated furnace of every early period had the exact same configuration or achieved the same product.

Why would preheating the air matter? Imagine blowing cold air into a very hot fire. Part of the fire's heat must warm that air before it helps sustain the next reaction. If incoming air is already hot, more energy can remain available for the smelting process. In any real furnace, performance also depends on air flow, fuel, ore, structure, and heat loss. Preheating is a physical principle, not magic, and neither the principle nor a modern demonstration automatically fixes the design of a furnace that operated fifteen hundred years earlier. To connect a reconstructed process to the archaeological past, researchers must examine the surviving furnace architecture and production debris.

The landscape supplied much of what those furnaces required. Charcoal could be made from trees, but charcoal production consumed substantial quantities of wood. In his analysis of landscape change, Schmidt used charcoal identified from furnace contexts alongside environmental evidence to investigate how fuel use altered local forests. For early sites near Kemondo Bay, charcoal indicated that smelters exploited a range of woody species associated with wetter forest environments. As the first millennium progressed, later fuel remains indicated increasing reliance on secondary regrowth and less accessible environments. A chemical or botanical sample from a furnace can therefore tell a story about the forest outside it.

This change should not be reduced to a simple tale in which ancient workers carelessly cut down every tree. People also cleared land for cultivation, constructed homes, harvested other forest products, and responded to climatic variation. Pollen and sediment records help reconstruct the broader picture, but separating the contribution of each process is difficult. Schmidt interpreted the archaeological and environmental sequence as evidence that intensive production and settlement transformed woodland resources in the first half of the first millennium. Environmental change was both an effect of human activity and a condition to which later workers had to adapt.

The sequence did not move straight toward larger output and permanent industrial occupation. Schmidt argued that much of the region experienced a decline or abandonment of the earlier production system during the latter part of the first millennium. Settlement and iron production subsequently reappeared in different forms, including activity in the thirteenth and fourteenth centuries. Later furnaces are associated in his account with more efficient use of fuel. These are regional archaeological interpretations that researchers can revise with new excavations and dating. They are not a documented census of every family that moved away or returned.

There is also a social question behind the engineering. Who could obtain ore, clay, wood, food, and the labor needed for repeated firings? Who owned a completed implement, and who repaired one? Did some workers specialize, or did production follow seasonal agricultural rhythms? Ethnographic accounts of more recent ironworkers can suggest questions and show that technical knowledge may be transmitted within families, apprenticeships, and ritual settings. They cannot substitute for evidence about two-thousand-year-old people. Modern Haya communities are not living fossils of a single ancient practice.

The history of iron is sometimes presented as if one inventor found a perfect recipe and everyone afterward copied it. Buhaya suggests a different kind of history. A technology can develop through experiments, adaptations, changes in fuel supply, shifting settlements, and new social demands. Skill is not exhausted by the furnace's hottest moment. It includes recognizing ore, choosing clay, managing charcoal, adjusting air, reading the fire, and knowing when to stop. The archaeological record catches only fragments of this chain, but those fragments are sufficient to establish long-term local expertise and alteration.

When we place Buhaya beside the Urewe pottery sites and the wider Great Lakes landscape, we have to resist two opposite simplifications. It is wrong to regard early East African metallurgists as mere passive recipients of a technology. It is equally wrong to claim that a single pot style or a modern ethnic name identifies every person who contributed to centuries of ironworking. Buhaya's furnaces give us a history of knowledge, energy, and environmental change. The iron may have left the furnace. The waste and charcoal help explain the work that made it possible.

## Evidence and reading

- Peter R. Schmidt, **[Archaeological Views on a History of Landscape Change in East Africa](https://www.jstor.org/stable/182542)**, *Journal of African History* 38 (1997), 393–421. Bibliographic abstract summarizes the furnace chronology, species evidence, forest change, and later reoccupation; full text was not reviewed here.
- Peter R. Schmidt and Donald H. Avery, **[Complex iron smelting and prehistoric culture in Tanzania](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17830304/)**, *Science* 201 (1978), 1085–1089. Abstract records the authors' preheating and steel-production interpretation based on ethnographic, technological, and archaeological research.

Craddock and collaborators, **[Early Iron Age iron-smelting debris from Rwanda and Burundi, East Africa](https://www.hmsjournal.org/index.php/home/article/view/218)**, *Historical Metallurgy* 41(1). Technical comparison of furnace clay and slag; its Rwanda/Burundi materials should not be treated as direct excavations of Buhaya.

## Evidence boundary

The preheated-air and steel claims belong to Schmidt and Avery's published reconstruction, not to an independently sampled census of all Iron Age furnaces. Schmidt's published abstract supports major landscape transitions but not a precise inventory of the labor, motives, or households affected. Fuel pressure was one part of an environmental history also shaped by settlement, agriculture, and climate.
