# Seven Silver Coins in Bamiyan: A Jewish Family and the Afghan Genizah

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

In May 1011, two men acknowledged a debt to a merchant named Abu Nasr, son of Daniyal, in Bamiyan, in what is now central Afghanistan. The sum was seven silver coins of a named denomination. Witnesses and a legal formula made the promise something more durable than an oral reminder. We can read about this particular transaction because a document survived and was later edited by scholars. The amount is small enough to tempt us into thinking we understand the entire life behind it. We do not. But it places named people, a legal claim, money, and a multilingual community in a city often remembered mainly for other kinds of history.

The document is part of a group of medieval manuscripts commonly called the Afghan Genizah. The name recalls the famous Cairo Geniza, but these are different collections with different histories of survival. The Afghan materials include letters, account books, contracts, and religious writings associated with Jewish communities and their neighbors between roughly the eleventh and thirteenth centuries. The National Library of Israel acquired part of the collection in the 2010s. Scholars including Ofir Haim and Samuel Thrope have described the documents and identified the people and institutions within them. A collector's label is not an ancient title that every original writer used.

Abu Nasr, often rendered Abu Netzer in summaries, was connected with a family of Jewish merchants in and around Bamiyan. The debt acknowledgment, identified in the library publication as manuscript Heb. 4 8333.216, was written in Persian and bears witness names. Two debtors are named in the modern edition. These details show that business relationships could cross religious and linguistic boundaries. A Jewish merchant could use forms recognizable within Islamic legal practice for a debt owed by Muslim-named individuals. The document does not prove that relations were always harmonious or equal. It does show a specific encounter in which different communities participated in a shared legal environment.

The form of the acknowledgment matters. A written statement of debt offers one party evidence to produce if the obligation is later disputed. It may specify who owed what, and who heard or witnessed the agreement. But a document that asserts payment is due does not tell us whether the debt was eventually settled. We can distinguish what the signers declared from what happened afterward. In the same way, an account book can record quantities and names without revealing every bargain that produced them. The survival of a legal claim should not be mistaken for a judicial verdict on the fairness of the underlying transaction.

The library's Afghan collection includes a merchant account book with many surviving pages, along with letters and religious manuscripts. An account book can organize a network of purchases, credits, and obligations more systematically than a single contract. Its lists may reveal the terms of everyday commerce, but they are still the records of people with reasons to keep accounts. Workers without the means to write, small producers outside the merchant's circle, and the households dependent on them could be largely absent. When historians reconstruct the economy of a city from these pages, the resulting picture is detailed and selective at the same time.

Languages are central to the story. Materials in the collection include Persian, Judeo-Persian, Arabic, Hebrew, and Aramaic, with script and language not always aligned in the way an English-speaking listener might expect. A religious text might use Hebrew while a business agreement used Persian; a merchant might move between linguistic registers according to audience and institution. This plurality was not a novelty that waited for modern globalization. Yet it is also not proof that every person in Bamiyan could read all these languages. Documents move between specialists and owners. The written archive records the capacities and choices of those who produced and preserved it.

Religious writings stand beside commercial papers. The collection contains fragments of Jewish liturgy and legal tradition, including a piece of the Mishnah. These texts show that communities maintained learned practices as well as businesses far from major centers often emphasized in histories of Judaism. Their presence does not imply that the entire city was Jewish or that every member of the Jewish community studied the same books. Different genres served different needs. A prayer text may offer evidence of ritual practice; a debt record may show legal procedure; neither provides a ready-made demographic count.

Bamiyan itself belonged to a much larger web of regions linked through eastern Iran, Afghanistan, Central Asia, and routes toward South Asia. Its Buddhist past is materially visible in other sites, while the eleventh-century manuscripts show later communities under different political and religious conditions. A town could change through the movement of rulers, trade, migration, and faith without losing all its older local relationships. A single long-distance label such as Silk Road can help locate the setting but cannot explain who sold food, what people argued about, or which debt mattered to one household on a particular day. The archive makes those smaller scales possible.

Not all the materials belong to the same decade. Some pieces are from the early eleventh century, while others document Islamic and Persianate life closer to the Mongol conquests of the early thirteenth. Combining them into one undated description of medieval Bamiyan would create a false simultaneous society. Their changing languages, practices, and political settings invite comparison across time instead. The surviving papers can expose continuity and disruption, but the historian must respect each item's date and kind. A manuscript copied in one century cannot be treated as eyewitness testimony for another without additional reasoning.

The modern recovery of the collection also matters. The manuscripts reached scholars through collecting and acquisitions, not through a fully documented archaeological excavation of a single sealed ancient archive. Reported places of origin must therefore be distinguished from secure findspots. Research on physical materials and internal place names can help locate documents, but neither makes the lost context reappear. It is tempting to call the collection an untouched time capsule; doing so would conceal the chain of people who found, sold, acquired, conserved, and edited the pages in modern times. Ethical collection history is part of the evidence, not a footnote.

There are political histories here as well. In the early thirteenth century, Mongol campaigns transformed the region, with profound effects on settlements and networks. Some later manuscripts from the broader collection belong to the world immediately before those changes. We should not use the catastrophe to reduce earlier Bamiyan to a foreshadowing of destruction. People maintained shops, loans, religious study, and family relationships without knowing how a future historian would arrange their centuries. The archive helps restore that everyday present. Events become more intelligible when we understand what networks and institutions existed before they were disrupted.

Consider the limits of our case study. We know something of Abu Nasr and his business connections because paper belonging to a literate family survived. We cannot infer that their wealth was typical, that all merchants made loans on the same terms, or that the whole population of the region shared their faith. The seven silver coins in the dated acknowledgment tell us about a particular legal claim, not the average household's annual income. If we want to know about farming or labor beyond the archive's circle, we must combine it with other material evidence and historical sources. A specific voice is valuable precisely because we can keep it specific.

The debt document ends for us without a reliable sequel. We do not know from that page whether the two men returned the seven coins, how the relationship changed, or how Abu Nasr remembered the encounter. What survives is a written attempt to bind an obligation in a city where Jewish and Muslim lives intersected through commerce and law. The wider collection adds worship, books, correspondence, and accounts to that picture. Bamiyan becomes not a blank stretch between empires but a place with named participants and institutions. Their papers remind us how much history can be recovered from ordinary decisions, and how carefully those decisions must be read.
