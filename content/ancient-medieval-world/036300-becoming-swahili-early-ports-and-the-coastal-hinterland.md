# Becoming Swahili: early ports and the coastal hinterland

Becoming Swahili was a long coastal and inland history. Early towns built with earth and timber, neighboring villages, pottery traditions, and accounts of origin all contribute evidence. We will travel along the coast and its hinterlands without assuming that every place followed one foundation story.

## Rooms beneath the Shore: Unguja Ukuu, Fukuchani, and Early Zanzibar

If we wanted to find one of the busiest landing places on the East African coast twelve or thirteen centuries ago, we should not begin by looking for a great stone palace. Much of the early settlement at Unguja Ukuu, on the island of Unguja in today's Tanzania, was made of timber, earth, and thatch. What has survived is more often a spread of broken pottery, the remains of cooking and craft work, and floor surfaces rebuilt repeatedly as households changed. The story is not simply about a port receiving goods from far away. It is about the people who constructed a place where fishing, farming, manufacturing, and ocean travel met.

Unguja Ukuu occupies a peninsula on the southwest side of Unguja, one of the islands of the Zanzibar archipelago. It offered access to sheltered waters, a coastline with mangroves and shallow approaches, and routes inland across an inhabited island. The earliest substantial occupation is dated to roughly the sixth or seventh century of the Common Era, with stronger confirmed sequences from the seventh century. The settlement became a major place of exchange well before the better-known coral-stone architecture of later Swahili towns. Calling it an early port does not mean that its inhabitants lived exclusively by trade. The boats used by visitors entered a landscape already requiring local knowledge, food production, water, building materials, and work.

A local scholar is central to the modern recovery of this history. Archaeologist Abdurahman Juma's research at Unguja Ukuu, published in 2004, brought together excavation, surface study, and a large program of coring to investigate the extent and formation of the settlement. Cores are narrow samples taken through layers of accumulated soil and debris. They can tell us where occupation deposits occur, how thick they are, and how they differ across a site. They do not give us a census of a neighborhood. What Juma helped establish was the scale of an early African settlement that had been neglected in narratives built around the standing monuments of later centuries.

Excavators found the kinds of remains that make a settlement intelligible as a place where people lived. There were local pots for storage and cooking, broken imported vessels, iron-working evidence, bead-making equipment, and middens containing the leftovers of meals. A midden is not just a rubbish heap in the casual sense: when excavated carefully, it can preserve a record of decisions made over many years. A discarded fish bone helps reveal diet and fishing practices. Burnt clay may indicate a wall, oven, or another structure. Pieces of imported ceramics may reveal the reach of commercial connections. No single object can explain the society, but the assemblage can be compared with other households and other places.

In 2017, Tom Fitton and Stephanie Wynne-Jones published a study using magnetometry and test excavations to examine the settlement's layout. Magnetometry measures disturbances in the local magnetic field, some caused by past burning, buried walls, pits, or occupation. It is a way to investigate land without excavating every square metre. Their work suggested a larger and more actively used settlement than a map drawn only around visible ruins might imply, including evidence for activity near the waterfront. It also identified a structure interpreted as a probable early mosque. Probable is important here. A geophysical pattern and a limited trench have to be interpreted; neither is a direct transcript of what people believed or did inside the building.

Other work gives us a much closer timescale. In a 2021 study, Stephanie Wynne-Jones, Federica Sulas, Welmoed Out, Søren Kristiansen, Tom Fitton, Abdallah Ali, and Jesper Olsen studied the sequence of a particular house. By combining stratigraphy and scientific dating, they distinguished two occupation phases, each shorter than forty years. Imagine what that resolution changes. Instead of saying that a site existed for three hundred years, we can begin asking whether one family repaired a floor, whether a structure was replaced within living memory, and whether occupation came in episodes rather than expanding smoothly. These are questions, not biographies recovered from the soil. But they restore generations to a landscape often reduced to centuries.

A nearby comparison makes the limits of a port-centered history even clearer. At Fukuchani, on northwestern Unguja, archaeologists investigated a much smaller early coastal settlement. Faunal specialists compared animal remains at Fukuchani with those at Unguja Ukuu. Both communities exploited near-shore marine resources, yet the balance of other foods differed. At Fukuchani, hunting and use of wild terrestrial animals were conspicuous; at Unguja Ukuu, domesticated sheep and goats contributed more heavily. The analysis also identified early occurrences of introduced animals including chicken, black rat, and domestic cat. Such animals illuminate movement and changing foodways, but their presence does not mean that every household participated in exactly the same kind of exchange.

Both places also depended upon crops and land-based production. Research at early Zanzibar settlements points to African grains such as pearl millet and sorghum, with diets combining cultivated plants, fish, livestock, and collected or hunted foods. Their relative importance changed across sites and time. When we call an island town maritime, it is easy to imagine an exclusively seafaring population. The evidence instead suggests an economy organized around overlapping kinds of work. Farmers supplied food. Fishers knew seasonal opportunities on the reef and shore. Potters and metalworkers made objects that could circulate locally or more widely. A trader's voyage required all of those activities, even when no text named the people doing them.

The imported materials deserve attention without taking over the story. Persian Gulf ceramics, glass, glass beads, and objects from other Indian Ocean networks reached Unguja Ukuu. Their presence is strong evidence for exchange; it does not prove that the pots were carried directly from their production sites, nor that a foreign merchant ruled the settlement. In fact, the local objects, houses, foods, and craft installations are indispensable to understanding how imported objects acquired value. A vessel that arrived by sea might have become part of a household's storage routine, a display object, a container for another commodity, or eventually a shard in a refuse deposit. We should ask how coastal families used imports, not simply where those imports originated.

Islamic history is present here, but the archaeological clocks need care. Some scholars interpret early structures at Unguja Ukuu as mosques, and the port's contacts with Muslim traders were extensive. Yet a religious structure must be identified through layout, construction, dating, and comparison, not through a desire to find the earliest possible conversion. Even if a building served Muslim worshippers, that does not establish the religious commitments of every resident. Nor does an Islamic-period glazed jar identify its owner as Muslim. The evidence allows us to study the emergence of practices and connections; it rarely allows us to place a date on an individual's change of belief.

Research published by Fitton and colleagues in 2022 assembled old site records, satellite and drone imagery, ground survey, environmental observations, and excavations in a geographic information system. That may sound like an entirely technical exercise, but it addresses a historical problem: the landscape we see now is not exactly the one early residents used. A shoreline changes. Mangroves shift. Construction and erosion expose some deposits and cover others. A satellite image tells us much about today's terrain, but may mislead if treated as a photograph of the seventh century. Combining methods makes the interpretation more defensible while also revealing where gaps remain.

Around the end of the first millennium, Unguja Ukuu's central occupation declined or changed; later activity makes its history more complicated than a simple abandonment date. Across the islands, other settlements gained prominence, some with coral-stone architecture and more visible religious monuments in the following centuries. We cannot assume that a named climate event, a new dynasty, or a sudden religious transformation explains every change. Houses may move, communities may reorganize, and trading partnerships may shift without leaving a written announcement. Archaeologists have to keep several possible causes in view until the site evidence discriminates between them.

The lesson from Unguja Ukuu and Fukuchani is not that every village was secretly a city. It is that categories such as port, village, and town can conceal the activities that connected them. A settlement with earth-built houses might participate in wide exchanges, while a small neighbor could maintain a different balance of food, labor, and social relationships. At this point in East African history, a household floor, the remains of a meal, and a local pot are as important to our understanding as a foreign coin. The coast was being made by people who lived on it, not only by ships that arrived there.

## Trade without a Stone Palace: Tumbe, Kimimba, and the Villages of Pemba

North of Unguja, Pemba offers another early island settlement whose trading connections did not depend on a stone palace.

A pot made in the Persian Gulf breaks on the floor of a house on Pemba Island. Its fragments remain there while the household is rebuilt, the walls decay, and generations of other people live nearby. Centuries later, excavators recognize the imported vessel among local cooking pots, beads, iron objects, and tools for making ornaments from shells. This is the kind of evidence recovered at Tumbe. We might be tempted to call the place a merchant city, then search for the palace, ruling family, and great mosque that we expect an important port to possess. Archaeologists Jeffrey Fleisher and Adria LaViolette ask us to resist that expectation. Tumbe was an influential trading settlement, but its visible social arrangements were rooted in village households rather than a recognizable hierarchy of monumental urban rulers.

Pemba lies north of Unguja in the Zanzibar archipelago, off the coast of what is now Tanzania. Its landscapes connect sheltered landing places, cultivation, fishing, and interior villages. Histories written from the perspective of much later periods sometimes call Pemba a rural supplier for larger ports. That label reflects, in part, its history after 1500 and especially the commercial plantation economy of the nineteenth century. It becomes misleading when read backward into the eighth or ninth. LaViolette and Fleisher's research shows that Pemba had its own important settlements, regional political relationships, and centers of religious and commercial investment long before the plantations for which it later became known.

Tumbe flourished approximately between the seventh and tenth centuries. Surface materials and excavations indicate an extensive settlement, at times estimated at roughly twenty-five hectares. Such a measurement requires caution. The area over which artifacts are scattered may record repeated construction across generations, and need not mean that every hectare was densely inhabited on the same afternoon. A timber-and-earth house might be rebuilt on a nearby patch of land, creating a wider zone of debris over centuries. That uncertainty should alter how we imagine Tumbe's population. We can still recognize a large and enduring settlement without converting the footprint of the entire site into a precise census.

In houses at Tumbe, imported goods were not confined to an unmistakable palace or specialized market quarter. Excavations encountered ceramics from outside East Africa alongside local vessels and domestic objects. Trade had entered the routines of household life. A glazed storage vessel might hold something useful after its arrival; pieces of glass, metal, and beads might be kept, exchanged, repaired, or discarded among objects made nearby. We do not know the names of those who owned them, and we should not divide the residents automatically into foreign merchants and African laborers. The house, not the dramatic long-distance origin of the sherd, is our starting place for social history.

One especially revealing class of finds consists of bead-grinding tools. Workers could use grooves cut into reused pieces of pottery or stone to shape small shell beads. Archaeologists have identified concentrations of these objects at Tumbe and other early coastal sites. James Flexner, Jeffrey Fleisher, and Adria LaViolette studied their variability and argued for production organized in households, while recognizing that the exact use of the grooved objects has been debated. It is plausible that some finished beads moved into exchange beyond Pemba, yet it is harder to reconstruct their destinations and prices. An object made for exchange need not be made in a large separate workshop. Production can be part of a household economy, taking place in the same compound as cooking, storage, and other work.

Research on plant remains adds another layer. At Tumbe and nearby settlements, archaeobotanical evidence identifies pearl millet among cultivated food plants. Plant remains survive unevenly; grains that were burned sometimes last longer in deposits than those that were eaten or decomposed. Nor can we infer how many hours one person spent farming from a few charred seeds. But their presence anchors the trading village in food production and in the skills required to cultivate crops on an island. When we describe Tumbe as prosperous because of Indian Ocean imports, we should remember that its sustained occupation also depended on people obtaining food season after season.

The archaeological contrast with nearby Kimimba is instructive. Work presented by Fleisher and LaViolette in their 2018 study of coastal rural life includes Kimimba, a smaller first-millennium village, alongside Tumbe. Their point is not merely that a rich site had poor neighbors. The material record shows more than one kind of village life within the same regional world. Some communities invested more heavily in imported exchange or specialized manufacture than others. A difference in the proportion of imported pots can reflect location, household choice, trade access, occupation date, or the sampling method. It is a clue to investigate, not permission to rank every village in an imagined ladder of civilization.

Why, then, do researchers call Tumbe a village if it was large and commercially connected? The word does not deny complexity. Fleisher and LaViolette's 2013 study argues that the social effects of exchange did not inevitably include strong material signs of social stratification. Trade occurred in domestic settings and apparently did not force a transition to the type of urbanism familiar from later stone towns. This is an argument from excavated contexts, not a claim that everyone possessed equal wealth or authority. Unequal relations could exist in forms that leave little archaeological trace. What the excavators found does not justify assuming a palatial government simply because foreign goods were present.

Pemba's history changed after around the year 1000. A regional survey by Fleisher identified substantial reorganization of settlement in northern Pemba during the eleventh century. Areas that had supported multiple rural settlements became less densely occupied as new towns grew, sometimes around important coral-built mosques. He called attention to a process known as synoecism: bringing people from several smaller places together into a new civic community. It is tempting to picture one ruler ordering everyone to move. The term does not require that scenario, and the archaeological evidence does not document each household's decision. It points instead to a major change in where people lived and how settlements related to one another.

The changing relationship between Tumbe and Chwaka makes this problem concrete. Chwaka, an important settlement of the later period, developed near the earlier village. It had a different architectural and religious setting, including more monumental buildings. In LaViolette and Fleisher's account, the two sites belong to a regional history of transformation rather than a simple tale in which a primitive village became a sophisticated stone city by following a fixed developmental plan. Tumbe's earlier commercial success had its own logic. The later town organized space, collective identity, and religious investment in new ways. A local change of settlement could involve continuity in families and practices even when the most visible buildings changed.

The role of Islam is another question best studied at this local scale. Later towns built coral mosques that were highly visible signs of collective investment. Their appearance cannot be read backward to prove that every household in an earlier village belonged to the same religious community. Nor does the absence of such a mosque mean that Muslim practices were impossible. The spatial distribution, date, and construction sequence of religious buildings provide evidence for changes in public life, while burials and other practices may help where they survive. Religious connections across the sea mattered, but they were incorporated into settlements with histories of their own.

After towns had grown, the countryside did not disappear from the story. Fleisher and LaViolette also investigated Kaliwa, a later village associated with the urban world of Chwaka. Its inclusion matters because a stone town and nearby rural settlements were not opposite stages in a single line of progress. The residents of villages could produce goods, provide food, sustain family relationships, move into towns, or remain in rural places while urban public spaces changed. A map labeling only surviving mosques and coral houses will systematically hide those contributions. Archaeological survey of the fields and smaller settlements around a town is necessary if we want to study the town's real social geography.

There is a danger in every attractive archaeological story. When we find imports, we call a place cosmopolitan. When we find large buildings, we call it political. When neither survives, we call its inhabitants ordinary and move on. Tumbe makes that hierarchy hard to defend. Households could be manufacturing goods, using imported objects, managing food supply, and participating in a far-reaching network without creating architecture that later travelers would praise. The important historical question is not how quickly every village learned to resemble a stone town. It is how different communities chose to organize labor, belonging, and exchange under changing conditions.

We can leave Pemba by remembering a floor rather than a monument. On that floor were the remnants of everyday activities and goods that had traveled across water. They reveal people living at once in several worlds: the island's fields and fisheries, networks of neighbors and relatives, and maritime routes extending beyond the horizon. Tumbe was not important only because it foreshadowed Chwaka. It was important because a village could be a durable center of connection on its own terms, and because the movements that later created towns depended on the social histories of villages around them.

## Pots across Rivers and Coasts: The Early Tana Tradition and Africa's Hinterland

Turning toward pottery distributions widens the view from particular island settlements to connections among coasts, rivers, and inland communities.

A piece of broken earthenware may not look like much of a historical witness. It has no inscription naming a king, and no ship is drawn on its surface to show how far its owner traveled. Yet pots made and used in East African communities during the seventh, eighth, and ninth centuries have transformed our understanding of the coast. Similar kinds of locally produced pottery occur in settlements by the ocean, on islands, and far inland. They make it possible to write a history of relationships among African communities that does not begin with visiting merchants or the ruins of stone-built towns. The question is not simply where one decorative style originated, but what shared and changing ways of making and using pots reveal about people.

Archaeologists often call this material the Early Tana Tradition, or ETT. Another name, Triangular-Incised Ware, is sometimes shortened to TIW. Its distinctive vessels include necked jars decorated with incised geometric patterns, along with other kinds of jars and bowls. The naming history itself matters. David Phillipson's 1979 report described Iron Age sites in the lower Tana River valley of Kenya and referred to pottery from places such as Wenje. A name derived from one region became a way to compare material found over a much wider geography. But the name does not establish that a single people moved outward from the Tana River and founded every settlement where comparable pots later appeared.

The chronological frame is roughly six hundred to nine hundred or one thousand of the Common Era, depending on the assemblage and site. Sites occur along the Kenyan and Tanzanian coasts, in the Zanzibar archipelago, on the southern Mozambican coast, and at inland locations. The breadth of this distribution was consequential. It challenged older accounts that saw coastal urban culture chiefly as something created by outsiders who arrived with Islam or imported pottery. Locally made jars linked the coast to African communities with their own technologies and histories. Yet the same evidence can be overstated if we assume that everyone using these vessels spoke the same language or obeyed the same political authority.

How do researchers recognize the tradition? It is not enough to select a handsome jar decorated with a triangle. They compare the vessel's rim, neck, base, wall thickness, fabric, firing, finish, and other characteristics, along with the decoration. A jar with a similar painted or incised motif may have been made differently. Some shapes are better suited to certain forms of cooking, carrying, or storage, although the precise contents are not always recoverable. A ceramic tradition is an archaeological classification based on a recurring combination of features. It is not a certificate of ethnic identity written by the person who formed the clay.

In 2011, Jeffrey Fleisher and Stephanie Wynne-Jones published a study revisiting Early Tana Tradition collections using common analytical criteria. Earlier excavations had been conducted at different times with different recording systems and research questions. That makes comparison difficult: a difference between published site inventories may reflect the methods of the excavators rather than a difference in people's past practices. The researchers found both broad similarities and meaningful regional variation in vessel forms and decoration. Those differences push us to ask how practices spread, changed, and were selected. They complicate the idea of one identical pottery culture covering the entire eastern African seaboard.

The same scholars helped make those comparisons auditable. Their Ceramics and Society project, published as a data paper in 2013, documented more than two thousand sherds with a common record of forty variables per sherd. Its eight-site dataset includes Ungwana and Manda in the northern Kenyan region; Bandarikuu, Tumbe, and Kimimba on Pemba; Unguja Ukuu on Zanzibar; Dakawa in the Tanzanian coastal hinterland; and Chibuene in Mozambique. This does not constitute an exhaustive inventory of all early settlements. The value lies in treating observations from distant sites in a comparable way, allowing another scholar to inspect the classifications rather than accepting a map of distribution on authority alone.

Notice how much of that list lies outside the picture of monumental Swahili cities. Dakawa matters because it is in the mainland hinterland rather than an island port. Kimimba matters because it was a village within the same regional world as the larger trading settlement of Tumbe. The pottery links these communities through practices of production and use even when their households had different access to imported goods. We cannot prove from a shared jar shape that a family in Dakawa routinely visited Pemba. But neither should we imagine the mainland as a passive supply zone waiting to be drawn into coastal history. The material distribution is a reason to investigate multiple lines of inland connection.

A coast is not an edge beyond which historical events cease. Rivers, tracks, fields, forest margins, and seasonal movements can carry goods and knowledge between settlements. Pots may travel with their makers, with families who move, with neighboring buyers, or with visitors carrying food in familiar containers. Techniques can also be learned from others and reproduced locally. Researchers can sometimes distinguish these possibilities by investigating clay recipes, production debris, and the distribution of particular forms. But a visual similarity in pots alone cannot identify a trade route, marriage arrangement, or population migration. Such interpretations need independently dated archaeological contexts and, where available, other evidence.

The label Early Tana also needs to be kept apart from later coastal identities. Kiswahili belongs to the Bantu language family, and linguistic history offers evidence of African continuities and changing contacts. Archaeological pottery is not speech, however. We cannot hear the language of an excavated pot's maker, nor tell whether the pot belonged to a person who called themselves Swahili. The term Swahili itself has a history. It can describe later language communities and coastal social worlds, but projecting its full later meaning unchanged into the seventh century would erase changes that are precisely what we want to understand.

This matters particularly when imported objects appear in the same layers. Early settlements yielded Persian Gulf ceramics, glass beads, and other goods from overseas networks. A blue-green glazed vessel is valuable evidence that something traveled. The local cooking jar beside it offers different evidence: the routines by which households consumed, stored, and prepared food, and the skills shared among nearby communities. Neither object by itself proves a religious identity. Early Islamic trade in the Indian Ocean was historically important, but the presence of imported ceramics from an Islamic-period production area is not proof that a village was governed by Muslims or that all its inhabitants had converted.

Archaeologists also have to work with older and younger pottery traditions. Earlier Iron Age ceramic assemblages occur along parts of the eastern African coast and its hinterland. Later, regional forms developed as towns grew and settlement patterns changed after about the year 1000. Rather than asking where the Early Tana people vanished, we can ask which techniques and practices continued, which stopped, and how the contexts of production changed. Similar decoration may endure when a settlement shifts location; a new jar form may appear without a wholesale migration. A chronological label can help us compare sequences but should not become a fictional account of a people appearing and disappearing.

There is a further problem in the history of the archaeological discipline. Researchers once concentrated more heavily on sites with visible stone buildings and rich imported artifacts. Small villages with earthen houses were easier to miss, harder to date, and less likely to be funded for excavation. As survey widened and pottery studies became more systematic, those villages began to change the map. In a 2023 reflection on the foundational pottery discoveries, Wynne-Jones returned to Phillipson's report from the lower Tana valley. The point of returning to a short field report is that an apparently modest observation can redirect an entire historical question: not who brought a civilization to the coast, but how long-standing African connections shaped its development.

We should be cautious about drawing lines between every archaeological dot. A map of similar pots is not necessarily a map of direct friendships, caravan routes, or political alliances. The distances are real, but the connections may have been indirect and assembled over generations. Equally, the failure to find a particular pot at a poorly investigated site is not proof that its residents were isolated. Different soils, later disturbance, and uneven excavation all affect what survives. The strongest reconstruction uses pottery alongside settlement layouts, dates, food remains, craft installations, and evidence for movement across land and sea.

Return to the sherd. It may have been made by hand from local clay, used for years, repaired or broken, discarded, and finally recovered by someone who could compare it with thousands of others. From that small object we cannot extract its maker's name. But a series of such objects, carefully dated and recorded, can reveal a shared material history extending far beyond any port. The Early Tana Tradition shows that early East African coastal connections cannot be separated from the African mainland. It also reminds us that comparison is most powerful when it preserves differences, rather than flattening many communities into one label.

## Who Founded the Coast? Shanga, Mijikenda Histories, and Disputed Origins

Farther north along the coast, Shanga lets us compare excavated settlement sequences with much later narratives of belonging.

On Pate Island in the Lamu archipelago of present-day Kenya, archaeologists excavated the buried settlement of Shanga. The early houses were not the grand stone buildings that later travelers associated with the Swahili coast. They were made largely of earth and timber. In the settlement's central spaces, investigators found traces of fences, posts, communal structures, and religious buildings that changed over centuries. The chronology begins in roughly the eighth century and stretches toward the fifteenth. Shanga gives us evidence that people were organizing a complex coastal community long before the surviving stone monuments became its most obvious feature. But this account has a second subject: the historical knowledge of communities on the coast's inland escarpment, whose voices have too often been kept separate from the archaeological story of the shore.

The excavation of Shanga is especially associated with archaeologist Mark Horton. His work reconstructs a sequence from early timber-and-earth occupation through successive rebuilding and, eventually, the use of coral in substantial structures. Some houses were circular, others rectangular. Their changes reflect choices about materials, form, and the organization of space rather than a sudden replacement of African building traditions by foreign ones. A stone wall may survive far longer than the mud-and-thatch structure it replaced. If we judge the town only by what stands today, we will mistake the later architecture for the beginning of the community's history.

The identification of early mosques has prompted debate. Horton proposed that some of the central buildings at Shanga had served Islamic worshippers by the late eighth or early ninth century. His interpretations draw on excavation plans, construction sequences, radiocarbon dating, and comparison with mosque forms elsewhere. Other scholars have questioned particular identifications, especially early buildings whose orientation and architectural features are not decisive. This is a question about how strongly the evidence identifies a structure's use, not a reason to disregard the coastal history of Islam. Securely recognized later mosques establish a long local history of Muslim institutions, while uncertain earliest phases should remain uncertain.

A mosque is not merely an object on an architectural plan. It is a place where people gather, and its rebuilding may indicate organized labor, ritual practice, and relationships extending beyond one household. Yet the physical remains do not tell us the names of everyone who prayed, the content of their conversations, or whether neighbors all agreed about religion. Islamic institutions could develop amid other practices and within preexisting social frameworks. We have to avoid imagining a clean line in the ground separating Muslim newcomers on one side and African residents on the other. The archaeology of Shanga makes better sense as the history of a changing community in which different practices were negotiated over time.

Horton also interpreted early arrangements of buildings and enclosed spaces through comparison with the settlement patterns of Mijikenda communities inland from the Kenyan coast. In some Mijikenda traditions, a kaya is a defended settlement and an ancestral place, and makaya is the plural. The comparison suggested ways that neighborhoods, ritual spaces, or clans might have been organized at Shanga. An analogy can be historically useful, especially where no contemporary written account explains what a fence enclosed. But an analogy is not direct identification: people separated in time and place may construct similar spaces for different reasons. Later Mijikenda institutions cannot simply be pasted into an eighth-century coastal plan.

The Mijikenda are communities speaking related Bantu languages in coastal Kenya, with rich traditions concerning kinship, settlement, authority, and sacred forests. Today, several kaya forest sites are recognized as heritage places protected and maintained through living community practice. UNESCO's description emphasizes the significance of the kayas as ancestral landscapes and the custodial work of elders. Much of the fortified-kaya history documented in these heritage records is placed around and after the sixteenth century. That chronological fact matters here. It would be a mistake to call every earlier coastal enclosure a kaya, or to claim that a sixteenth-century village is itself a preserved map of a settlement from the eighth century.

One influential group of Mijikenda oral traditions concerns a place often called Shungwaya or Singwaya. Many narrators described ancestors leaving an earlier homeland to the north and establishing settlements farther south. For decades, scholars tried to turn these stories into a precise migration itinerary.  Historian Thomas Spear recorded interviews with Mijikenda elders in 1971 and argued for a historical migration framework, while other scholars challenged his chronology and interpretations. These debates were partly about methods: what kind of evidence is an oral tradition, and what should happen when linguistic or archaeological evidence suggests a different timescale?

Later archaeological research changed the question. Richard Helm's surveys of settlement sites on Kenya's coastal escarpment showed occupation much older than the version of the migration story that placed the founding of all these communities around the sixteenth century. Evidence for local communities extended back many centuries, with changing settlement densities and relationships to the coast. This undermines a simple account in which the escarpment sat largely empty until migrants arrived as one group. It does not prove that no migration occurred, and it does not make oral traditions worthless. It means that a tradition about ancestry and belonging should not automatically be read as a date-stamped report of the first people to live in a district.

Historian Daren E. Ray revisited the controversy in an article published in 2022. He argued that older work often extracted a supposed factual core from oral testimony while treating narrators' interpretations as noise. That approach could leave the person telling the history almost invisible. Ray instead drew attention to one named speaker recorded by Spear: Thomas Govi, a Chonyi elder whose testimony explains the role of knowledge and ritual capabilities in relationships among clans. Govi was not merely a witness to be checked against an archaeological chart. He was a historian making an argument about how communities depended on one another.

Govi spoke about uganga, a term that might be rendered simply as medicine but which, in the relevant accounts, encompassed a much wider set of capabilities. It could include healing, making peace, dealing with conflict, working iron, or maintaining ritual relationships. Different clans held different forms of knowledge and were bound together partly through the need to draw on one another's skills. Ray treats this account as a way to rethink how groups formed alliances and incorporated newcomers. We must distinguish that modern historian's interpretation from a sentence dictated by Govi about a specific event eight hundred years earlier. What matters is that an African thinker supplied a theory of collective life, not just a location on a migration map.

This approach also changes the way we ask about Islam along the coast. The presence of mosques and relations with Muslim travelers may be studied alongside local systems for distributing knowledge and authority. Ray suggests that Islamic ritual knowledge could be incorporated through relations among groups rather than replacing every older institution at once. This is an argument built from a later archive and comparison; it is not direct documentary proof of the particular choices made at Shanga in the ninth century. It reminds us to ask who controlled religious instruction, how relationships were made, and how practices could circulate between communities whose histories were intertwined.

The history of the archive is part of the story too. Spear's collections recorded many oral accounts, but publication in English translation tended to standardize varied expressions. Recordings in local languages have not always been as accessible as the printed interpretation. Later scholars and community members could encounter a published account of Shungwaya and retell a version already shaped by academic debate. What looks like independent confirmation may therefore include the influence of earlier books. None of these difficulties licenses us to dismiss speakers as confused. They call for careful attention to who spoke, when, in what language, to whom, and for what historical or political purpose.

Even the categories Swahili and Mijikenda need histories of their own. They are meaningful names in today's landscape, but their political and social boundaries were not fixed for fifteen hundred years. Languages, marriage relationships, religious affiliations, and settlement choices could overlap or change. If we tell a story in which the shore belongs to merchants and the inland hills belong to separate timeless clans, we obscure relationships that archaeological and historical research has begun to recover. Shanga's building sequence and the escarpment's older settlements should not be read as identical histories, but they do belong in the same regional conversation.

Two kinds of caution now reinforce one another. An excavated wall cannot tell us the complete political meaning of a gathering space, while a later oral narrative cannot give us the literal floor plan of an eighth-century house. Yet each can enlarge the questions we bring to the other. Archaeologists can examine whether settlement patterns really fit an account of abrupt migration. Oral historians and living custodians of sacred places can help us understand why places, skills, and ancestral claims matter. Scholarly interpretations of that conversation must acknowledge the people whose historical reasoning supplied their starting points.

Shanga was a place built and rebuilt by local residents across generations. Mijikenda historical traditions preserve thought about ancestral settlement, practical knowledge, and belonging, even though their records do not give an exact founding chronology for Shanga. Bringing the two into the same history does not require forcing them to agree. It requires replacing the old search for a single foreign founder or a single migration event with a more difficult and more human question: how did communities on the East African shore and its hinterland build institutions, exchange knowledge, make religious choices, and remember the places they called home?

## Beyond the Beach Market: Chibuene and the Routes into Southern Africa

The route now turns south toward Chibuene and the inland paths beyond it. Objects that reached an inland settlement did not necessarily travel directly from one port.

Imagine finding a piece of blue-green glazed pottery beside a river hundreds of kilometers from the Indian Ocean. The vessel had been produced far away, probably in the Persian Gulf region. Its fragments were found in a settlement that was not a seaport and did not belong to a documented Muslim empire. Who carried it there, and along which roads or waterways? That question takes us to Chibuene, a coastal settlement in what is now southern Mozambique, and to the communities of the southern African interior. It also changes how we understand early Indian Ocean commerce. Ships mattered, but goods did not stop moving when they reached land.

Chibuene lies near today's Vilanculos, on the southern Mozambican coast. It occupied a shoreline connected to nearby lakes, fields, and settlements farther inland. Archaeological finds include local pottery, glass beads, imported glazed ceramics, and the remains of ordinary domestic life. The site was important in exchange networks around the eighth, ninth, and tenth centuries, although the wider occupation history extends across other periods as well. It is tempting to treat such a place as a minor version of a northern Swahili town. That would miss its southern setting, the people who sustained its economy, and the routes that connected it to African communities far beyond the shore.

In research published in 2012, Paul Sinclair, Anneli Ekblom, and Marilee Wood examined Chibuene's place in these networks. Their archaeological argument links a coastal community and imported beads to inland centers such as Schroda in the Limpopo region. The evidence is not a series of merchant receipts naming every exchange. Rather, it comes from dated deposits and the comparison of materials found at different places. Beads manufactured somewhere around the Indian Ocean could travel to the southern African interior through multiple hands. Their movement also meant choices by local traders, households, and leaders. A connection between sites does not automatically tell us that the same political authority governed them.

The geographical relationship requires more attention than a single arrow on a map. Travel from a sheltered landing place toward inland settlements meant moving through changing rainfall zones, river valleys, and communities with their own claims to land and resources. Goods might be exchanged several times before reaching their eventual users. We can propose networks from the distribution of objects, but archaeology seldom preserves the exact list of people who carried them. A foreign-made bead found inland does not establish a direct visit by a Persian or Arabian sailor. The labor of inland Africans, and the social trust or political pressure that enabled movement, belongs at the center of the story.

The exchange was not one-way. Coastal residents needed food, materials, and regional partnerships, while inland communities had their own reasons for acquiring beads, ceramics, or other objects. Later written records mention commodities such as ivory, animal products, and enslaved people in wider Indian Ocean trade. They cannot be used as a precise inventory for every transaction at Chibuene in the ninth century. What the archaeological evidence demonstrates more securely is movement of imported materials and the significance of networks through which they traveled. We should treat the nature, scale, and coercion of individual exchanges as questions requiring closer evidence, not fill the gaps from later commercial histories.

Anneli Ekblom has asked a different question about Chibuene: how did residents maintain their livelihoods in an environment where rainfall and resource availability could vary? Her 2012 study combines archaeology with environmental data, documentary material, and interviews concerning more recent local lives. It emphasizes the importance of diversified resources and social relationships in managing insecurity. A community might depend on cultivation, domestic animals, fishing, and access to relatives or partners in other places, with trade providing another means of obtaining what was needed. The argument helps us see long-distance exchange as part of local livelihood strategies rather than a separate commercial sphere reserved for a merchant class.

These forms of evidence do not all belong to one time. A contemporary interview can explain how living people think about risk or exchange, and environmental records can show broad patterns of vegetation or moisture. Neither lets us reconstruct without qualification the mind of a ninth-century farmer. Archaeological data may support continuities across time, but the specific institutions through which people shared food or negotiated assistance can change greatly. Ekblom's framework is helpful precisely when it encourages questions about resilience without pretending that past and present household arrangements are identical.

Chibuene is also a reminder that imported materials acquire meaning in African settings. Glass beads could be worn, exchanged, displayed, or stored, but the social significance of a particular bead is not written on its surface. A rich concentration of imported goods may correlate with a place of authority, or with production and commerce, or with an archaeological deposit accumulated for other reasons. To assess claims about emerging hierarchy, researchers compare the distribution of goods with settlement size, burials, buildings, and local industries. They do not infer the existence of a ruling dynasty solely from the distance a bead traveled.

An exciting new line of evidence came from excavations far inland in the Letaba region of northeastern South Africa. In a study published in 2025, Alexander Antonites, Annie Antonites, Christian Peterson, Tyron Hopf, and Ndukuyakhe Ndlovu reported fragments of imported glazed pottery and Asian glass beads at an early settlement designated Le6. Archaeologists recovered fourteen sherds consistent with turquoise alkaline-glazed wares of Persian Gulf manufacture. The ceramic fragments came from local archaeological deposits, not a medieval written account. Their presence demonstrates that the commercial horizons of the Indian Ocean reached communities hundreds of kilometers from the coast by the tenth century.

The chronology was investigated rather than simply guessed from the pots' appearance. Organic samples from a midden associated with the pottery were radiocarbon dated, producing a combined date range from the late ninth to the tenth century. The researchers emphasized the need to compare the form and manufacture of the imported ware with its dated archaeological context. They also noted that multiple fragments do not necessarily represent multiple complete pots; broken pieces from one vessel could be scattered through a deposit. The strength of the discovery lies in its association of distinctive imported material with a securely investigated inland settlement, not in an inflated count of trading ships or luxury goods.

The Letaba finds also complicate an older map of southern African commerce. Chibuene is a plausible point from which goods could move inland, and links to the Limpopo and other interior regions have long been proposed. But the 2025 authors argued that some material distributions might fit routes following the Limpopo River and its tributaries from farther south, perhaps using an entry point near present-day Xai-Xai. This is a route hypothesis, not a discovered road paved from coast to site. A ceramic assemblage can tell us that imported objects arrived. It does not automatically specify the port where a boat landed or the path taken by a carrier. More survey and excavation will be needed to decide among competing routes.

Why should a route dispute matter to a history listener? Because a simplified map can quietly assign power. If we draw every arrow from one famous port, we make that port appear to control the whole exchange system. If several African communities could choose among different paths, then the politics of transport, alliance, and access look different. Control of a beach market might bring advantages, but it would not guarantee control of every exchange inland. River corridors, local expertise, and intermediary settlements could shape the network. That gives inland communities an active historical role rather than making them distant customers waiting at the end of a maritime trade line.

The archaeological record also warns against using later monuments to explain the early period. Great Zimbabwe and Mapungubwe became important in different phases of the second millennium. Their histories are essential to southern Africa, but their monumental centers cannot be projected backward intact into the ninth century. Earlier sites like Schroda and Letaba allow researchers to examine the connections and social changes preceding those better-known developments. It is reasonable to ask whether trade contributed to later political transformations. It would be much less defensible to claim that a few imported sherds caused the rise of a particular kingdom hundreds of years later.

In the late twentieth century, Mozambique submitted Chibuene and the nearby site of Manyikeni to UNESCO's tentative list for possible World Heritage recognition. This is a useful heritage description of the surviving archaeology and the perceived importance of coastal-interior relationships, but a tentative listing is not the same thing as an inscription on the World Heritage List. Manyikeni also belongs to a later phase of southern African history, so its stone enclosure should not be mistaken for a monument already standing in Chibuene's earliest trading centuries. Heritage documents can help locate sites and summarize finds while still needing careful attention to their dates and purpose.

Think again about the imported vessel beside a river. It does not tell us its owner's language, religion, or the number of people who touched it. But it does disprove a picture in which the sea was the only active part of Indian Ocean exchange. Chibuene, inland centers, and the Letaba community participated in networks formed through African decisions, skills, and relationships. Some routes remain hypothetical, and the social consequences varied. The essential historical shift is to stop treating the coastline as the end of the story. The ocean opened connections, but people on land made those connections reach farther.
