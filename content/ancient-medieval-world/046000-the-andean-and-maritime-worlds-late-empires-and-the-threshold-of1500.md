# The Andean and maritime worlds, late empires, and the threshold of1500

By the threshold of 1500, several powerful states and trading worlds were changing at once. We begin in the Andes, then cross to maritime Asia and the eastern Mediterranean before considering Europe and the wider world. Crossing those distances will help us resist treating one region as the clock for all the others.

## Roads before the Empire: Andean States and the Rise of the Inca

A road crosses an Andean mountain slope. It climbs from one ecological zone toward another, sometimes near steep terrain where transport is difficult. When the Inca state expanded in the fifteenth century, it maintained and enlarged a remarkable network of such routes. Yet not every segment began with the Inca. People in the Andes had built paths, cultivated terraces, managed water, moved herds, and linked communities for centuries. To begin the story with an emperor ordering a road would mistake the last major extension of a system for its entire history. The political world of the Andes before 1500 contains older states, regional loyalties, and labor arrangements on which imperial expansion depended.

Andean geography encouraged diverse forms of production. Coastal valleys supported irrigated agriculture amid desert terrain. Highland communities used lands at different elevations for crops and pastoralism. Llamas and alpacas could supply wool, transport, and other resources, while fishing and coastal trade were important in different areas. Communities did not all solve environmental challenges in the same way. Cooperation among related households, exchange between zones, and the management of water were longstanding practices. The great states of the later Middle Ages did not invent Andean knowledge; they sought to mobilize and govern it.

On Peru's northern coast, the Chimú kingdom developed a powerful political center at Chan Chan. Its large adobe complexes, decorated walls, storage areas, and carefully organized spaces demonstrate sustained labor and planning. By the fourteenth and fifteenth centuries, Chimú power extended across several coastal valleys. Irrigation canals and agricultural lands made dense settlement possible, while workshops produced textiles, metal objects, and other goods. The surviving monumental compounds reveal political differentiation, but they do not mean everyone lived inside palace walls. Houses, farms, specialized workshops, and smaller settlements are necessary to understand the whole system.

The Chimú state was not a footnote to the later Inca. It had rulers, military expansion, skilled producers, and relationships with neighboring communities. Archaeologists document distinctive coastal styles and material connections, but some narratives of dynastic origins come through later oral or colonial transmission. We should not turn every archaeological change into the conquest of a named king. Metalworkers, builders, and agricultural workers can be visible through the products of their labor even when their personal names are lost. Chan Chan's layout suggests a highly stratified political society, but precise assignments of every compound require interpretation rather than certainty.

Farther south and in the highlands, communities such as the Wanka, Lupaca, and Qolla maintained different political structures. Religious centers including Pachacamac attracted visitors and patronage. Earlier Wari and Tiwanaku institutions had left architectural, road, and cultural legacies, yet later peoples were not simply replicas of those states. Their histories involved local alliances, rivalries, movement, and adaptation. One effect of telling Andean history only through the Inca is that the Inca's eventual subjects appear to have no history before subjection. The evidence shows the opposite: the empire grew through contact with places that already had durable institutions.

The Inca political center arose around Cusco. Later histories associate a major phase of imperial expansion with the ruler Pachacuti in the fifteenth century, conventionally beginning around 1438. These dates and individual deeds depend substantially on accounts recorded after the Spanish invasion, alongside archaeology and regional traditions. A useful chronology can be built from them, but exact narratives of each campaign and succession dispute should not be repeated without qualification. Expansion accelerated under Inca rulers during the fifteenth century, eventually creating a state known as Tawantinsuyu, or the realm of four parts. Its scale should not obscure how recently some territories had been incorporated.

Imperial government relied on relationships with local leaders, often known as kurakas. Authorities negotiated alliances, demanded labor, settled disputes, and sometimes relocated populations. Military force and threats of punishment were part of rule, but accommodation also mattered. Local communities might retain established leaders or practices while accepting demands from Cusco. That arrangement could shift if an uprising or a succession struggle changed the balance of power. The existence of an administrative system does not mean every province was governed identically or that all subjects experienced incorporation as beneficial.

The road network, known as the Qhapaq Ñan, helped integrate this varied landscape. Routes connected highland and coastal regions through bridges, stairways, rest stations, and storage facilities. Runners could carry messages, and officials and armies could move through places where rapid travel otherwise posed serious challenges. Roads also supported redistribution and communication. Their maintenance required labor from communities along the routes, while their alignments often incorporated older paths. A modern map may display a continuous network, but travelers had to deal with weather, elevation, local conditions, and the actual capacities of each section.

Labor obligation was central to imperial power. In systems often discussed under the term mit'a, communities supplied rotating labor for state projects or services. Work could involve agriculture, construction, transport, military duties, and other tasks. Imperial storage and redistribution were significant features of the system, and reciprocity could be expressed in political ideology. But labor owed to a conquering state was not identical to freely chosen cooperation. Nor should the Inca institution be confused without explanation with the much later colonial mita imposed by Spanish rulers. Similar words can describe arrangements with very different conditions and consequences.

Recording and administration did not require European-style alphabetic manuscripts. Khipus, arrangements of knotted cords, were used for recording information, often associated with quantities and administration; research into their broader functions continues. Their existence challenges the idea that societies without an alphabet had no sophisticated record-keeping. Yet the modern interpretation of particular khipus remains incomplete, and it would be equally misleading to pretend we can read each one as a fully translated narrative. Archaeological objects, colonial descriptions, and continuing Andean practices must be brought into conversation without collapsing their differences.

The empire also supported religious and ceremonial centers, integrating regional sacred places into wider institutions. State ceremonies, agriculture, relationships to ancestors, and local sacred landscapes all mattered. The Inca court promoted forms of worship associated with the Sun and royal ancestors, but regional traditions did not disappear when rulers asserted authority. Some shrines received new patronage or became part of imperial routes. The material record of temples and offerings documents practices, though personal belief is not directly recoverable from every artifact. An empire's official ideology is not an exhaustive statement of what its subjects thought.

The fifteenth-century transformation of the Andes therefore included both political concentration and continued local diversity. Coastal Chimú administration, highland community obligations, agricultural systems, and old roads became resources the expanding Inca state tried to govern. Resistance, negotiation, and continuity coexisted. After 1500 the consequences of Spanish campaigns and epidemics would alter this order, but they should not define the entire story retrospectively. Andean people had already built states and infrastructures that changed over generations. The road is evidence of their accumulated knowledge as much as of imperial power.

When we return to the mountain slope, imagine not one emperor but successive generations of travelers and workers: someone carrying provisions, someone repairing a bridge, someone recording contributions, someone moving an animal herd. The Inca imperial road gave these activities new political purposes and sometimes new constraints. Yet it inherited landscapes shaped by earlier societies. The story of the Andes before 1500 is about how a rapidly expanding state tried to govern a rich collection of existing histories, not about the sudden invention of civilization by a single dynasty.

## Ports between Monsoons: Maritime Asia before 1500

From the Andes we cross to maritime Asia, whose connections depended on monsoon routes rather than a single road system or imperial center.

Imagine a merchant waiting for the wind in a port at the western end of the Malay Peninsula. The ship cannot simply leave whenever its owner wishes. Sailing across parts of the Indian Ocean and adjoining seas requires attention to seasonal winds, currents, cargo, and supplies. While waiting, merchants might exchange goods, settle debts, learn of political disputes, or arrange another voyage. Ports such as Melaka became powerful partly because people repeatedly needed such places. By 1500, the seas linking East Africa, southern and western Asia, Southeast Asia, and China supported extensive commercial and cultural relations. No one state had invented these connections, and no single navy commanded them all.

The Indian Ocean trade system involved regional routes of different lengths. Goods could move through many transactions without a person making the entire journey from production to final use. Cloth from South Asia, ceramics from China, spices and forest products from Southeast Asia, metals, precious materials, and foodstuffs passed among different ports. Prices, payments, and legal practices varied. Monsoon patterns provided opportunities for planned sailing, but storms, piracy, warfare, and political restrictions created risk. The map of a route tells us less about commercial history than the decisions made by shipowners, crews, brokers, and harbor authorities.

In southern and western India, coastal ports connected inland production to overseas customers. Gujarat and the Malabar Coast had long-established trading communities; ports around Calicut were prominent by the late fifteenth century. Merchant networks included Muslims, Hindus, Jains, and people of other traditions. Labels of religion do not identify one universal occupation or economic interest. A ruler might protect certain merchants, regulate port dues, or compete for access to customs revenue. Local farmers and textile workers supplied much of what moved abroad. The goods visible in foreign accounts were only part of the economy that sustained a port.

Island Southeast Asia had its own centers of political authority. The kingdom of Majapahit developed in Java from the late thirteenth century and reached considerable prominence during the fourteenth. Court texts celebrated its rulers and relationships with places beyond Java. One work, often called the Nagarakretagama, survives as a literary and political source associated with the fourteenth-century court. Its claims about dependent territories cannot be translated directly into the borders of a modern nation-state. Tribute, maritime alliances, episodic military expeditions, and symbolic claims could overlap. The power of an island court was substantial but never identical to uninterrupted day-to-day control over every named coast.

On the Southeast Asian mainland, states including Ayutthaya in the Chao Phraya basin and the changing Khmer-centered polities near Angkor followed different paths. Religious patronage, trade, warfare, and regional settlement linked inland centers to ports. Khmer political transformation during the fifteenth century should not be summarized as the disappearance of a people or the inevitable failure of a hydraulic city. Communities adjusted their political centers and institutions. Meanwhile, Buddhist and Islamic networks were developing in various regions. Maritime commerce mattered, but it interacted with local religious traditions and political histories rather than determining them automatically.

Melaka's rise in the early fifteenth century made the strait between the Malay Peninsula and Sumatra an important commercial and diplomatic hub. Merchants from South Asia, China, and elsewhere met among Malay-speaking communities and other local inhabitants. The settlement's rulers developed institutions for commerce and claimed authority within a competitive maritime environment. The Malay Annals, composed and transmitted in later forms, preserve important historical literature about the sultanate, but they are not a ledger written by neutral observers. Archaeology and external records provide additional perspectives. A port's cosmopolitan population does not mean every resident had equal status or freedom.

Islamic institutions spread across parts of the Malay world and island Southeast Asia through several processes. Traders, scholars, teachers, rulers, and family networks participated in the making of Muslim communities. Conversion was not a single event commanded from one capital. In some places new sultanates formed, while local practices persisted and adapted. Religious language could shape diplomatic alliances and commercial trust, but beliefs were not reducible to trade advantage. The evidence for individual conversions is often limited, and later genealogies may link founding rulers to distinguished origins that served subsequent political needs.

China's relationship to the sea changed with political events. The Mongol Yuan dynasty ruled China during the thirteenth and fourteenth centuries, and maritime exchange remained important. In 1368 the Ming dynasty replaced Yuan rule. Early Ming emperors reorganized government, foreign relations, and official commerce. Their policies cannot be reduced to either complete maritime openness or permanent isolation. State-sponsored voyages coexisted with local traders and changing restrictions. Artifacts such as blue-and-white porcelain show connections with West Asian markets and designs, but an imported style alone does not reveal the instructions given to every workshop.

During the early fifteenth century, fleets commanded by Zheng He made expeditions under Ming imperial sponsorship. Voyages beginning in 1405 reached ports in Southeast Asia and South Asia, and some later itineraries extended to the Arabian Peninsula and East African coast. Zheng He was a court official whose work combined diplomacy, display, state interest, and navigation. These were expensive expeditions involving many sailors, specialists, and officials. They did not discover an empty Indian Ocean or introduce oceanic trade to people unfamiliar with seafaring. The fleets entered existing commercial and diplomatic worlds and could change local balances of power within them.

Our knowledge of the voyages rests on several forms of evidence: court records, commemorative inscriptions, later histories, material objects, and modern scholarship. Their purposes differ. A royal inscription might celebrate the emperor's virtue and the fleets' success. A foreign account might describe the reception of visitors at one port, not the entire mission. Even the dimensions of famous treasure ships have generated debate because textual figures and physical reconstruction do not always align easily. We can explain the importance of the voyages without treating every dramatic detail as established fact or presenting their cancellation as proof that Chinese people stopped sailing.

The labor that supported maritime exchange was widespread. Shipbuilding required timber, skills, and workers. Ports needed water, food, warehouses, and mechanisms for negotiating disputes. Traders sometimes organized through families and diasporic communities; servants and enslaved people could also be compelled to travel or labor. Muslim scholars moved through many of the same routes but pursued different purposes. Maritime geography created opportunities, not equality. Some merchants acquired influence at a foreign court; others lost ships, faced seizure, or paid heavy dues. The commercial world before 1500 was sophisticated and often unequal.

European navigators entered these seas near the end of the fifteenth century. Vasco da Gama's arrival on the Malabar Coast in 1498 is important to the history of Portuguese expansion, but it was not the creation of the trade system he encountered. African, Arab, Persian, Indian, Malay, and other navigators and merchants had been active there for many generations. Later Portuguese attempts to control routes and ports would profoundly change the balance of force, but that later history should not be imposed on the centuries preceding it. The inhabitants of Calicut, Melaka, or Java did not regard 1498 as the origin of their commercial lives.

The waters between monsoons thus offer a different approach to the world before 1500. Political authority mattered, from Java's courts to Ming fleets and Malay sultanates. Yet the system also relied on repeat journeys, ecological knowledge, negotiated trust, violence, and accumulated infrastructure. The sea linked many regions while leaving others largely outside its networks. To understand how these connections transformed the sixteenth century, we must first recognize the complex maritime societies already present. Their history did not begin when a European ship arrived.

## A City through Several Empires: Byzantines and Ottomans before 1500

At the Bosporus, a different crossing of land and sea brings us to the late Byzantine and early Ottoman worlds.

On the Bosporus, a ship could pass between the Black Sea and the Sea of Marmara beside a city whose name and institutions had changed repeatedly. Constantinople was an imperial capital, a religious center, a trading port, and a home to people with different languages and loyalties. In 1453, the city was taken by the Ottoman sultan Mehmed the Second. The conquest was a decisive political event, but it did not begin the history of Ottoman power or erase the people who had lived in the region. To understand this turning point, we need the preceding two centuries of Byzantine fragmentation, Anatolian principalities, Balkan politics, commerce, and competing claims to rule.

Constantinople had already suffered an extraordinary rupture in 1204. An army associated with the Fourth Crusade, originating in western Christian Europe, captured and looted the city, although the expedition had been framed in the language of crusading toward the eastern Mediterranean. The conquest fragmented Byzantine imperial authority and established a Latin-ruled government in Constantinople. Other Byzantine successor polities formed around Nicaea, Epirus, and Trebizond. We must be precise about the religious and political stakes: the conflict crossed lines among Christians, and its victims included residents of a city whose religious traditions the conquerors did not share. The event cannot be explained as a straightforward Muslim-Christian war.

In 1261, forces associated with the Byzantine state centered at Nicaea regained Constantinople. The Palaiologan dynasty subsequently ruled an empire with far less territory and revenue than earlier Byzantine states had commanded. Restoration required rebuilding damaged structures and negotiating external alliances. Genoese and Venetian merchants held important commercial positions in the wider region. The revived court could still patronize art and religious institutions, but could not easily recover all its former provinces. Calling it merely a declining remnant misses the political strategies and cultural achievements of a state living under serious constraints.

The Anatolian environment was also changing. After the weakening of Seljuq authority, several principalities emerged or expanded, alongside Byzantine territories and communities with overlapping affiliations. The political group associated with Osman became one of these regional powers around the turn of the fourteenth century. Early Ottoman origins are difficult to reconstruct with precision because some of the most familiar dynastic narratives were written later. The story of an inevitable imperial destiny belongs to retrospective claims. The early Ottoman rulers navigated local alliances, warfare, religious patronage, family relationships, and opportunities at the Byzantine frontier.

Osman's successors expanded in northwestern Anatolia and crossed into southeastern Europe in the fourteenth century. Their successes depended on local military and diplomatic conditions, not only on one army's religious zeal. Balkan rulers had competing ambitions; Byzantine claimants sometimes sought external support against domestic rivals. Frontier warfare could destroy settlements and uproot families, while alliances could also allow elites to retain influence under a new overlord. Christians, Muslims, and others lived within changing jurisdictions. The borders visible on a modern map were more fluid than the simple color change between one year and the next suggests.

By the later fourteenth century, Ottoman rulers exercised power across parts of the Balkans and Anatolia. Institutions for taxation, military service, judicial authority, and provincial administration developed through a mixture of new arrangements and practices inherited from subjects and neighbors. Some military systems drew on the service of people incorporated into imperial institutions, with obligations and opportunities that varied sharply by status. We should not project the full institutions of the sixteenth-century empire backward onto its early centuries. They developed through repeated political decisions, while local communities retained diverse customs and relationships.

Conflicts remembered through the battle of Kosovo in 1389 illustrate the importance of later memory. The battle was consequential for regional politics, but much of its familiar legendary detail entered written traditions after the event. Claims about national destiny, a single eternal religious frontier, or the motivations of all participants flatten a complex medieval setting. The battle did not end every form of Balkan autonomy in one day. Rulers and communities continued to negotiate and fight. Studying the event requires distinguishing what near-contemporary evidence can establish from stories that later generations used to make sense of identity and loss.

Ottoman expansion also suffered reversals. In 1402, Timur's forces defeated Sultan Bayezid the First near Ankara, leading to struggles over succession among Ottoman claimants. The polity recovered and reassembled authority under later rulers. This episode shows how contingent the empire's future was. It might be tempting to narrate uninterrupted growth from Osman to Mehmed, but the defeat and succession conflicts were genuine threats. The survival of the dynasty depended on alliances, warfare, and administrative resources. Many communities experienced the costs of these struggles regardless of which prince eventually ruled.

Constantinople itself confronted warfare, changing trade routes, and epidemic mortality. The Black Death reached the city in the mid-fourteenth century, compounding pressures on a reduced population. Yet religious and artistic production continued. Manuscripts, churches, mosaics, and diplomatic correspondence record a society that remained culturally active. Byzantine leaders sought political and military assistance from western Europe under terms that could produce disputes over church authority. Religious differences between eastern and western Christians had political consequences, but they did not exhaust the range of interests pursued by merchants, courtiers, and local populations.

In 1453, Mehmed the Second laid siege to Constantinople and conquered the city. Ottoman military preparation included artillery and organized forces, but the outcome also reflected the city's limited resources and the wider balance of power. For residents, conquest meant violence, fear, and displacement as well as the establishment of a new government. Byzantine emperor Constantine the Eleventh died during the final defense. The political authority of the Eastern Roman emperors in the city ended. Yet Greek-speaking communities, Christian religious institutions, and other inhabitants remained part of the region's later life. A capital could change rulers without every social and cultural practice vanishing.

Mehmed made the city the center of an ambitious rebuilding program. Hagia Sophia, formerly the principal Byzantine church, became an imperial mosque. New palaces, religious complexes, markets, and infrastructure helped reorganize urban life. Muslim, Christian, and Jewish populations occupied the city under an Ottoman order that structured rights and obligations differently by community and status. Building programs drew on multiple artistic traditions, including Byzantine, Iranian, and Islamic precedents. Some specialists and residents moved voluntarily; others were resettled through state coercion. The rebuilt capital was a product of both political ambition and human labor.

The sources require care. Byzantine authors described their fortunes through imperial and Christian frameworks. Ottoman chronicles often celebrated dynastic legitimacy and providential victory. Venetian and Genoese materials emphasize maritime commerce and diplomatic interests. Buildings supply physical evidence but not complete accounts of the workers or residents who sustained them. Later national histories have sometimes made these centuries appear as an inevitable confrontation of timeless peoples. The actual record shows changing coalitions, shared practical institutions, religious differences, and shifting political priorities. No one chronicle has the whole story.

The year 1453 deserves its prominence, but it should not carry the weight of every change in Eurasian history. The Eastern Roman political order ended in its old capital; Ottoman rule entered a new phase; surrounding societies adjusted. At the same moment, states and communities elsewhere continued histories that were not caused by events on the Bosporus. Return to the ship passing the strait. The city beside it had been shaped by centuries of residents and rulers before the siege, and it would keep changing afterward. Historical turning points matter most when we understand the longer processes into which they fall.

## After the Plague: Work, War, and Learning in Late Medieval Europe

Elsewhere in Europe, the generations after the Black Death negotiated changes in work and political authority.

A laborer negotiating wages in fourteenth-century England confronted questions much older than the Black Death: who controlled land, who set the terms of service, and what a household needed to survive. But plague mortality transformed the bargaining situation. When workers were scarce, some could demand higher pay or different conditions. Authorities reacted by trying to restrain wages and compel service. The resulting disputes tell us more about late medieval Europe than a simple claim that the continent suddenly emerged from a dark age. Between the fourteenth century and 1500, Europeans experienced disease, war, social conflict, artistic production, commerce, and political experimentation. These developments did not occur at one pace in every region.

The epidemics of the mid-fourteenth century killed a substantial share of people in many European communities. Their consequences depended on local arrangements for land, inheritance, work, and political authority. In some places, surviving tenants renegotiated rents or acquired plots previously cultivated by others. In other places, landlords and rulers tried to preserve obligations that had existed before mortality changed the labor supply. A village and a commercial city could respond quite differently. Economic history cannot infer that all peasants became prosperous or that every elite family lost power merely because labor was less abundant.

England's Ordinance of Labourers of 1349 and Statute of Labourers of 1351 are revealing documents. They aimed to regulate wages and services in a society whose governing elites feared the consequences of labor scarcity. The texts describe what authorities wanted people to do, not necessarily what people did. Similar efforts occurred in other parts of western Europe, but their provisions and outcomes varied. Historian Samuel Cohn's comparative work shows why a uniform account of post-plague labor law is inadequate. When we read a law, we should ask who wrote it, whose interests it protected, and how enforcement worked on the ground.

Conflict over work and lordship appeared in popular uprisings. The English rising of 1381, for example, involved grievances over taxation and social obligations, but its participants did not all share one program. Urban artisans, rural communities, and leaders articulated demands under different conditions. The revolt was suppressed, yet its existence demonstrates that ordinary people made political claims in a language of justice and obligation. Other cities experienced struggles involving guilds, workers, and ruling families. It would be inaccurate to turn each protest into a modern democratic movement, but equally inaccurate to imagine late medieval workers as politically silent.

War remained a central burden. The prolonged conflicts grouped under the name Hundred Years' War, from 1337 into the fifteenth century, involved shifting claims and coalitions around the crowns of England and France. Campaigns drew resources from rural households, towns, and nobles, and could destroy crops or disrupt trade. Soldiers' employment, ransom, taxation, and the movement of armed companies affected communities far from the most famous battles. The war was not one continuous battle, nor was it simply a duel between modern nation-states. Political loyalties were territorial, dynastic, and local, often changing as rulers and military conditions changed.

The later stages of conflict show how institutions adapted. French and English monarchies made different efforts to raise revenue and organize military force, while estates, town councils, and local elites negotiated claims over taxation. The role of figures such as Joan of Arc must be understood within specific military and religious circumstances rather than as an inevitable march toward modern nationalism. Judicial records and political chronicles preserve much of this story from the perspective of courts. The people who supplied grain, transport, and care to armies are generally less visible, although their labor made campaigns possible.

Cities connected agricultural districts to wider markets. In northern Italy, the Low Countries, parts of Germany, and elsewhere, merchants and craftspeople participated in networks producing textiles, metalwork, books, and finance. The prosperity of one commercial center could depend on trade with regions beyond Europe, including North Africa and western Asia. Guild regulations and municipal governments shaped access to work and political voice, sometimes excluding women, migrants, unfree people, and poorer residents. Urban culture offered opportunity, but was not an equal civic community. We can admire a beautiful building while asking who paid for it and who labored on it.

The movement often called the Renaissance developed in this varied urban and courtly setting. Scholars studied ancient texts and languages; artists and architects experimented with forms and representation; patrons used commissions to display status and civic identity. Florence became one important center, but it was not the sole source of learning or artistic change. Humanist scholarship drew on manuscripts transmitted through many communities, including Byzantine and Islamic intellectual histories. The term rebirth is a metaphor associated with particular cultural programs, not evidence that learning had died during preceding centuries. Medieval scholars, translators, and artisans had maintained and transformed bodies of knowledge over many generations.

Printing became a powerful new technology for reproducing texts in parts of Europe during the fifteenth century. Johannes Gutenberg's work in Mainz, associated with the printed Bible of the 1450s, helped establish a form of movable-type printing that spread widely. Movable type and printing already had long histories in East Asia, so a global account must not label Europe the first inventor of printing. The European system drew on local techniques, economic conditions, and markets. A printed book still required paper, type, skilled workers, capital, and readers. Wider availability of some texts did not mean universal literacy or immediate agreement about their contents.

Religious life occupied a large place in these societies. Churches, monasteries, lay confraternities, and charitable institutions provided worship, education, and social resources, while also possessing land and political influence. Criticism of ecclesiastical practices existed well before the sixteenth-century Reformation. Religious dissent could be punished, and minority communities sometimes faced persecution. In 1492, the Catholic monarchs Ferdinand and Isabella ordered the expulsion of Jews who did not convert from their realms, a major coercive act with lasting consequences for families and diaspora communities. This history belongs beside artistic patronage and expanding commerce, not in a separate footnote.

European relationships to the Atlantic also changed. Portuguese vessels explored and raided along parts of the West African coast during the fifteenth century, supported by interests in trade, prestige, and political power. These voyages encountered African states and societies with their own diplomatic and commercial institutions. Maritime expeditions depended on navigational knowledge, shipbuilding, finance, and coerced labor in some emerging systems. By the end of the century, European rulers were supporting more ambitious long-distance voyages. These decisions were not the inevitable result of the Renaissance; they emerged from concrete conflicts and opportunities in particular courts and ports.

In 1492, Christopher Columbus's expedition from Spain reached Caribbean islands inhabited by Indigenous people. That event is sometimes presented as a European discovery of a previously empty land, which is false. Caribbean communities had long histories of farming, navigation, political organization, and cultural life. The encounter initiated processes of domination and violent change that would accelerate in the sixteenth century. It also shows why a history of late medieval Europe cannot end with purely European achievements. Decisions made in European courts would have consequences for peoples who had not participated in those courts and had their own histories.

The sources for late medieval Europe are extensive but selective. Wage laws, tax rolls, urban accounts, trial documents, art, and books survive in different amounts across regions. A surviving regulation tells us the purpose of lawmakers; an artist's commission tells us about patrons and workers differently; a chronicle may glorify a commander. Historical comparisons must make room for both change and persistence. By 1500 Europe had experienced major political and economic adjustments. It had not become uniformly prosperous, enlightened, united, or destined to rule the world. The laborer negotiating wages remained one of the people whose choices and constraints made history.

## The World around 1500: Connections, Boundaries, and New Encounters

We can now widen the scale without making the year 1500 a simultaneous beginning or ending for every society.

Stand in the year 1500, not on a single shore but at several places at once. A trader in Melaka is bargaining over cargo; an official in Ming China is recording obligations; a farmer in the Andes is working land that supports an expanding empire; a family in a Caribbean village is living through changes brought by a European landing; an artisan in a West African town is making goods for local and distant exchange. These people do not occupy the same story simply because we assign them the same year. Some are linked by trade and government. Others inhabit worlds that had little or no direct contact with each other until the recent Atlantic crossings. A useful global history must explain both connection and separation.

The phrase world history can conceal a problem. We know the planet is one physical place, yet its inhabitants before 1500 did not participate in a single worldwide political or commercial system. Regional networks extended far across Afro-Eurasia and the Indian Ocean, while many American societies developed in distinct interrelated networks across their own continents. Pacific island societies navigated enormous distances and sustained their own histories. Saying that these histories were parallel does not make them inferior or incomplete. Contact is one kind of historical relationship; independent development is another.

Across much of East Asia, Ming rule in China followed the Mongol Yuan period. Court government, agriculture, markets, craftsmanship, and regional trade supported a large and varied society. China's rulers could launch major diplomatic expeditions, regulate maritime activity, and sponsor monumental and artistic projects. Their decisions affected neighbors but did not determine every transaction made in Southeast Asian ports. Korea's Joseon dynasty and Japan's competing political powers followed their own histories; the boundaries of Chinese political authority did not coincide with all East Asian cultural exchange. A court chronicle is one source among many for this region.

In South and Southeast Asia, powerful states and ports coexisted. Indian polities, including sultanates and kingdoms with distinct religious and political traditions, governed regions with intensive farming, urban economies, and commercial connections. Melaka mediated trade across a strategically important strait. Java and mainland Southeast Asia contained communities whose religions and political affiliations were changing. Merchants traveled with cloth, ceramics, spices, and other goods, but exchange depended on legal arrangements, ships, labor, and local knowledge. A vessel arriving from Europe in 1498 would join this established commercial world rather than create it.

The Ottoman state had taken Constantinople in 1453 and consolidated rule in parts of southeastern Europe and western Asia. Mamluk rulers governed Egypt and Syria, drawing on commercial and religious networks that connected the Mediterranean to the Red Sea and beyond. Other powers and communities shaped routes through Iran, Central Asia, and the western Indian Ocean. These states did not form a single political bloc. Military rivalries, diplomatic ties, taxation, and scholarly travel crossed borders in changing patterns. No map of one empire can stand in for the social history of the millions living outside its capital.

African histories around 1500 were likewise varied. Songhai held influence over parts of the western Sahel, where towns and markets connected river systems to trans-Saharan exchange. Timbuktu's scholarly communities developed through generations of teaching and manuscript work. On the East African coast, Swahili-speaking settlements participated in Indian Ocean commerce while maintaining locally rooted institutions. In southern Africa, political centers shifted after Great Zimbabwe's earlier prominence. Farther south and in other regions, societies followed distinct agricultural, pastoral, and political paths. African participation in world history did not begin with European ships appearing at coastal ports.

In the Americas, the Mexica-led Triple Alliance drew tribute from some central Mexican city-states while facing independent rivals. Maya peoples occupied diverse political communities, including Yucatán and the Guatemalan highlands. In the Andes, Tawantinsuyu expanded through warfare, diplomacy, administrative demands, and the use of roads that incorporated older infrastructure. These were powerful societies whose leaders and subjects faced complicated choices. They were not waiting for contact to become historical actors. Nor did a single empire represent the hundreds of languages and local histories across the two continents.

Farther north and across the Caribbean, different Indigenous societies organized agriculture, fishing, trade, settlement, and political relationships. The Taíno and other Caribbean peoples had histories well before the voyages of Columbus. North American communities maintained networks and cultural practices that cannot be reduced to the Mexica or Inca systems. Coastal and island geography invited maritime movement but did not create one universal American state. The political maps used for 1500 are therefore partial: they show larger polities more readily than small communities, seasonal movements, or relationships recorded through nonalphabetic traditions.

The Pacific also resists a single date. Settlement of many islands occurred long before 1500; some archipelagos were reached later than others. Māori communities in Aotearoa New Zealand developed after sustained Polynesian settlement beginning around the late thirteenth century. Oceania's navigational knowledge, language families, local ecologies, and social institutions belonged to their own histories. There is no basis for treating the ocean as an empty margin around the continents. The ability to cross distant waters required accumulated knowledge, organized labor, and decision-making, and different voyages and settlements followed different chronologies.

Consider the technologies that made connections possible. Oceanic vessels, pack animals, roads, writing systems, khipus, navigation techniques, and cultivated landscapes all supported coordinated activity. Yet they were not distributed uniformly, and none guaranteed the same social outcomes everywhere. A road built for the Inca state might facilitate transport while imposing labor obligations. A maritime route could enrich brokers while exposing sailors to danger. Printing could accelerate the reproduction of some texts without eliminating inequality in education. The history of a technology is also a history of who controlled it, used it, paid for it, and was excluded from its benefits.

The closing years of the fifteenth century introduced important new connections, especially across the Atlantic. In 1492, Columbus reached Caribbean islands inhabited by Indigenous peoples. European expansion initiated violent encounters and soon produced far-reaching consequences through warfare, coerced labor, dispossession, and disease. At the year 1500 boundary, many of those consequences were only beginning and their later scale should not be projected as a settled fact into the minds of contemporaries. In 1498, Vasco da Gama arrived on India's Malabar Coast, connecting Portuguese ambitions to established Indian Ocean commerce. Neither landing should be narrated as the discovery of people or places previously unknown to their own inhabitants.

The boundary between medieval and modern is itself a decision made by historians. Different changes became prominent at different times: new political systems, religious transformations, epidemic disruptions, commercial realignments, and imperial expansion. The year 1500 is useful for arranging a curriculum, but it is not an instant at which the planet changed eras. Some social institutions lasted across the boundary; others had already changed radically long before it. An account organized entirely around Europe's voyages gives insufficient attention to the societies that made the world those voyages encountered.

We also need to ask whose evidence survives. Ming administrative records, Ottoman chronicles, court literature from West Africa, Andean roads and khipus, Maya materials, and Pacific oral traditions reveal different kinds of history. The early European descriptions of unfamiliar peoples are often deeply informative and deeply partial at the same time. Their authors had motives, assumptions, and political obligations. Archaeology and the continued knowledge of Indigenous communities can correct omissions, though they do not automatically solve every chronological problem. Global history is strongest when it resists giving any one archive authority over the entire planet.

At 1500, no community knew the complete future. A sailor did not foresee all the consequences of a voyage, and a court did not control how distant peoples would respond to its decisions. What we can do is reconstruct the worlds they inhabited: already connected in many ways, still separated in others, filled with sophisticated institutions and unequal power. The long history from the first cities to this moment is not a ladder toward modern Europe. It is the cumulative history of many human societies, sometimes interacting, sometimes developing apart. To understand the centuries after 1500, begin with what was already there.
