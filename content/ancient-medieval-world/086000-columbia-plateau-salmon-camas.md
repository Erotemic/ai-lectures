# Where Salmon and Camas Meet: Life on the Columbia Plateau

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

At a constriction in the Columbia River, the water once broke into rapids and falls. Salmon attempting to move upstream were concentrated in a narrow, dangerous stretch. People knew the place, its seasons, and the work needed to fish there. Celilo Falls was not simply a natural feeding station. It was a gathering place where fishers, traders, families, and visitors formed relationships over many generations. The water that made travel difficult also made certain fishing technologies effective. To understand the Columbia Plateau, we can begin with that paradox: a barrier for boats could be a center of movement and exchange for people.

The Columbia watershed reaches across what are now multiple states and into Canada. Its peoples did not form one polity or speak one language. The plateau and adjacent regions supported distinct communities, including peoples identified today with the Yakama, Umatilla, Warm Springs, Nez Perce, Colville, and others. Salmon was a central food and cultural relation for many, but not all the plateau's resources could be reduced to it. Native Knowledge 360, produced by the Smithsonian National Museum of the American Indian, describes continuing First Salmon ceremonies and the way communities define themselves through responsibilities to fish. Such living testimony conveys values and relationships that archaeological bones alone cannot record.

Archaeology shows very long human occupation along the river, including evidence for activity near the falls thousands of years ago. It does not show that every fishing platform, village, or ownership rule recorded in recent centuries existed in the same form during the early medieval period. The Oregon Encyclopedia reports occupation of sites along Celilo stretching to at least about eleven thousand years ago. Dates like that reveal the depth of settlement history, not the continuous identity of a particular wooden scaffold. Riverbanks shifted, populations moved, technologies changed, and accounts were transmitted in forms other than an excavator's notebook.

Fishing was an active technology. Nets, traps, spears, and later well-documented platforms depended on knowledge of current, season, and fish behavior. Harvesting was followed by cleaning, drying, storing, and distributing food. The Northwest Power and Conservation Council cautions that archaeological evidence of very ancient fishing does not automatically demonstrate how much of the catch was dried for winter consumption at that time. That is an important limit: the presence of a fish bone establishes less than a complete calendar of meals. The labor of preservation made fish available beyond a single run, but its scale and timing need evidence from particular places.

Fish moved people as well as feeding them. Gatherings at places such as Celilo created opportunities to exchange goods, arrange visits, maintain kinship, and share news. In its regional account, the Smithsonian records the continuing importance of Celilo in Indigenous knowledge and notes that people came from considerable distances. The economic value of such a gathering was inseparable from its social and ceremonial value. A traveler might bring food from another ecological zone or a crafted object, but also join relatives and renew obligations. The word trade is accurate, yet incomplete, if it imagines only strangers bargaining for anonymous goods.

Beyond the river's main fishing sites lay landscapes shaped by roots, grasses, game, and seasonal travel. Camas was an important plant food in much of the wider region. Its bulbs required knowledge of timing, harvesting, and preparation, especially because similar-looking plants could be dangerous. Research on the Columbia Plateau describes access to root grounds, localized resources, and patterns of winter villages and smaller seasonal camps. That picture must not be copied onto every valley, altitude, or century. It instead asks how people maintained a portfolio of places, so that a community's life could follow several seasonal rhythms rather than a fixed year-round settlement.

Rights to useful locations mattered. A fishing place could be associated with particular families and social rules; root grounds, camps, and paths could have established caretakers or understood responsibilities. Those arrangements were not simply the absence of property because they were not written in European-style deeds. Nor should historical descriptions from the nineteenth century be presented as direct observations of the twelfth. Oral histories, landscape features, comparative archaeology, and colonial documents must be read with their different dates and purposes in view. The existence of regulation and stewardship is clear; their exact form changed among peoples and through time.

Environmental knowledge was also work. Fishing runs fluctuate; roots grow differently according to water, fire, and harvest; storage is vulnerable to decay and animals. Successful communities planned for variation, often by maintaining knowledge of several resources and places. Historian and archaeologist Ruthann Knudson described a pattern of localized but reliable foods and shifting seasonal tasks that complemented larger gathering events. That framework helps us ask better questions of the past, but it is a scholarly reconstruction, not a timetable that every ancestral household left behind. It points toward flexibility rather than a single fixed agricultural formula.

The plateau also belonged to broader exchange systems. It connected riverine travel, inland paths, and the resources of different ecological zones. Some materials can be tracked archaeologically through stone sources or other distinctive properties. Others, especially woven containers, dried foods, and oral agreements, leave much weaker traces. If an excavated object came from outside the immediate region, it shows some movement across distance; it does not prove the owner personally walked the entire route. The broader story is of chains of exchange and hospitality, often assembled through many local relationships rather than a single central market.

Celilo is also a case in why historical boundaries matter. The falls were inundated in 1957 after construction of The Dalles Dam. That event belongs to the modern history of river engineering, treaty rights, and Indigenous struggles to retain fishing practices. It cannot be moved backward into 1200, yet leaving it out would imply that the landscape we see today is the same one precolonial people used. Some of the physical evidence is now underwater. Historical photography, oral testimony, archaeology, and records made before flooding help describe features no longer visible. This modern rupture affects both communities and the historian's ability to inspect the past.

Treaty-era descriptions of fishing platforms and family rights are particularly informative but must be handled with chronology in mind. They show institutions in a region already profoundly transformed by contact, trade, disease, settlement, and coercive government policy. Continuities matter and are affirmed by living nations; so do changes. A careful lecture does not replace Indigenous history with the moment a federal dam was built, but it recognizes that modern dispossession shaped which practices could continue openly and which sites remain accessible. The historical task includes explaining why certain records exist and what pressures produced them.

What would a good historical map of the plateau show? Not just a river line and a dot labeled Celilo. It would show seasonal resources, paths to root-gathering places, locations of exchange, and relations among neighboring communities. Much of this map would be tentative because archaeological sites do not preserve every name and entitlement. Such uncertainty is no excuse for simplifying the region into unoccupied wilderness. There were rules, skills, ceremonies, repeated work, and long-distance connections. Political history can be found in who could fish, who organized a gathering, and how communities cared for the places on which their food depended.

At the end, return to the current and the fish. A salmon run was a natural event, but a fishery was a human relationship with that event, made durable by technology, knowledge, and institutions. Camas grounds and seasonal camps tell a parallel story about planning and stewardship beyond the banks. The people of the plateau were not waiting for agriculture, towns, or outsiders to make their history begin. Their histories were already being made in the choices of fishers, gatherers, hosts, elders, and travelers. The surviving evidence gives only fragments, but those fragments are enough to demand a fuller account of the place and its people.
