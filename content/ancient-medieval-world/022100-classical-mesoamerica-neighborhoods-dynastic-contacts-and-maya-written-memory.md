# Classical Mesoamerica: neighborhoods, dynastic contacts, and Maya written memory

A city neighborhood and a royal inscription describe power at different scales. Moving from Teotihuacan to Maya records, we will ask how migration, work, dynastic claims, and written memory can be studied together without treating all Mesoamerica as a single society.

## A City of Many Neighbors: Migration and Work at Teotihuacan

Walk through the ruins of Teotihuacan, in the Basin of Mexico, and the scale of the ancient city is immediately visible. The Avenue of the Dead crosses a planned urban landscape dominated by enormous ceremonial buildings. Yet most inhabitants did not spend their days climbing pyramids. They lived in residential compounds, prepared food, made goods, raised children, traded, and moved through neighborhoods whose occupants had different histories. The great question is not only how such a city was built. It is how people from many communities lived together and how a political order managed their shared space.

Teotihuacan grew during the early centuries of the Common Era and flourished long before the later Mexica capital of Tenochtitlan. Its largest phases belong broadly to the first half of the first millennium. Population estimates vary with assumptions about residential density and occupation dates, but scholars agree that it was a major urban center of the ancient Americas. We know its present name from much later Nahua usage; we do not possess an unquestioned ancient city name that its inhabitants used for themselves. Even the familiar name reminds us that history is reconstructed through layers of memory.

Many dwellings belonged to compounds that housed several households around courtyards and shared spaces. Such architecture suggests a way of living that differed from isolated single-family houses. Excavations find cooking and craft materials, burials, and items used in ritual. A compound was not necessarily egalitarian: the size of rooms, access to resources, and quality of goods could vary. But its walls let us ask about the organization of daily life. Who prepared meals, raised children, cleaned floors, built walls, and supplied workers? Urban history is a history of maintenance as well as construction.

Some neighborhoods contain objects and burial customs associated with distant regions. Archaeologists have identified a Zapotec-associated district often called Tlailotlacan, as well as a merchants' neighborhood whose material culture points toward connections with the Gulf Coast, the Maya region, and elsewhere. One could wrongly assume that every foreign-style pot belonged to a migrant or that a neighborhood was populated by one unchanging ethnic group. Objects travel, customs spread, and families form across communities. The archaeological question is how patterns of architecture, burial, objects, and environmental evidence combine, not how to assign a passport to each artifact.

Specialized crafts helped connect these areas to the broader city. Obsidian could be worked into blades and tools, and objects made in or distributed through Teotihuacan reached distant parts of Mesoamerica. Producing a tool involved acquiring raw material, learning techniques, managing waste, and finding buyers or recipients. A pyramid reveals public investment; a workshop reveals repeated skill. Both contribute to political economy. The presence of a large exchange network does not tell us whether merchants were independent, supervised by elites, or operating in several ways at once.

Consider migrants themselves. An individual arriving from another region might keep certain practices and adopt others. Children born in the city could learn a mixed set of customs. Families could maintain links through marriage, trade, pilgrimage, or political obligations. Describing Teotihuacan as a multiethnic city is therefore a starting point for research, not a complete explanation. The best evidence comes from patterns repeated across residences and burials, and even then it often reveals community-level tendencies rather than the choices of a named person.

The city's monumental core was not an empty stage for ceremonies. It required access roads, drainage, maintenance, food deliveries, and labor to repair surfaces. The scale of its planning implies coordination across many people. But archaeology has not produced a simple list of kings and reigns that can explain every phase. Rulers may have existed, and elites certainly shaped some monumental spaces, yet the evidence for the precise form of supreme authority is debated. It is safer to ask what institutions or alliances made large projects possible than to invent a single royal founder.

Public ritual and coercion also have a place in the story. Excavations of ceremonial precincts and burials reveal practices that cannot be reduced to peaceful neighborhood cooperation. The ancient city's imagery and deposits speak to power exercised through religion, spectacle, and at times violence. Such evidence must be interpreted with attention to context and date. A burial in a temple precinct is not a direct account of what happened to a captive, and iconography does not prove that all inhabitants shared the same beliefs or experiences.

During the sixth and seventh centuries the city's political and urban system changed profoundly. Archaeologists have identified burning and destruction in important public areas, alterations in occupation, and evidence of declining population. Yet the chronology and causes remain contested. Some fires may reflect attacks, other forms of conflict, or deliberate termination of public spaces. No single event explains the sequence across all neighborhoods. The city did not disappear from memory, and some areas continued to be inhabited or reused after its central institutions weakened.

The word collapse encourages a story of one sudden ending. Teotihuacan instead offers evidence for processes unfolding unevenly: public buildings damaged or abandoned, services disrupted, residents moving, and later groups occupying parts of the landscape. A statement that the city ceased to function as an earlier political center can be well supported without claiming that every person died or that Mesoamerican urban life ended. People carried skills, practices, and memories into changing regional networks. An architectural ruin is not the end of a population's history.

Our knowledge of the city reflects a bias toward durable materials. Stone platforms and burned walls often survive when clothing, baskets, spoken languages, and ordinary wooden objects do not. Archaeologists reconstruct social diversity through architecture, material styles, burials, and environmental analysis, but many personal identities remain inaccessible. We should not fill these gaps with modern stereotypes about a single ancient ethnicity or an all-powerful priesthood. A large city can be organized without leaving a full royal chronicle, and a recognizable artistic style can cross linguistic and political boundaries.

Teotihuacan also belongs to a wider American history. Its growth took place alongside urban and regional developments in Oaxaca, Maya lowlands, central Mexican valleys, and other areas. Some contacts involved commerce and religious exchange; others involved competition or interventions whose nature historians still debate. Objects associated with Teotihuacan at distant sites do not always mean direct occupation. Different combinations of local adoption, migration, alliance, and conflict can produce similar material traces. Regional histories need to be compared rather than folded into one imperial map.

The value of a neighborhood approach is that it turns a monumental site into an inhabited city. It asks why a family settled in one compound, how crafts circulated, what political institutions could coordinate water and construction, and how the city's transformation affected communities differently. The questions do not all have answers. But this is better than describing pyramids as if they rose without workers and stood without residents. The urban achievement was collective, structured by unequal power, and sustained by countless repeated tasks.

When we look at Teotihuacan now, we should see both the grand plan and the rooms beside it. The city was connected to many regions, but its neighborhoods were more than a collection of foreign curiosities. They were places where people made lives, adapted customs, and helped produce the wealth and authority visible in its architecture. Its changing fortunes warn against treating a city's peak as its entire history. The ruins preserve enough to ask how migration, work, and political power came together; they do not require us to pretend that we know every ruler's name.

## A Sea in the City: Teopancazco and Teotihuacan’s Neighborhood Economy

Within Teotihuacan, one neighborhood makes the city’s wider connections visible through food, materials, and specialized work.

In the ancient city of Teotihuacan, a workshop could be far from the coastline yet tied to people who knew the Gulf of Mexico. Teopancazco, a neighborhood center excavated south of the Ciudadela complex, preserves traces of such connections. Its rooms and burials speak to workers, religious practices, food preparation, and the movement of people over great distances. Instead of starting with Teotihuacan's Pyramids of the Sun and Moon, imagine trying to keep one neighborhood supplied with raw materials and trained hands. Urban power depended upon such practical connections, even when monumental art celebrated something grander.

The broader metropolis flourished in the first half of the first millennium CE. Many families lived in multifamily compounds organized around shared courts and functional spaces. Archaeologists call this arrangement corporate not because it resembles a modern corporation but because groups shared property, work, ritual and responsibilities beyond a single isolated household. Teopancazco appears to have served a broader neighborhood network. Its plan differs from ordinary residential compounds and includes sectors specialists interpret as places for ritual, crafts, and preparation of food for workers.

Excavators distinguish several periods of activity at Teopancazco, broadly spanning the second through seventh centuries. Some rooms changed use and some building levels covered earlier ones. This makes it dangerous to combine every find into one picture of a typical day. An object from an early workshop and a burial from a later phase may describe different communities. Linda Manzanilla's research emphasizes changes in urban structure and neighborhood relations through time. Chronology matters because foreign links, political institutions and craft specialties could expand or be reorganized as Teotihuacan itself changed.

The neighborhood's ties to the Gulf Coast appear in materials, styles and specialized practices. The researchers argue that Teopancazco took part in networks through which coastal resources arrived in the central Mexican highlands. Such connections are not a simple line on a map. Someone had to procure materials, move them through different hands, decide where they were stored and train others to turn them into desired objects. At the same time, one imported item does not prove that the person buried near it was born on the coast. Objects, skills and people move on overlapping routes.

Evidence for mobility comes from more than artifact design. Researchers have examined burials and biological indicators that can reflect childhood conditions different from those near the city. Teopancazco yielded 116 formal burials in the reported study. Some individuals appear to have moved into the Teotihuacan region during their lives. Yet mobility tests depend on geographic baselines, food, local variation and preservation. They rarely yield a named hometown. We learn that the urban population was not one unchanged local group, while keeping separate the biological evidence from cultural labels assigned to pottery or architecture.

Burials also show that neighborhood life included acts of violence, elaborate treatment of the dead, and unequal access to ritual settings. The published Teopancazco account records decapitated remains among the formal interments. That evidence warrants careful description. It does not identify the circumstances of every death, nor does it tell us whether a particular person was captive, resident, or outsider. A forensic observation must be distinguished from the motive ascribed to it. The presence of violence does not erase the evidence for specialized work and cooperation; both belong to the same complex urban history.

Some excavated spaces have been interpreted as administrative or even medical, but those labels need qualification. A concentration of tools or objects can suggest tasks without showing the titles of people who worked there. A room used for food preparation might serve specialized workers, visiting groups or a larger household. Teopancazco's significance is precisely the ability to test such interpretations against spatial patterns. The more we know about room sequences and associations, the less useful it becomes to call every decorated structure a temple or every productive room a market.

Foreign communities existed elsewhere in the city. INAH's synthesis describes a Zapotec-associated neighborhood, Tlailotlacan, with tombs resembling practices known from Oaxaca. Archaeologists also identify a merchants' neighborhood whose material assemblages connect different Gulf and Maya-region histories. Those districts were not necessarily walled ethnic enclaves whose residents never married beyond them. Nor did ceramic resemblance permit a census of languages. Teopancazco fits into this wider urban geography: a metropolis whose different neighborhoods could preserve regional ties, adapt practices and help sustain long-distance commerce.

The idea that a city grew from immigrants can become too comfortable if it overlooks political power. Groups competed for resources, influence and access to desirable goods. Some activities required collective investments; others placed people under coercion. A neighborhood that helped secure materials could gain standing with powerful institutions, but it might also face demands that ordinary families did not control. The archaeological record does not provide an organizational chart of Teotihuacan's government. Manzanilla uses the uneven evidence to argue for cooperating and competing corporate groups rather than a simple palace ordering every household.

Teotihuacan's later transformation makes the neighborhood evidence more valuable. During the sixth and seventh centuries major public spaces were damaged and settlement patterns altered. It would be misleading to narrate a single day of destruction followed by complete disappearance. Craft practices and social networks could change at different rates. Teopancazco's sequence gives archaeologists a way to ask when a specialized role was maintained, abandoned or reorganized. A major city can lose a form of central political authority while people continue producing, moving and remembering elsewhere.

The coastal reach of Teopancazco has a methodological lesson. If we began only with an imported object, we could tell a thin story of trade. If we began only with a burial, we could tell a thin story of migration. If we began only with a pyramid, we could tell a thin story of rulers. Combining workshops, architecture, burials and geographic comparisons produces a fuller picture, although the connections among those observations remain open to criticism. Teopancazco lets us see urban life as the product of neighborhoods carrying out specialized tasks in relation to distant landscapes.

## One Day in 378: Tikal, Teotihuacan, and the Politics of an Arrival

The scale now widens from a neighborhood to contact with a Maya royal center. Evidence of an arrival is not a complete explanation of everything that changed afterward.

A Maya monument at Tikal names a day in the fourth century that changed how a royal family told its history. An individual whose name is conventionally read Siyaj K’ahk’, often translated as Fire Is Born, arrived in the region in 378 CE. The ruler Chak Tok Ich’aak is also said to have died on that day. The juxtaposition is striking, but it is not a newspaper report of an invasion. It comes through inscriptions carved for later political audiences. This is a case in which historians can recover a pivotal event while still struggling to explain its exact character.

Tikal was a powerful Maya center in what is now Guatemala, with a long dynastic past before the arrival. Teotihuacan, far to the northwest in the Basin of Mexico, was a vast city with its own monumental traditions and far-reaching networks. Contacts between the regions long predated 378. Materials, symbols and architectural ideas could cross great distances through exchanges involving many communities. A weapon form or a building style cannot by itself prove that one government controlled another. The question is whether inscriptions and archaeological change together point to political intervention rather than only the prestige of distant fashions.

David Stuart's analysis of the monuments made a strong case that the texts indicate a disruptive foreign-linked political episode. He contrasted explanations based on local elites adopting Teotihuacan imagery with explanations involving real actors and coercive influence. Those need not be mutually exclusive across an entire century. One Maya court could borrow symbols because they were prestigious; another might have faced leaders or soldiers associated with the same foreign power. Inscriptions are crucial because they attach specific names and dates to situations that objects alone would leave open.

Stela 31 at Tikal is especially important because a later king used it to speak about earlier generations. It includes the 378 arrival and the death of the preceding ruler, then places subsequent dynastic events within a genealogy. The monument was designed to authorize a reigning king, not to present a detached inquiry into his father's accession. Later rulers had reasons to emphasize some events, frame others as honorable succession and omit details that would complicate a claim. For historians, that does not make the text worthless. It makes its circumstances part of the evidence.

The name associated with the arrival, Siyaj K’ahk’, appears in discussions of several Maya centers. Another figure, often called Spearthrower Owl in modern scholarship, is connected by epigraphic interpretation to the reorganized dynasty and Teotihuacan-associated imagery. Their political relationship and origin remain debated. Modern names can sound more certain than the readings that produced them, and not all titles refer to exactly comparable offices. It is safer to describe their roles in particular inscriptions than to imagine a complete imperial chain of command with governors assigned from a distant capital.

The new dynasty displayed central Mexican associations in royal costume and symbols, including militaristic imagery. Such choices mattered because courts communicated political legitimacy with pictures as well as words. A ruler might invoke Teotihuacan to claim access to power beyond his region, while simultaneously presenting himself as a proper Maya king within local traditions. This need not be either foreign or native in a simple sense. Reorganized courts could combine different traditions to make new authority recognizable to local nobles, rivals and allied communities.

A date inscribed on stone invites false precision. It can tell us when a ruler wished to record an arrival, but not the hour, number of participants, route of travel or words spoken. Even the conversion into a modern calendar depends on scholarly correlation conventions. Different published summaries can give a neighboring January day while agreeing on the year and Long Count record. We therefore do not need a dramatic scene in which armies march through a known gate at an exact moment. The chronicle of a conquest must not be fabricated from the clarity of one date.

The event also belongs to the history of other Maya cities. Connections among Tikal, nearby centers, and farther courts changed around the fourth and fifth centuries. A person linked to the intervention could affect several dynasties, but each city’s local inscriptions and archaeological sequence need separate study. Contact does not erase political variation. We should expect alliances, rivalry, emulation and contestation to coexist across the region rather than infer that all Maya kingdoms suddenly became provinces of Teotihuacan on the same day.

Why is the 378 episode sometimes called an entrada? The Spanish word means an entry or arrival and has become a convenient scholarly label for the recorded event. It can also suggest a military entry even where the scale and organization of violence are not fully known. The inscription's pairing of arrival and ruler death makes an entirely benign interpretation difficult, but that is not identical to a detailed battle report. Political interventions often involve intimidation, negotiated submission, dynastic marriage and ritual recognition as well as killing. The record seldom allows us to assign proportions to each.

It is tempting to begin the story at Teotihuacan and cast the Maya lowlands as a passive destination. But the inscriptions we are able to read were made by Maya specialists for Maya courts. Maya elites interpreted the event through their own political vocabulary and later remade it in their historical memory. An ambitious ruler could use a foreign association for legitimacy while still negotiating with neighbors who had their own interests. The very survival of the record shows local intellectual labor: scribes chose the structure of the account and artists selected how power would appear.

The lesson of Tikal's arrival is not that Teotihuacan conquered Mesoamerica. It is that ancient political networks could carry armed or coercive power across long distances and that local dynasties could use those encounters to rewrite their histories. In some settings, material signs of foreign influence are primarily about prestige or imitation. At Tikal, the named episode requires a stronger account of political disruption, while still leaving the form and duration of outside rule unresolved. Reading the stela closely makes the history both more concrete and less simplistic.

## Names Cut into Stone: Rival Kings and the Classic Maya World

Maya inscriptions offer a closer view of the dynastic claims within that region’s own political histories.

In a Maya city, a carved stone could name a ruler, mark a date, and place an event within a longer history of dynastic claims. To people who commissioned the carving, it was public memory and political argument at once. To historians, it is a rare chance to recover individual names across more than a thousand years. The most important consequence of deciphering Maya glyphs was not simply learning which day a temple was dedicated. It made possible a history of rival courts, alliances, marriages, and wars. That history is richer and less uniform than an image of a single Maya empire.

The Classic Maya world, roughly the third through ninth centuries of the Common Era, contained many political centers in what are now Mexico, Guatemala, Belize, Honduras, and neighboring regions. Cities shared features of language, calendrics, and ritual while differing in resources, architecture, and political influence. Some kings styled themselves divine lords, but no one dynasty governed every Maya-speaking community. Rulers relied on nobles, households, farmers, artisans, and warriors. A royal inscription could claim that a particular person embodied order; the city depended on people who never had their names written on a monument.

Tikal, in the tropical lowlands of present-day Guatemala, offers a long record. Its stelae preserve royal portraits and dated texts, and its architecture changed with periods of growth and competition. Stela 29 bears a date equivalent to 292 CE, making it a key anchor for early dynastic history. Later texts look backward and connect rulers to earlier founders. That backward reach matters: a monument erected in one reign may tell a story about an ancestor whose deeds are not independently recorded. The historian's task is to distinguish the carving's own date from the older event it commemorates.

In 378 CE, an event often called the Arrival or Entrada changed Tikal's ruling line and its connections with central Mexico. Archaeology and inscriptions show new forms of elite display associated with Teotihuacan, far away in the Basin of Mexico. Scholars debate the exact political relationship: intervention, alliance, migration, and local appropriation need not have occurred in one simple sequence. The evidence makes external contact undeniable, but it does not prove that Teotihuacan permanently administered all Maya cities. The event shows how far elite networks could extend while leaving much of their mechanism open to research.

By the sixth and seventh centuries, a network of rival kingdoms tied Tikal to Calakmul and smaller centers. Calakmul's Kaan dynasty developed alliances that could pressure an opponent without conquering every city between them. In 562, Tikal suffered a major political setback, often connected with Calakmul's strategy and its allies. Monumental activity at Tikal became less conspicuous for a time. Yet a pause in royal construction is not proof that ordinary life stopped. People still farmed, made pottery, worshipped, and raised children while a dynasty's public fortunes changed.

At Palenque in the western Maya region, rulers confronted their own succession crises and foreign pressures. K'inich Janaab' Pakal came to power in 615, still a boy according to the dynastic dates. His long reign saw major building, including the Temple of the Inscriptions, where a tomb and texts linked the ruler to a deep political and sacred past. Some inscriptions celebrate predecessors and supernatural origins. They are invaluable sources, but their claims about legitimacy are precisely that: claims made by people with reasons to secure a dynasty's status. The stone tells us what a court wanted its viewers to know.

The relationship between Calakmul and Palenque also shows that diplomacy was more than battles. Networks could be maintained through marriage, ritual recognition, and reciprocal displays of rank. A city of modest size might become strategically important because it connected larger rivals or supplied access to a route. When a ruler appears under the emblem of another dynasty, scholars must consider several possibilities: subordination, alliance, ritual affiliation, or a political relationship that changed over time. A map with arrows of conquest can only approximate this shifting field of obligations and prestige.

In 695, Tikal's ruler Jasaw Chan K'awiil defeated Calakmul's forces, and Tikal soon renewed its monumental projects. It is tempting to call this a final victory that ended the struggle. Yet Maya geopolitics did not become simple afterward. Competing courts continued to make claims, and dynastic influence moved among centers. Monumental building often followed political success because a king could devote resources to public commemoration. That does not make a pyramid a neutral victory report. The texts present achievements through the language and rituals of the court that paid for them.

The city of Copán, farther southeast, adds another lesson. A long sequence of rulers was commemorated with intricate sculpture and inscriptions, including the famous Hieroglyphic Stairway. Later in the eighth century, a ruler was captured and killed by a neighboring polity, upsetting the balance of power. This was not an isolated exception to otherwise peaceful history. Conflict and negotiation both belonged to Classic Maya politics. An inscription may call a captive a defeated enemy and display a date in precise notation, but it rarely tells us how a farmer in the disputed region understood the change of ruler.

Royal monuments also reveal the labor of writers and artists. Glyphs required trained specialists who knew conventions of language, calendar calculation, and visual design. Carvers produced work that would be recognized by an audience familiar with names and emblems. The result is not just a list of battles. It is sophisticated political communication, combining genealogy, ritual, and history. In some cases, art and writing were inseparable: the costume of a figure and the words beside it made one argument together. Reading the words alone or treating the images as decoration would miss part of the message.

We should also ask what the inscriptions leave out. Most surviving dynastic texts favor ruling families. They tell less about village councils, market negotiations, women's labor, or how food was managed through difficult seasons. Women sometimes appear in royal genealogies and political episodes, but they were not limited to the roles assigned them by monuments. Household archaeology, environmental evidence, and studies of agriculture must be joined to political epigraphy. Otherwise, a breakthrough in reading ancient names could accidentally narrow history again to the biographies of kings.

The decline of monumental inscription at some centers after the eighth century is another source problem. Political systems changed, some cities lost population, and settlement shifted, but there was no single moment when all Maya communities vanished. Maya languages and peoples continue today. Their presence makes the common phrase Maya collapse misleading when it is treated as the disappearance of a civilization. It is more precise to investigate the fragmentation and reorganization of particular political institutions in different places, and to follow the histories that continued after those institutions changed.

The glyphs transformed the study of Classic Maya history because they allowed researchers to test broad archaeological patterns against named events. A dynasty's claim of descent can be compared with building phases. A political defeat can be compared with a change in public construction. A foreign connection can be examined alongside objects and architectural choices. None of these tests removes uncertainty, but together they show a past in which people made decisions amid competing claims of power. The Maya world was neither one empire nor a loose collection of identical cities. It was a field of relationships that history can now recover, one carved record at a time.

## A Book of Returning Days: Maya Manuscripts, Astronomy, and Lost Libraries

Turning from stone monuments to surviving books changes both the medium and the conditions of preservation. The manuscripts belong to later periods than some of the royal histories just discussed.

Picture a book that can be unfolded across a table rather than opened at a spine. Along its surface are signs, numbers, figures, and sequences of days. A reader might follow the appearance of Venus, the phases of the moon, a ritual calendar, or an image of a deity. The manuscript is not written in European letters. Its maker used a system of Maya hieroglyphic writing joined to numerals and pictures. Books like this belonged to a much larger intellectual world than the few surviving volumes allow us to see. One of the central problems of Maya history is learning from these books without forgetting the enormous library that did not survive.

The manuscript now called the Dresden Codex is held in the Saxon State and University Library in Dresden, Germany. It is an accordion-folded work made from prepared paper-like material, with painted writing and illustrations. Its modern name records the city in which it was preserved, not a place where its original author lived. The surviving codex is generally dated to the Postclassic period, long after many of the famous Classic Maya inscriptions on stone. That fact should warn us against treating it as the one book in which all Maya knowledge was first created.

Several overlapping systems of counting time appear in Maya records. A ritual calendar combines a cycle of 260 days with named days and numbers. A 365-day cycle provides another way of organizing recurring time. Long Count dates locate events within a much larger numerical sequence. These systems were related but not identical. They could serve ritual decisions, commemoration, historical dating, and the arrangement of astronomical calculations. To say that Maya scribes studied the heavens is correct; to imagine that their mathematics was completely separated from religion would impose a modern division that the manuscripts do not themselves require.

The Dresden Codex contains a particularly well-known set of pages associated with Venus. The calculations work with an approximate cycle of 584 days for the planet's recurrence relative to the sun as seen from Earth. The pages organize intervals of visibility and invisibility into a repeating scheme. This was not a casual sequence guessed by looking once at a bright star. It reflects traditions of observation, calculation, and textual organization. Yet a table is not simply a modern astronomy textbook with pictures added. Deities, directions, and ritual meanings are part of how its numerical patterns are presented.

The numbers themselves are worth pausing over. A scribe could represent quantities with dots and bars and use positional ideas adapted to the calendar's structure. A Venus table can coordinate several cycles so that some dates recur in related ritual contexts. Mathematics here provides a disciplined way to ask when an event belongs within larger patterns of time. Scholars continue to debate how individual glyphs and pictures should be interpreted, and whether a passage emphasizes observation, a historical episode, or ritual symbolism. Accurate counting does not force a single reading of every image beside the count.

Other Dresden pages concern eclipse possibilities, divine figures, and almanacs. The eclipse tables organize intervals at which solar or lunar eclipses may occur. Such intervals cannot identify every eclipse visible from one village, because visibility depends on place and other astronomical circumstances. The accomplishment was to develop an actionable structure of possible events within a calendar and religious tradition. A successful table had to be understood by users trained to read it. We do not know how many people possessed that expertise, and nothing in the surviving manuscript gives a population-wide estimate of literacy.

The Dresden book is not alone. Three other preconquest Maya codices or substantial fragments are commonly recognized: the Madrid Codex, the Paris Codex, and the Códice Maya de México, formerly known as the Grolier Codex. Their histories of discovery and preservation differ considerably. The Mexican codex became controversial because of its collecting history and doubts about authenticity. A multidisciplinary research project undertaken by Mexico's National Institute of Anthropology and History in 2017 and 2018 concluded that it was a genuine pre-Hispanic manuscript, placing its production in the early Postclassic period. Scientific testing can address materials and age, but the circumstances of looting still leave gaps in provenance.

Four surviving works cannot represent the number of books once made. Maya writing appears on pottery, stone monuments, architecture, and other objects, and these media carried different kinds of text. A royal inscription may record a birth, an accession, a war, or a dedication; a manuscript might organize calendars, divination, or specialized ritual knowledge. The survival of royal names on stone should not make us assume that all writing was royal commemoration. The disappearance of most painted books is a severe distortion of what remains available to historians.

Colonial conquest and efforts to suppress Indigenous religious practice contributed to that loss. Diego de Landa, a sixteenth-century Franciscan official in Yucatán, described the destruction of Maya books that European church authorities regarded as incompatible with Christianity. His account is simultaneously a witness to violence and part of the colonial archive through which later scholars learned fragments about Maya writing. It is important not to turn his actions into a single event that explains every lost manuscript. Books could also perish through climate, neglect, conflict, and the fragility of their materials. But deliberate destruction forms a documented part of the story.

The history of decipherment further complicates the picture. Nineteenth-century scholars began recognizing mathematical patterns in the Dresden Codex even when they could not read most of its hieroglyphic text. Later advances in understanding Maya writing made it possible to connect more of the signs with language. This has transformed interpretations of Classic inscriptions as well as manuscripts. A modern printed translation is the result of many scholars' collaborative work, corrected over time. Even a chart that looks visibly numerical can carry interpretive choices about where a sequence begins, what an event means, and how one cycle relates to another.

The manuscripts do not belong only to a vanished people. Maya languages are spoken today, and communities continue to maintain knowledge and histories that cannot be reduced to the handful of codices in distant libraries. Present-day Maya societies are not interchangeable with the people who composed a particular thirteenth-century astronomical table. There have been centuries of political, religious, and linguistic change. But speaking only of the Maya in the past tense would repeat the archival error of imagining that the loss of written artifacts marked the disappearance of communities.

What can we know about a scribal workplace from these books? We can see coordinated calligraphy, page design, calendrical conventions, and skill in combining images and signs. We can infer training and transmission of knowledge. We usually cannot name the individual who painted a specific Dresden page or reconstruct that person's whole education. The manuscript's orderly surface is evidence of labor, revision, and specialized attention, but the working process itself is rarely documented. That gap is one reason to compare surviving books with inscriptions, artistic scenes, and the material evidence of the places in which such work may have been sponsored.

It would be easy to praise Maya astronomy only because a modern calculation can confirm some numerical cycles. That would miss its historical setting. The same pages joined practical time reckoning to religious obligations, agricultural possibilities, political concerns, and the interpretation of dangerous signs. The relevant question is not whether Maya scholars happened to think exactly like modern astronomers. It is how a community of specialists developed powerful methods for handling recurring time and gave those methods meaning within its world.

An unfolded codex can still teach us to see a world of intellectual work. Its numbers are precise; its images are not decorations waiting to be stripped away; its language is not reducible to arithmetic. Yet it also points beyond itself, toward other books and other readers we can no longer identify. The proper conclusion is neither that four manuscripts tell us everything nor that their loss makes history impossible. The books that survived show the sophistication of Maya textual traditions. The books that did not survive remind us to be careful about what we claim those traditions contained.

- **[Linda R. Manzanilla, Cooperation and Tensions in Multiethnic Corporate Societies Using Teotihuacan, Central Mexico, as a Case Study, PNAS 112 (2015)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4522775/)**. Access: open_article_index_and_text_excerpt_reviewed. Teopancazco occupation phases, sectors for craft, ritual and food, 116 burials, mobility and diverse links; some social functions are interpretive.
- **[Linda R. Manzanilla, ed., Multiethnicity and Migration at Teopancazco: Investigations of a Teotihuacan Neighborhood Center, University Press of Florida (2017)](https://www.jstor.org/stable/j.ctvx0749k)**. Access: publisher_book_description_only. Book description synthesizes excavation of 116 formal burials and ties to coastal raw materials and migrants; the book full chapters not reviewed.
- **[Linda R. Manzanilla, Social and Ethnic Identity in the Classic Metropolis of Teotihuacan, in The Handbook of Mesoamerican Archaeology (2021)](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/9781119160939.ch5)**. Access: chapter_summary_only. Treats multifamily compounds as a key unit of Teotihuacan urban social organization and considers migrant identities.
- **[Instituto Nacional de Antropología e Historia, The Foreign Neighborhoods of Teotihuacan: A City of Many Cultures](https://visitateotihuacan.inah.gob.mx/en/learn/teotihuacan-foreigner-neghborhoods/)**. Access: public_full_institutional_page_reviewed. Describes Tlailotlacan Zapotec tombs and the merchants’ neighborhood; inferences about diplomatic design are presented as possibilities, not directly demonstrated rules.

###### Wikipedia orientation (supplementary; not evidence for historical claims)

- [Teotihuacan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Teotihuacan) — orientation on the people, site or document.

###### Evidence boundary

Teopancazco is one documented neighborhood center, not a definitive model for all Teotihuacan. Burials, materials and isotopes indicate a diverse population and external connections; interpretations of a medical or administrative sector and of the political meaning of mobility remain hypotheses. Do not identify individual people by an artifact style alone.

###### Wikipedia orientation (supplementary; not evidence for historical claims)

- [Teotihuacan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Teotihuacan) — orientation on the people, site or document.
- [Tikal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tikal) — orientation on the people, site or document.

###### Evidence boundary

The 378 event is documented mainly in retrospective dynastic inscriptions, especially Tikal Stela 31, not a contemporaneous narrative of battle. The same-day death of Chak Tok Ich’aak supports interpretations of coercive intervention but does not establish the detailed military operations or long-term direct Teotihuacan rule. The chronology uses commonly accepted 378 CE conversion without relying on a particular modern day-of-month.
