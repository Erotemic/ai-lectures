# Late-medieval crises and African and American centers of power

Conquest, disease, and the growth of centers of power did not produce one common late-medieval experience. Eurasian empires and plague are part of the picture; so are African and American histories that require their own timelines. We will compare institutions and evidence while keeping those differences in view.

## Many Khans, Many Empires: Conquest and Government across Eurasia

Picture a messenger changing horses at a relay station somewhere on the immense Eurasian landmass. The message might report a military victory, summon an official, or order supplies. Someone had to feed the replacement horses, maintain the road, and decide which traveler could use the station. Mongol rule is often represented by mounted warriors moving rapidly across a map. It also depended on people who stayed in place: herders, scribes, artisans, farmers, translators, and tax collectors. During the thirteenth century, leaders from the Mongolian steppe conquered territories stretching from East Asia into eastern Europe and western Asia. The resulting states changed the lives of millions, but they were never a single, undifferentiated government.

The political world of the steppe had its own histories long before the rise of Temüjin, the man later known as Chinggis Khan, or Genghis Khan. Mobile pastoral communities raised animals, navigated seasonal grazing, traded with neighboring agrarian states, and made and broke alliances. Their societies could organize substantial armies and sophisticated diplomacy. In 1206, after years of conflict and coalition building, Temüjin was acclaimed as khan of a new political confederation. The empire that followed was not created out of an absence of political institutions. It rearranged existing relationships through military organization, claims of loyalty, distribution of rewards, and formidable coercion.

Mongol armies combined mobility with intelligence gathering, coordinated command, and the capacity to absorb specialists from conquered places. They fought in open country and adapted to fortified cities using engineers and siege techniques acquired through earlier campaigns. Campaigns against the Jin state in north China, the Khwarazmian realm in Central and western Asia, and other powers extended control across very different environments. Some cities surrendered under terms; others were assaulted after resistance, sometimes with catastrophic killing and destruction. Chroniclers left accounts of massacres, but the largest numerical claims are difficult to verify. To take conquest seriously is neither to repeat every dramatic figure as fact nor to minimize the people who were killed or displaced.

The conquest of Central Asia in the early thirteenth century exposed inhabitants of trading cities, irrigated districts, and oasis communities to military violence and political reorganization. The pattern of consequences varied. A devastated quarter might later be rebuilt; a skilled metalworker or scribe might be moved far from home; a family might lose its fields, animals, or relatives. A new ruler's command could draw on older tax systems while imposing fresh demands. We rarely know the ordinary residents as individuals. Yet archaeology, tax documents, and narratives written by people who lived under these governments remind us that changes of sovereignty were experienced at the level of household survival.

After Chinggis Khan's death in 1227, succession mattered as much as battlefield success. His descendants inherited claims over territories, troops, and revenue. Over subsequent decades, Mongol-led powers emerged with related ruling lineages but increasingly distinct interests. The Yuan dynasty, established by Khubilai in 1271, ruled China after the final defeat of the Southern Song in 1279. The Ilkhanids governed much of Iran and Iraq. The Golden Horde exercised power in the western steppes and over Rus principalities. Central Asian rulers associated with the Chagatai lineage developed their own arrangements. The familiar map of four khanates is a useful simplification, not a description of permanent, equal, or neatly bounded states.

Consider Baghdad in 1258. The city had been a center of Islamic scholarship, government, commerce, and religious life for centuries. Hülegü's forces captured and devastated it, ending the political rule of the Abbasid caliph in Baghdad. This was a profound rupture for the city and those who depended on its institutions. But it did not extinguish Islamic learning, religious communities, or urban culture throughout western Asia. Later Ilkhanid courts patronized Persian writing, manuscript production, architecture, and Islamic institutions. A single event cannot contain the whole history of the regions it changed. Neither the destruction nor the later recovery should be treated as imaginary merely because one followed the other.

In China, Mongol rulers faced an administrative problem of a different scale. Governing cultivated provinces and great cities required officials familiar with land registers, revenues, transport, and judicial procedure. Khubilai's Yuan state employed practices drawn from Chinese imperial traditions as well as administrators and networks associated with the Mongol world. Policies also placed people into categories that shaped access to office and privilege. These were changing and sometimes contested arrangements, not an unchanging division between two homogeneous populations. Court painting and official histories record one portion of the story; the work of growers, transporters, builders, and urban producers kept the machinery functioning.

Across the successor states, imperial authority and local practice had to negotiate. Rulers could impose census-taking, tribute, requisitions, and military service, while relying on provincial elites and existing institutions. Some subjects served as translators, astronomers, physicians, or craftsmen at distant courts. Court mobility and protected routes offered opportunities for envoys and merchants. Yet a relay network built for military and administrative communication was not simply a public highway. Its upkeep involved material burdens, and the protection available to an accredited traveler did not make every road safe for every person.

Religious life further complicates the idea of a single Mongol civilization. Different ruling households and subjects practiced traditions including Buddhism, Islam, Christianity, and forms of steppe religious life. The Ilkhanid ruler Ghazan adopted Islam in 1295, a notable change in the political and cultural orientation of his court. In other places, state sponsorship of religious groups varied with rulers and circumstances. It is misleading to describe Mongol government either as uniformly tolerant or as uniformly persecuting. We should ask which communities received support, at what moment, and which obligations or exclusions accompanied that support.

Artists and writers worked across networks that the conquests reorganized. In illustrated Persian manuscripts and decorative objects from the Ilkhanid period, East Asian visual motifs appear alongside long-established Iranian and Islamic artistic practices. These are tangible traces of contact, not evidence that every person moved freely across an imperial continent. Travelers such as Marco Polo later became famous in European retellings, but merchants, interpreters, diplomatic envoys, and craft workers moved in many directions. No one traveler supplies a representative view of the enormous territory. The artwork and correspondence left behind are better understood as the products of specific patrons, workshops, and audiences.

The supposed Mongol peace, often called the Pax Mongolica, needs similar care. Compared with some periods of warfare, protected roads and linked courts could support trade and communication across long distances. But the empire's creation involved devastating campaigns, and warfare among its descendants continued. A merchant convoy might benefit from new guarantees while farmers elsewhere bore heavy taxes or endured an army's passage. Political unity at the highest level was also short-lived. By the fourteenth century, Yuan, Ilkhanid, Chagatai, and western steppe governments followed separate political trajectories, some undergoing civil conflict, fragmentation, or replacement.

How do we know these things? Persian chronicles, Chinese dynastic histories, Mongolian narratives, European travelers, archaeological objects, and documentary fragments each answer different questions. The Secret History of the Mongols tells a politically charged account of Mongol beginnings. Persian historians could describe conquest from within institutions affected by it. Yuan records illuminate court procedure while giving imperfect access to village experience. A manuscript painting may show the patronage of a ruler, not the full circumstances under which the image's makers worked. Reading across languages and genres helps, but the surviving archive still favors governments, elites, and dramatic incidents.

The long-term significance of the Mongol conquests is not adequately captured by a choice between an empire of destruction and an empire of exchange. Both descriptions touch real processes, and neither is a complete history. Conquest destroyed communities and transferred wealth. New courts mobilized expertise, created artistic projects, and reshaped relationships among regions that had previously been connected in other ways. Those changes did not make Eurasia one culture or guarantee mutual benefit. Return to the messenger at the relay station: behind a single journey stood many political authorities, workers, obligations, languages, and lives. That is the scale on which this history must be understood.

## A Disease with Many Histories: Plague across the Fourteenth-Century World

Disease history crosses political boundaries, but it still requires particular places and dates. Evidence from Central Asia offers one entry into the fourteenth-century plague.

In a cemetery in what is now Kyrgyzstan, stones commemorate people who died in the late 1330s. Some inscriptions associate deaths in 1338 and 1339 with pestilence. Centuries later, researchers examined human remains from nearby burials and recovered genetic material from a bacterium called Yersinia pestis. They connected the strain to a major branching in the family tree of bacteria associated with the Black Death. This is a striking meeting of inscriptions, archaeology, and ancient DNA. It helps locate an important early source of the pandemic in central Eurasia. It does not supply a list of every route the disease took or prove the precise origin of every outbreak that followed.

The Black Death is often introduced as a European catastrophe in the years 1347 to 1353. The mortality in Europe was indeed enormous, but the geography of the crisis was larger. Epidemics struck communities around the Mediterranean and in parts of Asia and North Africa, carried through environments already shaped by movement, commercial links, war, and human settlements. Not all regions were exposed in the same way or at the same time. Nor can the devastation of one city tell us exactly what happened in a distant village. The historian's task is to follow the evidence across places without imagining an uninterrupted chain wherever records are missing.

Yersinia pestis is a bacterium that can produce different clinical forms of plague. Fleas and rodents are important in its ecology, while some infections can also be transmitted by respiratory droplets under particular conditions. But identifying the pathogen does not, by itself, reconstruct each medieval transmission event. Human transport, animal hosts, climate, settlement patterns, and chance all matter, and specialists still debate the relative roles played by different routes and reservoirs. A map with a few confident arrows can be easier to draw than to defend. Ancient genomes add powerful evidence, but the people whose bones they came from cannot tell us the entire history of their journeys.

By the later 1340s, plague was spreading across the Mediterranean world. Ports, shipping lanes, markets, and roads connected coastal settlements to inland towns. Medieval sources describe sudden illness, mass funerals, fear, and the breakdown of familiar routines. Some testimonies were written by witnesses; others were compiled from reports or revised in later retellings. The vividness of an account is not a measure of its statistical accuracy. An urban chronicler might describe streets and public rituals while saying little about harvesters in surrounding districts. A religious commentator might interpret catastrophe as a moral event rather than describe the bacterium that caused it.

In European cities, church bells, burial practices, and the organization of care changed under extreme pressure. Some communities struggled to provide graves and support the sick. Others recorded new wills or emergency decisions made by civic institutions. What survives is uneven: a well-kept register may make one town appear more visible than another that experienced comparable suffering but preserved few documents. Estimates of death tolls differ by place and by the way researchers reconstruct populations. We can say the pandemic killed a very large fraction of people across many affected areas without announcing a precise universal percentage.

Cairo, Alexandria, and other cities in the eastern and southern Mediterranean also confronted waves of pestilence. Their residents lived within trade, pilgrimage, administrative, and religious networks. Muslim, Christian, and Jewish observers sometimes interpreted illness through distinct medical, social, and theological frameworks, while often sharing everyday conditions of crowding, care, and uncertainty. To study this epidemic only through European chronicles misses much of its history. Yet evidence from western Asia and North Africa is also uneven; later demographic patterns do not allow us to recover every local episode or to assign the same mortality rate across regions.

War provided further vulnerabilities. Siege, dislocation, hungry populations, and the movement of armies changed who was exposed and whose livelihoods could recover. Still, accounts that attribute an entire pandemic to one siege or to deliberate actions by one army require particular scrutiny. They can reflect rumor, retrospective explanation, or specific incidents that do not explain the whole disease process. The connection between the plague and Mongol-era routes should be investigated, not converted into a story in which a single empire consciously carried a bacterium from one edge of Eurasia to another.

Disease affected more than the people who became ill. It deprived households of caregivers and income, interrupted apprenticeships, altered landholding, and left children or older relatives without support. For a farmer, the death of neighbors could mean both a desperate shortage of help and the possibility of negotiating different terms later. For a landlord or city government, missing workers threatened rents and revenues. These effects were shaped by prior inequality. An enslaved person, a migrant laborer, a tenant, and a wealthy merchant did not have the same capacity to respond, even if the pathogen was the same.

The labor question became politically charged. In England, rulers issued the Ordinance of Labourers in 1349 and the Statute of Labourers in 1351, seeking to restrain wages and constrain workers' bargaining power. Across western Europe, different governments attempted different kinds of labor and price regulation. Historian Samuel Cohn's comparative study cautions against imagining one automatic response dictated by supply and demand. Rules expressed elite fears as well as economic calculation. Their existence also does not demonstrate that magistrates successfully enforced them everywhere or that all workers experienced the post-plague economy alike.

Fear could also become persecution. In parts of Europe, Jews were falsely accused of causing the disease through poisoning or conspiracy, and communities suffered violence, expulsion, and murder. Such accusations were not evidence about how plague spread. They reveal the danger of blaming vulnerable neighbors for a crisis no one could control. Religious processions and movements of penitence were other responses, with different meanings for participants and observers. Historians must distinguish what frightened contemporaries believed from what historical and biological evidence establishes. To repeat an accusation without identifying it as false would distort the events.

Medical explanations available to fourteenth-century people differed from germ theory. Learned practitioners could work from established traditions of diagnosis, environmental influence, and bodily balance. Households also relied on prayers, isolation, care from family and neighbors, and local knowledge. Some measures may have changed exposure, but there is no reason to imagine a single medieval medical response. We should neither mock people for not knowing bacteriology nor turn every attempt at care into a successful modern intervention. The key historical fact is that decisions had to be made under severe uncertainty.

Survivors inherited altered societies. Property could change hands; charitable institutions faced fresh demands; employers and laborers contested obligations. Yet no single transformation follows mechanically from high mortality. Social structures, politics, military conflict, agricultural ecology, and local custom shaped the results. In some regions, labor arrangements changed markedly. Elsewhere powerful institutions continued or adapted. Epidemic history therefore extends beyond the moment of infection into the struggles over work, memory, religious authority, and the allocation of resources that followed.

The research methods themselves have a history. Before ancient DNA, historians used burial registers, letters, tax accounts, and archaeological sites to infer patterns of death and transmission. Genomic work has made it possible to identify plague bacteria in some historical remains and to compare their evolutionary relationships. The 1338–1339 central Eurasian finding is compelling because independent kinds of evidence reinforce one another. Its limits are just as important: surviving skeletons represent a small sample, and microbial ancestry does not trace a merchant's exact itinerary. Biological and historical questions overlap without becoming interchangeable.

When we speak of the Black Death, we name a crisis that unfolded in many communities, leaving different records and consequences. There was a pathogen, but not one social experience. There were connections across Eurasia and the Mediterranean, but no complete list of journeys. And there were people who buried their families, argued over wages, tended the sick, or faced persecution. The task is not to remove the human history from the biological one. It is to explain both, and to remain precise about where the evidence ends.

## More than a Pilgrimage: Mali, the Sahel, and the Work of Empire

Turning to Mali changes both the region and the historical problem. The history of Sahelian power cannot be explained as an effect of Eurasian plague.

A map made on the island of Majorca in 1375 shows a ruler in western Africa holding a golden object. The ruler is Mansa Musa of Mali. The image is famous because it compresses a much larger history into a single gesture: a king, gold, and distant recognition. But the map was made far from the king's court, decades after his journey to Mecca. It tells us something about how outsiders imagined Mali and the gold trade. It does not tell us the names of the miners who produced the gold, the boat crews crossing the Niger, or the merchants who negotiated its movement across the desert. To understand Mali, we need to travel beyond the picture.

The Sahel is the broad region south of the Sahara, with different climates and societies extending across an immense distance. It is not simply a narrow line between desert and farmland. River valleys, grasslands, towns, forests farther south, and shifting routes linked communities through agriculture, livestock, fishing, craft production, and exchange. States did not arise there because traders from the Mediterranean brought political life to an otherwise empty region. Local institutions, settlement histories, and farming practices predated the famous medieval kingdoms. The Mali Empire formed within these long-standing West African developments.

The history of Mali's founding is associated with Sundiata Keita, a thirteenth-century leader whose deeds are preserved in oral traditions, including performances of the Sundiata epic. These accounts are indispensable cultural and historical sources, but their forms have changed through time, and they cannot be treated as transcripts made by someone standing beside every event. Arabophone written sources and later chronicles offer additional perspectives, each with its own distances from the founding. The conventional story of Sundiata's victory over rival rulers is a starting point for inquiry, not a complete administrative history. We do not know every stage by which a regional coalition became a durable empire.

Mali's rulers drew power from access to agricultural regions, tributary relationships, armed forces, and the movement of goods. The Niger River system joined settlements that could be difficult to connect across other routes. A city beside the river might receive fish, grain, textiles, salt, and people from multiple directions. Trade caravans crossing the Sahara brought goods and travelers between West Africa and North African towns. Those exchanges were built by merchants, guides, pack-animal handlers, and local producers. They were vulnerable to shifting political conditions and the seasonality of routes. No sultan could simply command the desert to become a permanent, frictionless road.

Gold was especially important to Mali's external reputation. Gold-bearing zones lay in regions connected to the empire through political and commercial relations, and the exact organization of production differed from the picture of a single royal mine. Merchants, intermediaries, and rulers shared in profits and levies, but often under unequal conditions. Other goods mattered as well: salt, copper, cloth, foodstuffs, and enslaved people moved through trade networks. Calling this a trade empire without describing coercion would leave out people taken or sold against their will. The evidence is stronger for the existence of these exchanges than for an exact estimate of every annual flow.

Mansa Musa, who ruled in the early fourteenth century, undertook a pilgrimage to Mecca in 1324–1325. He and his entourage passed through Cairo, where accounts of his generosity and gold became part of his fame. The visit linked royal devotion, diplomacy, social display, and the circulation of wealth. Medieval Egyptian writers reported the event from their own perspectives, sometimes with dramatic assessments of its effects on gold prices. Historians cannot produce a meaningful modern personal net-worth figure from these narratives. Musa's public expenditure reveals access to resources and an ability to mobilize people; it does not provide a balance sheet or a count of the empire's total wealth.

The pilgrimage was also part of an Islamic intellectual and diplomatic world. Muslim communities had existed in parts of the western Sahel before Musa's reign, through merchants, scholars, and local processes of conversion. A ruler's support for mosques and scholars did not make every subject Muslim, nor did it eliminate practices linked to older religious traditions. Religious identities varied within and between towns and rural communities. Royal patronage could strengthen some institutions while leaving other forms of worship and social obligation in place. This diversity should be treated as ordinary historical complexity rather than a contradiction.

Timbuktu grew as a center of trade and learning, connected to the Niger bend and trans-Saharan routes. Mosques and manuscript traditions testify to scholarly communities that developed over generations. The Djingareyber Mosque is traditionally associated with Mansa Musa, while the structures visible today reflect repeated repair and rebuilding. Much of Timbuktu's best-documented intellectual prominence belongs to the fifteenth and sixteenth centuries, not solely to the moment of Musa's pilgrimage. To attribute every school, library, or surviving manuscript to one fourteenth-century ruler is to erase later scholars, patrons, copyists, and teachers.

A second kind of witness arrived after Musa's death. The traveler Ibn Battuta visited Mali in the middle of the fourteenth century and described aspects of court ritual, security, worship, and social life. His observations are valuable precisely because they come from someone who visited and compared what he saw with his expectations from other Muslim societies. But Ibn Battuta was one person, moving through selected places and judging practices through his own social and religious assumptions. What he praised or criticized cannot represent every Mali household. Nor does his narrative settle what had happened under the empire's earlier rulers.

Mali's subjects occupied different positions. Farmers and fishers supplied food; textile and metal workers created goods for household and elite use; traders managed risk; religious specialists taught and adjudicated; soldiers enforced demands. Town and countryside relied on one another. Gender, kinship, status, and freedom shaped whose labor could be commanded or whose property could be inherited. The famous royal pilgrimage is difficult to imagine without these communities, yet the documentary record seldom describes them by name. Their historical presence must be reconstructed cautiously from settlements, artifacts, later oral knowledge, and the economic requirements of the systems we can document.

The Mali Empire was neither identical to all of West Africa nor permanently fixed at its greatest extent. The neighboring Songhai political world around Gao had its own history. During the fifteenth century, Mali's influence changed and Songhai grew in importance. Sonni Ali's capture of Timbuktu in 1468 belongs to that later reorganization. Other Sahelian and forest societies followed their own paths. An imperial title or a map's colored boundary can disguise overlapping loyalties, local autonomies, and changes in control. Political influence often mattered more than modern-style continuous territorial administration.

The evidence presents difficulties that are themselves instructive. Arabic accounts were written by travelers, merchants, and scholars, often outsiders to the communities they described. Oral traditions were transmitted by specialists whose authority, audience, and performance mattered. Archaeologists study settlement remains, ceramics, buildings, metalwork, and landscapes; many material traces survive without a ruler's name attached. The history of West Africa should not be written by accepting the European map as accurate and treating the African sources as colorful ornament. Each source needs a question it is capable of answering, and the limits of the answer should be made visible.

Return to that golden object on the 1375 map. It is useful evidence of international recognition, and it invites curiosity about the ruler it depicts. But the civilization sustaining Mali cannot be held in a king's hand. It lived in fields along rivers, in caravans and marketplaces, in prayers and legal discussions, in coercive relations and everyday cooperation. When we restore those people to the history, Mansa Musa's story becomes more meaningful, not less. It is one episode within a changing Sahelian world whose political and intellectual traditions extended well beyond a single reign.

## The Walls and the Lives Beyond Them: Great Zimbabwe and Southern Africa

Farther south, Great Zimbabwe presents another center of power with its own institutions, resources, and regional connections.

The walls of Great Zimbabwe stand without mortar. Their builders placed shaped and selected blocks of local granite in carefully arranged courses, creating tall enclosures and passages in what is now southeastern Zimbabwe. If we look only for an architectural mystery, we miss the historical work that made the settlement possible. Stone had to be obtained and carried. Food had to support households and workers. Livestock, metal, pottery, and distant trade goods moved through a regional economy. The structures survive because many people organized labor and knowledge across generations. The question is not whether an African society could construct them. The evidence establishes that it did. The more interesting question is how that society worked.

Great Zimbabwe developed within a southern African history of farming, pastoralism, settlement, and trade. Communities speaking Bantu languages had long cultivated crops, raised cattle, made pottery, and produced iron in different parts of the region. They were not all governed by one kingdom or arranged along one path of development. By roughly the eleventh century, the settlement that became Great Zimbabwe was growing. The complex was occupied and transformed through the fifteenth century. Its trajectory must be studied alongside neighboring settlements and political centers, not represented as a city rising alone in an otherwise empty landscape.

The surviving site includes areas commonly called the Hill Complex, the Great Enclosure, and valley enclosures. These labels describe physical parts of the ruins, not an exact map of medieval social classes. Researchers have proposed different sequences and uses for the enclosed spaces. The archaeology of Shadreck Chirikure and Innocent Pikirayi challenges older efforts to assign every section to a single permanent ritual or domestic function. Buildings can change purpose as rulers, households, or institutions change. A monumental wall may display political authority without being primarily designed to stop an army. Careful construction does not establish one unchanging meaning.

Think about cattle. Herds were not just units of meat or milk. In many southern African societies, cattle could be central to wealth, marriage arrangements, political relationships, and social obligation, although specific practices varied. The exchange and redistribution of livestock affected who could create alliances, sustain dependents, or support collective projects. Farming provided another foundation: fields, food storage, and labor tied households to soils and rainfall. Great Zimbabwe's public buildings were not separate from these activities. Their scale suggests organized resources, but we must not infer an exact census or a fixed taxation system solely from the dimensions of the stonework.

Metals were another part of the region's economy. Gold mining and exchange connected parts of the Zimbabwe plateau and other southern African zones to trading systems reaching the coast. Copper, iron, and locally made objects circulated too. Inland networks involved African producers and intermediaries before goods reached the shores of the Indian Ocean. These histories were not simply responses to overseas demand; regional needs and political ambitions mattered. Traders who arrived by sea could purchase commodities, but they did not create the knowledge needed to mine, farm, herd, or build the interior settlements.

Archaeologists have recovered imported glass beads, ceramics, and other objects at Great Zimbabwe. Some originated through networks linking the Indian Ocean with parts of Asia. Such finds establish material connections across long distances, though they rarely trace a single object through every hand that carried it. A piece of foreign porcelain discovered inland does not mean foreign merchants governed the settlement, let alone designed its stone walls. It shows that a local community participated in exchanges that reached far beyond its immediate region. That distinction is crucial to both archaeological interpretation and historical accuracy.

East African coastal settlements acted as important intermediaries in these networks. Goods passed through ports and along routes whose prominence changed over time. We can connect Great Zimbabwe to coastal commerce while avoiding the assumption that a particular imported artifact arrived on one named ship. Traders had to negotiate languages, payments, environmental hazards, and local political relationships. Ocean traffic followed seasonal conditions, and inland journeys required people with regional knowledge. The connections were extensive, but they were neither continuous nor under a single commercial administration.

The stone complexes also need their absent buildings restored to the story. Most people did not live their lives inside the best-preserved monumental walls. Earthen houses, hearths, craft areas, fields, and livestock spaces left different traces and are more easily destroyed or overlooked. Many settlement features were made of materials that do not survive like granite. If we reconstruct the city solely from the stone structures, elites acquire an enormous voice and ordinary households nearly disappear. Archaeological research into ceramics, occupation layers, and spatial patterns can correct that imbalance, though it cannot always identify particular families.

Religious and political meanings remain debated. Carved soapstone birds and parts of the site continue to carry cultural significance, but a bird sculpture alone does not reveal the complete theology or institutional structure of the people who made it. Later Shona cultural traditions are important for developing and testing interpretations. They should be taken seriously rather than treated as decorative folklore. At the same time, no present-day tradition automatically provides a verbatim account of a specific thirteenth-century ceremony. Researchers must weigh continuity and change rather than deciding in advance that the answers are obvious.

Great Zimbabwe's modern interpretation has an especially consequential history. In the nineteenth and twentieth centuries, some European writers and colonial settlers attributed the ruins to outsiders, including invented biblical or Mediterranean builders. These claims were not supported by the archaeology. They were bound up with a refusal to recognize African achievement and local knowledge. Later excavations demonstrated the site's African origins, while also sometimes damaging deposits that could have provided further evidence. The struggle over authorship shows how politics can shape which questions investigators ask and whose explanations they dismiss.

African workers took part in the excavations, clearing, transport, and maintenance on which colonial-era research depended, but received little authority in published interpretation. Archaeological histories written only as a succession of European investigators can reproduce this imbalance. Recent scholarship has called attention to field labor, local expertise, and decisions about preservation. Recognizing these contributions does not mean rejecting material methods. It means understanding that excavation and conservation themselves occur within social relationships and can preserve or destroy evidence. A wall is a historical source; so is the record of how it was studied.

Why did Great Zimbabwe change and eventually cease to be the same kind of major center? Historians have explored political shifts, regional trade changes, environmental pressures, and relations among competing settlements. There is no responsible single-sentence answer in which the city simply collapsed because it exhausted its land or lost an overseas market. Other political centers, including those associated with the Mutapa sphere, developed in southern Africa. The history did not end when occupation and monumental building diminished at one site. People moved, institutions changed, and cultural practices continued in new settings.

Great Zimbabwe therefore teaches two histories at once. One concerns the people who built and used a powerful southern African center between roughly the eleventh and fifteenth centuries. The other concerns how later observers tried to claim, deny, excavate, and protect that past. The walls cannot speak all their makers' names, but they do not require an imaginary foreign architect to explain them. They belong to an African landscape of skilled labor, political organization, social inequality, religious meaning, and far-reaching exchange. We understand the site better when we study all of those relationships rather than asking the stones to stand alone.

## More than One Capital: Maya and Mexica Worlds before 1500

Across the Atlantic, Maya and Mexica histories belong to a separate political sequence. Their inclusion here is a comparison across the later medieval centuries, not a claim of contact with the African kingdoms.

A visitor entering Tenochtitlan in the late fifteenth century would have crossed water, causeways, and a landscape shaped by people. Houses, temples, markets, and cultivated areas occupied an island city in Lake Texcoco. Its prosperity depended on farmers, merchants, water management, military power, and tribute demanded from other communities. But to call it the capital of Mesoamerica would be misleading. Other powerful states existed, including some that resisted Mexica expansion. Farther southeast, Maya-speaking peoples maintained cities, political institutions, religions, and commerce. The period from 1200 to 1500 was not the aftermath of a vanished civilization. It was an era of living societies with intersecting but distinct histories.

Mesoamerica is a modern term for a region of shared and interacting cultural traditions, including parts of present-day Mexico and Central America. It was never a single empire. Languages, local governments, agricultural systems, trade relationships, writing traditions, and religious practices differed across its territories. Crops including maize and beans were central to many communities, although cultivation techniques were varied. Major cities rose and declined at different times. Looking at one famous monument and placing the entire region under a single chronology erases this complexity. In particular, the political changes of the ninth-century southern Maya lowlands did not mean Maya peoples disappeared.

In the northern Yucatán peninsula, the city of Mayapan became an influential political center during the centuries after Chichén Itzá's earlier prominence. Its walls and dense settlement document an organized community rather than an empty ceremonial ground. Historical reconstructions describe shifting alliances among regional elites and contested authority. Around the middle of the fifteenth century, political upheaval and the decline of Mayapan left northern Yucatán divided among multiple centers. People did not stop speaking Maya languages, cultivating fields, exchanging goods, or practicing religion when a capital lost control. The ending of a particular political arrangement is not the end of a people.

In the Guatemalan highlands, K'iche' and neighboring states formed a different political landscape. Their rulers maintained relationships of war, tribute, kinship, and ritual; important centers included Q'umarkaj, also known as Utatlán. Some later Indigenous histories preserve dynastic accounts and founding narratives. These texts are valuable, but the manuscripts familiar to scholars were copied or recorded under conditions shaped by Spanish conquest and Christian colonial rule. Archaeological remains, language history, and written traditions can complement one another. None should be reduced to the claim that highland Maya society was merely an offshoot of a distant central Mexican capital.

In central Mexico, the Mexica were among several groups competing for authority around the Basin of Mexico. Tenochtitlan, traditionally founded in the fourteenth century, grew within a regional system of cities and political alliances. Early Mexica leaders were not automatically dominant. They navigated rivalries involving places such as Azcapotzalco and Texcoco. Around 1427 or 1428, Tenochtitlan joined Texcoco and Tlacopan in the coalition usually called the Triple Alliance, whose victory over Azcapotzalco marked a major shift in the basin. The coalition's leading partners had their own rulers and interests, even as Mexica power later became preeminent.

Political expansion reshaped the region. Conquered city-states often retained local authorities but were obliged to provide tribute or other services. Demands could include food, textiles, valuable materials, or military cooperation. Tribute officials, local rulers, and transporting workers helped move goods toward powerful centers. The arrival of wealth at the capital depended on obligations imposed elsewhere. A flourishing market and a coercive empire could exist at the same time. Archaeological objects and later tribute lists show types of goods and relationships, but they do not provide an exact, universally enforced tax ledger for every community throughout the fifteenth century.

Tenochtitlan's urban life depended on the basin's environment. Causeways and canals organized movement. Chinampas, cultivated plots made and managed in wetland zones, supported highly productive agriculture, but their maintenance required continuing work and ecological knowledge. Marketplaces connected producers and consumers across districts. Craftspeople worked with stone, wood, feathers, fiber, and metals, each material carrying different costs and meanings. People had households, families, obligations, and celebrations; their lives were not exhausted by public ceremonies at temples. The city's built grandeur is inseparable from the labor of those who constructed and supplied it.

Religion and political authority were closely connected, but neither can be explained by a single monument. Deities, calendars, ceremonies, and institutions shaped communal life. Warfare and human sacrifice were part of some state rituals, and records of those practices need careful evaluation. Conquest-era Spanish accounts were written within conflict and polemic, sometimes stressing violence for their own political or religious purposes. Indigenous accounts preserve other positions, but many survive in later versions. Archaeology can substantiate ritual activity while seldom revealing the full meaning participants assigned to it. We should avoid either sensationalizing ritual violence or pretending it was absent.

Beyond the Mexica-led alliance stood polities that did not submit to it. In western Mexico, the powerful Purépecha or Tarascan state resisted Mexica conquest. Tlaxcalan polities to the east retained independence from Mexica rule. Across Oaxaca, Mixtec and Zapotec communities continued their own histories, sometimes negotiating tributary relationships and sometimes resisting them. The map of an empire is not a map of universal political agreement. For families in frontier areas, borders could shift through warfare, alliance, diplomacy, and migration. Their choices had consequences even when most surviving histories were written in the names of rulers.

Trade linked communities across Mesoamerica without eliminating local economies. Obsidian, ceramics, cacao, cloth, salt, and luxury goods moved through overlapping networks. A port or market might bring together travelers speaking several languages. Inland roads and maritime routes mattered differently depending on geography. These connections were extensive, but do not establish a single unified market with one currency or one authority. Long-distance exchange could coexist with household production and with tribute exacted by force. The history of trade must account for both negotiated movement and political obligations.

The evidence comes from several kinds of records. Maya inscriptions from earlier centuries help us understand long traditions of writing and dynastic commemoration, but they cannot narrate every event after 1200. Late pre-Hispanic and colonial-era codices preserve information in forms shaped by their makers and transmission. Archaeological surveys document settlement changes, and excavated objects show the movement of goods. Spanish accounts from the sixteenth century describe societies after war and epidemic had altered them. A source written after 1521 can preserve earlier knowledge while also reflecting colonial circumstances. That is why dates, institutions, and quotations require careful contextualization.

The year 1500 is not the climax of one Mesoamerican narrative. The Mexica-led alliance continued to expand in some directions, while other polities remained autonomous. Maya regions contained independent governments and communities with distinct languages and histories. In the decades after 1500, war, imposed rule, epidemic disease, and new alliances would transform the region, but those events should not be read backward as destiny. The existence of rivalries was not evidence that conquest had to succeed. It was evidence that the political world was active, contested, and changing before Europeans arrived.

Tenochtitlan, Mayapan, Q'umarkaj, and the many towns between them give us a way to speak about Mesoamerican history without reducing it to one empire or one catastrophe. Cities were built, sustained, disputed, and sometimes reorganized; languages and communities persisted. The important question is not which civilization vanished and which replaced it. It is how ordinary work, political power, religious commitments, and regional relations shaped the lives of people who occupied these places. Their history was underway long before outsiders tried to describe it.
