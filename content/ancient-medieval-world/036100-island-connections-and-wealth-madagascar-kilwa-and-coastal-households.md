# Island connections and wealth: Madagascar, Kilwa, and coastal households

Plants, coins, and house floors can illuminate connections across the western Indian Ocean. They also require different kinds of inference: a crop can suggest movement across the sea, while an object beneath a floor may belong to the social life of one household. We will keep those scales separate.

## Rice across an Ocean: Madagascar, the Comoros, and Early Island Settlement

A charred grain of rice is small enough to disappear into the soil. But when archaeologists recover such grains from an old settlement, identify the plant, and date the surrounding deposits, they acquire evidence of a journey much larger than any one seed. In the islands of the western Indian Ocean, plants brought from Asia help illuminate a history that written documents barely describe: the settlement of Madagascar and the Comoros by people moving between Africa and Southeast Asia. The story is not of a single ship arriving once. It is of communities, crops, languages, and habits becoming part of new island worlds over generations.

Madagascar lies off the southeastern coast of Africa, separated from it by the Mozambique Channel. Its people speak Malagasy, an Austronesian language whose distant relatives are found across island Southeast Asia and the Pacific. Linguists have long recognized that linguistic connection. Modern population genetics also documents ancestry linked to both Africa and Asia among Malagasy populations. Neither observation is a passenger list. A language does not tell us how many voyages occurred, and inherited DNA cannot identify the language each ancestor spoke or the terms under which a person traveled. The dates, routes, and relative contributions of different migrations remain subjects of investigation.

Archaeologists face a different problem. Where are the physical signs of those movements? Some early settlements on Madagascar and nearby islands were occupied during the first millennium of the Common Era, but the exact beginnings of human presence and the kinds of activity represented by the earliest traces are debated. A stone tool or a deposit of charcoal can indicate an activity without identifying the people's place of origin. Even pottery styles can cross water through learning and exchange. For decades, the clearest evidence for a Southeast Asian connection seemed to be language and later populations, with little obviously Southeast Asian material at excavated sites.

Researchers led by Alison Crowther and colleagues approached the question through plants. They studied archaeological crop remains from sites around the eastern African coast, the Comoros, and Madagascar. Their 2016 study reported Asian crops including rice and mung bean in island assemblages, with evidence also for crops associated with African farming traditions. This was a new way to see mobility: not through the design of a palace or the name of a dynasty, but through the food people grew, stored, prepared, and burned. Some of the relevant deposits are much later than the earliest proposed human arrival, so they cannot by themselves date a founding voyage.

A crop can reveal intention as well as contact. Merchants can transport rice for consumption, but a community relying on the plant regularly may bring seed, knowledge of cultivation, and people who know when and where it will grow. Fields require labor across seasons, decisions about water, and ways to protect harvests. The presence of rice therefore invites questions about households and landscapes, not just trade. It does not prove every farmer came from an Asian-speaking community. Once a plant enters an island economy, knowledge and seed can pass among people of very different ancestry and language.

At the site of Mahilaka in northwestern Madagascar, archaeological evidence points to an important settlement integrated into western Indian Ocean exchange. On islands farther north, including the Comoros, excavated plant remains contribute another part of the picture. Crowther and colleagues concluded that early Asian crop introductions were not confined to Madagascar and may involve routes through the Comoros or the East African coast. That is a research interpretation consistent with the material they studied, not the discovery of a named expedition. There is still room for other routes and for more than one phase of settlement.

What does the African side of this history look like? The East African coast had its own communities, maritime expertise, crops, and commercial networks. The western Indian Ocean was not an empty bridge waiting to connect two distant endpoints. African societies participated in exchanges involving food plants, cattle, technologies, religious practices, and labor. Over time the languages, livelihoods, and identities of Madagascar formed from interactions that cannot be reduced to a simple half-African, half-Asian formula. Genetic studies find substantial variation among Malagasy communities and do not settle the precise historical circumstances of migration.

The sea itself shaped the possibilities. Monsoon winds create seasonal patterns across the Indian Ocean, but reaching and settling an island also requires local navigation, knowledge of currents, the capacity to carry food and water, and the ability to learn unfamiliar coasts. We should not imagine every vessel following one permanent trade road. Some travelers may have moved through coastal settlements and intermediate islands. Others may have crossed longer stretches of open water. Our sources do not supply a logbook for these early voyages, and a plausible sailing reconstruction is not proof that an identified group used it.

Settlement changed environments, too. People brought crops, animals, fire practices, and ways to build houses and work soils. Madagascar's ecological history is complicated, and some changes in vegetation or animal populations have been used to argue for early human activity. Dates and causes are debated. One cannot turn every sign of burning into an intentional agricultural field, or every species decline into evidence of a particular migrant group. Climate variation, animal ecology, and different forms of land use must be examined together. Archaeological sites supply local snapshots, not a complete history of the island's forests.

A further challenge comes from the way archaeologists recover food. Seeds and grains survive unevenly; some are burned and preserved, while others decay. Plants grown from tubers or cuttings may leave fewer recognizable charred grains than cereals. An excavation that finds no rice is not necessarily evidence that nobody ate rice. Conversely, a handful of grains does not measure how important the crop was in every meal. Researchers use flotation, microscopy, comparisons with modern plants, and radiocarbon dating to make such small fragments informative. The method gives us material anchors while reminding us that what survives is only a portion of what people used.

By the later medieval centuries, Madagascar and the Comoros were tied into circuits of exchange reaching African ports, Arabia, South Asia, and beyond. But it would be a mistake to read later political divisions or contemporary cultural identities directly backward into the first centuries of settlement. Kinship, community boundaries, and social hierarchies developed across time. People who carried seeds were not necessarily free merchants; travelers could include families, workers, dependents, and people transported unwillingly. For the early periods we rarely have the documents needed to identify such lives individually. We can acknowledge that absence without inventing a cast of passengers.

The result is a history pieced together by several disciplines that do not always agree on dates or mechanisms. Comparative linguistics links Malagasy to Austronesian speech; genetics reveals the complexity of ancestry; archaeology finds settlements and objects; archaeobotany follows plants across water. When those strands converge, we can say something substantial: Madagascar's population history emerged from long-distance movements involving both African and Asian worlds, and the Comoros were implicated in those connections. When they diverge, we should keep the question open rather than force a tidy origin story.

Return to the charred rice. It survived because food met fire at one particular place and time. Later researchers identified it, compared it with crops known elsewhere, and made it part of a larger argument about maritime migration. A history once told mostly through the resemblance of words could now also be told through the remains of meals. The grain does not name the people who planted it. It does show that the human geography of the Indian Ocean was being remade not only through kings and cargoes, but through the slow, repeated work of making a home.

## Coins beneath the House: Kilwa and the Meaning of Swahili Wealth

The scale now narrows from movement across the ocean to a deposit beneath one house on the East African coast.

More than three hundred small copper coins were found together beneath the floor of a stone house at Songo Mnara, off the Tanzanian coast. Some carried names associated with rulers of nearby Kilwa. Their presence invites an obvious question: had someone hidden savings? Archaeologists offer a more specific possibility. Because the deposit was built into a foundation together with other valued objects, the coins may have marked a new house and tied prosperity to a place. They were both objects with economic uses and objects that could have social meaning. This small discovery leads beyond the great harbors and palaces of medieval East Africa into the rooms where people lived.

Kilwa Kisiwani and Songo Mnara were towns of the Swahili coast, a long belt of African communities connected by sea travel, farming, fishing, and trade. Their prosperity increased especially between the thirteenth and fifteenth centuries. Kilwa's merchants handled goods that moved between inland Africa and the Indian Ocean, including gold and ivory, while imported ceramics, beads, and other objects arrived from distant ports. These towns were not foreign colonies set down on an empty shore. Their language and culture developed in African coastal societies that interacted with Arabian and Asian worlds over many centuries.

The standing buildings show what wealth could make possible. Kilwa had an important congregational mosque and extensive stone architecture. The palace complex known as Husuni Kubwa was built in the early fourteenth century. Its scale advertised the power of the rulers who could command materials and workers. Yet a stone ruin hides most of the labor necessary to keep a town operating. Food had to arrive at markets, boats had to be repaired, timber obtained, stone cut, and households provisioned. Imported porcelain does not mean that every resident ate from imported vessels. Archaeology must examine where objects were found and who could use them.

The coins offer a route into these questions. Kilwa minted copper pieces bearing Arabic inscriptions, and a number survive with names associated with different rulers. Numismatists can compare inscriptions and styles to propose sequences. But the age of a coin is not always the age of its final use. The Songo Mnara deposit contains types apparently produced at different times, some much older than the house in which they were placed. This matters because the same coin could circulate, be kept, and gain new meaning for generations. Its original inscription was only one part of its later history.

Stephanie Wynne-Jones and Jeffrey Fleisher ask us not to divide coins neatly into either currency or symbols. Archaeological context shows multiple kinds of value. A copper piece might help in a transaction, but it could also be placed in a foundation or associated with an event worth commemorating. Without the context, a museum's isolated coin cannot tell us which use it had. This does not mean monetary exchange was absent. It means that a familiar modern category—money—does not cover everything an object did. The same problem appears in many societies, including those with sophisticated commercial systems.

The geographical networks behind the coins were extensive. Ocean navigation was seasonal, shaped by monsoon winds, currents, and the knowledge of pilots. Ships could move between East African ports, Arabia, western India, and beyond through relays of exchange. Not every foreign ceramic traveled on a ship that sailed directly from its production site to Kilwa. Goods could pass through multiple ports, each adding merchants, costs, and relationships. The idea of a single long-distance trade route can conceal this relay economy. It can also conceal the African inland producers whose labor supplied high-value exports.

At Songo Mnara, an excavated house is more informative than a reconstructed story of a merchant prince. The foundation deposit is associated with a particular building event. Its coins and accompanying material suggest that the people constructing or occupying the building placed value deliberately into its fabric. Researchers can describe the deposit's location and the range of coin types. They cannot identify the precise person who chose it or reconstruct a spoken ceremony from the objects alone. The distinction protects an important historical insight from being buried beneath an appealing but unsupported anecdote.

The towns also contained religious and social institutions. Mosques were sites of worship and gathering, and Arabic inscriptions linked local rulers and communities to broader Muslim traditions. Yet speaking of Islamic Swahili towns should not erase their African languages and local institutions. Muslim communities on the coast developed through particular histories of trade, settlement, and marriage, not through a uniform replacement of existing life. The meanings of stone houses, mosques, pottery, and coins were made locally. Indian Ocean connections expanded the possibilities available to residents; they did not define every aspect of their identities.

There is also a difference between trade volume and control. A ruler might collect revenue at a harbor, sponsor buildings, issue coins, or claim precedence among nearby settlements. None of these alone proves that every boat, cargo, or village belonged to the ruler. Archaeologists reconstruct political power through the distribution of goods, settlement patterns, monumental investment, and written accounts when they survive. Some powerful centers may have depended as much on partnership with merchants as on direct command. A city's skyline could proclaim authority, while its everyday economy required continual cooperation across households and ports.

We should be equally careful about decline. Portuguese attacks and commercial intervention in the early sixteenth century changed coastal politics, but the history of Swahili communities did not stop there. The monuments most visible to visitors today also underwent centuries of damage, rebuilding, and conservation. Songo Mnara's ruined houses are not evidence that the town's inhabitants had no descendants or that an entire culture disappeared. Many Swahili towns remained active, while networks shifted and political centers changed. The archaeological period of a particular complex is only one chapter of a longer coastal history.

Why did people place coins in a house? We can propose meanings because the pattern is documented, yet the exact intention is unknowable. Perhaps the deposit marked a foundation and an enduring investment; perhaps other customary expectations were involved. The coins would have been recognizable within a world of markets and political names, but they entered a new setting under the floor. This is the advantage of excavation over collecting objects without provenance. It reveals an action, not just an artifact. A copper piece can tell a different story when we know the room in which it was left.

Kilwa's rulers, the traders who crossed the sea, and the people who worked in stone houses belonged to the same coastal economy. Their resources and influence were not equal. The palace reflects one concentration of power. The foundation deposit reflects a choice made within a household, even if we cannot name that household. Together they make the medieval Swahili coast more than a map of imported luxuries. Its history was made by African communities deciding what to build, what to trade, what to keep, and how to connect a home to a world extending far beyond the horizon.

## Inside the Stone Town: Households and Public Life on the Swahili Coast

Keeping that household scale in view, we can ask how rooms, courtyards, and food remains illuminate wider life within the stone town.

A visitor arriving by sea at a medieval town on the East African coast might first notice coral-stone houses, a mosque, and the movement of boats. Yet an archaeologist studying that town can learn as much from a hearth, a courtyard, or a few charred seeds as from a surviving wall. At Songo Mnara, off the southern coast of present-day Tanzania, researchers have investigated how people lived within and around buildings during the fourteenth and fifteenth centuries. Their findings help us move beyond a history in which the Swahili coast is important only because foreign merchants came to its ports. These were African towns, inhabited by people with households, obligations, beliefs, and economies of their own.

Songo Mnara was connected with Kilwa Kisiwani, a major center in the western Indian Ocean. Maritime routes carried people, ceramics, beads, cloth, and knowledge around the coast and across the sea. Winds and sailing seasons mattered, but the ocean did not dictate one simple story of foreign influence. Coastal communities shaped exchange through their own skills and institutions. Merchants needed the cooperation of townspeople who organized landings, storage, food supplies, craftwork, and markets. Histories based only on imported objects risk erasing those local contributions. A foreign bowl in an African house tells us that an object traveled; it does not tell us that its owner was foreign or that the household imitated someone else's culture.

Stone buildings are part of the evidence, but their survival creates a bias. Coral architecture lasts in ways that earth-and-thatch houses often do not. If a historian maps only the visible stone ruins, much of the settlement disappears. Archaeologists working at Songo Mnara have therefore tested domestic spaces, open areas, and less monumental structures. Stephanie Wynne-Jones and Jeffrey Fleisher helped direct this work; other specialists have examined soils, botanical remains, and the spatial distribution of artifacts. Each method seeks a different trace of activity. Together they make it possible to ask who cooked, where people gathered, and how households used spaces that written histories barely mention.

The organization of a house is not transparent from its plan. A room enclosed by thick walls might have held people, stored objects, or served as a setting for a particular social event at different times. Archaeologists examine floor deposits and objects left in place, while remembering that many valuable things were carried away when a house was reused or abandoned. At Songo Mnara, attention to entrances, courtyards, and movement through a building can reveal patterns of access and separation. But we cannot assign each room to a named woman or man merely from its size or position. An excavation uncovers patterns of use, not complete domestic diaries.

Food remains bring another scale of life into view. Archaeobotanist Sarah Walshaw studied charred plant remains from Songo Mnara and compared samples from stone structures, earthen buildings, and ritual areas. Such evidence contributes to the history of cooking, agriculture, and craft activity. A seed recovered by flotation has survived a chain of events: cultivation or gathering, transport, processing, burning, burial, and excavation. It may represent food, fuel contamination, or some other use. Comparison across spaces can reveal meaningful differences, but it should not be turned into a universal menu for every Swahili family. Food systems joined coastal fishing and regional farming to imported and locally available ingredients.

Public places complicate the familiar division between rich houses and busy markets. Jeffrey Fleisher and Federica Sulas used geophysical survey, soil chemistry, test pits, and artifact distributions to investigate open spaces at Songo Mnara. Their study found evidence for activities and for the ways those spaces were maintained and protected. This matters because an open courtyard can be an institution without a roof: a site for gathering, work, circulation, or public recognition. A blank area on an architectural plan is not necessarily unused. Archaeology that studies absence of buildings can recover forms of social life that monument-focused surveys overlook.

Coins offer another example. Coins of types associated with Kilwa have been found at Songo Mnara, including deposits studied in relation to buildings. They reveal monetary imagery and some practices of storing or placing metal objects, but finding a coin does not prove a modern cash economy in which everyone paid wages by the day. Coin circulation can overlap with gifts, credit, customary payments, and ritual uses. Researchers have argued that a coin deposit within a building foundation may have been a way to invest value in a place, not simply money accidentally dropped. Context matters: a coin on a floor, in a foundation, and in a buried hoard may have three different histories.

Mosques were central structures in many Swahili towns. Their presence reflects the importance of Islam in local life, with religious practice shaped by communities on the African coast. Stone mosques connect these towns to wider Muslim networks, but worship was not imported as an unchanged package from a distant center. Congregations required local teachers, care of buildings, communal time, and knowledge transmitted between generations. Archaeology can locate some places of worship and the ways towns were arranged around them. It cannot assume that everyone understood religion in the same way or that all households practiced identically. A shared faith is not proof of social uniformity.

Women and families are especially difficult to recover from this material. The archaeological emphasis on household space opens useful questions about food preparation, child care, domestic production, and social relationships. But assigning a task exclusively to women from later ethnography would be an unjustified shortcut. Material remains rarely state the gender of the person who used them. Historians can discuss documented gendered roles in later Swahili societies, then ask which features are consistent with medieval archaeological traces and which remain unknown. This is more informative than declaring that every bead or cooking pot belonged to a woman. It also gives women's labor a place in the historical questions without making the evidence say too much.

Songo Mnara was not the whole Swahili coast. Towns such as Kilwa, Mombasa, and smaller settlements differed in political fortunes, access to trade, building materials, and local hinterland relationships. A specialized excavation in southern Tanzania cannot provide a universal architectural or economic model for all of East Africa. Inland farmers, herders, woodcutters, craftspeople, and carriers sustained coastal life through networks that did not stop at the beach. Political authority could involve merchant families, religious figures, and rulers, but specific arrangements changed from place to place. Treating towns as isolated trading posts makes the African landscapes around them vanish.

Our written sources create another imbalance. Foreign travelers and later chroniclers sometimes focused on rulers, mosques, marvels, and maritime wealth. They rarely described how a family divided its work over a week. Some chronicles were written after the events they claim to record and need to be evaluated against dates and archaeological contexts. Songo Mnara is particularly revealing because it lacks a rich surviving written narrative of its own, despite the scale of its remains. Excavation cannot supply every missing voice, but it shows that an unmentioned town was nevertheless a place of dense activity and historical significance.

The coast's past has also been distorted by claims that substantial African towns must have been founded by outsiders. Imported goods and connections across the Indian Ocean are real, but exchange does not cancel local agency. Architecture, foodways, languages, and settlement histories show African communities participating in and transforming those connections. A responsible account neither denies movement from overseas nor uses it as the explanation for everything impressive on the coast. People can borrow, trade, intermarry, travel, and still build institutions rooted in their own places. The question is not whether Swahili history was African or connected to the wider world. It was both.

The lives of ordinary residents also depended on material constraints. Coral stone had to be cut and transported. Drinking water and fuel had to be supplied. Buildings needed repairs. Boats required timber, equipment, and crews; markets required goods and trustworthy relationships. Elite monuments do not stand apart from these activities. They depend on them. Investigating the placement of hearths, the use of public spaces, and the sources of food reveals the many people whose work allowed towns to flourish. It also helps us think about social differentiation: some residents occupied stone houses, while others may have lived in structures that leave fewer traces.

By the fifteenth century Songo Mnara was part of a sophisticated network of African coastal towns. Its architecture and objects provide evidence for trade and political importance, but its smallest discoveries tell a complementary story. Seeds, soils, pottery, and courtyards reveal patterns of eating, making, gathering, and keeping a home. The most valuable question is not only what goods traveled across the Indian Ocean, but what people did with them after they arrived. When historians attend to household life, the Swahili coast becomes more than a chain of ports on someone else's map. It becomes a set of inhabited places with histories made from within.
