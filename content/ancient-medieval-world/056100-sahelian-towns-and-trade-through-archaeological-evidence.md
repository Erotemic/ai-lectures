# Sahelian towns and trade through archaeological evidence

The Sahel has urban and agricultural histories that precede the famous accounts of gold crossing the Sahara. We will begin with settlements and household landscapes, then move to cities of the inland Niger delta and the material work of medieval trade.

## Houses of Stone on the Sahel: Farming and Community at Dhar Tichitt

Across parts of southeastern Mauritania, stone-walled enclosures run along escarpments above the lower plains. Some outline household compounds; others belong to larger settlements. They are the remains of communities that lived in an environment where cultivation, herding, and seasonal movement were all important. The ancient settlements of Dhar Tichitt and neighboring plateaus rarely appear in the standard histories built around Egypt, Mesopotamia, or the kingdoms of later West Africa. Yet they offer a major body of evidence for how societies in the Sahel organized labor, space, and food production long before the medieval empires of Ghana and Mali.

The word dhar refers here to the plateau landscapes where many of the archaeological sites occur. Dhar Tichitt forms part of a broader group of archaeological regions, including Dhar Walata and others. The communities did not leave a written chronicle that tells us their rulers' names or the dates of each building. Archaeologists instead document walls, ceramics, tools, plant remains, graves, and the relationships among settlements. These are traces of repeated work. A settlement's plan may reflect how households lived and stored goods, but its stones cannot announce the language of every inhabitant.

The sequence developed over long stretches of the second millennium before the Common Era. Earlier phases in the region included mobile herding and hunting communities; later phases saw the growth of substantial stone-built settlements and increasingly important cultivation. Archaeologists describe agropastoral economies, combining livestock with crops. It is misleading to imagine an instant leap from wandering herders to a fully formed city. The number and size of settlements varied, and different sites may have specialized in different activities. People could move seasonally while maintaining places where they invested labor in walls, storage, or fields.

One crop is especially important to the story: pearl millet. It is well adapted to many Sahelian environments and became central to farming in parts of West Africa. Archaeological studies of the region discuss millet remains and impressions in pottery, along with grinding stones and storage features. A seed or its imprint can demonstrate cultivation or processing in a particular context, but it cannot tell us the total harvest or whether every household grew the crop. Grain production depended on rainfall, soil, planting choices, and networks of labor. A stone enclosure is a useful place to begin asking where those harvests went and who managed them.

What does it take to build with stone on such a scale? People had to gather material, decide where walls belonged, and repair them after weathering or use. The shape of a compound may guide circulation among living, work, and storage spaces. It may also reveal boundaries of household cooperation or restriction. Archaeologists study the arrangement of features within and between compounds rather than assuming every wall was defensive. A large settlement can emerge from aggregated household spaces without requiring a palace in the middle. Equally, the absence of a preserved palace is not proof that social hierarchy was absent.

Research at Dakhlet el Atrouss and other sites tests exactly that problem. Archaeologists have examined differences in the size and placement of buildings, material assemblages, and burial architecture to consider whether economic and political differences became more pronounced over time. The evidence supports serious discussion of social differentiation, but it does not provide a list of hereditary classes. A well-built enclosure could indicate many things, including a larger household or different work needs. A valuable object could have circulated through exchange without identifying its owner's official rank. A theory of inequality has to be built from patterns, not one impressive ruin.

Much of the region's history has been organized through pottery categories and radiocarbon dates. Ceramic styles help correlate sites, but similarities can spread through exchange, imitation, or shared practice. Carbon dates need to be associated with specific archaeological layers. Some of the foundational excavations were conducted in the late 1960s, and their dates supported a long-standing chronological framework. In 2026, researchers reported new accelerator mass spectrometry dates from preserved charcoal samples from those early excavations. Their reassessment offered greater precision while also making gaps in the original sequence more evident. A chronology should be revised when new measurements support it, not protected simply because it appears in an established diagram.

That revision changes how a listener should understand the words early and late. A potsherd in a later layer may have been made earlier and reused; a charcoal sample may have come from fuel cut before a building was occupied. A radiocarbon result is an estimate with a probability range, not a calendar label attached to every stone. Archaeologists compare multiple samples, stratigraphic relationships, and material patterns to determine phases of occupation. When studies disagree, we should identify the specific evidence behind their models. The disagreements reveal the work needed to turn an impressive landscape of walls into a responsibly dated history.

Climate is part of the explanation for change. Across the Holocene, the Sahara and Sahel experienced significant shifts in rainfall and vegetation. The availability of water could influence where millet farming, animal grazing, and settlements were viable. Research proposes that increasing aridity affected communities in the Tichitt-Walata zone toward the end of major occupation phases. But it is hazardous to say drought emptied all the settlements on one date. People could alter farming, shift herds, reorganize trade, or move among regions. Environmental change created pressures and opportunities whose social consequences varied.

These settlements matter also because they interrupt a familiar sequence that begins West African history with later written descriptions of Ghana. The landscapes of Mauritania and Mali had deep histories of cultivation, village growth, exchange, and social organization before Arabic geographers wrote of powerful Sahelian states. It is tempting to identify every earlier complex settlement as a direct ancestor of a named medieval kingdom. That requires evidence linking languages, populations, institutions, and chronologies across centuries. Stone walls alone cannot establish such a line. We can recognize early West African complexity without forcing it into the genealogy of a later empire.

The everyday population is difficult to reconstruct individually, but the archaeological questions are concrete. Where was millet ground? Were cattle kept near homes or moved to distant grazing? How did people handle water in a dry season? Were some compounds linked by shared spaces or paths? What kinds of objects were made locally, and which came through exchange? Each question can be tested through excavation, plant identification, animal bones, the chemistry of materials, and spatial survey. The answers may vary even among neighboring sites. A community is a pattern of activity that can change within a landscape, not merely a dot assigned to an archaeological culture.

Excavation itself is selective. A field season exposes only part of a settlement, and a museum collection often overrepresents portable objects. Regional surveys reveal features beyond the trench: isolated structures, field boundaries, burial places, and routes between settlements. Today remote sensing can help locate stone-built compounds, but images from above do not determine when they were used or who lived within them. Groundwork and excavation remain necessary. The region also deserves histories written with local archaeological institutions and communities, not simply outsiders arriving to map impressive ruins.

The dhars give us a different entry into early West African history. No ruler's inscription tells us to admire his conquest; instead, walls, pottery, seeds, and dated charcoal call attention to cooperation and adaptation. These materials suggest substantial settlements, agriculture, pastoralism, and changing social relationships during the second millennium before the Common Era. They also make visible the uncertainties that still surround chronology and political organization. The real achievement is not the discovery of a lost empire. It is the ability to reconstruct a long history of people making workable communities along the edge of a changing desert.

## A City without a Palace? Djenné-Jeno and the Inland Niger Delta

Farther east and in a later sequence, the inland Niger delta offers a different urban landscape from the stone compounds of Mauritania.

What would convince you that a place was a city? Tall walls? A ruler's palace? A monument bearing a king's name? In the inland delta of the Niger River in present-day Mali, archaeology offers a striking challenge to that checklist. At Djenné-Jeno, also written Jenné-Jeno, people occupied a settlement from approximately the third century before the Common Era. It grew into a substantial urban center long before the later fame of Timbuktu and the Mali Empire. Excavators found crafts, houses, and evidence of a complex regional economy. What they did not find was an obvious royal citadel. That absence has changed how scholars discuss the relationship between cities, political power, and inequality.

The inland Niger delta is a changing landscape. Seasonal floods spread water across plains, channels, and low rises. Fishing, grazing, farming, and transport depend on where water goes and when it recedes. A high place may become an island during one season and part of a continuous plain in another. Communities learned to use different parts of the environment at different times. Rice farming, herding, fishing, and the production and movement of goods supplied livelihoods that could support larger settlements. The resources and skills of the delta supported urban growth long before medieval trans-Saharan networks became prominent in written accounts. The caravans were part of a later, larger history, not the origin of the region's capacity to sustain towns.

Roderick and Susan Keech McIntosh, whose excavations helped establish the site's chronology, found evidence of iron-working populations there by about 250 BCE. As the settlement expanded through the first millennium CE, its built areas and craft activities changed. By approximately 750 to 1100 CE, the site covered more than thirty hectares, with other occupied mounds in the surrounding region. These dates emerged from archaeology rather than from later written stories of a city foundation. They show that the history of urban life in the middle Niger is much older than a narrative that begins only with the rise of the famous Sahelian empires.

The settlement was made largely of earth, and that shapes the evidence. Earthen architecture, pottery, grinding stones, metalworking debris, and burials offer clues to domestic and craft life. They do not preserve a complete set of written laws or personal diaries. The objects have to be dated, compared, and interpreted in relation to the places where they were found. A fragment of pottery is not an anecdote about one household; a large area of related deposits, however, can reveal the duration and character of occupation. Excavation builds history from many partial observations, with each interpretation open to revision as more sites are studied.

The most intriguing feature may be the broader cluster. Djenné-Jeno was associated with numerous nearby settlements and occupation mounds, some showing signs of specialized activities. Scholars describe an urban complex made up of related places rather than a single city enclosed within monumental walls. Perhaps one settlement concentrated iron-working while another specialized in different resources or forms of production. Networks of exchange and interdependence could unite the cluster without requiring every activity to be directed from a central palace. It is a reminder that a city can be a relationship among places as well as a collection of buildings at one spot.

This observation has led scholars to discuss heterarchy: forms of social organization in which authority is distributed across several groups or institutions rather than arranged in one simple chain of command. Roderick McIntosh and others have proposed that Djenné-Jeno may have combined sophisticated urban life with less centralized political control than many textbook models expect. The argument rests partly on an absence of familiar signs of kingship or elite concentration. But the lack of an excavated palace is not proof that everyone was equal. Excavations are incomplete, materials decay, and authority can operate through institutions that leave little monumental evidence. Heterarchy is a researched interpretation, not a magically recovered constitution.

The people who made this city possible were not only merchants. Iron-workers needed fuel and raw materials. Potters required clay and knowledge of firing. Farmers and fishers supplied food; herders managed animals; boat crews and carriers moved goods across a landscape that changed with the seasons. Skilled labor had value before a distant ruler could tax it. We can infer economic specialization from archaeological distributions, but cannot automatically assign one occupation to an entire lineage or tell whether a particular craft worker enjoyed high status. The material record gives patterns of work rather than a roster of named individuals.

Trade connected the middle Niger to other ecological zones. The floodplain's products did not all come from the same places, and goods could circulate through multiple communities before reaching users. The direction and scale of older exchanges must be reconstructed from materials and their contexts, not copied backward from what later Arabic geographers said about medieval desert caravans. Long-distance trans-Saharan trade became important, but it was not the cause of every settlement or craft tradition south of the Sahara. Djenné-Jeno's early chronology is one reason scholars reject a model in which African urbanism began only when external merchants brought it.

The site also complicates how we think about the relationship between cities and states. A wealthy and powerful state can sponsor walls, store taxes, and erect monuments. An urban region may instead gain coherence through market ties, specialist production, ritual practices, and negotiating relationships among communities. The absence of obvious monarchy need not mean the absence of conflict, status differences, coercion, or exclusion. People can experience unequal power through kinship, control of land, religious institutions, or access to trade. Those distinctions are harder to measure archaeologically than the size of a stone palace, but they belong to the same historical inquiry.

The place name raises an additional problem. The archaeological site called Djenné-Jeno and the later town of Djenné are connected geographically and historically, but they are not simply identical settlements frozen in place. Urban populations and activities shifted over time. The earlier site declined and was largely abandoned by roughly 1400 CE, while the later town developed as a major commercial and religious center. We should not assume one moment of complete destruction or one ordered evacuation. Changes in river landscapes, economic ties, political institutions, and religious life may have operated across generations. The evidence does not support a single dramatic explanation for every shift.

Archaeologists do not work in a social vacuum. The excavated sites are part of the heritage of living communities in Mali. Conservation faces risks from erosion, changes in settlement, illicit digging, and political insecurity. Early excavations by the McIntoshes transformed scholarship, but they did not exhaust the landscape or settle every question. Contemporary archaeological work must contend with whose priorities shape research, how objects are preserved, and how knowledge is shared locally. The story of a site includes both the communities that built it and the people responsible for its care now.

There is a temptation to turn Djenné-Jeno into a perfect opposite of a royal capital: a city of peace, equality, and freely cooperating specialists. That would be no more justified than insisting that every city must have a king. The evidence supports a remarkable early urban complex, diverse production, and a serious case for distributed rather than highly centralized authority. It does not provide a complete account of justice or social relationships. Scholarship advances when a challenging case makes us ask better questions, not when the case becomes a new idealized template.

The comparison with other regions is illuminating if used carefully. Mesopotamian tablets can record taxes and office holders; Egyptian monuments can name rulers; Djenné-Jeno offers neither archive in the same form. Yet the remains of settlement, work, and exchange are as much historical evidence as royal inscriptions. Different records make different kinds of power visible. Calling one civilization advanced because it left durable royal monuments and another simple because it did not would confuse an archaeological preference with a measure of human achievement.

When the seasonal waters rise around the inland delta, the landscape changes without becoming empty. The history of Djenné-Jeno is similar: centers and their relationships shifted, while knowledge, work, and communities continued. The city invites us to separate urban complexity from the expectation of an emperor and a palace. It asks us to listen for histories carried by fields, furnaces, waterways, and clusters of houses. We cannot recover every voice. We can, however, see that the middle Niger supported long-standing and inventive forms of collective life on their own terms.

## Before the Gold Crossed the Desert: Tadmekka, Gao, and the Work of Trade

At medieval trading centers nearer the desert routes, small workshop remains reveal some of the work hidden behind the image of a gold caravan.

At Tadmekka, near the southern edge of the Sahara in present-day Mali, archaeologists found small traces of a much larger economic process. Fragments of ceramic moulds carry evidence of molten gold, and pieces of crucible retain mineral and glass residues. They are not the sort of monuments that announce the name of a king. They are the remains of work. In the ninth and tenth centuries, specialists at this trading center appear to have prepared gold for movement through commercial networks stretching across desert and savanna. Their tools open a way to reconstruct trade from the hands of makers rather than from later stories about a fabulous land of gold.

Tadmekka is also known through the site of Essouk, where desert routes could meet settlements, herders, and traders with different languages and political affiliations. Caravans needed water, provisions, knowledge of paths, and arrangements for protection. The camel was important, but a camel could not create a trade network by itself. Trade required people who could prepare loads, repair equipment, obtain grain and fodder, negotiate credit, and decide which routes were safe. We often summarize this as trans-Saharan exchange, but that phrase compresses many separate journeys and local relationships into one line on a map.

The coin moulds are particularly revealing. Sam Nixon and colleagues argue that they provide direct evidence for early West African gold pieces made by pouring molten metal into ceramic forms. The resulting pieces may have been blank rather than inscribed with the name of a ruler. If so, their purpose was not necessarily to announce sovereignty like some coins from imperial mints. They could have created portable and recognizable portions of metal for exchange. That interpretation remains an archaeological argument about the objects and their context, not a surviving written statement by a Tadmekka mint official.

Laboratory study of crucible fragments adds another layer. Thilo Rehren and Sam Nixon identified evidence that craftspeople used glass in a process for working gold concentrate. The glass helped separate precious metal from some accompanying mineral material. This is a practical innovation rooted in local knowledge of materials, heat, and the behavior of mixtures. The discovery changes what we can say about technology south of the Sahara in the early Islamic centuries. Traders did not merely carry raw goods toward distant places where expertise supposedly began. West African specialists processed valuable material before it entered wider exchange.

The Niger valley provides other evidence of early urban and commercial life. Archaeological work at Gao has identified settlement and large structures earlier than some chronologies once proposed from surviving written records. Arabic epitaphs from Gao-Saney and related sites provide names, religious language, and dates from later centuries. These sources reveal contact with literate Islamic networks, but they do not turn every inhabitant of Gao into a member of a single homogeneous community. A burial inscription names the person commemorated by a family or institution; a marketplace might have included people whose names never reached a stone.

Gao and Tadmekka were connected to different environments. The river valley supported agriculture, fishing, and transport by water. Desert-edge towns depended on access to routes and populations who could bring animals, salt, and other commodities. The goods moved in more than one direction. Gold is prominent because outsiders prized it and because its remains attract archaeological attention. But grain, textiles, leather, tools, and people also moved through these systems. A caravan's profitability relied on everyday supplies as much as on the occasional high-value cargo. Describing the region only as a source of gold misses the economies that kept gold moving.

Archaeology does not provide an annual balance sheet. Gold can be melted down and circulated repeatedly. The absence of a large hoard does not mean production was small, and the discovery of a few mould fragments cannot measure an entire century's output. Dating depends on stratigraphy and associated finds; reconstructing a technical process depends on microscopic and chemical analysis. Both can be strong without yielding every answer. We can say that skilled goldworking took place at the site. We should be more cautious about exactly who directed it, how a worker was paid, or what share of commerce belonged to a particular ruler.

The region's evidence also includes writing, but writing survives selectively. Arabic inscriptions reveal people who used written formulas of identity, piety, and remembrance. Their existence speaks to connections with a broad religious and commercial world. Yet the inscriptions are not a census of languages spoken at home, and they do not date the first encounter with Islam in every community. An epitaph might survive for someone wealthy enough to commission stone; a caravan porter or fisher might leave no comparable trace. Written and archaeological records need to be interpreted together rather than treated as competing accounts of the same population.

Who exercised power along these routes? There were rulers who sought tribute and protection payments, urban leaders managing trade, merchants handling partnerships and credit, and mobile communities whose knowledge of movement was indispensable. Control might be stronger at a river crossing or market than along every kilometer of desert. Archaeological evidence does not allow us to map a single state boundary across the whole commercial system. It does show economic concentration at particular nodes. A small workshop in Tadmekka can be as historically informative as the name of a dynasty because it reveals where valuable goods acquired a form suitable for travel.

The technology has another important lesson. Glass beads were trade goods in their own right, but glass could also serve as a material in the furnace. Objects commonly placed in separate museum cases—ornament, metal, ceramic vessel—were brought together in productive work. A specialist needed to judge temperatures and materials without a modern instrument panel. The knowledge was likely learned and transmitted through practice, though the excavated crucible cannot tell us whether training occurred in families, workshops, or another arrangement. Archaeology gives us the result of a process. It invites, but does not license, a complete invented biography of its maker.

The history of West Africa before the great fame of Mali and Songhay was already a history of complex towns and networks. Later empires did not conjure trade from empty space. They encountered cities, routes, religious communities, and technologies with longer local histories. At the same time, continuity should not erase change. Political authority, settlement patterns, and the mix of traders shifted across centuries. The evidence at Gao is not identical to the evidence at Tadmekka, and neither site represents every society of the Sahel. Their combination is powerful because the differences reveal a region with several centers and pathways.

A textbook map may draw an arrow labeled gold from West Africa across the Sahara. At Tadmekka, that arrow becomes a sequence of human acts: someone obtained gold-bearing material, someone prepared a ceramic vessel, someone controlled the fire, and someone carried a portable piece of metal into a world of exchanges. The archaeology cannot name them all. But it demonstrates that the trade was built from local technical and social knowledge. The story of Saharan commerce is not simply about what reached the Mediterranean. It is about the people, places, and skills that made valuable goods ready to move.
