# Mainland Southeast Asia II: Bagan, Dvaravati, and Cham sacred landscapes

Sacred buildings rest in inhabited landscapes. Bagan, Dvaravati, and Cham communities offer different evidence for the fields, labor, patronage, and routes around temples. We will move back to an earlier period when leaving Bagan for Dvaravati, then change regions again along the Vietnamese coast.

## The Fields beneath the Pagodas: Bagan and the Making of a Sacred Landscape

The plain of Bagan, beside the Ayeyarwady River in present-day Myanmar, is known for temples and stupas rising above the surrounding land. It is easy to tell its history by counting monuments. But each building required labor, materials, land, and continued care. Who supplied those things? What did donation mean for the farmers whose fields supported a religious institution? Bagan's surviving inscriptions, murals, archaeological remains, and living traditions offer ways to answer some of these questions. Its importance in the eleventh through thirteenth centuries was not only that rulers built sacred monuments. It was also that religious giving became entangled with the organization of political power and everyday work.

The Ayeyarwady linked Bagan to other landscapes through water transport, commerce, and movement of people. The surrounding dry zone was not uniformly fertile, and communities depended on a combination of river access, fields, gardens, and water management. The UNESCO World Heritage account of Bagan describes a sacred landscape and a regional empire, with religious endowments playing a major role in governance. It also stresses that the region remains inhabited and farmed. The monuments are part of a layered place, not a deserted stage on which an ancient dynasty once appeared. In the medieval period, too, the visible temples were supported by settlements whose less durable traces are harder to identify.

A donation can sound simple. A person gives land, an object, or resources to a Buddhist monastery in search of merit. But its consequences depend on what is given and who must maintain it. An endowed field may produce crops to support religious residents and buildings. Livestock must be fed, and workers may be assigned duties. A monastery needs storage, water, repair, and ritual service. Acts of merit-making were therefore also economic arrangements. The World Heritage documentation describes Bagan's system as one in which the king often acted as a major donor, linking religious authority and resource distribution. The presence of royal donations does not mean ordinary people had no part in religious life.

The famous Myazedi inscription gives us a concrete document. Dated to 1113, it records an act associated with Rajakumar and is written in four languages: Pyu, Mon, Old Myanmar, and Pali. It is an important witness to the multilingual world of Bagan and to the practice of recording Buddhist benefaction in durable form. The inscription does not mean everyone in the capital could read four languages. It was produced for a particular commemoration and audience. Its different written languages remind us that political and religious life involved people whose speech and traditions were not identical. A state monument can express multilingual connection while revealing very little about everyday conversation.

Many inscriptions name donations of land, livestock, buildings, and labor. Historians examine their language to ask who owned or controlled these resources and who gained from their dedication to religious institutions. Some records mention enslaved or bound temple workers, a fact that must not disappear behind the pious language of generosity. The donor's statement may commemorate devotion, but the labor obligations of another person are a different part of the same transaction. An inscription is also a public claim of legitimacy. It may preserve what the donor wanted remembered rather than a neutral account of the full costs of a gift.

One need not imagine the landscape as a simple contest between palace and monastery. Officials, wealthy laypeople, families, and religious communities all participated in giving. The ability to donate varied widely with resources. A large temple foundation required a different scale of support from an offering by someone with little property. Later buildings and inscriptions also demonstrate changes over time: the thirteenth-century sacred landscape was not identical to that of the first large royal foundations. Scholars use dates in inscriptions and building characteristics to reconstruct these developments. They also recognize that some texts are later copies or retrospective traditions, not contemporary records of the rulers they describe.

Temple architecture made religious ideas visible. Interior murals and sculpture could depict episodes associated with the Buddha's lives and teachings. Stupas and monasteries offered settings for worship, instruction, and community gatherings. Yet a decorated wall does not tell us exactly how each visitor responded. The buildings were experienced through movement, sound, ritual, and repeated visits, not only as architectural plans. Some imagery can be interpreted through Buddhist texts; other decorative choices have local histories that are harder to reconstruct. A lecture about a sacred landscape should therefore avoid treating it as an outdoor museum built for modern spectators.

The landscape's economy presents questions that are often harder to answer than its architecture. Where were the villages that supplied labor? How did water move between fields and settlements? Were some households obligated to serve particular institutions, and how were these obligations negotiated? Archaeological surveys and recent studies of residential patterning and water management seek traces that written donations leave out. Their results cannot simply be generalized from one excavated area to the whole Bagan plain. But they make it possible to place farmers and artisans within the account rather than treating them as an invisible supply of construction workers.

Religious giving could concentrate property over time. When land or revenue was set aside for a monastery, its legal or customary obligations changed. Historians have long debated how the growth of endowed religious property affected royal resources and political institutions. There is no reason to assume that one mechanical effect explains all later transformations at Bagan. Endowments might support education, ritual, storage, and social relations while also limiting how some assets could be mobilized by rulers. Their political consequences depended on local decisions and changing power. A donation record demonstrates a transaction; a theory about fiscal decline requires additional evidence.

Bagan's rulers belong to the history, but they are not the whole explanation. Anawrahta, Kyansittha, and later royal figures feature prominently in chronicles and traditions. Some of those narratives were composed long after the events described, and scholars separate their claims from contemporary inscriptions wherever possible. The identification of one ruler with the beginning of every religious or political institution oversimplifies a long process. The multilingual Myazedi monument, for example, is especially useful because it gives us an early, datable document. It does not authorize every story later told about the reigns surrounding it.

The regional setting also matters. Bagan interacted with populations speaking different languages and maintaining different religious practices. The presence of Pyu, Mon, Myanmar, and Pali in a single inscription is not proof that those groups occupied neatly separated neighborhoods or had equivalent political influence. Language in a formal record follows conventions and intended audiences. The same person might operate within several linguistic settings, while others had no access to written patronage. A kingdom's boundaries and cultural composition cannot be reconstructed from a few royal monuments alone. The inscription is strongest evidence for the act and language of commemoration it records.

The thirteenth century brought changes in regional political relations, including the impact of Mongol expansion and shifts in power. These developments are sometimes compressed into a single story in which an invasion suddenly ended Bagan. The archaeological and documentary history is more complicated. Religious buildings continued to be used; communities did not simply vanish from the plain. Political authority, construction activity, and patterns of endowment changed, but different processes need their own chronology. The date of a major campaign does not give a precise end date for every monastery or field. Monumental continuity and political transformation can occur together.

The modern landscape has its own responsibilities. Bagan is a place of continuing Buddhist practice, agriculture, tourism, research, and conservation. Earthquakes, restoration, and development affect what survives and how it is interpreted. The inscriptional record has been collected and studied through institutions with their own histories and priorities. Archaeology that treats the plain only as a spectacle risks excluding the people who maintain its religious and social life. Preserving buildings and documenting fields are related tasks, but they do not always impose the same demands on residents. The fact that Bagan remains lived in belongs to its historical significance.

We began among the pagodas, but the most revealing movement is outward toward the land that supported them. Temple endowments connect the ruler's reputation with farmers' work. A quadrilingual inscription reveals a political world of multiple languages while still speaking in the voice of a donor. Archaeology can recover settlements and water systems that the monuments themselves do not describe. Bagan's history is neither a tale of pure religious devotion nor a tale of kings commanding a passive population. It is a study of people organizing land, labor, learning, and worship through institutions whose traces remain visible in stone and text.

## Wheels of the Teaching: Buddhist Towns of Dvaravati

Before following another sacred landscape, step back from Bagan to earlier Buddhist towns in what is now Thailand.

Across the central plains of present-day Thailand, archaeologists have found standing stone Buddhas, enormous sculpted wheels associated with Buddhist teaching, reliefs, inscriptions, and traces of towns. A name appears on some early inscribed objects: Dvaravati. Historians use it to discuss a cluster of traditions and polities that flourished roughly between the sixth and eleventh centuries. But the more carefully the sites are studied, the less satisfactory it becomes to imagine a single kingdom ruling every town on a modern map. The problem is not whether Buddhism mattered. Its material presence is unmistakable. The problem is how religious art, political names, and the lives of residents fit together.

Many accounts of Southeast Asia move quickly from early ports to the monuments of Angkor, Bagan, or Srivijaya. Central Thailand then becomes a corridor between bigger powers. The Dvaravati evidence challenges that habit. Communities around Nakhon Pathom, U Thong, Khu Bua, and other sites organized large settlements, supported artisans, and participated in regional exchanges. Some works of art were monumental; others were smaller objects handled or seen in daily life. The people who commissioned these things were not merely copying distant models. They selected and transformed ideas in local environments.

Even the name is a research question. Sanskrit inscriptions on some silver objects include a formula interpreted as referring to a lord of Dvaravati. These pieces have often been called coins, but scholars debate their monetary as opposed to ceremonial use. A name on an object supports the existence of an identifiable political or prestigious title. It does not draw the borders of a kingdom or tell us who paid tribute to it. Specialists Wesley Clarke and Matthew Gallon emphasize that the term has become a broad archaeological category and that settlement and domestic evidence remain incompletely documented.

The Buddhist sculptures are striking evidence of creative exchange. Many figures share conventions developed in South Asia, but the forms and working methods are not identical. The Metropolitan Museum of Art has described distinctive Dvaravati approaches to the face and body of the Buddha, along with large wheels and narrative reliefs. The wheel can represent Buddhist teaching, and images placed along the route around a sacred structure might be encountered as people performed ritual movement. Such objects show the presence and support of Buddhism. They cannot by themselves tell us which teachings every local family understood or followed.

A monument also suggests an economy. Sculptors needed stone or clay, tools, training, and sponsors. Buildings required builders and provisions. People traveled to places of worship, and some residents provided food or services to visitors. Yet there is a difference between observing that substantial patronage existed and assuming that a king directed all production. Religious communities, merchants, families, and local leaders may have supported different projects. To compare sites, archaeologists consider the context and distribution of sculpture, not only the resemblance between famous pieces displayed in modern museums.

Language complicates the picture further. Inscriptions associated with the period include Sanskrit and forms of Mon, and later communities preserved other languages and traditions. Historians commonly connect Dvaravati with Mon-speaking societies, but language on an inscription is not a count of who lived in the town. A donor might choose a prestige language for a public dedication while speaking something else at home. The term Mon can describe historical linguistic connections without assuming an unbroken, uniform ethnic state. Political organization may have changed within and between settlements over centuries.

The towns occupied landscapes where movement by river and overland routes was consequential. Wetlands, seasonally changing water, cultivated plots, and connections toward the coast shaped settlement choices. Nakhon Pathom has yielded materials interpreted as evidence of domestic and longer-distance trade. A Thai archaeological study of excavations in 2009 and 2010 describes nonlocal glass beads and Tang-period Chinese ceramics among the finds, alongside ties to wider southern networks. Such artifacts indicate connections, but not one permanently open route or a merchant's complete itinerary. Each item may have passed through multiple intermediaries.

U Thong and nearby settlements likewise demonstrate that a site cannot be reduced to its most famous stupa. Ritual buildings existed within landscapes that included workshops and households. Excavated pottery, construction debris, and imported goods can help reconstruct local activities, although the dating of different settlement types remains uneven. The Oxford Handbook of Early Southeast Asia stresses that research long privileged monuments over domestic contexts. That affects what we can say about cooking, childhood, household organization, and agricultural labor. We know far more about prominent sculptures than about the people who fed their makers.

Religious change was not necessarily a replacement of local practice with something entirely imported. Buddhist images, texts, and architectural forms circulated through contacts with South Asia and neighboring regions. Communities interpreted them in relation to local patrons and places. Specialists investigating Dvaravati art have shown that styles could move through artists, objects, and marriage alliances among elites, not just armies. A ruler might display political prestige by sponsoring a Buddhist image. Other people might approach the same image as a place for devotion. The material object therefore connects several kinds of activity whose meanings need not be identical.

Who ruled these places? Some scholars have described one state; others favor networks of centers with shifting relationships. The shape of settlement, evidence for defensive works, royal titles, and the size of monuments may inform the debate. But the phrase Dvaravati kingdom sometimes implies more certainty than the sources allow. A Buddhist image can be found far from a political center because a workshop's style was admired, a donor moved, or artisans traveled. Distribution of art is not a map of administrative obedience. Research should distinguish an artistic horizon from a governing territory.

Centuries later, political and religious landscapes in the region looked different. New ruling centers emerged, and the rise of Khmer-speaking and Tai-speaking political formations reshaped connections. Older sacred sites could be reused or reinterpreted. These changes do not mean that a single Dvaravati people disappeared overnight. Archaeological labels represent constellations of material traits that can transform, overlap, and endure. A language may continue even when a political name ceases to appear. Temples can be renovated; a town can move; a style can circulate after its original patrons are gone.

A careful history of Dvaravati combines several kinds of witnesses. A Sanskrit dedication tells us how an elite actor wished to be remembered. A stone Buddha reveals technical mastery and the importance of Buddhist imagery. Imported beads show contacts beyond the local plain. A domestic excavation records a household that monumental histories often overlook. None can do the work of all the others. That is why the most promising research does not ask only which king ruled Dvaravati, but how multiple towns organized religious, commercial, and social life.

The monumental wheel is an apt ending, though not a metaphor that explains everything. It may represent the turning of Buddhist teaching, yet its stone was shaped by people living in a concrete landscape, supported by real households and materials. Rather than treating central Thailand as an empty passage between empires, we can recognize an interconnected world of Buddhist communities, local patrons, and changing political arrangements. Its surviving evidence is rich enough to teach a substantive history, and incomplete enough to reward questions that move beyond the sculpture gallery.

## River Valleys to the Sea: Cham Worlds before 1500

Farther east along the Vietnamese coast, Cham communities linked sacred places to river valleys and maritime routes.

At My Son, in the hills of central Vietnam, brick towers stand in a basin drained by the Thu Bon River. The river runs from the mountains toward the coast, connecting sacred buildings with cultivated valleys and maritime routes. Temple walls carry traces of worship, political ambition, and craftsmanship, but they are not the whole history of the communities that made them. Historians use the term Champa for a long series of Cham-associated polities and cultural traditions on the central and southern Vietnamese coast. Their relationships changed over more than a millennium. It is more useful to begin with the river valleys than to imagine an unchanging kingdom covering a continuous strip of shoreline.

The area contained several environments. Coastal plains supported farms and settlements, rivers connected inland goods to ports, and mountains formed barriers as well as places of exchange. Different valleys could support different political centers and networks of patrons. Maritime travel joined these places to trade circuits involving South Asia, island Southeast Asia, and China. But coastlines did not eliminate local geography: what happened near My Son need not have happened in exactly the same way at another center farther south.

The earliest histories have been reconstructed from archaeology, Chinese records, and inscriptions in Sanskrit and Cham. Chinese texts refer to a polity often rendered Linyi; scholars debate how it relates to later Cham centers. Excavations at Tra Kieu and Go Cam show connections with the Han world during the early first millennium CE. William Southworth's archaeological overview emphasizes that these findings do not justify treating every named polity as one continuous dynasty. Political names in the available records reflect outside observers and local claims, which changed as rulers, alliances, and regions changed.

The My Son sanctuary is associated with worship over many centuries, especially forms of Hindu practice centered on Shiva, while Buddhism also had important institutions at various times and places. Brick towers, carved stone, and dedicatory inscriptions testify to artisans and sponsors who worked with ideas circulating through Asia. UNESCO describes the relation between the sanctuary and the river landscape; its monument account is a useful starting point for studying material remains. But a heritage site's conventional description of a single Champa kingdom cannot settle the archaeological debate about the organization of multiple coastal polities.

Some inscriptions commemorate land, wealth, or other resources devoted to temples. A donor or ruler could use religious endowment to establish prestige, honor a deity, and support specialists who maintained a sanctuary. The inscription may celebrate legitimate authority and generous action. A historian asks where the resources came from, who did the work, and what happened to the households on which those gifts depended. The temple's patron appears by name; the farmer or potter may not. The asymmetry is a feature of the evidence, not proof that everyone outside the court lacked a role in political and religious life.

The engineering of Cham temples deserves attention in its own right. Fired-brick construction required material selection, controlled firing, transport, skilled assembly, and decoration. Sandstone sculpture adds another chain of work, from quarrying to carving and placement. The persistence of sacred sites also required repair. Materials could be reused, buildings enlarged, and political meanings reworked. An art historian can sometimes identify a sculptural workshop or a regional style, but a style is not automatically a boundary on a map. It can move because a patron commissioned an artist or because communities exchanged ideas.

At Tra Kieu and other sites, excavations have recovered material evidence for defensive enclosures and settlement patterns that matter beyond worship. Southworth notes that research into ordinary ceramics and domestic sites remains less complete than studies of sculpture and architecture. This is a serious limit: trade goods in a port do not explain how every family ate, and a royal temple cannot tell us the normal size of a household. Where archaeological sequences are poorly dated, historians need to state a broad period rather than attaching a site to a famous ruler just because the assignment makes a tidier narrative.

Water made the region productive and connected, but it also gave local communities choices about where to settle and how to exchange goods. Rivers provided ways to move inland products toward the coast. People could meet traders from distant places without leaving their own valley. A ceramic vessel of nonlocal manufacture or a borrowed religious design may show participation in a network. It cannot tell us whether a particular exchange was peaceful, taxed, coerced, or organized through kinship. Political geography must be assembled from multiple kinds of evidence rather than from the travel of a single object.

Modern scholarship has questioned older maps that divide Champa permanently into a fixed number of principalities. One model identifies several named regions, but other analyses emphasize changing configurations of river valleys and coastal centers. Still other specialists point to fifteenth-century inscriptions suggesting a powerful ruler with substantial territorial reach at that particular time. These views need not contradict one another across all centuries: a polity could be more unified in one period than another. These arguments matter because a map of a permanently unified state may make expansion, defeat, and economic change seem more straightforward than they were. A political center could prosper through a particular river mouth or alliance and then lose influence when routes shifted. Neighboring Cham-associated centers could compete, cooperate, or claim different relationships to larger powers.

Conflicts with polities to the north and west were part of the history, but we should not reduce everything to a long war between modern nations. Relationships with Chinese states, Khmer centers, and later Vietnamese kingdoms shifted over centuries. Some local populations moved; others remained while political language, tribute, and land claims changed. The military success of one ruler does not explain the entire social history of a valley. Nor can later national borders tell us what a tenth-century merchant thought counted as home.

There were also multiple languages, beliefs, and social roles in these communities. Sanskrit often served as a prestige language in public inscriptions; Cham inscriptions provide other perspectives, though both kinds of text overrepresent those able to sponsor writing. Buddhist and Hindu practices existed in locally specific forms. The same coast contained people whose work involved farming, fishing, forest products, craft production, and maritime trade. Some labor was coercive, as in other stratified societies, but the particular arrangements need documentary or archaeological support. It would be easy to invent a single Cham household; the available record calls for attention to differences.

Scholar My Ket Chau has argued that monumental visual culture helped Cham communities represent relationships and authority across a dispersed coastal world. Her study of large pedestal-shrines and other sculptural programs compares sites instead of making each a separate, self-contained school of art. This approach makes the temple part of a network: it is a place where people materialized political claims and shared religious forms. The approach remains an interpretation, not a translation of every object into one settled message. Where scholars disagree about a carved image, that disagreement belongs in the historical account.

By the fifteenth century, political changes altered the power of major Cham centers, but not the existence of Cham communities. It is particularly important not to narrate the history as the disappearance of a people when one capital was conquered or an older political arrangement weakened. Cham languages, religious traditions, and cultural practices continued in later centuries and continue among living communities. The earlier history should therefore be connected to later transformations without collapsing them into a story whose conclusion is already decided.

Return to the river flowing past My Son. It joined worship and political patronage to fields and ports, inland routes and the sea. Temples make visible a part of that world which wealth and craft preserved exceptionally well. Archaeology of settlements, inscriptions, and maritime exchange broadens it. The name Champa helps organize the story, but river valleys, changing alliances, and the lives of people beyond courts explain why the story cannot be told as one uniform coastal empire. The history of Cham worlds was made at many centers, not only where the tallest towers still stand.
