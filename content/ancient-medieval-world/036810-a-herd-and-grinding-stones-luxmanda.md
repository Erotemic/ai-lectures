# A Herd and Grinding Stones: Working Life at Luxmanda

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

A large grinding stone seems to explain itself. You imagine grain being reduced to flour and a household preparing a meal. But the stone does not tell us which plant was ground, whether it was cultivated, or even whether food was always its main use. These are the questions archaeologists have asked at Luxmanda in northern Tanzania, a settlement of livestock-keeping people occupied roughly three thousand years ago. Here, many of the most consequential finds are not monumental buildings. They are concentrations of broken animal bones, pottery, tools, dung-derived deposits, and unusually large grinding stones. From them a history of domestic work begins to emerge.

Luxmanda lies near Mount Hanang in the Mbulu highlands, farther south than many earlier excavated Pastoral Neolithic settlements. The term Pastoral Neolithic is an archaeological label for several long-standing traditions of livestock use, stone-tool production, pottery making, and exchange in parts of Kenya and Tanzania. It does not identify a single kingdom or language. Research led by Katherine Grillo and Mary Prendergast described Luxmanda as an open-air settlement with substantial occupation deposits. Radiocarbon dates place its major early occupation around three thousand calibrated years before the present, or approximately a thousand years before the common era.

The animals were central to life there. Identified bones and teeth include large numbers of cattle, sheep, and goats; wild species are present in small amounts in the analyzed faunal sample. Many bones bear evidence of processing and breaking. These remains point to the repeated tasks of acquiring, preparing, sharing, and discarding food, but the shape of the discarded bones alone cannot give us a household recipe. Archaeologists have also found ash, pottery, and other domestic refuse. Such deposits are the cumulative record of people returning to activities again and again, not a photograph of one dinner.

The settlement extends across roughly three hectares, a useful corrective to an image of early herders who owned only what they could carry and left no substantial residential place. Archaeological teams combined surface survey, excavation, and geophysical measurements to explore the organization of activity areas. Some deposits may derive from the gathering of livestock and accumulation of dung, while other features reflect hearths or refuse disposal. Finding a dwelling floor or an enclosure is rarely as simple as seeing an intact wall. Researchers must distinguish an ancient activity from material moved there later by erosion, animals, roots, or people.

Then there are the stones. At Luxmanda, excavators encountered a concentration of twelve large lower grinding stones in a feature together with handstones, pottery, stone-tool debris, and dung-derived material. Some lower stones were heavy enough to be difficult to carry from one seasonal camp to another. In a 2020 report, Grillo and colleagues asked whether features like these require a more flexible account of pastoral mobility and gatherings. The stones suggest repeated investment in particular places. They do not tell us that every member of the community lived there permanently or that every stone was in use at the same moment.

What were people processing? For decades, researchers debated whether the Pastoral Neolithic involved cereal cultivation. Grinding equipment, stone axes, historical linguistic arguments, and analogies with recent pastoral livelihoods made agriculture plausible to some scholars. Others emphasized that the region lacked directly identified crop remains from the relevant sites. This distinction matters. Grinding can reduce the seeds of wild grasses, crush tubers, process animal products, or prepare non-food materials. A stone bearing wear proves that work occurred, not by itself that a domesticated crop grew nearby.

A study published in 2026 gives us more specific evidence. Carolina Jiménez-Arteaga and colleagues examined microscopic plant remains on Luxmanda grinding stones, combining starch analysis with the identification of silica bodies, called phytoliths, and samples of surrounding sediments. Their study reports evidence of processed local grasses, including panicoid grasses, legumes, and underground plant storage organs. The researchers also found indications of wild rice and a broad range of plant parts. This establishes that plants were actively worked into the material life of a pastoralist settlement. It does not establish that people at Luxmanda cultivated domesticated cereal fields.

We should distinguish plant processing from the domestication question. A domesticated plant is one shaped through cultivation and selection over generations, sometimes acquiring traits that make it dependent on human harvest or planting. A person gathering a wild grass and grinding its seed is doing important food-producing work, but that activity need not involve sowing the grass. Conversely, plant use might have served medicine, craft, or animal care, not only human meals. The 2026 authors themselves caution that their microscopic evidence does not turn every grinding feature into a cereal-dehusking workshop. The details of the residues constrain the story; they do not fill every gap in it.

The value of this result extends beyond a botanical inventory. If livestock bones preserve readily while seeds and plant tissues often decay, then archaeological narratives can become overly centered on animals simply because animals leave better traces at some sites. A grinding stone offers a different archive. Combining animal remains, residues, soils, and tool positions produces a more balanced picture of what occupied people's time. Herding requires labor; so does collecting and processing plants, repairing vessels, maintaining tools, and making decisions about when to move or stay. We cannot assign those tasks to a particular sex or age group without evidence.

Luxmanda also points toward contacts beyond one settlement. Stone tools made from obsidian have been linked to distant sources in Kenya's central Rift Valley, and its material traditions resemble those found at other sites to the north. This suggests connections across considerable distances. A shared stone source does not document the exact journeys of a named trader or herder, and similar pots do not by themselves identify a people's language. But such evidence does show that this household economy belonged to a broader social geography, rather than existing in isolation below Mount Hanang.

Our understanding will continue to change as research methods improve. The same site can be read through a carefully dated bone, microscopic starch, an animal's tooth, or the distribution of material across an excavation. Each method has its own errors and silences. Luxmanda's importance is not that it finally supplies a fixed recipe for the first pastoralists. It shows what happens when we make ordinary work the object of historical investigation. Alongside the cattle and goats were people making meals and materials from plants. A herd did not eliminate other forms of knowledge, and a grinding stone does not need to prove agriculture to tell us something important.

## Evidence boundary

The 2026 microbotany findings are summarized from the publisher's accessible abstract and highlights, not represented as a full methods audit. Identified plant residues demonstrate processing, not domesticated crop cultivation. Stone features indicate place investment but do not determine exact settlement permanence, occupation schedules, or the gendered division of work.
