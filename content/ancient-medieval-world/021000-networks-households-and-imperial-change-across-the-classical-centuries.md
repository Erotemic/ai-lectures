# Networks, households, and imperial change across the classical centuries

Connections across the classical centuries were uneven. Some societies exchanged goods across seas and frontiers; other cities developed far outside those networks. We will keep that distinction in view while moving between trade, household evidence, and the changing Roman and Iranian empires.

## Routes without a Single Silk Road: Merchants, Oceans, and Intermediaries

Suppose a piece of silk appears in a distant market far from the place where its fibers were spun. How many journeys brought it there? The answer might include farmers who raised silkworms, workers who made thread and cloth, local traders, caravans, ports, officials, tax collectors, and buyers who never saw the distant landscapes where the material began. The object can tell a story of connection, but not necessarily of one traveler walking from one empire's capital to another. This is a central problem in the history often called the Silk Road: a useful name has encouraged us to picture a single continuous road where there were actually changing networks of land and sea routes.

The expression Silk Road was popularized in the nineteenth century, long after the ancient traders whose movements it supposedly describes. Historians often speak now of Silk Roads in the plural. Between about 300 BCE and 300 CE, movement through Central Asia changed as different states, confederations, oasis communities, and merchants gained or lost control of particular routes. Silk was one valuable product, but animals, metal, wool, glass, perfumes, precious stones, and ordinary supplies also circulated. Many objects changed owners several times. A foreign artifact found in a tomb proves that a material traveled; it does not, by itself, prove the birthplace, identity, or itinerary of the final person who carried it.

Han rulers became involved with the regions west of their core territories for military and diplomatic reasons as well as for trade. Negotiations and conflicts with Xiongnu powers shaped much of their northern policy. Envoys and commanders explored opportunities through oasis regions around the deserts of Inner Asia, while local political actors made their own choices about alliance, tribute, and commerce. Accounts of particular diplomatic journeys are often written from a Chinese court perspective. They can show what the court sought to learn or achieve, but we should not treat all the communities named in these records as stations on an imperial supply line. Their own networks could predate or outlast Han intervention.

Further west, Central Asian cities and pastoral communities formed the connective tissue of overland exchange. The Kushan realm linked portions of northern India and Afghanistan with wider networks, and Parthian territories connected Iranian and Mesopotamian markets. Trade did not require a treaty uniting all these rulers. It required workable arrangements over many smaller stretches, along with water, fodder, guards, porters, pack animals, and ways to settle debts. The expense and risk of hauling a luxury good across difficult terrain helped determine what traveled far. We should not assume that a sack of everyday grain moved over the same distances as a small quantity of valuable cloth or stones.

The northern routes were only part of the story. Maritime travel connected the Red Sea, the Horn of Africa, Arabia, the Persian Gulf, western India, and other parts of the Indian Ocean. Sailors made use of seasonally changing winds, including the monsoon system, but those winds were not discovered by a single Greek or Roman captain. Knowledge of coastal routes, currents, seasonal timing, and dangerous approaches accumulated among communities with long experience at sea. Ports could flourish because they connected inland producers with ships, not because rulers far away drew lines across a map. Warehouses, interpreters, merchants, and crews mattered more to the cargo's journey than the fame of any emperor.

A Greek-language text known as the Periplus of the Erythraean Sea, generally dated to the first century CE, provides a striking view of this maritime world. Its unidentified author describes ports and trading possibilities from Egypt's Red Sea coast through the Arabian and African shores toward India. The document reads in places like practical information for people interested in exchange. It gives names and commodities that help historians reconstruct connections. But a merchant-oriented text reflects its author's experience and commercial interests. It is not a neutral directory of every community, and its account of distant regions becomes less secure where the writer's direct knowledge was limited.

The Red Sea ports of Roman Egypt offer an archaeological counterpart. At Berenike, excavations have recovered evidence for commodities and biological materials whose origins lie beyond the local desert environment. Black pepper is among the best-known imports, and finds also indicate links with other Indian Ocean regions. These remains allow historians to test the trading text against objects and plant material. They do not automatically provide a full bill of sale or prove that a Roman imperial office organized each voyage. The difference between a state controlling a port and a state personally conducting all commerce is crucial. Traders and investors had choices, and much of their cooperation took place beyond what written sources preserve.

Western Indian ports were not merely collection points for foreign demand. Local merchants and producers participated in circuits that linked inland agriculture, textile manufacture, spices, coastal shipping, and political authority. The Periplus mentions destinations and goods such as pepper, while archaeological finds of coins and imported objects provide another perspective. Roman coinage found in South Asia has often inspired sweeping stories about a massive trade deficit draining Rome's wealth. A coin could arrive through several kinds of transactions and circulate long after its original owner was gone. Without context, quantity and distribution are not enough to reconstruct the balance sheet of an ancient economy.

Connections also carried ideas and religious practices, although they did not move as if they were packages. Buddhist institutions in Gandhara and neighboring regions developed with the support of donors, rulers, monastic communities, and travelers. Artistic conventions traveled and were transformed. The movement of a sculptural form does not mean that a community adopted an entire foreign philosophy, and the spread of Buddhism cannot be reduced to merchants transporting texts. Local teachers, translators, ritual practice, family patronage, and political choices mattered. Long-distance mobility created possibilities for exchange; what people made of those possibilities was never predetermined.

The people who made these routes functioned at different levels of visibility. A port official might leave a tax document. A donor might be named on a reliquary. A laborer lifting cargo or tending caravan animals might remain anonymous. Merchants could gain wealth, but voyages involved danger, delays, shipwreck, warfare, and uncertain prices. A state could benefit from customs revenues while local producers struggled with compulsory provisions or unstable demand. The stories preserved in elite literature often praise rare luxuries or complain about extravagance. They say far less about maintenance work, credit, trust, and everyday consumption.

We should also resist the picture of a completely interconnected ancient globe. There was significant exchange across much of Eurasia and parts of Africa, but there was no continuous commercial system uniting all regions of the planet. American societies developed their own networks independently. Even between the Han and Roman worlds, most people did not travel between the imperial centers, and evidence for a direct official relationship is limited and debated. A chain of connected markets is different from a single global market in which every price and political decision moves together. The distance between those two ideas is important for understanding both ancient history and later globalization.

Calling these connections Silk Roads can be helpful if the plural remains meaningful. It asks us to reconstruct many overlapping routes, distinct time periods, and intermediaries rather than celebrate one imaginary highway. From a loom to an oasis, from a Malabar pepper-growing district to a Red Sea harbor, historical exchange happened through particular people solving practical problems. The grand map shows what could connect. Archaeology, documents, and the study of environments help us discover what actually did, who organized it, and what the evidence cannot tell us. The world was connected in important ways, but the connections were made and remade, journey by journey.

## Cities without the Roman Calendar: American Worlds across the Classical Centuries

The American cities that follow were outside the trading connections just described. Their position in this comparison does not imply a route from Rome or Asia.

Around the first century of the common era, monumental buildings were taking shape in central Mexico, artists were painting sacred narratives in the Maya lowlands, and people along the Peruvian coast were organizing water, agriculture, and ceremonial life in ways suited to sharply different environments. None of these developments needs a Roman empire, a Han dynasty, or a caravan crossing Central Asia to explain it. The chronology is shared only because historians use common dates to compare events. The histories themselves emerged from networks within the Americas, with their own traditions of urban planning, authority, art, and environmental knowledge.

In the Valley of Oaxaca, the hilltop center of Monte Alban had been developing since about 500 BCE. Its terraces, public structures, carved monuments, and connections with surrounding communities reveal a long process of political centralization. The city is associated with Zapotec history, but labels inherited from later periods do not let us recover every early resident's name or political allegiance. Some carved monuments contain signs that scholars examine as writing and political statements. Their interpretation is not always straightforward. Public art can show that rulers or institutions wished to display power without telling us that their subjects accepted every message or that the society was organized exactly as a later state.

In the Basin of Mexico, the city later known as Teotihuacan began to grow around the end of the first millennium BCE. Its great pyramids, broad avenues, and large residential compounds belong to a long sequence rather than to one moment of foundation. The monumental ceremonial core took major shape during roughly the first two and a half centuries CE, while many of the famous apartment compounds expanded especially after that time. It would be misleading to give the city its population and institutions at their fourth- or fifth-century peak when describing its earliest phases. Cities change; the ruins we see now contain buildings made at different times for different purposes.

The planners of Teotihuacan created a striking relationship between architecture, mountains, and public space. Archaeologists examine the orientations of its streets and monuments, the movement of water, workshops, offerings, and the layout of dwellings. Its later neighborhoods reveal migrants from other Mesoamerican regions living alongside people with local traditions. Yet the city leaves historians a challenging problem: its political system is not as directly legible as that of the better-documented Classic Maya dynasties. We should not invent a complete royal succession because we see an imposing pyramid. Nor should the absence of a familiar portrait of kings lead us to assume there were no important governing institutions.

In the Maya lowlands of present-day Guatemala and neighboring regions, settlements such as El Mirador and Nakbe developed large public constructions during the Preclassic period. Agricultural production, causeways, ceremonial architecture, and the management of landscapes supported concentrations of people. The term Maya names a broad collection of related histories and languages, not one kingdom extending uniformly across the region. Cities competed and connected, and their political forms changed over time. Recent landscape surveys have revealed networks of sites and constructed routes that are difficult to appreciate from the perspective of one excavated pyramid alone. These discoveries enlarge the questions scholars can ask; they do not yet settle every argument about centralized control.

A remarkable set of murals from San Bartolo in Guatemala offers a closer view of early Maya intellectual life. Paintings made around 100 BCE depict complex mythic and ceremonial imagery, and earlier fragments contain writing. Research on a calendar notation from the site has strengthened evidence for developed Maya recordkeeping well before the later famous stelae. We can describe preserved figures and the contexts in which the paintings were buried. Their interpretation also involves comparing images with much later indigenous texts, including traditions recorded after European conquest. Such comparisons can illuminate continuities, but they must never imply that a later text records an ancient ceremony without change across a thousand years.

The circulation of artistic forms, precious materials, and people connected the regions of Mesoamerica in different ways. Archaeologists find obsidian transported beyond the places where it was quarried, as well as conventions of sculpture, architecture, and pottery shared across neighboring areas. Movement can represent exchange, political pressure, intermarriage, migration, or imitation. A material's source can sometimes be studied scientifically, yet identifying the quarry does not tell us the conversation that accompanied a transaction. This is why the history of ancient Mesoamerican networks cannot be drawn as a series of arrows labeled conquest whenever two sites contain similar designs.

The Andean region presents another set of histories. Along the south coast of present-day Peru, communities associated with the Paracas and later Nasca traditions produced elaborate textiles and ceramics. The arid coast preserves some materials particularly well, but it also creates a biased record: surviving burial cloth may show what was prepared for a funeral rather than what people wore while farming. In valleys where rainfall was limited, fields depended on careful use of water descending from the Andes. People combined agriculture with coastal resources and long-standing highland contacts. Political life here cannot be understood solely through stone monuments, because textiles, pottery, irrigation, and burials carry so much of the evidence.

The enormous designs now called the Nasca and Palpa geoglyphs were made over many centuries, with different phases spanning the late centuries BCE and the first centuries CE. Their lines, geometric forms, and images were created through manipulation of the desert surface. The most responsible interpretation distinguishes observed form and date from explanation of purpose. Many researchers connect the geoglyphs with ritual movement, place, and environmental knowledge, while precise meanings remain debated. There is no need to supply an exotic mystery where the achievement of sustained local planning and labor is already substantial. These were made by people who lived in the region and knew its terrain.

On Peru's north coast, societies grouped under the label Moche developed prominent regional centers from the early centuries CE onward. River valleys supported irrigation in a dry coastal environment, while fishing and other maritime resources were important. Moche artists created modeled ceramics that portray animals, humans, ritual figures, and scenes of social life. The political organization behind shared styles remains debated: different valleys may not have belonged to a single uniformly administered state. Some images depict captives and violence; excavations reveal richly furnished burials alongside more ordinary settlements. These sources make social inequality visible, but cannot be read as illustrated diaries of every event.

Far to the north, in what is now the Ohio River valley, Indigenous communities built geometric earthworks and maintained exchange relationships across large portions of eastern North America. Archaeologists often use the term Hopewell for related traditions most prominent in the early centuries CE. The name comes from the modern history of excavation, not a self-designation preserved from the builders. Materials such as copper, shell, and mica reached ceremonial contexts through extensive contacts. Large earthworks required planning and coordinated labor, but their presence does not prove that the builders lived under an emperor or inhabited permanent cities in the same form as Teotihuacan. Different social institutions can produce monumental achievements.

We cannot reconstruct each region with the same kind of evidence. Maya inscriptions can sometimes be read as language and dates; some earlier signs are debated. Teotihuacan's painted walls provide rich visual information but do not offer a continuous narrative of named rulers. Nasca textiles and desert lines preserve kinds of evidence that written chronicles cannot. Hopewell earthworks and distributions of materials support inference about gatherings and exchange without telling us precisely how people named their communities. Archaeological interpretation depends on careful dating, context, and Indigenous knowledge, and on admitting when a beautiful object does not answer a political question.

A world history organized by the classical centuries should include all these societies without pretending they were in contact with Rome or Han China. Their histories help expose the limits of a definition of civilization built around a single imperial model. People could organize enormous constructions through many arrangements of authority; rich cultural practices could flourish without written court chronicles; and centers could grow or shrink without ending the lives of all surrounding communities. The right comparison is not which American society became most like an empire elsewhere. It is how different people created enduring forms of social life out of the places, resources, knowledge, and relationships available to them.

## An Empire at the Door: Homes, Labor, and Inequality in the Classical World

Returning to the eastern Mediterranean, a household archive narrows our view from networks of exchange to a particular encounter with law.

In the early second century CE, a woman named Babatha kept legal documents concerning property, family relationships, and disputes in the region near the Dead Sea. They survived in a cave where her bundle was found many centuries later. A royal monument from that time might tell us the name of an emperor or celebrate a victorious army. Babatha's papers tell us that somebody needed to defend claims over land and money and could sometimes use written legal procedures to do so. This is a powerful change of scale. The experience of empire was not confined to courts and battlefields. It entered houses, fields, workshops, and relationships between people who could command labor and people who could not refuse.

Babatha's archive includes transactions produced under different political and legal circumstances around the Roman annexation of the Nabataean kingdom in 106 CE. It offers glimpses of property management, marriage contracts, guardianship, loans, and litigation. The documents do not make Babatha representative of every woman in the eastern Mediterranean; she possessed resources and access to legal channels many people lacked. They do show that women in some settings could own and protect assets and that legal life was complicated by language, family claims, and overlapping traditions. A change in the official designation of a province did not simply erase the social arrangements through which households operated.

Start with the question of food. Roman cities, Han capitals, the Maurya heartland, Hellenistic Alexandria, and the cities of Mesoamerica all depended on people producing and moving food. Those producers faced different climates, crops, soils, and forms of authority. A Nile farmer worked within a river system whose seasonal floods affected possibilities for cultivation. A household in northern China might raise grain and produce textiles while meeting registration and service obligations. In a dry Peruvian valley, irrigation could be essential to supporting a regional center. These communities were not interchangeable examples of one ancient agricultural system. Their common problem was that elites and institutions drew support from work occurring far beyond the monumental center.

Government could make a household legible to officials through registers, surveys, contracts, and tax demands. Excavated Qin and Han bamboo slips reveal bureaucratic attention to people, grain, transportation, and legal requirements. Papyri from Hellenistic and Roman Egypt preserve accounts, petitions, tenancies, and payments. Such documents tell us a great deal about what authorities considered measurable and enforceable. They also expose a bias: a registration may count people by a legal category without giving us their names as they spoke them within a family. When the archive records a disputed payment, it may preserve a conflict only because someone with influence wanted it adjudicated.

Property and landholding were central to inequality, but possession was not the same kind of right everywhere. In Roman settings, citizenship and legal status affected transactions and access to institutions. In Hellenistic Egypt, government, temples, landlords, tenants, and local communities participated in different claims over land and labor. In Han China, powerful families could accumulate influence even while the imperial state asserted authority over registers and taxation. We should resist forcing these histories into a single ladder from communal land to private property. Rules of inheritance, gender, tenancy, taxation, and service changed across time and place, and so did the ability to challenge a powerful neighbor.

Enslavement was an essential feature of many ancient economies and societies, but its scale and organization varied. Conquest supplied enslaved people to Greek and Roman worlds; people also entered servile conditions through other mechanisms. They might work in domestic service, mining, workshops, agriculture, or public institutions, facing coercion and restrictions that differed by law and circumstance. Some were manumitted, or formally freed, and became part of new social networks. A former slave's inscription can preserve pride in work or family, but cannot show that enslavement was benign. No single ratio of enslaved to free labor can responsibly be applied to all empires, or even to every province of one empire.

The contrast with Egypt is informative. Studies of Greek and Roman Egypt suggest significant changes in slaveholding practices under Ptolemaic and Roman rule, while other dependent labor relationships persisted. Surviving papyri make the region unusually well documented for certain questions, but they are not a statistical sample of the whole ancient world. We must not use an Egyptian contract to fill every gap in Roman rural history. Nor should a general royal law be mistaken for evidence that all laborers experienced its rules in the same way. Historical comparison works by identifying both the shared problem of labor extraction and the different institutions through which it occurred.

Gender and family shaped access to property and authority. Roman legal sources often assume male household heads, yet inscriptions and papyri show women arranging transactions, commissioning memorials, and exercising agency under specific conditions. Han texts promote norms about kinship and conduct, while household production depended heavily on women's labor, including fiber and textile work. Ancient Indian sources describe different social ideals and practices concerning household obligations and status. None of these traditions should be compressed into one universal formula for women's lives. We need to ask which region, which century, and which social position a document describes before making claims about what a woman could do.

Social divisions were also expressed through religious institutions and ideas of moral conduct. Ashoka's edicts urge proper treatment of servants and respect toward others, but do not establish a society free of hierarchy. Roman household religion and civic ceremonies created relationships among family, community, and public authority without putting everyone in the same legal position. Buddhist communities depended on gifts and patronage, as did temples and other institutions in different regions. A religious teaching might criticize certain behaviors while an institution still depended on elite donations or the labor of ordinary people. We should investigate the actual arrangements rather than assume that ethical ideals described everyday practice.

In cities, artisans and merchants occupied spaces shaped by demand, regulations, patronage, and access to resources. Hellenistic courts and temples commissioned specialized work, but many objects were made for ordinary buyers. Roman cities relied on builders, traders, bakers, and transport workers, some free and some enslaved. In Teotihuacan, apartment compounds provide archaeological evidence for collective residential arrangements and, in some cases, craft production; the best-known examples expanded especially after about 250 CE. They offer insights into a city whose rulers are much harder to reconstruct. A courtyard or a tool assemblage can tell us how space was organized, though we must be cautious before naming every occupant's job or relationship.

Ancient sources often give more words to people with wealth and rank. The powerful commissioned monuments; officials copied records; educated authors wrote histories. That skew does not mean historians must abandon the lives of those who left fewer words. Skeletal evidence can illuminate diet and health under carefully controlled interpretations. Houses show patterns of habitation; agricultural installations show work; damaged tools reveal repeated use. Archaeologists and historians can compare such evidence with written accounts. Yet absence remains a serious limitation. We cannot recover every household's arguments, pleasures, and fears by writing an imaginative scene and pretending it was documented.

One danger in comparison is to rank these societies by how closely they resemble modern assumptions about liberty or economic progress. Some institutions protected certain forms of security while denying others; an elite woman with property might possess rights unavailable to poorer men, while an enslaved worker might create wealth without any claim to its rewards. Circumstances could change with war, taxation, migration, famine, inheritance, or emancipation. There was no one classical experience of being a woman, a farmer, or a merchant. The common question is how status, law, production, and power met in particular lives—and who could persuade an institution to recognize a grievance.

The small bundle of Babatha's papers brings us back to that question. Her documents survived by chance, and many others have been lost. They provide a personal point of entry without permitting a complete biography of her inner life. Across the classical world, empire reached people through assessments, orders, property claims, military service, market relations, religious duties, and sometimes legal remedies. Some prospered through those institutions; some endured them at great cost. A serious history keeps the conqueror and the tax collector in view, but it also follows the grain, the loom, the household register, and the person who had to carry a claim to court. That is where the power of an empire became part of everyday life.

## When Rome Changed Shape: Emperors, Cities, and the Eastern Empire

From those household obligations, we can widen the scale again to the changing institutions of Roman and Byzantine rule.

In the sixth century, visitors entering the church of San Vitale in Ravenna could see an emperor standing in a mosaic, holding a ceremonial vessel among soldiers and clergy. Justinian was represented as a ruler whose authority joined the army, the church, and the state. He did not ordinarily govern Italy from Ravenna. His court lay across the Mediterranean, in Constantinople. Yet the image asserted that he ruled here too. It survives from a century often placed after the “fall of Rome.” How could an emperor still claim Roman rule in Italy after Rome was supposed to have fallen? To answer, we need to follow several centuries of change without assuming that every part of the empire followed the same timetable.

By about 300 CE the Roman state was much larger than any city, even Rome itself. Its taxes, soldiers, roads, courts, and supply networks stretched across the Mediterranean and deep into inland provinces. At the end of the third century, Diocletian and his colleagues divided governing responsibilities among multiple emperors. This arrangement, conventionally called the tetrarchy, sought to deal with military and political pressures. It also underlines an important distinction: dividing an emperor's workload did not necessarily mean announcing the creation of separate civilizations. Provinces could change administrators while households continued to farm, trade, litigate, and pay what the state demanded.

Constantine emerged victorious from another period of civil war and established an imperial capital on the Bosporus, inaugurated as Constantinople in 330. Its site linked the Black Sea with the Mediterranean and offered access to eastern frontier provinces. Constantine favored Christianity, but the move from religious tolerance and imperial patronage to an officially Christian political order took time. The empire remained a place of Jews, Christians with competing doctrines, followers of traditional cults, and people whose practices did not fit neat categories. The capital itself accumulated palaces, public spaces, churches, and defenses. It was a Roman capital, not a post-Roman state created in a single moment.

The map of Roman power in the west nevertheless changed. Imperial courts operated in several cities, and field armies increasingly depended on alliances, recruitment, and negotiation with groups whose leaders had their own ambitions. The Goths' sack of Rome in 410 was a shock to writers who regarded the city as a symbol of permanence. The Vandals took Carthage in 439 and controlled important North African resources. In 476 the military leader Odoacer removed the western emperor Romulus Augustulus in Italy. That year is a convenient textbook date, not an observed moment when roads crumbled, Latin stopped being spoken, or every Roman office disappeared. Roman law, taxes, landownership, episcopal institutions, and habits of administration survived in different combinations.

Former imperial territories developed different political arrangements. In Italy, Ostrogothic rulers claimed legitimacy partly by preserving Roman official forms. In Gaul, Frankish rulers competed and negotiated with local elites and bishops. In North Africa, the Vandal monarchy presided over a population with deeply rooted Roman institutions and Christian communities. For farmers and townspeople, a change of king could alter the destination of tax payments or the burden of maintaining soldiers, while familiar local intermediaries remained. Conflict and confiscation were real, but so were continuity and adaptation. Calling these regions simply “barbarian” tells us little about the complex ways people governed and were governed.

Farther east, the court at Constantinople retained an emperor, professional officials, a monetary system, and important tax-producing provinces. Greek was widely spoken in the eastern Mediterranean, while Roman legal tradition remained authoritative. Modern historians usually call this realm Byzantine. Its inhabitants understood themselves as Romans. The modern label can help us locate their distinctive later history, but it can conceal the degree to which the eastern state was a continuation of Roman imperial government. The same state also changed: religious institutions gained political influence, frontier pressures shifted, and urban life varied markedly between the great capitals and smaller provincial centers.

Justinian ruled from 527 to 565 and attempted to strengthen this imperial order. One project collected and reorganized centuries of Roman legal writings, imperial constitutions, and juristic arguments. The collection now called the Corpus of Civil Law did not invent a legal system out of nothing; it selected, preserved, and revised older material for a sixth-century government. It could give officials and students an authoritative reference, while everyday disputes still depended on judges, languages, local influence, and access to the courts. The legal compilation's later importance in European law should not be mistaken for immediate, uniform application throughout every village Justinian claimed to govern.

In Constantinople, factional tension and opposition to imperial policies exploded into the Nika revolt of 532. Parts of the city burned. Imperial forces eventually crushed the uprising at enormous human cost, although the numerical death toll in narrative sources must be handled cautiously. Justinian then rebuilt the capital's principal church, Hagia Sophia, as a vast statement of religious and imperial patronage. The building required architects, skilled artisans, stone, timber, transport, money, and labor. A splendid church tells us about engineering and patronage, but it also directs attention toward the resources extracted from the empire's inhabitants to make such building possible.

Justinian's generals campaigned in Vandal North Africa and Ostrogothic Italy, bringing large territories under eastern imperial rule. The wars were not merely the restoration of familiar institutions for grateful populations. Long campaigning in Italy damaged towns, agricultural districts, and transport networks. Some people changed political allegiances repeatedly as armies advanced and withdrew. When we look at Ravenna's mosaics, we see both the emperor's claim to renewed Roman unity and an Italian community drawn into expensive struggles between rulers. Even after imperial successes, parts of Italy passed to Lombard control later in the sixth century.

A further danger arrived through disease. In the early 540s, an epidemic described by contemporaries reached Constantinople and other regions. Modern work connects the outbreak with plague, but the precise demographic losses and the scale of its long-term effect remain debated. Procopius, an important sixth-century writer, provided a vivid account of suffering in the capital. He also wrote from particular political positions and used literary techniques. His testimony should be compared with other written sources, archaeology, coinage, and the limits of what any one city can reveal. It is enough to say that disease compounded the pressures imposed by warfare, taxation, and disrupted livelihoods without claiming a single numerical catastrophe across the entire Mediterranean.

The church became increasingly central to local administration and communal life. Bishops could distribute aid, negotiate with political leaders, sponsor buildings, and manage disputes. Monasteries attracted donations and organized religious communities. But Christianity was not institutionally uniform, and theological disagreements connected to law, language, and imperial loyalty sometimes divided communities. In Egypt, Syria, Armenia, and other regions, churches developed histories that did not simply mirror Constantinople's priorities. A government may declare its preferred doctrine; such a decree does not explain what every believer did or believed.

The seventh century brought further strain. A prolonged war between the eastern Romans and the Sasanian Empire reached across Syria, Egypt, Mesopotamia, and beyond. Emperor Heraclius recovered territory after major campaigns, but decades of mobilization had already altered resources and loyalties. Soon afterward, armies organized under the early Muslim caliphate took control of Syria, Palestine, and Egypt. These conquests did not erase Roman institutions overnight, and Greek and Coptic documents show important continuities under new rulers. They did, however, remove major eastern provinces from Constantinople's political control and force the remaining empire to adapt to a different strategic world.

The evidence for these transformations is layered. Imperial laws tell us what rulers wanted to regulate; they cannot prove every order was obeyed. Court historians describe wars and personalities, often with strong literary and political agendas. Mosaics, buildings, coins, graves, and settlement surveys show patterns that royal documents miss, but cannot supply the private thoughts of those who used them. Even the words “Roman,” “Goth,” and “Greek” shifted in meaning according to context. An accurate history must allow an inhabitant of sixth-century Italy to belong to a community with Roman habits while living under an Ostrogothic king, and later an eastern Roman administrator.

The Roman world did not end everywhere in 476. In the west, institutions were divided, reworked, and attached to new forms of kingship. In the east, an imperial government continued, sometimes reasserting its claims with force and sometimes surrendering territory. Across both, households bore costs, local cultures persisted, and Christian institutions reshaped public life. Ravenna's mosaic makes a political argument on behalf of Justinian. Our task is not to accept or dismiss that argument automatically, but to place it beside the lives and institutions the mosaic cannot show. Roman history became several histories, connected even as they diverged.

## A King of Kings beside Other Worlds: Sasanian Iran and Its Neighbors

Across Rome’s eastern frontier, Sasanian Iran had its own political language and institutions. The next case returns to that neighboring imperial history.

At a cliff called Naqsh-e Rostam in southern Iran, a stone relief presents a mounted king receiving a ring of authority from a divine figure. The image proclaims the legitimacy of Ardashir, founder of the Sasanian dynasty in the third century CE. It is tempting to take the scene as a direct photograph of how kingship worked. But a sculpted investiture is a carefully composed argument. Someone commissioned it, artists made it, and viewers were expected to recognize the claim that royal power had sacred sanction. The carving gives us an entrance into Sasanian history, provided we remember that those who farmed royal land or paid taxes did not get to commission equally prominent accounts of their lives.

Ardashir defeated the ruling Arsacid, or Parthian, dynasty around 224 CE and built a new political order centered on Iranian territories. The Sasanians ruled for more than four centuries. Their kings sometimes described themselves as heirs to earlier Persian imperial traditions, while using institutions and practices inherited from the more recent past. From their western heartlands they governed Mesopotamian cities, Iranian plateaus, and regions reaching toward Central and South Asia, though the boundaries and strength of their control changed repeatedly. A map colored uniformly for the years 224 to 651 would hide rebellions, client kingdoms, military frontiers, and the varying autonomy of great landholding families.

A particularly striking Sasanian image commemorates the victory of Shapur the First over the Roman emperor Valerian in 260. It is evidence of an extraordinary military and symbolic event. But the rivalry between Rome and Iran was not one continuous battle. These powers traded, exchanged embassies, negotiated frontiers, sheltered refugees, and sometimes paid subsidies or made alliances with smaller political communities. Armenia and Mesopotamia were not simply empty squares between two armies. Their rulers, religious institutions, and households pursued their own strategies within a dangerous imperial neighborhood.

Ctesiphon, on the Tigris near modern Baghdad, became a central royal city. Archaeological and textual evidence indicates that its wider urban complex contained communities with different religious affiliations. It was connected to irrigation agriculture and the movement of goods along rivers and roads. Royal palaces could be imposing, but the city required food, pottery, labor, and transport from far beyond court precincts. For a farmer in a canal-fed district, imperial politics might be encountered as a tax assessment, a change of officials, or an army's demand for supplies. The court's magnificent architecture was sustained by less visible institutions of extraction and coordination.

The Sasanians were associated closely with Zoroastrian religious traditions. Royal imagery, fire temples, and priestly institutions attest their public importance. Yet historians disagree about how early and how completely a centralized religious establishment took shape. Some later accounts portray the dynasty's founding as the moment a single state church was established. Specialist study of the evidence suggests a longer, contested process shaped by kings, priestly figures such as Kartir, and powerful families. It is more accurate to describe changing patronage, ritual authority, and sometimes coercion than to imagine a uniform policy applied unchanged for four hundred years.

Religious life was much more varied than the royal iconography suggests. Jews lived and studied in Sasanian Mesopotamia, where rabbinic traditions that eventually formed the Babylonian Talmud developed over generations. Christian communities also grew, including churches oriented toward Syriac language and institutions. Some Christians lived under suspicion when Roman emperors patronized Christianity across the frontier; others played significant roles in local life. Mani, active in the third century, taught a religious movement that spread widely and faced persecution. Neither the court's preferred religion nor any one community's surviving texts can represent all the people of the empire.

Sasanian kings relied on aristocratic families, administrators, soldiers, priests, and provincial authorities. The vocabulary of social hierarchy in later Iranian texts places warriors, religious specialists, scribes, and producers in ordered categories. Such ideal schemes tell us how educated authors wished society to appear, not the exact occupation or wealth of each person. Landholding families possessed resources that emperors needed; administrators depended on local knowledge; villages maintained irrigation and cultivation. This negotiation between royal authority and powerful intermediaries made the empire durable, but also meant that succession crises and elite rivalries could bring sudden political change.

Agricultural wealth was especially important in Mesopotamia and Iran's cultivated districts. Canals, fields, livestock, and workshops supported both ordinary livelihoods and military campaigns. Households did not all possess the same access to land or freedom of movement. Some people worked as dependents or under coercion; wealthy estates could command disproportionate resources. Evidence for these arrangements comes through law, later literary descriptions, seals, excavated settlements, and administrative traces. Much remains incomplete. It would be misleading to turn a royal fiscal category into a full account of every rural worker's experience.

Trade connected Sasanian territories to Central Asia, the Indian Ocean, the Persian Gulf, and the Roman world. Silk, glass, textiles, and precious-metal vessels circulated along routes that changed with politics and demand. Silver plates showing a king hunting were made for elite display and gift exchange; their iconography traveled into regions far beyond direct Sasanian rule. An object with an Iranian design found in another country proves contact or imitation, not necessarily that an Iranian governor administered its owner's community. The spread of artistic motifs is a different historical process from territorial conquest.

The sixth-century ruler Khosrow the First, usually dated to 531–579, became famous in later Iranian and Arabic writing as a model of just kingship. The reputation has a history of its own. Accounts of tax, military, and administrative reforms may preserve important changes, but praise written generations later needs to be separated from contemporary evidence. Khosrow's reign included war, diplomacy, and cultural exchange. Greek and Indian intellectual traditions reached Iranian settings through translators and learned communities, although individual stories of book transfers require source criticism. Royal claims to justice existed beside the practical business of collecting revenue and maintaining armies.

Arabian politics mattered to the Sasanians too. States and confederations across the Arabian Peninsula developed relationships with Iran and the eastern Romans, particularly around the Persian Gulf and the Red Sea. These were not passive extensions of imperial rivalry. Local rulers used foreign alliances to pursue regional interests, and religious networks crossed imperial boundaries. The question of who controlled a route or town might matter more to traders than a distant ruler's formal title. This broader geography helps explain why a history of late antique Iran belongs alongside histories of Arabia, the Horn of Africa, and South Asia.

In the early seventh century a major war with the eastern Romans transformed western Asia. Sasanian forces took important Roman territories, including Jerusalem in 614 and Egypt soon afterward. Emperor Heraclius eventually counterattacked, and the conflict ended without a lasting Sasanian conquest of the eastern Mediterranean. The war was costly, and Sasanian internal political instability intensified. Subsequent armies from Arabia defeated Sasanian forces in a sequence of campaigns during the 630s and 640s. The final ruler, Yazdegerd the Third, died in 651. The conquest was a dynastic and political ending; the languages, religious communities, landholding arrangements, and artistic practices of Iranian societies did not disappear on that date.

Our sources ask for caution. A royal relief is evidence of royal self-presentation. A coin can reveal a ruler's name, image, and assertions of authority; it cannot establish total obedience wherever it traveled. The historian of an enemy empire may dramatize battles to praise his own ruler. Religious documents preserve disputes through the concerns of their communities. Scholars compare these records with archaeology and texts in Middle Persian, Greek, Syriac, Armenian, and other languages. The gaps are real, and responsible history marks them rather than silently building a complete picture around one famous king.

Sasanian history matters not simply because a later empire conquered its lands, but because the dynasty helped shape the institutions and culture of western and central Asia over centuries. Roads, urban communities, aristocratic networks, scholarly traditions, and religious institutions survived political reversals and entered new settings. The stone king receiving a ring gave one court's explanation for why it deserved to rule. The wider history is made of all the negotiations, work, migrations, and disagreements that the relief leaves outside its frame.
