# Many Courts, One Canal: China from Division to the Early Tang

In northern China, a visitor to the caves at Yungang can stand before enormous figures of the Buddha cut into rock. These monuments were made with imperial patronage in a period when no single government ruled all the territories once administered by the Han dynasty. The sculptures bear traces of cultural and artistic exchanges reaching across Central Asia and into South Asia. Their existence challenges a familiar narrative: that centuries of political division were only an empty interval between two unified empires. In fact, people governed, traded, migrated, translated sacred texts, and created new institutions while rival courts claimed authority over different regions.

The Han imperial state ended in 220 CE. The following era is remembered through the Three Kingdoms and later the Jin dynasty, but those labels conceal many separate episodes of warfare, migration, and reorganization. The Jin briefly reunited much of China in the late third century before its western government was overthrown in the early fourth. Political centers moved, armed groups competed for territory, and people from northern districts relocated toward the Yangtze River and regions farther south. Migration was not simply a movement of cultivated northerners bringing history to an empty south. Southern societies already possessed communities, landholding systems, and distinctive cultural histories that newcomers encountered and changed.

In the south, courts based at Jiankang, near present-day Nanjing, developed literary, administrative, and religious institutions. Northern regimes were varied; many rulers came from groups with histories outside the Han court's conventional cultural categories. Modern descriptions sometimes contrast “Chinese” and “non-Chinese” dynasties too neatly. Elite identity, language, marriage, political service, and local belonging changed across generations. People could adopt court institutions while maintaining other affiliations. Dynastic histories composed later often organized this complex world into legitimate and illegitimate lines of succession, and those categories are themselves part of the evidence we must interpret.

One major northern power was the Northern Wei, established by leaders associated with the Tuoba and Xianbei. Its rulers controlled large parts of north China in the fifth century. They faced the practical questions familiar to other states: how to collect revenue, register people, secure soldiers, negotiate with landholding elites, and present a credible form of kingship. Reform and settlement policies changed over time. Under Emperor Xiaowen in the late fifth century, the court moved to Luoyang and adopted measures favoring Chinese language, dress, and institutions among elites. These policies were contested. They were political choices, not proof that one culture had simply displaced another.

Buddhism became a major part of political and social life. Translators and monks worked to render texts written in Indic and Central Asian languages into Chinese. Their choices mattered: philosophical terms did not always have direct equivalents, and a translation could open new debates. The scholar-monk Kumarajiva, active in Chang'an around the beginning of the fifth century, is associated with influential translations. Monasteries offered spaces for learning, worship, patronage, and sometimes economic activity. Support came from emperors and other donors, while periods of persecution remind us that royal relations with religious institutions could be unstable.

Yungang and Longmen make these developments visible. Royal and private donors paid for caves, images, and inscriptions, while many workers cut stone and supplied materials. Images incorporate forms that can be compared with artistic traditions along long-distance routes; styles changed as workshops and patrons made new choices. Yet a sculpture's resemblance to one from a distant region does not prove a single traveling artist made both. Nor does the survival of a monumental cave mean most rural families could afford such a donation. Archaeology gives us remarkable evidence of Buddhist patronage and technique, but it does not turn every inhabitant into a devotee of the court's preferred religion.

Agricultural and fiscal changes matter just as much. Northern states sought ways to organize and tax cultivated land; the Northern Wei is associated with an equal-field system in which the state attempted to allocate certain categories of land under specified obligations. The details of actual implementation varied, and legal ideals should not be confused with a universal census. In the south, migration and the growth of riverine agriculture transformed the economic geography of the Yangtze region. Households, landlords, and officials were not affected in the same ways. Dynastic reunification would later draw on these varied regional developments rather than simply recreate the Han economy.

In 581 a military leader, Yang Jian, founded the Sui dynasty, and by 589 his government had conquered the southern Chen dynasty. Reunification meant that one court could claim authority across much of China after centuries of division. But this was not a return to a frozen past. New administrative arrangements, law, taxation, land policies, and military organization depended on institutions developed during the preceding centuries. The court had to manage differences among former northern and southern subjects. Its authority depended on roads, documents, transport, and people willing or compelled to make those systems work.

The Grand Canal is a striking illustration. Sui rulers undertook major work connecting water routes and improving the movement of grain, armies, and officials between the Yangtze region and northern centers. Some waterways were older; the Sui expanded and linked them on a grand scale. Canal building required labor and imposed heavy burdens. A canal could enrich traders and strengthen the capital's food supply while harming households conscripted to dig and maintain it. The material result lasted beyond the dynasty that commissioned it. A public work can therefore be both an achievement in infrastructure and a record of coercion.

Sui expansion and military campaigns placed the government under intense pressure, including costly expeditions against the kingdom of Goguryeo on the Korean peninsula and in adjoining regions. Rebellions spread, and the dynasty collapsed in 618. The Tang dynasty followed. Its rulers inherited institutions and infrastructure, but had to secure them through warfare, negotiation, and administration. The Tang capital at Chang'an became one of Eurasia's major urban centers. Merchants, officials, scholars, religious specialists, and travelers passed through a city shaped by court planning and international connections. Cosmopolitan exchange was enabled by institutions; it did not remove hierarchy or the burdens of government.

The early Tang court combined military power with new forms of political organization. Emperor Taizong ruled from 626 to 649 and became a prominent model in later political writing, which must be read with attention to its moral purpose. Officials used law, examinations, appointments, and inherited relationships to secure careers, though the examination system had not yet become the sole route to office. Frontier politics linked Tang rulers to Turkic and other Inner Asian powers. Diplomacy, alliance, and battle were intertwined. The empire did not occupy a sealed Chinese core surrounded by people with no independent institutions.

The Korean peninsula, Japan, and other neighboring societies were also part of this changing regional history, without being mere recipients of Tang policy. Korean kingdoms competed with one another and negotiated with Chinese courts. In the seventh century, conflicts involving Goguryeo, Baekje, Silla, and Tang forces reshaped political arrangements. Japanese rulers and religious institutions studied continental models while making choices in their own settings. Shared Buddhist texts and official practices circulated through travelers and scholars, but the shape of adoption depended on local power and interpretation. Connections should never be mistaken for political or cultural sameness.

We know this history from court annals, law codes, inscriptions, Buddhist writings, excavated tombs, and artworks. Official dynastic histories explain succession and elite politics; a merchant's receipt or monastic inscription can expose other concerns. Later authors sometimes praised reunification so strongly that they described the earlier centuries chiefly as disorder. The evidence suggests instead a period of creative and violent institutional change, followed by new experiments in coordinated rule. Neither division nor unity explains everything about a community's prosperity, security, or learning.

The Yungang images, the canal, and the avenues of Chang'an belong to the same broad story. One shows how a court in a divided world invested in a universal religious language. Another shows how rulers mobilized labor to join regions physically. The last shows the possibilities and inequalities of a new imperial capital. China's history between the Han and Tang is not a blank space separating great dynasties. It is a long process in which people and institutions made the political reunification of the seventh century possible—and gave that reunification consequences that reached far beyond any emperor's lifetime.
