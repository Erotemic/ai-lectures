# After the Plague: Work, War, and Learning in Late Medieval Europe

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

A laborer negotiating wages in fourteenth-century England confronted questions much older than the Black Death: who controlled land, who set the terms of service, and what a household needed to survive. But plague mortality transformed the bargaining situation. When workers were scarce, some could demand higher pay or different conditions. Authorities reacted by trying to restrain wages and compel service. The resulting disputes tell us more about late medieval Europe than a simple claim that the continent suddenly emerged from a dark age. Between the fourteenth century and 1500, Europeans experienced disease, war, social conflict, artistic production, commerce, and political experimentation. These developments did not occur at one pace in every region.

The epidemics of the mid-fourteenth century killed a substantial share of people in many European communities. Their consequences depended on local arrangements for land, inheritance, work, and political authority. In some places, surviving tenants renegotiated rents or acquired plots previously cultivated by others. In other places, landlords and rulers tried to preserve obligations that had existed before mortality changed the labor supply. A village and a commercial city could respond quite differently. Economic history cannot infer that all peasants became prosperous or that every elite family lost power merely because labor was less abundant.

England's Ordinance of Labourers of 1349 and Statute of Labourers of 1351 are revealing documents. They aimed to regulate wages and services in a society whose governing elites feared the consequences of labor scarcity. The texts describe what authorities wanted people to do, not necessarily what people did. Similar efforts occurred in other parts of western Europe, but their provisions and outcomes varied. Historian Samuel Cohn's comparative work shows why a uniform account of post-plague labor law is inadequate. When we read a law, we should ask who wrote it, whose interests it protected, and how enforcement worked on the ground.

Conflict over work and lordship appeared in popular uprisings. The English rising of 1381, for example, involved grievances over taxation and social obligations, but its participants did not all share one program. Urban artisans, rural communities, and leaders articulated demands under different conditions. The revolt was suppressed, yet its existence demonstrates that ordinary people made political claims in a language of justice and obligation. Other cities experienced struggles involving guilds, workers, and ruling families. It would be inaccurate to turn each protest into a modern democratic movement, but equally inaccurate to imagine late medieval workers as politically silent.

War remained a central burden. The prolonged conflicts grouped under the name Hundred Years' War, from 1337 into the fifteenth century, involved shifting claims and coalitions around the crowns of England and France. Campaigns drew resources from rural households, towns, and nobles, and could destroy crops or disrupt trade. Soldiers' employment, ransom, taxation, and the movement of armed companies affected communities far from the most famous battles. The war was not one continuous battle, nor was it simply a duel between modern nation-states. Political loyalties were territorial, dynastic, and local, often changing as rulers and military conditions changed.

The later stages of conflict show how institutions adapted. French and English monarchies made different efforts to raise revenue and organize military force, while estates, town councils, and local elites negotiated claims over taxation. The role of figures such as Joan of Arc must be understood within specific military and religious circumstances rather than as an inevitable march toward modern nationalism. Judicial records and political chronicles preserve much of this story from the perspective of courts. The people who supplied grain, transport, and care to armies are generally less visible, although their labor made campaigns possible.

Cities connected agricultural districts to wider markets. In northern Italy, the Low Countries, parts of Germany, and elsewhere, merchants and craftspeople participated in networks producing textiles, metalwork, books, and finance. The prosperity of one commercial center could depend on trade with regions beyond Europe, including North Africa and western Asia. Guild regulations and municipal governments shaped access to work and political voice, sometimes excluding women, migrants, unfree people, and poorer residents. Urban culture offered opportunity, but was not an equal civic community. We can admire a beautiful building while asking who paid for it and who labored on it.

The movement often called the Renaissance developed in this varied urban and courtly setting. Scholars studied ancient texts and languages; artists and architects experimented with forms and representation; patrons used commissions to display status and civic identity. Florence became one important center, but it was not the sole source of learning or artistic change. Humanist scholarship drew on manuscripts transmitted through many communities, including Byzantine and Islamic intellectual histories. The term rebirth is a metaphor associated with particular cultural programs, not evidence that learning had died during preceding centuries. Medieval scholars, translators, and artisans had maintained and transformed bodies of knowledge over many generations.

Printing became a powerful new technology for reproducing texts in parts of Europe during the fifteenth century. Johannes Gutenberg's work in Mainz, associated with the printed Bible of the 1450s, helped establish a form of movable-type printing that spread widely. Movable type and printing already had long histories in East Asia, so a global account must not label Europe the first inventor of printing. The European system drew on local techniques, economic conditions, and markets. A printed book still required paper, type, skilled workers, capital, and readers. Wider availability of some texts did not mean universal literacy or immediate agreement about their contents.

Religious life occupied a large place in these societies. Churches, monasteries, lay confraternities, and charitable institutions provided worship, education, and social resources, while also possessing land and political influence. Criticism of ecclesiastical practices existed well before the sixteenth-century Reformation. Religious dissent could be punished, and minority communities sometimes faced persecution. In 1492, the Catholic monarchs Ferdinand and Isabella ordered the expulsion of Jews who did not convert from their realms, a major coercive act with lasting consequences for families and diaspora communities. This history belongs beside artistic patronage and expanding commerce, not in a separate footnote.

European relationships to the Atlantic also changed. Portuguese vessels explored and raided along parts of the West African coast during the fifteenth century, supported by interests in trade, prestige, and political power. These voyages encountered African states and societies with their own diplomatic and commercial institutions. Maritime expeditions depended on navigational knowledge, shipbuilding, finance, and coerced labor in some emerging systems. By the end of the century, European rulers were supporting more ambitious long-distance voyages. These decisions were not the inevitable result of the Renaissance; they emerged from concrete conflicts and opportunities in particular courts and ports.

In 1492, Christopher Columbus's expedition from Spain reached Caribbean islands inhabited by Indigenous people. That event is sometimes presented as a European discovery of a previously empty land, which is false. Caribbean communities had long histories of farming, navigation, political organization, and cultural life. The encounter initiated processes of domination and violent change that would accelerate in the sixteenth century. It also shows why a history of late medieval Europe cannot end with purely European achievements. Decisions made in European courts would have consequences for peoples who had not participated in those courts and had their own histories.

The sources for late medieval Europe are extensive but selective. Wage laws, tax rolls, urban accounts, trial documents, art, and books survive in different amounts across regions. A surviving regulation tells us the purpose of lawmakers; an artist's commission tells us about patrons and workers differently; a chronicle may glorify a commander. Historical comparisons must make room for both change and persistence. By 1500 Europe had experienced major political and economic adjustments. It had not become uniformly prosperous, enlightened, united, or destined to rule the world. The laborer negotiating wages remained one of the people whose choices and constraints made history.
