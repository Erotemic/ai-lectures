# Java and Bali: fields, water associations, ferries, and political obligation

A temple, an irrigation calendar, and a ferry charter bring different obligations into view. Across Java and Bali, we will ask how fields, water, transport, and institutions shaped one another. Similar problems do not imply identical rules on the two islands.

## Rice Fields and Stone Temples: Java before 1500

In central Java, stone reliefs show people, buildings, processions, and stories. Nearby landscapes contain fields, rivers, villages, and the remains of religious monuments. It is tempting to start with a great temple, name the dynasty that built it, and move to the next dynasty. Yet a temple was not built from dynastic ambition alone. It required workers, materials, food, routes, and institutions able to organize resources over time. To understand Java between about seven hundred and fifteen hundred, we must bring the monumental record together with inscriptions about land, taxation, and local obligations. The result is a history of rulers and religious communities, but also of farmers and households whose work made the celebrated architecture possible.

Borobudur, built in the eighth and ninth centuries in central Java, is a massive Buddhist monument with terraces and narrative reliefs. It is part of a broader sacred landscape that includes the temples of Mendut and Pawon. A modern visitor can read its sculpted panels as a statement of Buddhist learning and visual craft. But the monument is not a transcript of ordinary life. Reliefs follow religious, artistic, and courtly conventions; a scene resembling a marketplace is not a census of markets. The dating and association with the Sailendra dynasty rest on historical and archaeological arguments, not on an inventory naming every laborer who set a stone in place.

Prambanan, another major compound in central Java, presents a different religious emphasis, centered on Shiva and connected with other Hindu and Buddhist sites. Its famous temple groups and reliefs complicate any attempt to label a whole century simply Buddhist or simply Hindu. Elite patronage could change, and different religious communities coexisted in a landscape with many traditions. The UNESCO descriptions of Borobudur and Prambanan give useful introductions to architecture and chronology. They should be supplemented by inscriptions and regional research when we want to understand who controlled land or how religious foundations were maintained. A monument's current designation is not the same as its medieval social world.

Old Javanese inscriptions open another kind of record. Many announce that a village, part of a village, or a defined area received a changed tax status, often called a sima. These inscriptions concern obligations, privileges, and relations among kings, officials, local leaders, and religious institutions. Research by Miho Yamasaki on the inscriptions of King Balitung's period describes sima grants as a tool connecting rulers to people exercising regional authority. A grant could exempt certain dues or redirect resources to a foundation. It was a public act with consequences that extended beyond the ruler's name on the stone.

Imagine the questions a village might face when its tax status changed. Who collected payments before the grant, and who had the right afterward? Which craftspeople or merchants received an exemption? What fields, paths, or water sources were included? Was the decision accepted locally, or did it require witnesses and ceremonies to make it enforceable? An inscription may identify offices and boundaries but seldom records the private reaction of each household. The historian can explain the legal mechanism without pretending every person welcomed it. A royal favor could shift resources toward a monastery or temple while also changing the obligations borne by people who worked nearby.

Rice fields were central to much of this world, though Java contained multiple ecological zones and livelihoods. Wet-rice cultivation depended on water, soil, and coordinated effort. Farmers managed planting and harvesting seasons, while irrigation arrangements could require cooperation among households and local authorities. Some inscriptions describe fields and land designated for religious support; others indicate disputes or the creation of new cultivation. When a text mentions irrigated land, it is a clue to both productivity and regulation. It is not proof that a single centralized government personally directed every ditch and terrace. Local knowledge and institutions mattered.

Java's volcanic landscapes created opportunities and risks. Fertile soils could support cultivation, while eruptions, floods, and river changes affected settlement. Archaeologists study the environmental context of monuments rather than treating their positions as eternal. But it would be a mistake to explain a political transformation by naming one volcanic event without precise supporting evidence. Dynasties moved political centers for combinations of reasons, and long-term landscape change was only one possible factor. A convincing explanation must join dates, inscriptions, settlement patterns, and geology, with uncertainty where they disagree. Environmental change does not remove human choice.

By the tenth century, major political centers had shifted toward eastern Java, and later kingdoms developed new combinations of court ritual, warfare, trade, and regional relationships. The names of rulers and dynasties change more readily in surviving texts than the rhythms of farming and local social life. Yet those local activities were never frozen. A new court might create grants, demand service, sponsor literature, or negotiate with religious communities. Inscriptions are valuable because they show administration happening in named places, sometimes far from the capital. They also reflect the interests of those who could authorize and preserve such statements.

In the late thirteenth and fourteenth centuries, the kingdom known as Majapahit became a major political and cultural center in eastern Java. Its history is often told through claims of influence across much of island Southeast Asia. The court poem commonly called the Nagarakretagama, composed in 1365 and associated with the writer Prapanca, celebrates the reign of Hayam Wuruk and names places connected with the court's world. Such a text is historically precious. But a poem praising a ruler is not a modern map of directly administered territory. A named place might be a diplomatic partner, trading contact, ceremonial participant, claimed dependency, or something else. The nature of the relationship must be argued, not assumed.

The archaeological landscape of Trowulan, associated with Majapahit, provides a different scale of evidence. Surveys and excavations have found brick structures, waterways, tools, ceramics, and objects of domestic and craft use. The Indonesian submission to UNESCO's tentative heritage list describes a broad complex containing both monumental features and traces of settlement and production. As with every heritage nomination, some interpretations in that submission are proposals rather than conclusions universally established by specialists. Still, the material record demonstrates why a capital cannot be reconstructed only from descriptions of its palace. People cooked, made goods, used water, and occupied spaces that court poetry had little reason to celebrate.

Trade linked Java with other parts of Southeast Asia and beyond, but exchange took many forms. Imported ceramics and coins can attest to movement of goods. Local ports connected sea travel with inland routes and agricultural regions. A merchant arriving from elsewhere needed local intermediaries who could arrange supplies, storage, and agreements. Not every coastal town was a possession of the capital, and not every imported object indicates a migrant household. Archaeological trade evidence and royal claims about distant places answer different questions. A history centered only on maritime networks can overlook the land-based production that made exchange possible.

Religious life also took varied forms. Buddhist and Hindu temples, local traditions, and later Islamic communities belong to overlapping histories of Java. Changes in royal patronage cannot be equated with the immediate replacement of ordinary people's practices. A religious monument reflects donations and authority, but the people around it might have participated in different ways. In some periods and places, writers used Sanskrit or Old Javanese literary forms that require specialist interpretation. Translators and epigraphers make these sources accessible, and their names deserve attention because identifying an inscription's meaning is skilled historical work, not an automatic reading of stone.

Gender and household life are less directly documented than dynastic titles, but the questions remain essential. Who cultivated fields, maintained irrigation, prepared food for temple labor, and cared for dependents? What could women own or donate, and how did households respond to changes in dues? Some Javanese inscriptions name women in positions connected with grants or authority; that evidence cautions against treating political and economic life as entirely male. It does not justify a claim that the same rights or obligations prevailed for every woman. Reading against the elite grain means asking whose work a monument assumes and whose name never appears in its records.

A final problem is the shape of the story itself. It would be convenient to say that one kingdom built Buddhist Borobudur, another built Hindu Prambanan, and Majapahit then united the archipelago. But historical processes do not fit that simple staircase. Religious patronage overlapped, political reach varied, administrative grants followed local negotiations, and maritime connections did not automatically become provincial government. Borobudur, Prambanan, Old Javanese sima inscriptions, and the remains of Trowulan are different kinds of evidence from different centuries. Their value increases when we resist turning them into proof of one timeless Java.

The common thread is work organized through place. Fields and waterways supported settlements; settlements supplied people and resources for religious foundations; courts used inscriptions and ceremonies to mark claims; merchants connected the island to other seas. Monuments remain visually powerful, but their material histories point outward to villages, gardens, workshops, and the decisions of households. By fifteen hundred Java had experienced many political and religious transformations. Its history is not a procession of rulers carried forward by architecture. It is a history of communities making and remaking the conditions under which temples and kingdoms could exist.

## The Water Calendar: Fields and Irrigation Associations in Bali

Across the strait in Bali, irrigation associations reveal a different arrangement of water, cultivation, and shared obligation.

On a hillside in Bali, water descends from one rice field to another. A channel turns it aside; a small gate gives one terrace more while another waits. The fields are not independent rectangles. If everyone takes water at the same moment, fields below may run short. If one valley plants rice continuously while its neighbors fallow, insects and diseases can move between the crops. This is an ecological problem, but it is also a problem of social agreement. The Balinese irrigation associations known as subak have become a classic example of how farmers, temples and political authorities negotiated that problem.

Rice cultivation is older than the written evidence for the particular associations. Some Old Balinese inscriptions from the late ninth century already refer to fields and technical labor related to water. The Bebetin inscription, commonly dated to 896 CE, mentions a specialist associated with irrigation tunnels. Later inscriptions use terms related to kasuwakan, a forerunner of the word subak. These are valuable because they show that rice-water management had recognized technical and institutional vocabulary. They do not tell us that a modern-style water-temple hierarchy existed in precisely the same form across the island.

A particularly concrete kind of source is a royal order about land and water. A document might allow residents of a village to extend a canal or take water for fields, or specify exemptions connected with sacred sites. Such a grant reveals a claim that villagers wished to secure and a ruler was willing to recognize. It is not the transcript of a farmers' meeting. Yet it tells us that irrigation crossed the line between local practice and court authority. A king who could recognize a water right participated in the political organization of farming, even if villagers performed much of the labor.

The names of irrigation areas matter. An association organized around a common water source did not have to match the limits of a single residential village. A channel could serve fields owned or worked by people living in several settlements. That is why the relevant group might be defined by a shared intake rather than a shared street. Modern subak institutions show how workable this can be, but historical scholarship reads the inscriptions to identify which boundaries and officers actually appear in a given century. A water boundary, a village boundary and a tax boundary could describe different overlapping territories.

The religious dimension is not an optional decoration added after the engineering. Water temples and ritual calendars organize relations among cultivators and the larger landscape. Offerings, festivals and decisions about planting can help people recognize their shared dependence on a source of water. UNESCO describes surviving Balinese networks in which temples serve as focal points for coordinating the movement of water. But a present-day festival cannot by itself give us the date when its medieval predecessor began. For ancient history we need inscriptions, sites and carefully argued comparisons.

Anthropologist Stephen Lansing and ecologist James Kremer proposed an influential explanation of why coordinated planting schedules could be effective. Water sharing is only part of the problem. If neighboring fields are synchronized for planting and fallow periods, pests may lose a continuous supply of host crops. If schedules are too similar across a larger area, water demand may peak at the same time. The model explores how coordination at different scales can balance these pressures. It is a tool for thinking about a living agroecological system, not a machine that produces dated evidence for a particular eleventh-century temple.

The appealing image of farmer cooperatives working independently of rulers has also been challenged. Brigitta Hauser-Schäublin argues that regional lords and kings were involved in the economic and ritual organization of irrigation. Henk Schulte Nordholt's archival study of later periods likewise questions a purely bottom-up account, showing the importance of dynastic projects and changes under colonial administration. These arguments do not cancel cooperation among farmers. They change the question from whether the state or the village controlled water to when each could intervene, and what arrangements were possible at different scales.

A hillside landscape can make authority look deceptively simple. Water moves downhill regardless of who claims to rule. A farmer near the source has an immediate advantage over someone farther below; a court may grant rights but lack the personnel to check every gate; a temple may offer rules that all parties see as legitimate. People need practices for sharing losses as well as gains. For historians, the task is to discover where formal rules appear, how power is distributed and whether changing ecology demanded new forms of agreement, rather than declaring in advance that ancient irrigation was entirely democratic or entirely coerced.

The archaeology of terraces and canals can supplement the texts, but dating built agricultural landscapes is difficult. People extend, repair and reshape them. A channel whose bank looks old may have been rebuilt many times, and a temple in use today may contain architectural phases far later than the first irrigation settlement nearby. The historical depth of cultivation is real, yet a single impressive view of terraced hills tells us little about which terrace was operating in 950. Researchers must combine site-level chronology with inscriptions and environmental study to avoid treating a living landscape as a frozen ancient photograph.

The labor of cultivation also matters. Before a ruler could receive rice and before a temple could host a festival, people prepared nurseries, transplanted shoots, cleared weeds and kept channels open. The sources name officers and rights more readily than those daily movements. Women and men worked within households and communities whose precise responsibilities differed and cannot be inferred from a technical term alone. An irrigation association's legal existence was not the same as social equality among all of its members. Looking at the field calendar alongside its grants keeps both ecological coordination and hierarchy visible.

Why did this system last in changing forms? Not because every participant always cooperated willingly, nor because a perfectly designed template was once handed down and never revised. Farmers who shared water repeatedly confronted problems that individual households could not settle alone. Authorities could reward compliance or change the terms of access. Ritual institutions could give collective decisions a durable setting. The inscriptions show some of these relationships emerging in the medieval period, and later history shows that their balance kept changing. Longevity is a history of adjustment rather than proof of institutional stasis.

A water calendar links a physical resource to shared time. A gate opening today changes another person's harvest weeks later. Bali's long irrigation history is therefore about much more than spectacular terraces. It concerns how communities across a watershed made decisions without erasing competing rights and authorities. By listening to both the dated inscriptions and the modern debates among historians and anthropologists, we gain a history of agriculture as an institution: technical, religious, political and subject to revision.

## Seventy-Nine Crossings: Ferries, Taxes, and Law in Majapahit Java

Returning to fourteenth-century Java shifts our attention from field water to river crossings and the rights recorded in a royal charter.

Imagine a cart reaching the bank of a broad Javanese river in 1358. Its driver has goods to bring inland but no bridge. A ferry can carry the load across, provided someone operates the boat, maintains access to the landing and holds recognized rights to the crossing. Multiply that ordinary problem along the river network, and you begin to see the infrastructure of the Majapahit kingdom in eastern Java. A royal charter from 1358 records privileges associated with dozens of ferry sites. It is a reminder that a state could depend on moving carts and people as much as on monuments.

Majapahit is often introduced through the splendor of its court, the fame of Gajah Mada and the extent of places associated with its ruler. Those subjects matter, but the kingdom's power was practiced through local arrangements. The rivers and markets of East Java connected agricultural production, workshops, ports and the capital. Ken Hall's analysis of maritime trade and statecraft reads the Canggu ferry charter alongside other commercial records. It shows a court negotiating with people who performed transport and collected dues, not merely proclaiming sovereignty over a territory.

The charter refers to seventy-nine ferry crossing districts. The number is meaningful because these were recurring nodes of movement, not a single royal road. Canggu on the Brantas River served traffic between the coast and the inland court. A boat crossing carried economic consequences: delays mattered, an authorized fee could distinguish legitimate revenue from predatory collection, and recognition by a ruler could change the position of those who operated the service. Even if the charter does not give a detailed manual for constructing ferries, it places them inside the visible structure of government.

The 1358 grant is especially valuable because it records a change in the way certain obligations were defined. Hall describes an older category of royal revenues, derwaya haji, and its replacement for the ferrymen by a levy called pamuja. These terms are not simply two labels for the same universal modern tax. They describe obligations made meaningful within the political and ceremonial order of the court. A charter could change who collected or received resources, which intermediaries lost leverage and what new recognition a group received. Fiscal reform was also a struggle over status.

The ferry community gained rights to take part in ceremonial occasions linked to the ruler. Why would a transport charter mention festivals? Because royal ritual was a way to make authority visible. A group that supplied flowers, money or other goods to a court ceremony might also obtain acknowledgement and a direct relationship with the king. The same document could regulate movement, taxes and religious observance. Describing its ceremonial clauses as mere superstition would miss their practical political function: they established a place for particular people in a wider system of obligation and privilege.

Land grants show a similar mixture of privilege and authority. In Old Javanese records, a sima could be land associated with exemptions or special legal and fiscal rights. Such a grant might support a religious foundation or reward service. Scholars of the inscriptions stress that exemption never means the complete absence of obligations; it means that some revenues were changed, redirected or removed under conditions. A village could receive a document recognizing rights while still sustaining a local hierarchy. We should therefore ask exactly which taxes, services and jurisdictional arrangements a particular text changes.

Some documents concern litigation. Kenneth Hall discusses jayasong, certificates of victory granted to successful parties in legal disputes. The point of such a certificate was that a claim could be cited again if challenged. The written account might emphasize a victory and show litigants appealing to royal judicial authority. Like the copper plates of other Asian kingdoms, these records were produced for people with access to recognized legal procedures. A widow, farmer or merchant might appear if a dispute reached the record, but people unable to bring a case usually remain in the silence around it.

Majapahit-era society also appears in a famous court poem composed by Prapañca in 1365. Often called the Nagarakretagama, the work names itself Deśawarṇana, a description of districts. It celebrates the reign of Hayam Wuruk and recounts royal journeys through eastern Java. The poet names places, religious establishments and local relationships in a way that can be compared with inscriptions. But a literary tour cannot be read as a complete census of villages. The landscapes and people are selected to tell a story about the ruler and his order.

Read the poem alongside the ferry charter and a different picture appears. The court might celebrate itself as the center of a wide world, while the ferry inscription records particular transport communities bargaining for recognition. They are not contradictory documents. The poem makes a claim about political and religious order; the charter describes one way in which that order was financed, enacted and contested. A court could be powerful without directly administering every village. Its reach grew through obligations that residents, religious institutions and local elites had reasons to honor—or reasons to challenge.

This matters when modern maps color large areas of Southeast Asia as a single Majapahit empire. Names of places in a court poem can mark alliance, tribute, prestige or a claim of superiority rather than a permanent staffed province. The legal sources speak most clearly where the court actually issued grants and recognized disputes. Some distant places had their own rulers and networks that cannot be erased by the perspective of a Javanese author. A history of influence should say what kind of relationship is documented before drawing a border.

An ordinary ferry operator made decisions that could affect the revenue reaching the court, but the same operator lived within local relationships. A landing might depend on workers with boats, families who supplied provisions and people who maintained an approach road. Most of them are not named in the charter. Their absence is the consequence of the record's legal purpose, not a demonstration that these tasks were unimportant. The charter gives us a view of official recognition. To imagine the actual crossing, we must keep the legal text and the material demands of transport in conversation without inventing biographies of its workers.

The ferries of 1358 belong beside the rice fields, shrines and markets of Java. A wealthy state was not a machine operating independently of local economic life. Tax choices changed incentives and status. Religious ceremonies transformed contributions into public legitimacy. Legal documents gave some people ways to defend a claim while leaving many others unnamed. The Brantas River carried goods, but it also carried arguments about who had authority over movement and who would benefit from that movement. That is a history visible only when we look beyond the court's most famous monuments and its grandest claims.

#### Evidence boundary

The Old Balinese inscriptions establish irrigation terminology and specific grants in the ninth–twelfth centuries; present-day subak organizations and temple networks supply an analogy, not proof of unchanged medieval operation.
