# The Water Calendar: Fields and Irrigation Associations in Bali

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

On a hillside in Bali, water descends from one rice field to another. A channel turns it aside; a small gate gives one terrace more while another waits. The fields are not independent rectangles. If everyone takes water at the same moment, fields below may run short. If one valley plants rice continuously while its neighbors fallow, insects and diseases can move between the crops. This is an ecological problem, but it is also a problem of social agreement. The Balinese irrigation associations known as subak have become a classic example of how farmers, temples and political authorities negotiated that problem.

Rice cultivation is older than the written evidence for the particular associations. Some Old Balinese inscriptions from the late ninth century already refer to fields and technical labor related to water. The Bebetin inscription, commonly dated to 896 CE, mentions a specialist associated with irrigation tunnels. Later inscriptions use terms related to kasuwakan, a forerunner of the word subak. These are valuable because they show that rice-water management had recognized technical and institutional vocabulary. They do not tell us that a modern-style water-temple hierarchy existed in precisely the same form across the island.

A particularly concrete kind of source is a royal order about land and water. A document might allow residents of a village to extend a canal or take water for fields, or specify exemptions connected with sacred sites. Such a grant reveals a claim that villagers wished to secure and a ruler was willing to recognize. It is not the transcript of a farmers' meeting. Yet it tells us that irrigation crossed the line between local practice and court authority. A king who could recognize a water right participated in the political organization of farming, even if villagers performed much of the labor.

The names of irrigation areas matter. An association organized around a common water source did not have to match the limits of a single residential village. A channel could serve fields owned or worked by people living in several settlements. That is why the relevant group might be defined by a shared intake rather than a shared street. Modern subak institutions show how workable this can be, but historical scholarship reads the inscriptions to identify which boundaries and officers actually appear in a given century. A water boundary, a village boundary and a tax boundary could describe different overlapping territories.

The religious dimension is not an optional decoration added after the engineering. Water temples and ritual calendars organize relations among cultivators and the larger landscape. Offerings, festivals and decisions about planting can help people recognize their shared dependence on a source of water. UNESCO describes surviving Balinese networks in which temples serve as focal points for coordinating the movement of water. But a present-day festival cannot by itself give us the date when its medieval predecessor began. For ancient history we need inscriptions, sites and carefully argued comparisons.

Anthropologist Stephen Lansing and ecologist James Kremer proposed an influential explanation of why coordinated planting schedules could be effective. Water sharing is only part of the problem. If neighboring fields are synchronized for planting and fallow periods, pests may lose a continuous supply of host crops. If schedules are too similar across a larger area, water demand may peak at the same time. The model explores how coordination at different scales can balance these pressures. It is a tool for thinking about a living agroecological system, not a machine that produces dated evidence for a particular eleventh-century temple.

The appealing image of farmer cooperatives working independently of rulers has also been challenged. Brigitta Hauser-Schäublin argues that regional lords and kings were involved in the economic and ritual organization of irrigation. Henk Schulte Nordholt's archival study of later periods likewise questions a purely bottom-up account, showing the importance of dynastic projects and changes under colonial administration. These arguments do not cancel cooperation among farmers. They change the question from whether the state or the village controlled water to when each could intervene, and what arrangements were possible at different scales.

A hillside landscape can make authority look deceptively simple. Water moves downhill regardless of who claims to rule. A farmer near the source has an immediate advantage over someone farther below; a court may grant rights but lack the personnel to check every gate; a temple may offer rules that all parties see as legitimate. People need practices for sharing losses as well as gains. For historians, the task is to discover where formal rules appear, how power is distributed and whether changing ecology demanded new forms of agreement, rather than declaring in advance that ancient irrigation was entirely democratic or entirely coerced.

The archaeology of terraces and canals can supplement the texts, but dating built agricultural landscapes is difficult. People extend, repair and reshape them. A channel whose bank looks old may have been rebuilt many times, and a temple in use today may contain architectural phases far later than the first irrigation settlement nearby. The historical depth of cultivation is real, yet a single impressive view of terraced hills tells us little about which terrace was operating in 950. Researchers must combine site-level chronology with inscriptions and environmental study to avoid treating a living landscape as a frozen ancient photograph.

The labor of cultivation also matters. Before a ruler could receive rice and before a temple could host a festival, people prepared nurseries, transplanted shoots, cleared weeds and kept channels open. The sources name officers and rights more readily than those daily movements. Women and men worked within households and communities whose precise responsibilities differed and cannot be inferred from a technical term alone. An irrigation association's legal existence was not the same as social equality among all of its members. Looking at the field calendar alongside its grants keeps both ecological coordination and hierarchy visible.

Why did this system last in changing forms? Not because every participant always cooperated willingly, nor because a perfectly designed template was once handed down and never revised. Farmers who shared water repeatedly confronted problems that individual households could not settle alone. Authorities could reward compliance or change the terms of access. Ritual institutions could give collective decisions a durable setting. The inscriptions show some of these relationships emerging in the medieval period, and later history shows that their balance kept changing. Longevity is a history of adjustment rather than proof of institutional stasis.

A water calendar links a physical resource to shared time. A gate opening today changes another person's harvest weeks later. Bali's long irrigation history is therefore about much more than spectacular terraces. It concerns how communities across a watershed made decisions without erasing competing rights and authorities. By listening to both the dated inscriptions and the modern debates among historians and anthropologists, we gain a history of agriculture as an institution: technical, religious, political and subject to revision.

## Evidence boundary

The Old Balinese inscriptions establish irrigation terminology and specific grants in the ninth–twelfth centuries; present-day subak organizations and temple networks supply an analogy, not proof of unchanged medieval operation. Lansing’s cooperative model and Schulte Nordholt’s dynastic critique relate to different emphases and sources; neither should be treated as a complete account of every medieval watershed.
