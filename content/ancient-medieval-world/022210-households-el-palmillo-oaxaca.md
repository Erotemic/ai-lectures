# The Work behind the Walls: Families and Crafts at El Palmillo

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

Imagine beginning the day on a residential terrace above the eastern Valley of Oaxaca. A wall keeps the slope from washing down. A hearth must be tended, fiber prepared, stone tools repaired, and provisions brought up from fields or exchanged with neighbors. At El Palmillo, archaeology makes those activities visible without supplying a diary. The settlement was built along a hill, not around a famous royal inscription. Its terraces let us study the people whose work sustained the cities and towns of Classic-period Oaxaca, roughly during the first millennium of the Common Era.

El Palmillo lies in the eastern Tlacolula arm of the Oaxaca valley. It was one among several substantial hilltop terrace settlements, while Monte Albán remained an important regional center in earlier parts of the Classic sequence. Surface mapping at El Palmillo, Guirún, and Mitla Fortress shows that a valley cannot be reconstructed by surveying only its largest plaza. Houses were spread across altered slopes; terraces had to be built and maintained. Regional settlement depended on many communities, each with its own access to fields, routes, craft workers and ceremonies.

Feinman, Nicholas, and Haines excavated residential terraces at El Palmillo. The floors, retaining walls, and modifications reveal occupation that lasted through more than one building episode. A terrace might be extended, a wall repaired, or domestic space reorganized. When historians call a settlement stable because its place-name lasts for centuries, this is what stability can mean materially: many decisions to invest again in the same location. The archaeology does not imply that one family remained on exactly the same floor for the entire occupation. It shows repeated commitments to a built landscape.

Stone artifacts offer a view of specialized work. Chipped-stone production leaves cores, flakes, finished blades and discarded pieces; their distribution across houses can distinguish tool use from intensive manufacture. The El Palmillo stone-tool study compared six excavated terraces. Some craft work appeared widely at different intensities, while other activities were concentrated in fewer homes. That matters because an economy can contain specialization without confining all producers to a palace workshop. Neighbors could make different goods, trade with one another and depend upon networks reaching beyond their hill.

Another activity involved the maguey plant. Its leaves can supply strong fibers used to make cord, nets, and cloth, while parts of the plant also have food value. The excavators reported equipment connected with fiber processing, including spindle whorls and tools that could help prepare tougher leaves. The objects are not preserved shirts. A whorl tells us that fibers were twisted into thread, but it does not usually identify the hands of the spinner, the final buyer, or the full form of the finished product. In a warm climate, durable tools can outlast the work they enabled.

Measurements of whorls from eight El Palmillo residences suggest that households did not all spin identical material. Some equipment was suited to one range of fibers and some to others. The mix varied among residences. This is better evidence than assuming every domestic space produced the same goods simply because the same kind of artifact appears everywhere. But whorl size is not a label written by its maker. Researchers have to connect weight, form, wear and archaeological context to hypotheses about thread. Several materials can overlap in the same range of tools.

Households also differed in access to goods from elsewhere. The excavations found differences in nonlocal obsidian and semiprecious materials, but the overall contrast among the sampled terraces was not as sharp as a simple picture of rich and poor districts might predict. That leaves several explanations open. Some resources may have circulated through exchange, reciprocal relations, or different kinds of household connections. A rare object could have been acquired through several routes. An artifact inventory is evidence of what entered the archaeological deposit, not a bank account showing every family's lifetime wealth.

The combination of craft specialization and relatively modest contrasts in certain exotic goods suggests a web of horizontally connected households. One house could make more stone tools and another more fiber goods, with exchanges helping both. That does not mean the community had no hierarchy, obligations, or conflict. Labor might have been shaped by age, skill, status and kinship in ways that archaeology cannot assign to named individuals. The important discovery is a range of economic relations that cannot be reduced to centralized orders from a single ruler.

Domestic space was also a place of ritual and ancestry. Archaeological work at the site has documented offerings, hearths, and burials associated with living spaces. A house could therefore be where food was made, work was coordinated, and relationships to the dead were maintained. But the meaning of a particular offering cannot be recovered simply from its contents. A buried vessel does not tell us every word spoken over it. The strongest method compares placement, repeated practices, building history, and evidence from neighboring communities before proposing an interpretation.

A terraced neighborhood was never independent of the valley below. Cultivation needed land and water; fiber and stone products needed people who could use them. Routes between communities joined domestic producers to regional centers, perhaps through household exchanges, specialists, ritual occasions and political obligations. Rather than presenting the city as a separate machine that supplied a passive countryside, El Palmillo lets us see the region as a collection of producers and consumers. Archaeologists must still distinguish what is physically mapped from what is inferred about political organization or market institutions.

The study of El Palmillo has an interpretive advantage and a limitation. Its excavated households make women, children, artisans, and ordinary workers plausible subjects of history, but tools do not reliably reveal the social identity of their users. We cannot declare every spinner a woman, every stoneworker a man, or every high terrace an elite home without further evidence. Household members learned work over time; one activity could have been shared, another guarded as a specialized skill. Archaeology traces the patterned consequences of those choices more readily than the choices themselves.

When we return to Monte Albán's carved stones, the domestic terraces give the monuments a different meaning. Regional authority required more than inscriptions and ceremonial plazas. It rested in landscapes where people spent years maintaining walls, feeding families, preparing fibers, making tools and exchanging goods. At El Palmillo, the record is fragmentary but specific. It shows that economic complexity can emerge through households that perform different work while remaining connected. A history built from only kings and ceremonial centers would not see this part of Oaxaca at all.

## Evidence boundary

El Palmillo is a sampled Classic-period terraced settlement, not an average household in all Oaxaca. Excavated tools, whorls and terraces show tasks and differences among sampled homes, but do not identify every producer, labor obligation or ethnic self-identification. Published abstracts and extracts were used where full articles were unavailable.
