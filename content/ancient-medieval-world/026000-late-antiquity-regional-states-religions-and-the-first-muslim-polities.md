# Late antiquity: regional states, religions, and the first Muslim polities

Late antiquity was not simply a waiting room between Rome and the Middle Ages. Regional power, religious communities, and new forms of government were taking shape across South Asia, China, the Mediterranean, and Arabia. Their timelines overlap without becoming a single sequence.

## Beyond a Golden Age: Gupta-Era India and Regional Power

A tall stone pillar at present-day Prayagraj, formerly Allahabad, carries words praising the fourth-century ruler Samudragupta. The inscription describes military victories, relationships with other rulers, and the virtues of a powerful king. It is a remarkable source, not least because it lets us hear an argument from the ruler's own political world. But its purpose was praise. It was composed to establish authority, not to provide a neutral census of the subcontinent. What if we used it to begin a history of Gupta-era South Asia without allowing royal success to become the whole story? That would lead us from court inscriptions to village fields, regional dynasties, religious institutions, and the lives of writers and craftspeople.

Gupta rulers established power in much of northern India from the early fourth century CE. Chandragupta the First and Samudragupta are central to the dynasty's initial expansion, while Chandragupta the Second presided over a period of royal patronage and far-reaching political influence. Yet the degree of direct control varied from place to place. Some rulers offered tribute or acknowledged superior prestige without surrendering all local government. Others remained independent or contested Gupta power. Describing a “Gupta Empire” is useful, but the words should not make us imagine one official sitting in every village from the Himalayas to the southern seas.

Inscriptions and coins are particularly important for reconstructing these relationships. Gold coins display kings in carefully chosen roles, sometimes performing rituals or demonstrating martial skill. Pillar and stone inscriptions preserve genealogies, land donations, and claims of victory. The Allahabad pillar text, associated with the court poet Harishena, distinguishes conquered regions from subordinate rulers who maintained some authority. Such categories express an ideal political map. Archaeologists and historians compare these words with coin finds, other dynastic inscriptions, and evidence from places where the Gupta court had little presence. The result is a picture of overlapping political powers rather than a single homogeneous state.

South Asia's regional histories were not side stories. The Vakataka dynasties in parts of the Deccan held important territories and developed political and marriage connections with Gupta rulers. Other communities and states shaped the south and east of the subcontinent on their own timelines. Urban centers, ports, temple sites, forested regions, and agricultural settlements interacted through trade and conflict. Regional languages and religious practices had histories that cannot be fully recovered from Sanskrit court poetry. A Gupta-centered map may draw the eye northward, but a good history must keep the rest of the peninsula in view.

Consider land. Grants recorded on copper plates and stone could transfer rights to revenues, fields, or privileges to religious beneficiaries and other recipients. The language of a charter does not always show what happened to people already using the land. Some grants promised exemptions from certain obligations or named officials who were not to interfere. Behind those formulas stood cultivators, herders, and local power holders. Grants could help integrate territories into new political networks, but they could also redirect resources and strengthen inequalities. The relationship between text and enforcement remains a historical question, not an automatic consequence of the inscription's existence.

The period is often called a golden age of classical Indian culture. There are good reasons for the admiration: sophisticated Sanskrit literature, mathematics, astronomy, sculpture, and architectural developments are associated with these centuries. But golden for whom? The label usually reflects the surviving works and patronage of literate elites. We should examine the achievements rather than reject them, and also ask who had access to education and resources, who performed labor, and which regional traditions became less visible when historians centered the imperial court. Artistic brilliance does not by itself measure the living conditions of all inhabitants.

Sanskrit had long been a language of learning and religious life, and it became a prominent vehicle for elite literary and political expression. Poets associated with the era, including Kalidasa, shaped drama and verse through techniques and subjects that continued to influence later readers. Assigning exact dates to some authors is difficult, because surviving biographical traditions are often late and manuscripts may preserve works through generations of copying. Literary histories can identify styles, references, and patronage networks without pretending to know every scene in an author's life. The intellectual world included religious debates and regional vernacular traditions as well as famous Sanskrit texts.

Mathematical and astronomical work provides another example. Aryabhata composed a major treatise in 499 CE, offering methods for calculation and models of celestial motion. He belonged to an intellectual tradition that drew on earlier work and would be debated and developed by later scholars. The story of mathematics is not a simple handoff from one named genius to another. It also requires teachers, manuscripts, numerical practices, and communities prepared to examine competing methods. We should not attach every invention associated with Indian mathematics to the Gupta court or treat all discoveries from many centuries as a single event.

Religious practice was changing in diverse ways. Traditions centered on Vishnu, Shiva, and other deities received new forms of temple patronage; Buddhist and Jain communities also sustained scholarship, pilgrimage, and artistic production. Sites associated with these traditions cannot be made into a single religious map. Patronage from rulers and wealthy households could support sacred buildings, images, and monasteries. But a sculpted deity or a donated shrine tells us directly about a patron, artisan, or community connected to that object, not automatically the beliefs of everyone in the surrounding district. Debates over ritual authority and social hierarchy continued across these settings.

At Ajanta in the western Deccan, painted caves and monastic spaces preserve extraordinary evidence of Buddhist narrative art and patronage. Important phases of this work belong to the fifth century and are connected with Vakataka-era networks, not simply with the Gupta kings. The paintings represent stories, donors, architectural ideals, and practices of artistic workshops. Their survival is selective. Other paintings have vanished, and the institutions that commissioned what remains were not representative of every household. The very beauty of Ajanta helps us understand why a cultural history must attend to local patrons and artists rather than making all surviving monuments trophies of one political dynasty.

A Chinese Buddhist pilgrim often known as Faxian traveled in South Asia around the beginning of the fifth century. His account describes monastic communities, pilgrimage places, and observations of social customs. It is valuable precisely because it brings a different kind of witness alongside court-sponsored inscriptions. Yet Faxian traveled with religious interests and described places through the goals and assumptions of a Buddhist visitor. His journey did not make him an all-seeing observer of every region. Compare a travel account, a land grant, a poem, and a coin: each can illuminate the same century while answering a different question.

Social inequality is harder to reconstruct than royal success. Normative texts discuss social ranks, duties, family authority, and forms of exclusion, but prescribed conduct does not prove that everyone complied. Household arrangements varied by region, community, wealth, and occupation. Women might participate in religious patronage or property transactions while confronting restrictions that differed across settings. Craft specialists, agricultural workers, and merchants made possible the worlds praised in court literature. Their names survive less frequently. A history that celebrates learning but omits the conditions supporting it leaves out most of the people who lived through the period.

From the late fifth and sixth centuries, Gupta political power declined amid changing regional alliances and military pressures, including conflicts associated with groups called Hunas in South Asian sources. It is misleading to describe a single invasion that suddenly ended Indian civilization. Successor dynasties and regional courts continued building institutions, commissioning religious art, and sustaining learning. Some local relationships to land and power changed substantially. Historians argue about the character of those transformations using archaeological and epigraphic evidence, and not every region followed the same pattern or timetable.

The Gupta era therefore gives us more than a list of emperors or a label of greatness. It shows how courtly patronage, literary achievement, religious institutions, revenue rights, and regional politics could be connected without becoming one unified story. The Prayagraj inscription made a persuasive case for Samudragupta's authority. Our history should listen to that claim while asking who composed it, who was expected to see it, what independent evidence survives, and what it cannot tell us about the fields and households beyond the pillar's shadow. The achievements matter; so do the unequal and varied lives around them.

## Many Courts, One Canal: China from Division to the Early Tang

In China, political division and reunification unfolded on a different schedule from the regional changes in South Asia.

In northern China, a visitor to the caves at Yungang can stand before enormous figures of the Buddha cut into rock. These monuments were made with imperial patronage in a period when no single government ruled all the territories once administered by the Han dynasty. The sculptures bear traces of cultural and artistic exchanges reaching across Central Asia and into South Asia. Their existence challenges a familiar narrative: that centuries of political division were only an empty interval between two unified empires. In fact, people governed, traded, migrated, translated sacred texts, and created new institutions while rival courts claimed authority over different regions.

The Han imperial state ended in 220 CE. The following era is remembered through the Three Kingdoms and later the Jin dynasty, but those labels conceal many separate episodes of warfare, migration, and reorganization. The Jin briefly reunited much of China in the late third century before its western government was overthrown in the early fourth. Political centers moved, armed groups competed for territory, and people from northern districts relocated toward the Yangtze River and regions farther south. Migration was not simply a movement of cultivated northerners bringing history to an empty south. Southern societies already possessed communities, landholding systems, and distinctive cultural histories that newcomers encountered and changed.

In the south, courts based at Jiankang, near present-day Nanjing, developed literary, administrative, and religious institutions. Northern regimes were varied; many rulers came from groups with histories outside the Han court's conventional cultural categories. Modern descriptions sometimes contrast “Chinese” and “non-Chinese” dynasties too neatly. Elite identity, language, marriage, political service, and local belonging changed across generations. People could adopt court institutions while maintaining other affiliations. Dynastic histories composed later often organized this complex world into legitimate and illegitimate lines of succession, and those categories are themselves part of the evidence we must interpret.

One major northern power was the Northern Wei, established by leaders associated with the Tuoba and Xianbei. Its rulers controlled large parts of north China in the fifth century. They faced the practical questions familiar to other states: how to collect revenue, register people, secure soldiers, negotiate with landholding elites, and present a credible form of kingship. Reform and settlement policies changed over time. Under Emperor Xiaowen in the late fifth century, the court moved to Luoyang and adopted measures favoring Chinese language, dress, and institutions among elites. These policies were contested. They were political choices, not proof that one culture had simply displaced another.

Buddhism became a major part of political and social life. Translators and monks worked to render texts written in Indic and Central Asian languages into Chinese. Their choices mattered: philosophical terms did not always have direct equivalents, and a translation could open new debates. The scholar-monk Kumarajiva, active in Chang'an around the beginning of the fifth century, is associated with influential translations. Monasteries offered spaces for learning, worship, patronage, and sometimes economic activity. Support came from emperors and other donors, while periods of persecution remind us that royal relations with religious institutions could be unstable.

Yungang and Longmen make these developments visible. Royal and private donors paid for caves, images, and inscriptions, while many workers cut stone and supplied materials. Images incorporate forms that can be compared with artistic traditions along long-distance routes; styles changed as workshops and patrons made new choices. Yet a sculpture's resemblance to one from a distant region does not prove a single traveling artist made both. Nor does the survival of a monumental cave mean most rural families could afford such a donation. Archaeology gives us remarkable evidence of Buddhist patronage and technique, but it does not turn every inhabitant into a devotee of the court's preferred religion.

Agricultural and fiscal changes matter just as much. Northern states sought ways to organize and tax cultivated land; the Northern Wei is associated with an equal-field system in which the state attempted to allocate certain categories of land under specified obligations. The details of actual implementation varied, and legal ideals should not be confused with a universal census. In the south, migration and the growth of riverine agriculture transformed the economic geography of the Yangtze region. Households, landlords, and officials were not affected in the same ways. Dynastic reunification would later draw on these varied regional developments rather than simply recreate the Han economy.

In 581 a military leader, Yang Jian, founded the Sui dynasty, and by 589 his government had conquered the southern Chen dynasty. Reunification meant that one court could claim authority across much of China after centuries of division. But this was not a return to a frozen past. New administrative arrangements, law, taxation, land policies, and military organization depended on institutions developed during the preceding centuries. The court had to manage differences among former northern and southern subjects. Its authority depended on roads, documents, transport, and people willing or compelled to make those systems work.

The Grand Canal is a striking illustration. Sui rulers undertook major work connecting water routes and improving the movement of grain, armies, and officials between the Yangtze region and northern centers. Some waterways were older; the Sui expanded and linked them on a grand scale. Canal building required labor and imposed heavy burdens. A canal could enrich traders and strengthen the capital's food supply while harming households conscripted to dig and maintain it. The material result lasted beyond the dynasty that commissioned it. A public work can therefore be both an achievement in infrastructure and a record of coercion.

Sui expansion and military campaigns placed the government under intense pressure, including costly expeditions against the kingdom of Goguryeo on the Korean peninsula and in adjoining regions. Rebellions spread, and the dynasty collapsed in 618. The Tang dynasty followed. Its rulers inherited institutions and infrastructure, but had to secure them through warfare, negotiation, and administration. The Tang capital at Chang'an became one of Eurasia's major urban centers. Merchants, officials, scholars, religious specialists, and travelers passed through a city shaped by court planning and international connections. Cosmopolitan exchange was enabled by institutions; it did not remove hierarchy or the burdens of government.

The early Tang court combined military power with new forms of political organization. Emperor Taizong ruled from 626 to 649 and became a prominent model in later political writing, which must be read with attention to its moral purpose. Officials used law, examinations, appointments, and inherited relationships to secure careers, though the examination system had not yet become the sole route to office. Frontier politics linked Tang rulers to Turkic and other Inner Asian powers. Diplomacy, alliance, and battle were intertwined. The empire did not occupy a sealed Chinese core surrounded by people with no independent institutions.

The Korean peninsula, Japan, and other neighboring societies were also part of this changing regional history, without being mere recipients of Tang policy. Korean kingdoms competed with one another and negotiated with Chinese courts. In the seventh century, conflicts involving Goguryeo, Baekje, Silla, and Tang forces reshaped political arrangements. Japanese rulers and religious institutions studied continental models while making choices in their own settings. Shared Buddhist texts and official practices circulated through travelers and scholars, but the shape of adoption depended on local power and interpretation. Connections should never be mistaken for political or cultural sameness.

We know this history from court annals, law codes, inscriptions, Buddhist writings, excavated tombs, and artworks. Official dynastic histories explain succession and elite politics; a merchant's receipt or monastic inscription can expose other concerns. Later authors sometimes praised reunification so strongly that they described the earlier centuries chiefly as disorder. The evidence suggests instead a period of creative and violent institutional change, followed by new experiments in coordinated rule. Neither division nor unity explains everything about a community's prosperity, security, or learning.

The Yungang images, the canal, and the avenues of Chang'an belong to the same broad story. One shows how a court in a divided world invested in a universal religious language. Another shows how rulers mobilized labor to join regions physically. The last shows the possibilities and inequalities of a new imperial capital. China's history between the Han and Tang is not a blank space separating great dynasties. It is a long process in which people and institutions made the political reunification of the seventh century possible—and gave that reunification consequences that reached far beyond any emperor's lifetime.

## Many Churches, Changing Empires: Christianity in Late Antiquity

Returning to the Mediterranean brings us to Christian communities whose institutions changed alongside imperial power.

In Ravenna, two baptisteries from late antiquity preserve mosaics of the baptism of Jesus. A visitor can admire both without necessarily noticing that their commissioners belonged to Christian communities with sharply different understandings of the relationship between Jesus and God. These buildings were made in a world where rulers, bishops, monks, and ordinary worshippers debated matters they considered central to salvation and communal life. There was no single afternoon when “the church” was invented, defined for everyone, and left unchanged. Christianity developed through texts, ritual, disputes, political patronage, local communities, and institutions stretching far beyond the Roman imperial frontier.

Christianity began among Jewish communities in the eastern Mediterranean in the first century CE, in response to the life and teachings of Jesus of Nazareth and his followers' convictions about his death and resurrection. Those convictions are religious beliefs held by Christians; historians study how they were expressed and transmitted rather than independently confirming supernatural events. The letters associated with Paul and the writings that later formed the New Testament preserve some of the earliest surviving Christian testimony. They also show disagreements about communal rules, identity, worship, and authority. Christian groups existed long before an emperor decided to support them.

During the second and third centuries, communities spread across cities and regions of the Roman Empire and beyond it. They organized gatherings, support for members, rituals, and teaching. Bishops and other leaders gradually acquired authority that could be challenged by rivals. Surviving texts often present one interpretation as the rightful faith and others as errors. Such works are evidence of the debates, not a substitute for independent description of all participating groups. The existence of competing gospel traditions, interpretations, and local practices makes the early Christian world historically rich and difficult to summarize with a single set of beliefs.

Roman policies toward Christians varied by place and time. Some periods brought local hostility or episodic persecution; other years allowed communities to grow openly. The persecutions associated with Diocletian in the early fourth century were serious, but they did not affect every province identically. Constantine's rise brought major changes. In 313, the arrangements traditionally associated with Constantine and Licinius provided for religious toleration and restitution of confiscated Christian property. Constantine patronized church institutions and participated in disputes. Yet the legalization and privileging of Christianity should not be confused with the immediate disappearance of other religious traditions or with full agreement among Christians.

The Council of Nicaea in 325 gathered bishops to address disputes including the relationship between the Father and the Son. It produced a creed and decisions that became important within many later Christian traditions. But a council is an event in a continuing argument. Theological language changed, and rival interpretations persisted despite imperial support for one position. Bishops could be exiled or recalled as rulers and councils changed policies. To know what an imperial council declared is not to know what every person in a Syrian village, Egyptian monastery, or western Roman town believed the following morning.

By the end of the fourth century, imperial law increasingly endorsed forms of Christianity regarded as orthodox by the court. Theodosius the First's legislation was significant, but laws and social practice did not always align. Local churches gained property and influence, and clerical offices became important parts of civic life. Bishops might negotiate with armies, distribute charity, settle disputes, and sponsor buildings. They could also participate in coercion against rival religious communities. Support for the poor could coexist with the accumulation of institutional wealth. The church's growth therefore belongs to social and political history as well as to the history of ideas.

Monastic movements took distinctive forms in Egypt, Syria, Palestine, and elsewhere. Some individuals pursued solitary lives of discipline and prayer. Others joined organized communities with rules about work, worship, property, and leadership. Pachomian communities in Egypt and the wider tradition associated with Basil in the Greek-speaking east contributed to different forms of collective religious life. Monasteries copied texts and sometimes held land; they also depended on labor, gifts, and local relationships. Monasticism was neither completely outside society nor simply another arm of imperial administration. Its practitioners could criticize the priorities of wealthy patrons while relying on material support.

Debates became particularly consequential in the fifth century. The Council of Ephesus in 431 and the Council of Chalcedon in 451 addressed disputes about how to describe Jesus's divine and human reality, as well as questions of episcopal authority. Chalcedon's formulation of Christ as one person in two natures gained official support in the eastern Roman Empire. Other churches developed different theological languages and rejected the council's authority. It is misleading to describe their beliefs only by hostile labels assigned by opponents. For example, many churches now called Oriental Orthodox describe their teaching as miaphysite, affirming a united divine-human nature rather than simply denying Christ's humanity.

The differences were not confined to Constantinople. Egyptian Christian traditions developed in Coptic alongside Greek. Syriac-speaking communities in western Asia produced theology, poetry, biblical translation, and histories that cannot be reduced to the Byzantine court's agenda. Churches of the East developed important institutions in Sasanian territory, with their own administrative and theological history. Armenia and the kingdom of Aksum also formed Christian communities connected to but not ruled in daily religious matters by every Roman emperor. The movement of bishops, merchants, pilgrims, books, and artisans connected these worlds without making them institutionally identical.

Architecture and images provide another kind of evidence. The mosaics of Ravenna, the churches of Constantinople, and the church buildings of Aksum or Egypt testify to patronage and ritual space. Their decoration may celebrate rulers, saints, and theological claims. A building can tell us something about the resources available to a community and the kinds of objects it deemed appropriate for worship. It cannot tell us by itself whether every worshipper understood the same doctrinal arguments. Excavation may reveal changes in a site's use that written accounts ignore, but archaeology also depends on what has survived and been investigated.

Texts were copied, taught, translated, and disputed. Greek and Latin Christian literature circulated alongside Syriac, Coptic, Armenian, Ge'ez, and other traditions. Some texts survive because powerful churches preserved them; other voices are represented mainly through quotations by their opponents. This archive is not neutral. A historian who reads only later declarations of orthodoxy will miss disagreements and local experiences. Manuscripts must be dated, attributed, and compared. The exact wording of an ancient text may depend on a chain of scribes and translators rather than the author's original manuscript lying before us.

Religion also shaped relations with other communities. Christians and Jews lived alongside one another across the Mediterranean and western Asia, sometimes sharing spaces or intellectual traditions, sometimes experiencing conflict and discriminatory policy. Zoroastrian, Manichaean, and other religious traditions had their own institutions. In some cities, rulers' attempts to impose a religious boundary brought violence; in others, practical coexistence endured. The simple sentence “the empire became Christian” cannot describe all these relations. It describes a change in public authority and patronage more readily than a single universal change of personal belief.

In the seventh century, war and the growth of Muslim-ruled states changed the political environment of Christian communities in Syria, Palestine, Egypt, and Mesopotamia. Some churches found themselves outside direct Byzantine rule while continuing their liturgy, learning, and local administration. Greek, Syriac, Coptic, and increasingly Arabic became languages through which these communities negotiated new circumstances. Later histories sometimes portray this transformation as the immediate end of a Christian Near East. Evidence instead shows Christian communities persisting, adapting, and debating across many centuries. The timing of change differed significantly from place to place.

Christianity's late antique history is therefore a story of institutions as much as ideas: who could appoint a bishop, build a church, resolve a dispute, protect a manuscript, collect revenue, or define the boundaries of a community. It is also a story in which sincere beliefs matter to people, even when historians do not claim to adjudicate their truth. The two baptisteries at Ravenna remain witnesses to different Christian worlds occupying the same city. Their coexistence reminds us that a religion can have shared texts and sacred figures while developing through complex, sometimes bitter arguments about what those things mean.

## Arabia and the First Muslim Community: History, Memory, and the Qur'an

In Arabia, the sources for the first Muslim community pose their own questions about text, memory, and historical reconstruction.

Two early leaves of the Qur'an preserved at the University of Birmingham contain passages in an old form of Arabic script. Scientific analysis of the parchment places the animal's death within a range extending from the late sixth to the mid-seventh century. That is remarkably close to the period in which Islam arose. Yet the test dates the skin, not the moment someone wrote on it. The distinction captures a central challenge in studying the beginnings of Islam. We possess early textual witnesses and material objects, together with later accounts that preserve vital traditions. The task is to respect the importance of these sources while asking exactly what each can establish.

Arabia around 600 CE was not an isolated empty space between empires. Its communities included pastoral groups, farmers in oasis settlements, merchants, artisans, political elites, and people bound by different forms of dependence. Their religious lives were varied. Some worshipped several deities, while Jewish and Christian communities and monotheistic practices were also present in parts of the peninsula. South Arabian states had histories of inscriptions and political rivalry. Connections across the Red Sea, toward Iraq, and toward the Levant shaped commercial and diplomatic relationships. What any single town's economy depended on remains a matter for evidence rather than a tidy story of caravans transporting everything across a desert.

According to the Muslim biographical tradition, Muhammad was born in Mecca around 570 and belonged to the Quraysh. Narratives describe his marriage to Khadija, a businesswoman, and his emerging role as a religious messenger. These accounts matter enormously to Muslim historical memory. They also largely come to us through works compiled after Muhammad's lifetime, often using earlier reports. The Qur'an is a different sort of source: it contains proclamations, warnings, arguments, and religious instruction in language associated with the earliest community. Its arrangement does not provide a straightforward day-by-day biography. Historians read the sources together while recognizing that they answer different questions.

In Islamic belief, Muhammad received revelation from God through the angel Gabriel, beginning around 610. The Qur'an calls its hearers to worship one God, attend to judgment and accountability, and consider the treatment of the vulnerable. Some passages argue with people who reject its message; others address issues of communal life, law, and relationships with earlier religious traditions. A historian can discuss the emergence, transmission, and historical setting of these proclamations without treating the divine origin of revelation as something archaeology can independently verify or disprove. Respect for religious conviction is compatible with careful methods of historical inquiry.

The message was contested in Mecca, according to traditional accounts, and some early followers faced opposition. The precise social and political circumstances of each episode are not equally well documented. It is better to say so than to turn later narrative detail into a complete contemporary transcript. Yet the existence of a growing community, its conflict with other groups, and its eventual move to a different town are central to the historical trajectory supported by early Islamic tradition and later political developments. Religious allegiance was beginning to become a basis for collective life as well as personal conviction.

In 622, Muhammad and his followers migrated to Yathrib, later called Medina. This migration, the hijra, became the starting point of the Islamic calendar. Medina was an oasis with existing communities, including Jewish groups and competing local interests. The early Muslim community had to negotiate relationships, security, resources, and authority. A document traditionally called the Constitution of Medina is often discussed as evidence of these arrangements, but its composition, transmission, and precise dating require scholarly caution. The idea that a city simply accepted one leader and instantly acquired a uniform social order understates the political work involved.

In Medina, the community's religious and political responsibilities expanded. Muslim tradition describes agreements, disputes, expeditions, armed conflicts, and changing relationships with groups in the surrounding region. Battles associated with Badr, Uhud, and the trench around Medina became part of the remembered formation of the community. They should be situated in a society where allegiance, protection, and survival could be matters of urgent practical concern. For those affected by fighting or displacement, the creation of a new political order was not merely a theological debate. Yet precise numbers, speeches, and motives reported in later narrative accounts are not all independently verifiable.

Mecca came under Muhammad's authority around 630. In Muslim memory, the Kaaba, a sanctuary of earlier Arabian religious importance, was rededicated to the worship of one God. The sanctuary remained at the center of Muslim pilgrimage. The shift was religious and political: old relationships of power were transformed as new obligations and allegiances spread. The details of how local communities across Arabia joined or resisted the new order differed. To understand the peninsula in these years, we must avoid projecting the later territory and institutions of the caliphates back onto Muhammad's lifetime.

Muhammad died in 632. His death created questions that mattered enormously to the community: who could lead, how authority should be exercised, and how his religious message should guide action. Later Muslim traditions remembered the earliest leaders and disputes in different ways, contributing to distinct accounts of legitimate succession. Historians can document the resulting political histories without treating the most consequential disagreements as simply a contest between one correct tradition and one mistaken one. The variety of later remembrance is itself part of Islam's history.

The Qur'an's textual history requires separate attention. Its passages circulated in recitation and writing among early Muslims. Islamic tradition attributes the establishment of a standard written text to the caliph Uthman in the middle of the seventh century. Modern scholarship debates the evidence for textual standardization, but early manuscript witnesses make it difficult to imagine that the entire corpus was invented centuries afterward. Scholarly conclusions do differ in detail. We must distinguish the wording and date of a manuscript, the date of the parchment, the history of the written consonantal text, and the later development of reading traditions.

The Birmingham leaves are especially instructive because their physical evidence is concrete and its limits are explicit. Radiocarbon measurements gave a likely range for the parchment's manufacture from animal skin. The writing contains part of three surahs. The script and textual comparison provide other evidence for its age and place within manuscript history. Neither the radiocarbon result nor the pages establish who first heard those passages or precisely when they were collected. A useful historical conclusion is not weakened by stating its limits. On the contrary, being clear about what the test dates makes the result more meaningful.

Material evidence also includes early Arabic inscriptions, coins, and documents from the regions conquered by Muslim armies. Those objects become more numerous in the decades after Muhammad's death. Some non-Muslim writers who witnessed early expansion recorded encounters with Arab forces and their religious ideas. These accounts are valuable but partial. Outsiders misunderstood events, and later writers reworked memories. Neither an early Arabic text nor a Christian chronicler offers an omniscient narrative. Careful scholarship weighs them together rather than treating any one document as a magic key to the entire seventh century.

This context also changes how we understand the place of women, households, and people outside elite circles. Khadija's remembered importance points to the presence of women's economic and social agency in Islamic tradition. Quranic passages discuss family, inheritance, charity, and the obligations of believers, suggesting concerns that extended beyond warfare and kingship. But written prescriptions cannot tell us exactly how every family arranged property or resolved conflict. Early Muslim society involved real people living within existing Arabian systems of kinship, labor, wealth, and status while trying to define new collective duties.

The beginnings of Islam belong to the history of the late antique world, yet they should not be reduced to a by-product of Roman or Sasanian weakness. Arabia had its own political and religious trajectories. Muhammad and the community around him developed a message and institutions that changed the peninsula and then, through later conquests and conversions, much wider regions. We can study these changes historically while recognizing that Muslim belief gives them meanings beyond what historical method can establish. The earliest written leaves make the beginning tangible. They do not replace the human communities, conflicts, recitations, and decisions through which a new religious and political history unfolded.

## After the Conquests: Caliphs, Taxpayers, and the Making of an Empire

After the conquests, the scale changes from an emerging community to the practical government of a large and diverse population.

Imagine a village official in seventh-century Egypt asked to supply grain, money, or labor. The language of his records might be Greek or Coptic. The government collecting the revenue had changed, but the fields, irrigation channels, and much of the local administrative knowledge remained. Documents from early Muslim-ruled Egypt reveal how a new imperial order grew through negotiations with institutions that already existed. The history of the caliphates cannot be told only by tracing armies across maps. It must also explain how households were registered, how rulers claimed authority, how different religious communities lived under the new state, and why the process of religious conversion lasted longer than the first conquests.

Muhammad's death in 632 created a question of leadership within the Muslim community. Abu Bakr became its first caliph according to the sequence accepted in Sunni historiography. His short rule included campaigns against Arabian groups that opposed, renegotiated, or rejected the authority of the Medinan leadership. These conflicts are often called the Ridda wars. Later Muslim historians interpreted them through debates about faith and allegiance; the political circumstances of different groups were not identical. For people in Arabia, the consolidation of rule involved military pressure and competing visions of community, not merely an orderly administrative succession.

Under Umar, whose caliphate is usually dated 634 to 644, Muslim-led armies expanded into territories governed by the eastern Romans and the Sasanians. The victory at the Yarmouk in 636 helped break Byzantine control of much of Syria. Campaigns in Mesopotamia and Iran defeated Sasanian forces over several years. Egypt came under Muslim rule through warfare and agreements in the early 640s. Events that appear in a few lines of a chronology were experienced as sieges, forced movements, negotiations, payments, and changes of garrison. Dates and details in later battle accounts sometimes disagree; the broader sequence of conquest is better supported than every reported conversation between commanders.

The Sasanian dynasty collapsed, while the eastern Roman empire retained Constantinople and other territories. Local responses to conquest varied. Some communities fought, some negotiated terms, and some attempted to preserve familiar institutions under new rulers. It is difficult to recover every group's motives. The fact that communities opposed an existing imperial church or tax regime does not prove that they uniformly welcomed a conquering army. Nor does the imposition of a new ruler mean that inhabitants immediately changed their language or religion. Political control and cultural transformation moved on different clocks.

The new governments relied on settlements and garrison centers. Basra and Kufa in Iraq and Fustat in Egypt developed as important administrative and military places. They brought soldiers, families, merchants, and officials into networks through which income and orders could be distributed. Fiscal records and later historical writing refer to registers for payments and military support, though their precise forms evolved. The rulers had to keep armies supplied without destroying the productive landscapes on which revenue depended. A conquering force could win a campaign quickly; creating dependable administration required repeated decisions by officials and local communities.

Egypt's documentary record is especially valuable. Greek, Coptic, and Arabic papyri survive from different phases of the seventh and eighth centuries. They reveal correspondence about collections, transport, work obligations, and the changing languages of authority. The evidence does not show an instant replacement of Coptic and Greek by Arabic. Officials and scribes continued using inherited forms while adapting to new demands. A papyrus from Egypt is powerful evidence for a particular place and transaction; it cannot automatically stand in for all of Syria, Iraq, or Iran. Comparison across regions is essential before describing a common imperial policy.

Taxes were central to this negotiation. Land revenue, levies on people, and obligations in goods or labor were collected under arrangements that changed with region and period. The later vocabulary of jizya and kharaj is historically important, but specific meanings and administrative practice developed over time. It would be misleading to project a fully formed medieval legal classification back onto every treaty of the 630s. For cultivators, the pressing issue might be the amount demanded and the officials collecting it. For community leaders, guarantees of worship, property, or security could be negotiated but were not the same everywhere.

Most inhabitants of the newly conquered territories did not immediately become Muslims. Christians remained numerous in Egypt and the Levant; Jewish, Zoroastrian, and other communities continued in different regions. Religious conversion unfolded over generations and centuries, connected to social relationships, institutions, family choices, political incentives, and belief. Some people were pressured, and legal distinctions could disadvantage non-Muslims. But a history that imagines an immediate mass conversion following each victory is contradicted by documents, architecture, and the continued life of non-Muslim institutions. We need to distinguish the religion of rulers, the legal position of groups, and the faith of particular individuals.

The caliphate's leadership was itself contested. Uthman, the third caliph in the Sunni sequence, ruled from 644 to 656 and was killed amid opposition. Ali, related to Muhammad by kinship and marriage, became caliph and faced civil conflict. The struggles conventionally called the First Fitna ended in a political order dominated by Muawiya, who established the Umayyad dynasty in 661, with Damascus as its principal capital. Sunni and Shia historical and theological traditions remember this succession differently. A responsible historical account names the disputes and their consequences without pretending that centuries of religious meaning can be settled by a simple label in a modern timeline.

In 680, Husayn, a grandson of Muhammad, and members of his group were killed at Karbala by forces acting for the Umayyad government. The event became central to Shia remembrance and to wider Muslim reflection on legitimate authority, sacrifice, and injustice. Historians distinguish the event's political setting from later devotional and narrative traditions that developed around it. The persistence of Karbala in religious life reminds us that institutions are shaped not only by tax offices and armies but also by collective memory and moral argument. The political history of the first caliphs is inseparable from the communities that continued to debate what leadership should mean.

Under the Umayyads, especially during the reign of Abd al-Malik from 685 to 705, the state increasingly articulated an imperial identity through public monuments, coins, and administrative reforms. The Dome of the Rock in Jerusalem, completed around 691 or 692, made a conspicuous architectural and religious statement in a city sacred to several communities. Coins and inscriptions can show changing formulas of political and religious authority. Arabic gained ground as an administrative language, although the speed of change differed by place. Artisans drew on Byzantine and Sasanian techniques to create buildings and objects commissioned under new rulers. Adaptation did not imply cultural emptiness before Islam.

The empire's geographic reach extended across North Africa and later into Iberia, while campaigns and political relationships expanded eastward into Central and South Asia. These developments did not produce equal or uncontested authority everywhere. African and Asian communities had established political traditions, and local actors made choices within changing military circumstances. Rebellion, competition among elite groups, and disputes over revenue repeatedly challenged the Umayyad court. The empire was powerful, but its territory was not a blank surface over which one administrative design could be stamped without negotiation.

The year 750 marks the overthrow of the Umayyad ruling dynasty in most of the caliphate and the rise of the Abbasids. It is a useful boundary for this introductory account, but not the end of early Islamic transformation. The Abbasids inherited populations that spoke many languages and practiced several religions. They also inherited contested theories of authority and a repertoire of Persian, Roman, Arabian, and local administrative practices. Later Baghdad's scholarship, urban life, and institutions emerged from these connections rather than appearing suddenly after a dynastic revolution. Some Umayyad power also survived in Iberia, demonstrating that a date of regime change in one center cannot describe every province.

To study the caliphates, then, we must listen to more than later chroniclers of rulers and battles. Papyri show material obligations; coins display official assertions; buildings reveal patronage; Christian, Jewish, and Muslim texts preserve different memories; archaeology shows settlement and craft traditions. All have limits. A tax demand may explain what an official wanted without telling us whether the harvest succeeded. A treaty preserved in a later history may encode a later legal ideal. Historical understanding grows from comparing such evidence, not assuming that one source's vocabulary describes every century.

The first century and a half after Muhammad's death brought enormous political expansion and institutional experimentation. It also brought violence, adaptation, social differentiation, and new opportunities. A household in Egypt might continue cultivating the same fields while learning to deal with different rulers. A family in Syria might preserve a Christian liturgy under a Muslim government. A soldier's descendants might grow up in a garrison town that became a center of commerce and learning. None of these stories alone defines the caliphate. Taken together, they explain how conquest gradually became government, and how government reshaped a world that remained religiously and culturally diverse.
