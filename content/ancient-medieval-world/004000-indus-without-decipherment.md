# The Cities That Do Not Name Their Kings: Life in the Indus World

At Dholavira, in what is now Gujarat, a city's designers confronted a problem that was both simple and demanding: water was scarce, and rainfall was seasonal. They worked with channels, walls, and reservoirs to capture and manage it. In the Indus city of Mohenjo-daro, in present-day Pakistan, excavators found carefully planned streets, brick architecture, wells, and drainage. These places preserve decisions about how people lived together. Yet if we ask for the name of the official who authorized a reservoir, or the title of the person who settled a dispute between neighbors, we run into a silence that is fundamental to their history.

The urban Indus or Harappan civilization developed out of earlier communities in the region of the Indus and adjacent river systems. Its mature urban phase is generally placed around 2600 to 1900 before the common era, though sites have different local sequences and some settlements continued much longer in altered forms. Its geographical reach extended across large parts of present-day Pakistan and northwestern India. The term Harappan comes from a major archaeological site, not from a name we can confidently identify as the civilization's own. A modern archaeological label should not be mistaken for a language, ethnic identity, or political title shared by everyone who lived there.

The scale of urban coordination is visible in bricks and streets. Standardized proportions appeared across many settlements. Skilled workers made beads, seals, pottery, shell objects, metal goods, and textiles. Public and domestic spaces were planned in different ways at different sites, with water management especially prominent. Trade connected settlements within the Indus region and reached beyond it. Archaeologists have identified materials and objects indicating contact with the Gulf and Mesopotamia. But similarities in weights, seals, or brick dimensions do not by themselves reveal a single emperor issuing orders. Standards may have spread through shared practices, professional networks, governing bodies, or combinations we cannot yet separate.

Mohenjo-daro's elevated areas and lower town show urban planning, while the building commonly called the Great Bath suggests a setting in which water and communal or ritual activity may have been important. Calling it a bath is an archaeological description, not direct testimony about the ceremonies conducted there. The absence of a clearly identified royal palace or an Egyptian-style pyramid in a surviving excavation does not prove that hierarchy was absent. Dholavira contains differentiated spaces and fortifications; archaeological interpretations include signs of social ranking. The question is what kinds of authority existed and how they operated, not whether every society must leave behind the same form of royal monument.

Small inscribed seals are among the Indus world's most recognizable artifacts. Many carry animal images and short sequences of signs. Scholars agree that the signs form a structured system, but there is no accepted decipherment establishing the underlying language or the signs' complete communicative functions. Some researchers interpret the sequence as a script; others have proposed alternatives, and arguments continue over how to test competing hypotheses. Claims that a particular seal definitively names a king, a god, or a specific modern community should be treated skeptically unless supported by demonstrable decipherment. An attractive story is not an epigraphic result.

This is why the Indus world poses such a valuable challenge to the way history is taught. When an Egyptian ruler left an inscription, scholars could eventually read the name and compare its claim with archaeology. In the Indus case, we often know the arrangement of buildings much better than the names of their inhabitants. Written evidence, even if it survives physically, has not become readable testimony. The streets and artifacts still speak through their distribution, manufacture, and wear. They answer questions about craft, exchange, and planned construction while leaving other questions open.

Water systems show the strength of that material record. At Dholavira, reservoirs and channels were built in a landscape dependent on seasonal streams. At Mohenjo-daro, wells and drains indicate repeated attention to water and waste within densely built neighborhoods. Maintenance required labor across time, not only a founding decision. Who cleaned a drain? Who repaired a wall? Who could insist that a neighbor keep a passage clear? These questions are necessary to explain how the city functioned, even when we cannot assign answers to a named institution. A system's technical design is visible; its political enforcement is harder to reconstruct.

The city depended on people beyond its streets. Farmers grew crops that varied with regional conditions; herders raised animals; coastal and inland communities supplied raw materials and food. Craftspeople acquired agate and other stones, worked metal, and produced goods that traveled. Some of the material record reveals specialization, but the categories by which people identified their own occupations are largely lost to us. A bead workshop proves skilled production, not whether the worker was independent, attached to a household, or obligated to a political authority. We should recognize the missing evidence rather than fill it with the social structure of a different civilization.

The Harappan world's connections with Mesopotamia give us a second, indirect kind of testimony. Mesopotamian records refer to places including Meluhha, commonly associated with the Indus trading sphere, and archaeology finds objects and materials suggestive of movement across the Arabian Sea and the Gulf. The correspondence is important, but a Mesopotamian outsider's name for a trading partner is not the same as an indigenous account of that society. Nor can a foreign reference explain exactly which communities participated or who profited. Exchange networks produce evidence of contact more readily than evidence of equal bargaining power.

What happened when the large Indus cities changed? Older stories sometimes describe a sudden violent end or the destruction of a civilization. Archaeology instead reveals varied changes across a wide region: reduced prominence of some large urban centers, shifts toward smaller settlements in some areas, altered material traditions, and continuing regional life. The phrase de-urbanization is often more useful than disappearance. People moved, adapted, and reorganized; they were not necessarily eliminated. Chronology also matters. Transformations occurred over generations rather than on one historically recoverable day.

Environment was part of this process, but not a magic explanation. The summer monsoon and winter rainfall affect agriculture and river behavior in different parts of South Asia. Studies of cave deposits reconstruct changing precipitation patterns around the period of urban transformation. Other work on ancient channels has challenged accounts that posit a single great Himalayan-fed river vanishing during the cities' heyday. A climate proxy measures conditions at its own site with a model and uncertainties; it does not directly record the motives for a family's relocation hundreds of kilometers away. Historians have to link environmental observations to archaeological change rather than assume that matching dates establish a single cause.

There are also difficulties in the history of discovery itself. Excavation began under colonial conditions, and subsequent scholarship has developed across national borders with different institutions, priorities, and political pressures. Archaeological sites now lie in multiple countries. Modern arguments about national origins can turn undeciphered signs or disputed river identifications into declarations of present-day identity. But a third-millennium settlement is not a straightforward answer to a twenty-first-century political question. It deserves explanation on the basis of its own material history, not enlistment in a simplified modern ancestry claim.

We can therefore say a great deal without claiming to know everything. People in the Indus sphere designed complex settlements, managed water, exchanged goods, developed specialized crafts, and shared conventions across a large area. They had families, conflicts, skills, and institutions even if we cannot reconstruct them in the terms of a readable legal archive. They adapted to regional environmental changes and did not cease to have a history when some cities contracted. The lack of decipherment limits our knowledge of names and narratives; it does not make the people unknowable or their achievements less real.

A good history of the Indus world ends neither with a list of mysteries nor with a confident invented king. It ends with a discipline of listening to what the evidence can actually say. The reservoirs are still there. The bricks still bear the marks of construction. A seal is still inscribed. We know enough to describe a durable and complex urban society. We do not yet know enough to give that society an authoritative voice of its own. Keeping those two truths together is not a weakness in the history. It is how the history remains open to discovery.

## Companion: undeciphered does not mean absent

Claims about planning and craft are anchored in excavated sites and conservation records. Claims about language, office-holders, ritual use, and the causes of decentralization remain limited by undeciphered inscriptions and by uneven site coverage. Climate records are environmental proxies rather than direct evidence of particular historical decisions; competing river reconstructions are noted explicitly.
