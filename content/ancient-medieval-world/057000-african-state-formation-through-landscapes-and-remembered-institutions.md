# African state formation through landscapes and remembered institutions

States take shape through landscapes, work, alliance, conflict, and remembered institutions. The Lake Chad region, Mapungubwe, and Kongo offer different cases across a large part of Africa. Their placement together is a comparison, not a claim that one kingdom produced the next.

## Around the Moving Lake: Kanem, Bornu, and the Peoples of Central Africa

Lake Chad has never been a fixed blue patch on the map. Its shorelines and wetlands have changed with rainfall, rivers, and the seasons. Across the surrounding plains, people cultivated crops, raised animals, caught fish, traded, and formed communities with different languages and political ties. Medieval states emerged in this setting, most famously Kanem and the later Bornu-centered polity. Their histories are often reduced to a list of rulers and a caravan route to North Africa. But a lake basin does not become an important region simply because outsiders cross it. To understand Kanem and Bornu, we need to begin with the people whose livelihoods made power possible.

The basin lies at a meeting place of ecological zones. Saharan and Sahelian lands stretch northward; farming and grazing regions lie toward the south and west; the lake and its connected rivers offer wetlands and fisheries. These environments supported different livelihoods, sometimes pursued by the same community at different times. Fish could be dried, crops stored, and salt obtained from places where it could be exchanged. Pastoral mobility tied grazing areas together. Political authorities might seek to tax or protect such activities, but their control could not erase the seasonal needs of households. Water levels and routes shaped opportunity while leaving room for human decision.

Reconstructing the earliest centuries of Kanem is difficult. Scholars have used king lists, oral historical traditions, and reports by Arabic-writing geographers. The sources do not agree neatly on dates or the circumstances of state formation. A research project focused on the Lake Chad region emphasizes how much of the story was written by outsiders and how limited systematic archaeological investigation remains for some early centers. Claims that a single dynasty began in one precisely documented year should therefore be treated cautiously. What we can say is that political institutions existed around the lake well before the early modern period, and their organization changed considerably over time.

From about the ninth century onward, writers working in North African and broader Islamic scholarly networks recorded information about Kanem's connections beyond the Sahara. Authors such as al-Yaqubi, al-Muhallabi, and al-Bakri are important because they give some of our earliest written descriptions of the region. But they did not conduct a modern survey of every lake-shore village. Their interests included rulers, routes, commerce, and religious affiliations. The existence of their reports confirms that knowledge and goods circulated across large distances; it does not make their descriptions comprehensive or free from the assumptions of their informants.

One major route crossed the central Sahara through the Fezzan toward Lake Chad. Its importance shifted with the fortunes of oasis towns, rulers, merchants, and caravan communities. Travelers needed water, protection, pack animals, knowledge of distances, and agreements with people along the route. A long-distance commercial network was the product of many local arrangements. Markets in North Africa demanded goods from the south, while communities around Lake Chad had their own needs and connections. It would be misleading to portray Kanem as a remote appendage of the Mediterranean, waiting to become important when a caravan arrived.

That commerce included a violent dimension. Medieval sources describe the capture and export of enslaved people from regions connected to Kanem. Military pressure and subjugation could turn distant human lives into sources of wealth and political leverage. The people so taken rarely appear as individual narrators in surviving elite accounts. We should not neutralize this history by calling them simply trade goods. At the same time, evidence for slavery in these routes does not tell us that all economic activity in the basin was organized around slave raiding. Farming, fishing, herding, local markets, and craft production continued to sustain a much larger range of lives.

Kanem's rulers and political elites developed connections with Islamic scholarship and institutions. Muslim identities and practices became important among ruling groups, but religious change did not occur uniformly or all at once among the communities around the lake. Later traditions and chronicles sometimes present royal conversions as decisive moments. For a social history, the harder questions concern how worship, law, marriage, teaching, and local authority changed over generations. No ruler's declaration could by itself make every household adopt an identical set of practices. The variation in evidence is a reason to separate the history of a court from that of its subjects.

In the thirteenth century, Kanem expanded its influence northward along important Saharan routes, including toward the Fezzan. Historians debate the precise scope and durability of control, and the history of oasis communities has its own agency. Political influence over a route can mean military reach, alliances, tribute, or temporary occupation rather than continuous administration of every well and market. Such distinctions matter when we draw boundaries on maps. A corridor of movement does not function like a modern national border, and local brokers can be essential even under an ambitious ruler.

The story also moved west of Lake Chad. The political center associated with Kanem faced conflict, internal change, and shifts in its regional relationships; over time the Bornu region became increasingly important. Histories of the Sayfawa or Sefuwa ruling line are preserved through later traditions and chronicles, which need to be read critically. It is tempting to describe a tidy transfer of an unchanging kingdom from one side of the lake to the other. In reality, different communities and institutions entered changing relationships across centuries. The histories of Kanem and Bornu are connected, but they should not be flattened into a single stationary capital with a new address.

Who lived outside the royal narratives? Farmers whose harvests depended on seasonal conditions; fishers working the changing shallows; herders negotiating pasture; smiths and other craft workers; merchants and transport specialists; religious teachers; and communities affected by warfare or captivity. The documents seldom describe an ordinary family in enough detail for a biography. Archaeological evidence, local oral traditions, and environmental history can help restore the social setting. Yet we must resist filling every gap with a fictional representative family. An honest reconstruction explains what work was required and what evidence makes that inference reasonable.

Relations with other African regions deserve particular attention. Scholars have long known more about Kanem's northern trade than about its links toward the Nile, Darfur, or regions west and south of the lake. Archaeological research now asks whether material evidence can demonstrate some of these probable connections and revise older routes reconstructed mainly from Arabic geography. A plausible itinerary on a map is a research question, not proof that a caravan used that line continuously. This distinction changes the way we tell African history: North Africa was one significant direction of connection, not the only direction in which goods, people, or ideas could travel.

The lake itself reminds us that environmental history cannot be a simple explanation for every political event. Water levels changed over centuries, and pastures, fields, and fisheries were sensitive to such changes. But rulers also made decisions about warfare, alliance, settlement, and taxation. Households responded to pressures in different ways. If we attribute every change in political authority to a shrinking shoreline, we erase the choices and conflicts of the people involved. Conversely, a history of kings that ignores water, soils, and food production would not explain how their states operated.

Method matters especially here. Medieval Arabic texts preserve valuable evidence, but they are filtered through writers, translations, and sometimes later compilations. King lists provide political memory but can rearrange or compress chronology. Archaeological surveys may identify occupation or craft activities without naming a particular king. Oral histories can preserve relationships and meanings absent from written chronicles, though their chronologies require careful work. A strong account brings these forms of evidence into conversation instead of allowing one court-centered narrative to dictate every conclusion. The work of recovering early Kanem–Bornu is still developing.

The famous medieval empires of Mali and Songhai occupied other parts of the African continent, but their prominence in global surveys should not make the Lake Chad basin a footnote. Kanem–Bornu connected pastoral, agricultural, lake, and desert economies and helped shape political relations across Central Africa. Its ties to North Africa were important, and its connections within Africa deserve equal historical scrutiny. The people of the basin were not passive objects of trade routes or environmental conditions; they built the networks and communities on which rulers depended. The moving lake provides the setting, but the history belongs to the people around it.

## A Hill above the River: Cattle, Gold, and Power at Mapungubwe

Moving far south from the Lake Chad region brings us to Mapungubwe. The comparison concerns institutions of power, rather than a migration from one kingdom to the other.

Near the meeting of the Limpopo and Shashe rivers in southern Africa, a steep hill rises above a landscape of settlements and fields. Around the thirteenth century, Mapungubwe Hill became a place where some people lived and were buried apart from others. Archaeologists recovered gold ornaments from burials there, including the small gold-covered rhinoceros that has become an emblem of the site. It is easy to start with the rhinoceros and tell a story about royal treasure. But the wealth on the hill rested on cattle, cultivation, craft, and exchange throughout the valleys below.

The landscape did not become important overnight. Archaeological work traces communities in the wider Limpopo-Shashe region through a succession of settlements and changing patterns of occupation from roughly the tenth century onward. Sites often discussed together include Schroda, K2, and Mapungubwe. Their histories are not identical stages of a single royal plan. Ceramic traditions, settlement layouts, and economic activities changed as communities responded to opportunities and pressures. By the period around 1220 to 1300, the hilltop and surrounding areas were parts of a polity with pronounced social differentiation. The precise extent and mechanisms of its influence are reconstructed from material evidence rather than a surviving census or royal archive.

Cattle mattered for food, wealth, and social relationships. Keeping herds required grazing, access to water, protection, and organized work. People also grew crops in an environment where rainfall and river conditions could shift. Farmers, herders, metalworkers, and people transporting goods sustained the regional economy. When archaeologists study the distribution of houses, refuse, pottery, animal bones, and storage areas, they find evidence for more than the display of elite power. The daily work of communities made it possible for some individuals to accumulate resources and convert them into political influence.

Mapungubwe's position connected inland production to wider routes. Gold and ivory could move toward ports of the East African coast, while glass beads and other materials traveled inland through traders and intermediaries. A glass bead found in a settlement demonstrates access to an exchange system, but not necessarily a direct voyage between that house and a port in India. Trade was made through many local steps, each involving people and institutions of its own. Wealth accumulated in the region partly because inhabitants could participate in these networks, but trade alone does not explain every aspect of social order.

The organization of space on and below the hill is especially revealing. UNESCO's account of the cultural landscape describes a move toward separation between elite residence or burial and the settlements of others. Archaeologists interpret the hilltop as associated with sacred leadership and political authority. That inference is stronger because it draws on buildings, access, burials, and the distribution of high-status materials together. It should not be exaggerated into a floor plan of a modern palace. We do not know the personal names, speeches, or full chain of offices of the people who lived above the valley.

Gold is a material that survives and attracts attention. In the 1930s, excavators recovered items associated with a small group of graves on the hill, including beads, foil-covered objects, and ornaments. The rhinoceros was produced by shaping thin gold over a supporting form, not by casting a solid animal of gold. Researchers have revisited the original reports and the surviving artifacts to reconsider the arrangement of the burials. Some details of the early excavation remain unclear, and scholars disagree about the original assembly or function of particular gold-covered objects. Archaeological wealth is not a license to assign a personal identity to each grave.

The modern discovery of the hill was not an innocent encounter between scientists and a forgotten ruin. In 1932 local knowledge helped outsiders reach the site. Accounts preserved by South African National Parks name Mowena as a person who indicated the difficult route up the hill. The subsequent search disturbed objects and burials before formal excavation began. Research later took place within a South African political order that denied Black communities equal recognition and authority. Historians must distinguish what the excavations found from the assumptions of the people who excavated. Indigenous African achievement needs no foreign author to explain it.

The famous gold objects are now part of a contested and curated heritage. Museum preservation can protect fragile artifacts and make them available for study; it also raises questions about the treatment of human remains and relationships to descendant communities. Researchers revisiting archival accounts of the burials have emphasized gaps in the records and the need for care in interpreting skeletons and grave objects. A museum label can provide a working description, but the original act of burial belonged to a community's understandings of death and power, many of which cannot be directly recovered from the surviving gold.

What happened around 1300? Occupation patterns changed and Mapungubwe ceased to be the region's principal center of power. Changing rainfall and agricultural conditions have long been part of explanations. Shifts in trade, political relationships, and the organization of settlements also require attention. A drought reconstruction may establish environmental stress without proving that every household moved for the same reason. UNESCO links the changing climate with dispersal, but a single-cause story would be more confident than the combined evidence allows. People did not vanish from southern Africa when the hilltop lost its former position.

Histories of Great Zimbabwe are connected to developments in the wider region, but a line drawn from Mapungubwe to Great Zimbabwe should not erase the communities that lived in between. Political forms, stone architecture, cattle economies, and trade developed through processes of continuity and change. It is possible to identify shared practices without assuming that one royal court simply packed up and moved north with every subject in tow. Archaeologists need settlement chronologies, regional surveys, and material comparisons to test such connections. Social history is made across landscapes, not only in capitals.

The people who produced and carried this wealth rarely appear in displays. Making a gold-covered object required artisans with tools and technical knowledge. Keeping cattle alive and tending crops required work across seasons. Beads passed through hands that never saw the hill. Children learned the uses of local plants, paths, and waterways; households negotiated access to labor and land. These are reasonable questions suggested by the evidence, not reconstructed biographies of particular individuals. A full account of Mapungubwe has to keep the labor visible even when names and personal testimony have not survived.

The hill remains a powerful historical place because several strands of evidence converge there. Its position and settlement pattern reveal spatial inequality. Its graves preserve costly objects and difficult archaeological contexts. Its surrounding landscape records agriculture, herding, and trade. Its modern excavation history reveals how knowledge can be preserved and distorted at once. Mapungubwe was not simply a precursor to something else or a treasure hoard outside world history. It was a society whose people shaped southern African political and economic life, and whose history has to be read from both the summit and the ground beneath it.

## Before the Portuguese Arrived: How the Kingdom of Kongo Took Shape

Farther west in the lower Congo region, another state took shape before Portuguese visitors began recording their encounters with it.

When Portuguese visitors reached the lower Congo River near the end of the fifteenth century, they encountered a political world that was already organized. Inland stood the kingdom of Kongo, with a capital on a high plateau, rulers able to negotiate with outsiders, and relationships among provinces that had been developing for generations. The outsiders' arrival generated more written evidence, but it did not create the political institutions they began describing. Our difficulty is that much of what we know about Kongo's earlier history comes from documents written after that encounter. We have to reconstruct the period before the written archive becomes abundant without allowing the archive to write the beginning of the story for us.

The name Kongo referred to a kingdom in west-central Africa, centered in territory now divided among Angola and the two modern states called Congo. It should not be confused with the boundaries of any one of those countries. Its center, Mbanza Kongo, occupied a plateau and served as a royal and ceremonial capital. Historical and archaeological evidence shows that the urban settlement existed in local building materials before the Portuguese added churches and masonry structures. Its political significance therefore cannot be explained simply as a result of European-style architecture. The kingdom's history belongs first to the people and landscapes of central Africa.

What did a capital need to govern? It required routes carrying people, foods, textiles, news, and obligations between settlements. It needed mechanisms for recognizing authority in provinces and hearing disputes. The royal household drew on tribute and revenues, while regional officials and local leaders retained their own relationships. Archaeology preserves parts of the urban setting; early writings describe later versions of these institutions. The precise arrangement around the mid-fifteenth century cannot be copied directly from a description composed two hundred years afterward. Even when an institution's name stays familiar, its powers may change.

An older, influential origin story tells of a founding figure often named Lukeni lua Nimi and a conquest that united territories under a new ruling line. Such accounts have historical value, but they were passed on and recorded in different political circumstances. One version portrays a forceful conqueror. Another, composed after periods of civil conflict, presents a founder who resolves disputes. These are not merely decorative differences in storytelling. They can express changing ideas of what makes kingship legitimate: the ability to command, the ability to reconcile, or the ability to combine both. A tradition may preserve remembered political struggles while also reflecting the concerns of the people who retell it.

Historian John Thornton has examined the chronological order of these accounts and, in research published in 2025, proposed a more complicated reconstruction. Rather than imagining Kongo as the first great kingdom to appear in an otherwise politically simple region, he argues that an earlier large polity called Mpemba may already have existed. In his interpretation, Kongo's emerging rulers developed a base associated with Mpemba Kasi, made alliances with neighboring authorities, and eventually established dominance through the conquest of Mpemba. This is an interpretation of surviving traditions and early documents, not a recently recovered eyewitness chronicle of a battle in the fourteenth century.

Why does that proposal matter? It changes what we ask about state formation. If the people drawn into Kongo already lived under substantial political institutions, then the kingdom's rise involved reorganizing existing governments, not inventing centralized authority from nothing. Thornton argues that Mpemba itself may have possessed provincial structures, while other polities made strategic choices about whether to support an expanding power. That helps explain why an emerging king might grant special privileges to allies. But the dates and sequence remain open to further research. The survival of a sixteenth-century report about an earlier polity is not proof that every detail reaches back unchanged to its supposed foundation.

Mbata offers a particularly revealing example. A letter from King Afonso in 1514 indicates that the ruler of Mbata exercised a role in recognizing or certifying royal authority. Later accounts describe a different relationship. Thornton reads the discrepancy as evidence that constitutional arrangements changed and that later traditions adapted to new realities. Notice the date: 1514 is after Portuguese contact. The document is firm evidence of a particular political claim made then, and indirect evidence for arrangements that may have older roots. It cannot be silently relocated to the year 1400. That separation of testimony from reconstruction is crucial to responsible early Kongo history.

The political map was also shaped by negotiation. If an ally was allowed to keep a hereditary office, or if a conquered district retained local leaders, the king's authority might depend on honoring agreements. Governance could thus be neither a simple collection of independent clans nor a uniform chain of officials appointed from the capital. Thornton's work questions some inherited descriptions of Kongo as fundamentally organized by timeless clan rules. An office or lineage recorded in a later source needs to be studied in its historical moment. Calling an institution traditional does not mean it was the same in every century.

Descriptions of the kingdom often place a mwene, or ruler, at the top of a hierarchy, but a political title is only the beginning of the question. Who received produce from an area? Who could remove a regional official? Who judged a dispute when local and royal claims conflicted? Later records suggest arrangements involving delegated revenues and contested provincial power. They provide useful clues about earlier developments while warning against assuming a fixed constitutional blueprint. The king had to govern people with local histories and claims. Central authority could grow by granting powers as well as by taking them away.

The history of Mbanza Kongo itself teaches another lesson about evidence. The Portuguese added buildings of stone that became prominent archaeological and historical landmarks. Earlier buildings made in local materials were more vulnerable to loss and rebuilding. If we count only standing stone structures, we risk making foreign construction seem like the start of urban life. UNESCO's record of Mbanza Kongo identifies a royal residence, customary court, and sacred places within a longer history of the capital. Yet even this preserved landscape must be studied with attention to later transformations, including Christianity, colonial interventions, and changing local uses of space.

European arrival transformed Kongo's external relationships. Its rulers used diplomatic contact in pursuit of their own purposes, and Christianity became important in the kingdom's subsequent history. Later Atlantic commerce, including the trade in enslaved people, produced violent changes that demand their own account. But beginning with those later developments would obscure the earlier political work of consolidation. We should avoid reading the sixteenth or seventeenth centuries backward as if every later conflict, alliance, or economic institution had already existed in precisely the same form. Pre-contact Kongo deserves a history of its own, even when much of the surviving evidence was written afterward.

Kongo was not an empty stage awaiting the first Portuguese text. It was one part of a larger political landscape in which communities, rulers, and neighboring polities developed relationships over time. Its earliest history remains partly reconstructive: later memories can be compared, archaeological places can be investigated, and the earliest dated letters can be situated rather than stretched backward. The result is not a single founding tale whose every detail we claim to know. It is a better question about how people built a kingdom from earlier institutions, negotiations, and competing forms of authority.
