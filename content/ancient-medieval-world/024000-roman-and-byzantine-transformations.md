# When Rome Changed Shape: Emperors, Cities, and the Eastern Empire

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

In the sixth century, visitors entering the church of San Vitale in Ravenna could see an emperor standing in a mosaic, holding a ceremonial vessel among soldiers and clergy. Justinian was represented as a ruler whose authority joined the army, the church, and the state. He did not ordinarily govern Italy from Ravenna. His court lay across the Mediterranean, in Constantinople. Yet the image asserted that he ruled here too. It survives from a century often placed after the “fall of Rome.” How could an emperor still claim Roman rule in Italy after Rome was supposed to have fallen? To answer, we need to follow several centuries of change without assuming that every part of the empire followed the same timetable.

By about 300 CE the Roman state was much larger than any city, even Rome itself. Its taxes, soldiers, roads, courts, and supply networks stretched across the Mediterranean and deep into inland provinces. At the end of the third century, Diocletian and his colleagues divided governing responsibilities among multiple emperors. This arrangement, conventionally called the tetrarchy, sought to deal with military and political pressures. It also underlines an important distinction: dividing an emperor's workload did not necessarily mean announcing the creation of separate civilizations. Provinces could change administrators while households continued to farm, trade, litigate, and pay what the state demanded.

Constantine emerged victorious from another period of civil war and established an imperial capital on the Bosporus, inaugurated as Constantinople in 330. Its site linked the Black Sea with the Mediterranean and offered access to eastern frontier provinces. Constantine favored Christianity, but the move from religious tolerance and imperial patronage to an officially Christian political order took time. The empire remained a place of Jews, Christians with competing doctrines, followers of traditional cults, and people whose practices did not fit neat categories. The capital itself accumulated palaces, public spaces, churches, and defenses. It was a Roman capital, not a post-Roman state created in a single moment.

The map of Roman power in the west nevertheless changed. Imperial courts operated in several cities, and field armies increasingly depended on alliances, recruitment, and negotiation with groups whose leaders had their own ambitions. The Goths' sack of Rome in 410 was a shock to writers who regarded the city as a symbol of permanence. The Vandals took Carthage in 439 and controlled important North African resources. In 476 the military leader Odoacer removed the western emperor Romulus Augustulus in Italy. That year is a convenient textbook date, not an observed moment when roads crumbled, Latin stopped being spoken, or every Roman office disappeared. Roman law, taxes, landownership, episcopal institutions, and habits of administration survived in different combinations.

Former imperial territories developed different political arrangements. In Italy, Ostrogothic rulers claimed legitimacy partly by preserving Roman official forms. In Gaul, Frankish rulers competed and negotiated with local elites and bishops. In North Africa, the Vandal monarchy presided over a population with deeply rooted Roman institutions and Christian communities. For farmers and townspeople, a change of king could alter the destination of tax payments or the burden of maintaining soldiers, while familiar local intermediaries remained. Conflict and confiscation were real, but so were continuity and adaptation. Calling these regions simply “barbarian” tells us little about the complex ways people governed and were governed.

Farther east, the court at Constantinople retained an emperor, professional officials, a monetary system, and important tax-producing provinces. Greek was widely spoken in the eastern Mediterranean, while Roman legal tradition remained authoritative. Modern historians usually call this realm Byzantine. Its inhabitants understood themselves as Romans. The modern label can help us locate their distinctive later history, but it can conceal the degree to which the eastern state was a continuation of Roman imperial government. The same state also changed: religious institutions gained political influence, frontier pressures shifted, and urban life varied markedly between the great capitals and smaller provincial centers.

Justinian ruled from 527 to 565 and attempted to strengthen this imperial order. One project collected and reorganized centuries of Roman legal writings, imperial constitutions, and juristic arguments. The collection now called the Corpus of Civil Law did not invent a legal system out of nothing; it selected, preserved, and revised older material for a sixth-century government. It could give officials and students an authoritative reference, while everyday disputes still depended on judges, languages, local influence, and access to the courts. The legal compilation's later importance in European law should not be mistaken for immediate, uniform application throughout every village Justinian claimed to govern.

In Constantinople, factional tension and opposition to imperial policies exploded into the Nika revolt of 532. Parts of the city burned. Imperial forces eventually crushed the uprising at enormous human cost, although the numerical death toll in narrative sources must be handled cautiously. Justinian then rebuilt the capital's principal church, Hagia Sophia, as a vast statement of religious and imperial patronage. The building required architects, skilled artisans, stone, timber, transport, money, and labor. A splendid church tells us about engineering and patronage, but it also directs attention toward the resources extracted from the empire's inhabitants to make such building possible.

Justinian's generals campaigned in Vandal North Africa and Ostrogothic Italy, bringing large territories under eastern imperial rule. The wars were not merely the restoration of familiar institutions for grateful populations. Long campaigning in Italy damaged towns, agricultural districts, and transport networks. Some people changed political allegiances repeatedly as armies advanced and withdrew. When we look at Ravenna's mosaics, we see both the emperor's claim to renewed Roman unity and an Italian community drawn into expensive struggles between rulers. Even after imperial successes, parts of Italy passed to Lombard control later in the sixth century.

A further danger arrived through disease. In the early 540s, an epidemic described by contemporaries reached Constantinople and other regions. Modern work connects the outbreak with plague, but the precise demographic losses and the scale of its long-term effect remain debated. Procopius, an important sixth-century writer, provided a vivid account of suffering in the capital. He also wrote from particular political positions and used literary techniques. His testimony should be compared with other written sources, archaeology, coinage, and the limits of what any one city can reveal. It is enough to say that disease compounded the pressures imposed by warfare, taxation, and disrupted livelihoods without claiming a single numerical catastrophe across the entire Mediterranean.

The church became increasingly central to local administration and communal life. Bishops could distribute aid, negotiate with political leaders, sponsor buildings, and manage disputes. Monasteries attracted donations and organized religious communities. But Christianity was not institutionally uniform, and theological disagreements connected to law, language, and imperial loyalty sometimes divided communities. In Egypt, Syria, Armenia, and other regions, churches developed histories that did not simply mirror Constantinople's priorities. A government may declare its preferred doctrine; such a decree does not explain what every believer did or believed.

The seventh century brought further strain. A prolonged war between the eastern Romans and the Sasanian Empire reached across Syria, Egypt, Mesopotamia, and beyond. Emperor Heraclius recovered territory after major campaigns, but decades of mobilization had already altered resources and loyalties. Soon afterward, armies organized under the early Muslim caliphate took control of Syria, Palestine, and Egypt. These conquests did not erase Roman institutions overnight, and Greek and Coptic documents show important continuities under new rulers. They did, however, remove major eastern provinces from Constantinople's political control and force the remaining empire to adapt to a different strategic world.

The evidence for these transformations is layered. Imperial laws tell us what rulers wanted to regulate; they cannot prove every order was obeyed. Court historians describe wars and personalities, often with strong literary and political agendas. Mosaics, buildings, coins, graves, and settlement surveys show patterns that royal documents miss, but cannot supply the private thoughts of those who used them. Even the words “Roman,” “Goth,” and “Greek” shifted in meaning according to context. An accurate history must allow an inhabitant of sixth-century Italy to belong to a community with Roman habits while living under an Ostrogothic king, and later an eastern Roman administrator.

The Roman world did not end everywhere in 476. In the west, institutions were divided, reworked, and attached to new forms of kingship. In the east, an imperial government continued, sometimes reasserting its claims with force and sometimes surrendering territory. Across both, households bore costs, local cultures persisted, and Christian institutions reshaped public life. Ravenna's mosaic makes a political argument on behalf of Justinian. Our task is not to accept or dismiss that argument automatically, but to place it beside the lives and institutions the mosaic cannot show. Roman history became several histories, connected even as they diverged.
