# Backlog — Ancient and Medieval Histories

**Status:** The initial ninety lectures (001000–090000) are written, indexed, and source-mapped; twenty-four additional inserted companion lectures now bring the track to 114. Unit 6 (041000–050000), on the period around 1200–1500 CE, completes the initial chronological bridge from early urban worlds to the modern-world track. This is introductory coverage, not a declaration that every region, society, or source tradition has been addressed in sufficient depth.

The chronology is a scaffold, not a sequence in which one region necessarily learns from another. Each unit should integrate changes to governance with work, family, belief, material culture, economic organization, and what the historical evidence actually allows us to know.

## Next research campaigns (not yet indexed)

The initial bridge and forty regional/documents-based depth lectures are written. The priorities below remain **research proposals**, not existing lectures or assigned identifiers.

- **Original-language and archaeological evidence.** Review Persian, Arabic, Chinese, and Indigenous textual traditions with specialist translations and edition references; expand archaeological field reports rather than relying on heritage summaries alone.
- **Regions still thinly treated.** Expand beyond the new case studies to Indigenous southeast North America, the Plains and Arctic; inland and eastern African polities, family and kinship histories, Southeast Asian villages beyond Angkor, and further Central Asian communities and languages.
- **Everyday social histories.** Expand household labor, enslavement and coercion, migration, environment, women and kinship, commercial credit, and religious practice with evidence specific to place and period.
- **Cross-track synchronization.** Add clear chronology handoffs to the existing Deep Time, World History and Modern World curricula; avoid rewriting stronger specialist lectures already available elsewhere.

## Research rules for expansion

1. Verify dates, locations, named people, terms, and contested interpretations from primary evidence and independent high-quality scholarship wherever possible. Institutional summaries and survey texts are starting points, not substitutes for specialist sources.
2. Record a source, the specific aspect it supports, and its evidentiary limit for every spoken paragraph. Do not confuse a historian's reconstruction with a quotation from an ancient witness.
3. Explain consequences for actual communities: coercion, slavery, migration, trade, religious practice, and everyday work should not vanish behind imperial maps.
4. When a famous label is disputed (a civilizational collapse, a golden age, a unified nation, a religious founding chronology), explain competing arguments and indicate what the available evidence can resolve.
5. Preserve the independent pack boundary, stable local IDs, text-only delivery, and narration markers. Do not impose a word count or duration quota.

### Remaining high-value targeted research

- **North America:** southeastern Woodlands beyond Cahokia, Plains bison and agro-pastoral histories, Arctic and sub-Arctic societies, and further indigenous-language source work. Do not apply later ethnographies uncritically to earlier centuries.
- **Africa:** regional histories beyond court documents, economic and family life beyond the Qasr Ibrim elite archive, histories of slavery and coercion within and across regional economies, and additional archaeological landscapes in the Horn and southern Africa.
- **Asia:** mainland Southeast Asia beyond the Khmer and Bagan cores, island Southeast Asia beyond Java, Korean and Japanese household records, and oasis communities beyond Old Uyghur Turfan and Sogdian letters.
- **Method:** add independently checked first-language editions, community-led interpretation, dated excavations and clearly labeled archive biases, prioritizing substantive new insights rather than repeating generic caveats.

### Following this installment

- **North American comparative archaeology:** more Great Basin and Southwest communities beyond Chaco, broader Pueblo and Plains migration histories, and deeper work with community-led sources.
- **Africa and the Middle East:** Ethiopian and Nubian lay archives where available, non-elite life beyond the Geniza's Jewish documentary sample, western African ironworking and ecological history, and Red Sea communities.
- **Asian networks:** Korean local documents, South Asian regional courts beyond Maurya and Gupta, western Central Asian rural communities, early Philippines and maritime Indonesia beyond Java, and non-elite Southeast Asian settlements.
- **Method:** review contemporary original-language texts with edition-level citations, explain source-transmission history, test local archaeological chronologies, and distinguish material facts from community traditions and scholarly inferences.

### After the fourth depth installment

- **South Asia:** fuller pre-1500 Tamil literary and social history, the Deccan beyond stone donations, regional religious debate, coastal trade in the Bay of Bengal, and evidence of non-elite agriculture beyond royal grants.
- **Indigenous North America:** additional Great Basin, California, Southwest and Plateau histories developed with community-led and contemporary archaeological evidence; distinguish ecological history from narratives of disappearance.
- **Northeast and Central Asia:** Balhae regional societies, Silla and Goryeo local documents, oasis and agricultural communities beyond Bactrian parchment records, and better original-language editions.
- **Sources and method:** update exact original inscription locations, publication and translations; compare accessible full archaeological research with abstract-level findings; check descendant-community explanations before applying archaeological culture names to peoples.

### Following the fifth depth installment

- Extend California histories beyond the Chumash and oak-woodland case studies with community-led northern California, inland, and desert histories.
- Examine local records of Goryeo and Balhae outside court-centered collections; medieval Korean printing is not a substitute for landholding and household history.
- Expand the South Asian literary history with specialist studies of manuscript transmission, Tamil Jain and Buddhist writing, and gender in later devotional poetry rather than treating all Tamil texts as one ancient period.
- Seek excavated, not only acquired, documentary archives in the Hindu Kush and Central Asian regions, and compare sources from Muslim and non-Muslim communities without assuming identical legal practice.
- Explore pre-1500 Philippine settlements and material exchange, precolonial Madagascar, and further African rural archaeology as additional geographic depth campaigns.
