# The Village as a Taxpayer: Farming and Property in Byzantium

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

In the year 1103, a record associated with the monastery of Iviron on Mount Athos concerned a village called Radolibos. The documents studied by historian Kostis Smyrlis identify one hundred and twenty-two peasant families living there. It is a precise number, and for that reason it feels intimate. We can begin to imagine a village with many households, fields requiring work, and obligations that reached beyond its boundaries. But precision in a fiscal record is not the same thing as a census of every human life. We must ask why a monastery wanted this number and how its relationship to land made the residents legible to people far away.

The eastern Roman, or Byzantine, Empire ruled changing territories around the eastern Mediterranean for more than a thousand years. Its inhabitants did not all live in Constantinople, and their daily institutions cannot be described only through emperors, bishops, and wars. Agriculture sustained cities, armies, markets, religious foundations, and local communities. A village might have a recognized collective identity for tax purposes, even while residents differed in wealth, status, or occupation. Alan Harvey's account of the Byzantine village emphasizes its role as both a place of social organization and a unit the state could assess. That double role brought advantages and dangers.

What does it mean for a village to pay tax? It does not imply that a village was a single person with a purse. Rather, officers and landholders could attach assessments and obligations to identifiable holdings or communities. Neighbors depended on one another for irrigation, paths, shared resources, and possibly collective action in disputes. The same proximity made it easier for authorities to identify a place as a fiscal unit. When a farmer left land idle or a plot was abandoned, the consequences might reach other taxpayers and the wider revenue system. Yet we should not project one unchanging Byzantine tax formula across hundreds of years. The evidence changes with empire, region, and century.

One source of extraordinary interest is a text usually called the Farmer's Law. It addresses disputes concerning fields, animals, fences, damage, theft, and cooperation among cultivators. Historians have debated its date and how to classify it: was it an official enactment, a compilation of practice, or a text assembled from several legal traditions? Walter Ashburner traced the disagreement among earlier specialists, some of whom dated it to the eighth or ninth century and others to the reigns of earlier emperors. The uncertain date does not make the text useless. It changes what kind of claim we are entitled to make about the communities described in it.

Suppose an animal damages a neighbor's crop. There must be some answer to a practical question: who bears the loss? A legal compilation can specify what should happen when boundaries are crossed or duties neglected. But it does not tell us the frequency with which livestock escaped, how every village handled compensation, or whether poorer households could obtain the same remedy as influential ones. Norms are evidence for the problems jurists and lawmakers thought required regulation. They are not recordings of every quarrel. If we study the Farmer's Law as though it were a journalist's report from one particular village, we will ask it to do work it was not designed to do.

By the tenth century, another conflict occupies imperial legal texts: powerful landowners acquiring property from less powerful cultivators. Emperors from Romanos the First to Basil the Second issued measures intended to defend small property holders and protect the taxable village community. Some rules gave relatives and neighbors a preferential opportunity to buy land before an outsider did. Historians refer to this as a right of pre-emption. It mattered because control of a plot could change not just one family's livelihood but the social balance of the village and the revenue that the state expected from it.

The language of these laws often contrasts the powerful with the poor. That is politically useful language, but not every landowner fits into one of two neat groups. A peasant might hold some property while renting another plot, or a family might prosper enough to become an important local actor. Smyrlis points out that monastic archives reveal mid-level proprietors and town residents with land, though the preserved records most readily show people when their properties reached the monasteries. Modern history must look beyond a simple story in which giant estates swallowed all villagers without opposition. It was a struggle involving institutions, court decisions, purchases, donations, and local relationships.

Monasteries belong centrally in this story. A religious foundation might receive land as a donation, buy a holding, or litigate over its boundaries. Monastic archives survived in some places where the records of nearby families disappeared. Their charters can therefore illuminate local land use with striking clarity while also skewing our perspective toward the rights of institutions that preserved them. To say a monastery owned or administered land does not mean every resident who worked there had taken religious vows. Many were ordinary cultivators whose labor maintained households and generated resources used by the monastery.

Radolibos is valuable precisely because the records give a village-scale view. The number of recorded families makes it possible to think about settlement and estate management without treating a royal capital as the only arena of power. Yet we do not know the complete biographies of those families. A document concerned with rights and liabilities may preserve names or categories relevant to the institution, leaving out childhood, illness, neighborly assistance, or resentment at an official demand. The 1103 record anchors a question about a real community. It should not license us to invent the name of a shepherd or the words of a widow in that community.

The broader countryside also changed during the eleventh and twelfth centuries. Some large properties expanded, while circumstances after war or political disruption could cause changes of ownership or a return to local control. In Smyrlis's analysis, the evidence of villages in monastic archives does not support one straight line from independent peasants to a landless population. There were purchases and donations, contested rights, and differences between localities. We should be especially careful about comparing a village in Greece, a settlement in Asia Minor, and a rural community in southern Italy as though all occupied the same administrative position.

What could villagers do when property was threatened? The laws reveal a conception of rights that families and neighbors might invoke. Monastic archives show that groups of people could appear in land agreements and disputes. But whether a rule was enforced depended on the capacities of courts, officials, and local interests. James Howard-Johnston argues that the tenth-century imperial legislative campaign was more effective than an older pessimistic view allowed. Other historians highlight the continued growth of large holdings. It is useful to recognize this disagreement. A ruler's declared aim, an individual court outcome, and the total transformation of landholding are three different kinds of historical evidence.

The village as a taxpayer is therefore a village seen from an administrative angle. From that angle it is a place with assessments, recognized property, and obligations that can be transferred or disputed. From the angle of residents it was also a cluster of homes, cultivated ground, households with unequal resources, and relationships that no surviving register lists in full. Both histories belong together. The law shows what government and jurists tried to protect or regulate; the archive shows some transactions made in actual places; neither makes the village into a mere entry in a ledger.

One hundred and twenty-two families at Radolibos. We can keep the number without pretending that those families are all equally known. It marks the moment a local community entered a surviving administrative record. Behind it stood fields and paths, decisions about land, and people who would not necessarily have described their home primarily as a taxpayer. Medieval history changes when a village becomes a subject in its own right. The records are still incomplete, but the question we ask of them has shifted from how an emperor ruled to how a community endured the demands and opportunities of that rule.

## Evidence boundary

Village communities were legal and fiscal units, but social organization varied by century and locality. The Farmer’s Law has disputed composition and was transmitted in later manuscripts; it is not a comprehensive ethnography. Tenth-century legislation expresses state intentions and debated effects. The 1103 Radolibos figure is a count in particular monastic documentation, not a representative Byzantine census.
