# Mainland Southeast Asia I: Pyu and Khmer towns, water, and land records

A monumental city is also a place where people live, move water, and contest claims to land. Pyu and Khmer examples will let us compare walls, habitation, beads, irrigation, and inscriptions. They belong to different places and centuries, rather than to a single city’s uninterrupted growth.

## A House inside the Walls: The Pyu Cities before Bagan

A city can be known for its walls long before anyone finds a home inside it. At Sri Ksetra, in the dry zone of what is now Myanmar, monumental brick defenses, gateways and religious buildings had drawn attention for generations. Yet the architecture of ordinary habitation remained difficult to recover. Walls establish that people enclosed and organized a large space; they do not tell us who swept a floor or repaired a roof. When archaeologists identified a sequence of domestic surfaces at the Yahanda mound, they gained a different way into the history of the Pyu cities.

Pyu is a name for related early historic communities and polities in the Ayeyarwady basin, before and alongside the emergence of Bagan. Important sites include Beikthano, Halin and Sri Ksetra. Their development stretched across many centuries, approximately from the late first millennium BCE into the first millennium CE, though each place followed its own chronology. It is tempting to imagine the oldest city handing leadership to the next in an orderly succession. Archaeology gives a messier picture of changing settlement, construction, exchange and religious practice rather than a single baton passed between capitals.

The cities occupied areas where managing seasonal water could support farming and dense settlement. Brick walls and moats framed places of authority, but water channels and storage works connected them to cultivated ground outside the most visible monuments. Metalworking and ceramic production appear in the archaeological record. The words urban and agricultural should not be made opposites here. A city depended on people who grew food, maintained systems and moved resources between fields, workshops and ceremonial centers. The question is how those activities were coordinated, not which single institution did everything.

At Sri Ksetra, archaeologists excavating Yahanda in 2015 encountered hardened work surfaces, pottery and iron nails. Several layers of occupation yielded radiocarbon dates around the fifth to seventh centuries CE, with some contexts perhaps continuing later. A wooden building once stood there: postholes and traces of floors are easily overlooked when compared with a monumental stupa, yet they tell us that life went on in structures more perishable than brick. The excavated patch was limited, so a single house cannot stand in for every neighborhood. It can disprove the idea that the only recoverable Pyu architecture was monumental.

Below some of those living surfaces were traces of cremation burials and a brick-and-earth terrace. That ordering is intriguing. Later habitation had been built above a place where the dead had been commemorated. In one area a posthole passed close to an earlier burial. The excavators suggested that the placement may have been deliberate, but they did not claim to know the mind of the builder. Such a detail invites us to imagine continuity of place: a location with a ritual significance might later support daily activity, or people may have reshaped an old ritual ground for reasons no longer recoverable.

Burial practice in the Pyu cities is itself a complicated chronology. Urns and funerary terraces appear alongside a growing variety of Buddhist images and buildings. It would be incorrect to say that cremation proves Buddhism in every layer; older rites could be adapted within changing religious settings. Janice Stargardt stresses that some funerary forms do not have unmistakable Buddhist symbols. The task is to date the vessels, their context and associated structures before identifying a religious meaning. Old customs may remain recognizable even as the words people use to explain them change.

Buddhism nevertheless became important in the cultural history of these centers. Brick stupas, devotional images and inscriptions make connections with wider South and Southeast Asian religious worlds visible. Those contacts were not necessarily a one-time package called Indianization delivered from outside. Local potters could adapt decorative conventions. Patrons could select different ritual forms. Residents might take part in practices that had traveled along commercial routes while continuing activities with older local roots. The Pyu evidence is more persuasive when we see adoption as a series of decisions by communities rather than the replacement of one culture with another.

The writing found in Pyu contexts permits another kind of question. An inscription can sometimes give a name, donation or date, but early texts are unevenly preserved and do not form an uninterrupted royal chronicle. The archaeologist's radiocarbon date is not a written year, and a sculpted image's style cannot always be anchored to an exact decade. Combining several methods creates a stronger chronology than any one alone. The same principle holds for claims about how far Pyu political influence extended: written and material signs of contact do not amount to a known administrative map.

It is worth returning to a practical object: the pot. At Yahanda the domestic pottery included cord-marked wares, while some decorated vessels bore motifs associated with religious imagery found in wider networks. Changes in these assemblages can help archaeologists build sequences of occupation and contact. But a decorated sherd is not the passport of its owner. The vessel could be made locally, copied from another model or obtained through exchange. Different techniques can travel without the movement of an entire population, and people can move without bringing a complete package of material styles.

UNESCO recognizes the three cities as a connected heritage landscape because their monuments and agricultural features illustrate the duration of early urbanization in the region. That recognition brings useful attention and conservation resources. It also encourages a tidy visual story centered on brick walls and religious buildings. Excavation unsettles the tidiness. A small sequence of floors, repairs and abandoned features may reveal different eras occupying nearly the same ground. The city's present outline is an aggregate of centuries, not a snapshot of one day when all the walls, drains, homes and temples stood as we see them.

Later histories of Myanmar often move rapidly from the Pyu to Bagan. The older settlements certainly changed as new political centers and linguistic traditions developed, but archaeology has not supplied a simple moment when one entire people vanished and another took their place. The difficult work is to trace continuing sites, changing buildings, texts and regional relationships across long intervals. Narratives of invasion or replacement may be influential, but they need to be tested against the particular settlement evidence rather than substituted for it.

A house inside Sri Ksetra's walls is therefore more than an archaeological footnote. It opens a view onto the households and workshops that monuments depend upon but rarely name. It reminds us that Buddhist shrines were built in places with older practices, and that water engineering and residence had their own histories. The Pyu cities were neither mere preludes to Bagan nor copies of Indian cities. They were long-lived local experiments in organizing settlement, ritual and the material work of urban life.

## Beads along a Changing Delta: Angkor Borei and Óc Eo

Southeast of the Pyu cities, the delta communities around Angkor Borei and Óc Eo present another early urban and trading landscape.

At a burial site near Angkor Borei, in southern Cambodia, archaeologists recovered more than a thousand small beads made of glass and stone. Some would have caught light with vivid color; others were shaped from minerals that had traveled a considerable distance. A bead is small enough to pass easily from hand to hand, yet its chemical composition can connect workshops and exchange networks across the Bay of Bengal. These finds give us an entrance into an early Mekong delta history that cannot be reduced to an imperial list or an account of ships arriving from India.

The Mekong delta is not a stable platform where people once built a town and left it unchanged. Rivers shift, channels silt up, and seasonal floodwater alters which routes are easiest to follow. Communities had to decide where to build on elevated ground and how to reach other settlements by boat. In that environment, Angkor Borei and the sites around Óc Eo, across today's Cambodian-Vietnamese border, formed parts of a broader world of workshops, shrines, agricultural production and exchange. Modern national boundaries do not describe the network in which those communities lived.

Excavations by the Lower Mekong Archaeological Project suggest that Angkor Borei was occupied from at least the fourth century BCE. Over time a settlement landscape developed that included sizable centers and smaller communities. Written Chinese accounts refer to a polity conventionally called Funan in the first millennium CE. These accounts are valuable, but their place names and categories were produced by outsiders with their own diplomatic interests. Archaeologists must decide whether those names correspond to particular sites and periods. No single foreign description can provide a full map of the delta's political life.

Consider the canal traces that appear to join places in the delta. Some were mapped from aerial photographs and later investigated by drilling and excavation. They could help people navigate a landscape cut by shifting rivers and wetlands; some waterworks may also have affected fields and drainage. To date a canal, however, researchers must recognize which sediment was deposited while the channel worked and which material entered it after it ceased to flow. An old grain in a young deposit can mislead the date. The shape visible from the air is not a date by itself.

A geoarchaeological investigation near Angkor Borei combined optically stimulated luminescence and radiocarbon dates. The results suggested excavation or renewed digging sometime between the first millennium BCE and the middle of the first millennium CE. Charcoal from channel infill pointed toward early fourth- to early fifth-century changes, but the deposits were mixed and the estimates broad. The authors resisted the most dramatic inference: a canal falling out of use is not evidence that the entire delta was deserted. A route can lose importance because water, sediment, commerce or the needs of its users have changed.

The beads let us approach contact from another angle. Alison Carter and colleagues examined glass and stone beads from the Vat Komnou cemetery at Angkor Borei and compared them with material from Óc Eo and other sites. They identified types of mineral-soda glass associated with production networks in Sri Lanka or South India. Chemical and morphological evidence also linked some agate, carnelian and garnet objects with South Asian materials or industries. This is more precise than saying that an object looked foreign. It still does not tell us which boat carried it, or whether it was traded once or ten times.

The surprising conclusion of that study concerned exchange within Southeast Asia itself. The bead assemblage from Angkor Borei had similarities with inland Cambodian and Thai sites, rather than merely matching coastal ports. The researchers describe a Mekong interaction sphere in which valuable objects helped forge alliances among communities. Think of an imported bead as an object that could become politically meaningful only after it reached a local network. A long-distance connection mattered, but so did the nearby relationships that gave its new possessor a reason to display, exchange or bury it.

At Óc Eo, excavations have uncovered traces of timber structures, pottery, craft areas, glass-working evidence and brick architecture from different phases. A Vietnamese heritage nomination describes early raised dwellings and later concentrated glass-jewelry work around Gò Óc Eo. These claims draw attention to residents rather than treating the place solely as a dock for overseas goods. Yet a nomination is not itself a substitute for stratigraphic publication. The useful approach is to combine its site summaries with independent studies of dated deposits and materials.

A workshop makes a different historical sound from a court. It calls for fuel, raw materials, working space, specialized skill and buyers. Some people may have made beads while others fished, cultivated or traveled with goods. Imported materials might be transformed locally before moving onward. The existence of craft production does not mean that the whole town lived by luxury trade, and the survival of prestige objects biases us toward wealthy consumers. Pots, posts, refuse layers and landscape features are needed to ask about the labor that made an elite object's journey possible.

The delta's history has often been written as a preface to Angkor. Such a sequence can be useful for tracing later religious and political traditions, but it risks making early communities important only because of what came after them. Angkor Borei and Óc Eo occupied an environment that required its own ways of managing movement, settlement and exchange. Some methods continued in different forms; others changed as waterways and centers of authority shifted. The names used by later courts are not sufficient to fill the many centuries between the sites' earliest occupations and Angkor's monumental age.

When an archaeologist identifies the mineral ingredients of a glass bead, when a field team digs through canal infill, and when a historian reads a Chinese account of Funan, they are not simply collecting three copies of the same fact. Each source describes a different aspect of life. A trade object can reveal connection without political control. Sediment can date a change without naming who ordered it. A diplomat's text can name an authority without describing the houses that supported it. The strength of the reconstruction comes from their intersection, and from acknowledging the spaces they cannot close.

The bead at Angkor Borei is therefore not merely evidence that early Southeast Asia received goods from elsewhere. It is evidence that people in the delta drew distant materials into regional relationships of their own making. The canal is not merely a line on a map; it records repeated attempts to move through a changing environment. Together they put households, craftspeople, river travelers and local alliances back into a history too often told as a chain of foreign influences and famous empires. A delta connected worlds, but it also created its own.

## Beyond the Temple Walls: Water, Work, and Households at Angkor

Moving forward to the Khmer world of Angkor makes the scale of water management and settlement particularly visible.

The towers of Angkor Wat are among the most recognizable monuments of Southeast Asian history. It is easy to look at the temple and imagine that the city ended where its stone walls and moat ended. Yet the surrounding landscape holds traces of roads, ponds, embankments, house sites, canals, and reservoirs extending far beyond the great monuments. In the centuries when the Khmer rulers supported these buildings, hundreds of thousands of people may have lived across a dispersed urban and agricultural landscape. Their work rarely appears on a tourist postcard. If we want to understand Angkor, we must look past the towers toward the fields and communities that made monumental life possible.

Angkor was a series of political and ceremonial centers in what is now northwestern Cambodia, associated with the Khmer Empire from the ninth to the fifteenth centuries. Its environment was shaped by monsoon rains, seasonal water storage, soils, and relations with the great Tonle Sap lake. Royal patrons built temples and supported large construction projects, but the landscape was already inhabited and worked. The empire's history did not begin when one king placed his name on a monument. People cultivated land, traveled between settlements, and maintained local religious and social life under changing authorities.

For much of the twentieth century, scholarship focused heavily on stone monuments, sculptures, and inscriptions. Those materials remain essential, but they preferentially preserve the decisions of rulers, religious foundations, and elites. Houses made of perishable materials can vanish from view. A temple inscription may list grants of land, valuables, or people assigned to work for a foundation without telling us what every individual thought of those obligations. A monumental bas-relief may show a procession or battle but cannot serve as a census of ordinary livelihoods. The history of Angkor must combine textual and architectural evidence with archaeology of the less durable landscape.

Airborne laser scanning, known as lidar, has changed that picture. By measuring the ground surface beneath vegetation, researchers mapped archaeological features that were difficult to see from roads or within forest cover. A team led by Damian Evans and colleagues revealed planned spaces, occupation mounds, ponds, and engineered networks beyond the famous temple enclosures. Later surveys extended the view to other Cambodian urban landscapes. These are powerful findings, but a laser scan records shape rather than directly dating every household or identifying its inhabitants. Excavation, radiocarbon dating, and other methods remain necessary to turn a landscape map into a historical account.

Angkor's built environment was dispersed. Densely used centers were linked with a larger region in which agriculture, gardens, residential spaces, and religious sites existed together. Some researchers describe this as low-density urbanism: a city whose functions and population are spread across a broad engineered landscape rather than packed behind one continuous wall. The concept does not imply the region was thinly inhabited everywhere. It draws attention to a different arrangement of urban and rural life, with varied densities and numerous local centers. Asking where the city ends becomes a historical question rather than a line we can draw around Angkor Wat.

Population is difficult to reconstruct in such a setting. A 2021 study combining lidar, excavation, radiocarbon evidence, and demographic modeling estimated that the greater Angkor region may have held roughly seven hundred thousand to nine hundred thousand people at its thirteenth-century height. The estimate applies to a large region of about three thousand square kilometers, not to people crowded within the temple walls. It is a model dependent on assumptions about household density and the dates of occupation features, not an ancient census. Even the study's authors identify unresolved questions about how provincial agricultural regions supplied the center. We can use the model to grasp scale without mistaking its precision for certainty.

Water linked architecture to livelihood. Angkor's landscape included reservoirs, called barays, canals, embankments, ponds, and channels. They could affect drainage, storage, transport, agriculture, and ritual meaning in different combinations. It would be too simple to say that every reservoir was built only to irrigate rice, or that all water moved through a single perfectly designed system. The structures changed across centuries, and their functions remain subjects of research. Building and maintaining them required labor and local knowledge. An embankment that served one neighborhood could have consequences for people living farther downstream.

Rice cultivation was central to many communities, but we should not reduce every household to one crop. Fields, gardens, trees, fish, craftwork, and local exchange helped sustain the dispersed settlement. The rhythm of planting, harvesting, managing water, and maintaining houses could be affected by the demands of rulers or religious foundations. Large constructions required workers, skilled artisans, materials, food, and transport. Some labor may have been organized through obligations attached to land or institutions. The social categories recorded in inscriptions are difficult to translate directly into modern notions of employment, tenancy, or slavery; precise conclusions must depend on the particular text and its historical context.

Khmer inscriptions are especially important for questions of property and obligation. They record transactions, land grants, donations to temples, and disputes, sometimes naming officials and the people or resources associated with a foundation. Historians have examined how these records reflect changing positions for middle-ranking officials and shifts in the control of property. The inscriptions illuminate real economic relationships, but they are often legal or commemorative documents written to defend claims. They say much more about who needed a transaction remembered than about the full life of a farmer or craft worker. It is possible to learn from an archive while refusing to let its elite viewpoint become everyone's viewpoint.

Royal ceremonies and religious practice also depended on everyday production. Major temples were associated with Hindu and later Buddhist institutions, while religious life throughout the region was more varied than a list of official royal dedications might suggest. A change in royal patronage did not automatically replace the practices of every village. Temples needed offerings, workers, repair, and ongoing administration. Local shrines and household observances may leave fewer durable traces than monumental sculpture. This difference in survival helps explain why descriptions of Angkor can seem dominated by kings and gods even though most people lived outside court ceremonies.

Angkor was connected to other Southeast Asian worlds, including neighboring political centers and maritime networks. The Khmer state competed, negotiated, and exchanged with regions beyond its core. Those connections matter, but they should not be narrated as if goods moved effortlessly from a coastal ship directly to a royal temple. Overland transport, local markets, intermediaries, and seasonal constraints shaped movement. Villages could benefit from exchange or bear costs from taxation, military demands, or struggles over land. A history that follows the rice and timber as carefully as the royal inscriptions can reveal how distant politics reached ordinary households.

The later history of Angkor is often told as a single collapse. Scholars have examined changes in climate, water infrastructure, regional political competition, settlement patterns, and shifts in religious institutions. Evidence for droughts and intense monsoon episodes can help explain pressures on a complex water system. But it would be wrong to imagine that one climatic event toppled an unchanging city. Urban life reorganized over time, and people continued to inhabit and use the wider region. The monuments were not simply swallowed by a vacant jungle. Communities, religious institutions, and cultural practices persisted in changing political circumstances.

The archaeological landscape itself has a modern social history. Angkor remains a lived place, with villages and farming communities within the wider heritage region. Protection of temples, forests, waterways, and archaeological features affects residents and their livelihoods. Collaborative research involving Cambodian scholars and heritage institutions is essential to interpreting and conserving this landscape. Modern mapping can make ancient houses visible while also raising questions about access, tourism, and management. People living near a monument are not intruders in a history that ended centuries ago; they belong to the continuing life of the place.

The lesson of Angkor is not simply that its city was larger than we once thought. It is that the category city can hide the fields, waterways, and dispersed households that sustain political and ceremonial centers. Laser maps, stone inscriptions, excavations, and local knowledge illuminate different layers of that history. We can count some structures and model population, but we still need to ask who worked, who controlled resources, and how obligations differed among communities. Beyond the temple walls lay not an empty background but the inhabited, cultivated world in which the monuments made sense.

## A Field Written in Stone: Land, Law, and Obligation in the Khmer World

Inscriptions bring a smaller scale back into focus: particular fields, transfers, witnesses, and claims within Khmer institutions.

Imagine an old stone inscription near a Khmer temple. It names a plot of land, people involved in a transfer, and offerings intended to keep a religious foundation functioning. It may record witnesses, boundaries, or an argument over who was entitled to give the property. The stone is not a map of the whole empire. It is a deliberate public record, made because somebody wanted a claim to endure. Yet it opens a more intimate question than the design of a royal monument: who had the power to sell a field, to grant its produce, or to require another person to work it?

The Khmer Empire flourished in mainland Southeast Asia from around the ninth century into the fifteenth, with political and ceremonial centers in what is now Cambodia. Angkor's reservoirs, temples, and dispersed settlements required labor and resources, most visibly agricultural produce. Kings issued claims and rewards, while local authorities, families, and religious institutions negotiated their own interests. There was no need for a single modern-style code of property law to make these relationships binding. The surviving inscriptions show many particular arrangements, in specific places and political circumstances. They also show how often people felt a right needed to be recorded and defended.

To read these records, historians work across languages and genres. Sanskrit could give an inscription a prestigious religious and royal register. Old Khmer could record details of persons, transactions, boundaries, and material obligations. The famous Sdok Kak Thom inscription, dated to the eleventh century, contains substantial texts in both languages and recounts the history and claims of a priestly family. The linguist Chhany Sak-Humphry analyzed its Old Khmer grammatical structure as well as its Sanskrit portions. Such a document is extraordinarily rich, but its retrospective account also serves a family's position. The words were written by interested parties, not by a neutral government archivist.

Land presents a translation problem before it presents a legal problem. To grant a village or a field could refer to claims over cultivation, revenues, labor obligations, or privileges associated with a religious institution; it need not mean ownership of every particle of soil in the modern sense. Some resources could be shared or claimed collectively, while particular families or officials made transactions. The historian Eileen Lustig emphasizes that royal allocations and temple endowments must be read within a political economy of overlapping rights. A contemporary phrase like private property may be useful as a question. It cannot simply be imposed as the answer.

Imagine the practical steps behind a purchase recorded on stone. Someone had to recognize the land being transferred. Someone had to agree that the seller possessed a right to transfer it. Relatives, officials, and witnesses could matter because other people might later contest the transaction. A record might list the goods given in return, such as valued objects rather than coins in a modern market. Those details reveal the institutional work of buying and donating land. They do not tell us how frequently every household sold land, or whether a laborer cultivating the plot ever heard the inscription read aloud.

Religious foundations were powerful participants in this economy. A temple required land, food, ceremonial offerings, building maintenance, and people to perform many kinds of service. A donor could give resources for the worship of a deity while also making a public statement about status and loyalty. Inscribed endowments can therefore be both devotional and political. When an inscription preserves the transfer of labor attached to an estate, it is recording a consequential relationship among people, not merely an architectural budget. We should be careful with English labels such as servant, slave, or employee when the historical categories and degrees of compulsion remain specific to the record.

Angkorian land transactions also reveal that royal authority did not replace local negotiation. A king might confirm a gift, grant an exemption, or recognize an established claim. But royal intervention appears alongside purchases, community holdings, family rights, and disputes among officials. Historians reconstruct these practices by comparing documents rather than assuming that one royal decree settled property relations everywhere. An inscription often records the moment a claim was formalized or disputed. It cannot be read as a daily diary of quiet years when households cultivated, argued, borrowed, or shared land without leaving durable text.

Eileen and Terry Lustig examined inscriptions from the late ninth through late twelfth centuries and identified changes in the status of intermediate officials. Titles including vāp and loñ appear among people buying and donating land. The authors argue that some of these officials lost ground as higher-ranking actors gained advantages in control over land and fiscal privileges. Their article identifies a shift in how these ranks appear in the epigraphic record, not a census showing that everyone with such a title ceased to exist. A disappearing title in stone may register a change in office, status, inscription practice, or several things at once.

This research makes the empire's scale feel different. The great temple projects demanded resources, but an imperial economy was built through many small acts: assessing fields, hearing a petition, naming a boundary, granting a privilege, feeding the workers who tended a shrine. Sometimes those acts strengthened royal power. Sometimes they protected local claims or created room for conflict. When we ask whether Angkor was centralized, the useful question is not simply yes or no. It is which decisions could be made locally, which required recognition from an elite patron, and how those relationships changed across centuries.

The archive has a bias that is especially easy to miss. Stone survives because people deliberately paid to carve and preserve claims, often in religious spaces. It disproportionately records families, patrons, and institutions able to commission those texts. Cultivators who lacked that access appear more often as categories of labor or entries in lists. Women may be named as donors, relatives, or participants in a legal claim, but those mentions cannot establish a uniform rule for every woman's rights. Even a well-translated inscription is strongest as evidence for the transaction it describes, and weaker as evidence for what everyone in the region was permitted to do.

Archaeology broadens the view. Houses, rice fields, ponds, and canals surround temples across the wider Angkorian landscape. These traces tell us that land was worked and inhabited on scales far larger than individual shrines. They cannot by themselves reveal the names of tenants or the legal status of each field. Inscriptions, meanwhile, can describe legal relationships while remaining silent about where a household cooked, how often a harvest failed, or who repaired an embankment. Putting the two archives together is more informative than choosing either stone writing or aerial mapping as the whole story.

The central insight is that law was something people did. It took shape through gifts, purchases, endowments, confirmations, disputes, and arrangements that joined human labor to land and religion. Some of these practices reproduced social inequality; others gave communities and particular people ways to assert claims. The inscriptions make rights visible at moments when they mattered to somebody powerful enough to record them. Reading them carefully does not uncover a perfect system. It uncovers a changing society in which controlling a field also meant controlling relationships, obligations, and memory.
