# Signs above the Valley: Writing, Power, and Households at Monte Albán

The city of Monte Albán stands on a hill above the Valley of Oaxaca in southern Mexico. Building a city there was a decision about power as well as geography. The hill was not the richest agricultural land. Food came from fields below, while labor carried stone, earth, water, and offerings uphill. From the plaza, people could see the valleys whose villages supplied the city. Around five hundred years before the Common Era, several communities began concentrating their political and ceremonial lives there. Monte Albán grew into a center that endured for more than a millennium. Its history is not only written in pyramids. It is written on carved stone.

One of the earliest clues comes from San José Mogote, a settlement that preceded the great hilltop center. A carved slab found in a temple passage shows a body lying in apparent defeat, accompanied by a calendrical name. Archaeologists place the monument before Monte Albán's foundation. The stone was laid where people could walk across it. That placement matters: an image of a defeated enemy became part of the way a community entered a ceremonial building. Joyce Marcus and other specialists read this as political display. We cannot recover the captive's account of events, but we can see a claim that victory and public authority belonged together.

Early carvings at Monte Albán have long been called the Danzantes, or dancers, because some figures appear to twist across the stone. The label survives, but it is misleading. Many carved bodies show wounds, mutilation, or signs of subjection. They are more plausibly representations of captives and enemies than an assembly of celebratory dancers. The images formed part of major public construction. This is a warning about how names given by later observers can quietly become explanations. Before asking who is depicted, we should ask what the artist actually carved and where the image was displayed.

The Zapotec writing system is among the earliest known in Mesoamerica. It included calendrical names, signs for places, and combinations of glyphs that could make political relationships durable in stone. It was not simply an ornamental variant of later Maya writing. Different societies developed writing practices suited to different institutions. We can read some Zapotec signs with confidence, and specialists debate others. A sign that identifies a ruler by a day name is not a full royal biography. An apparent place name is not proof of an accurately surveyed border. The writing is essential evidence, but it does not speak with a single transparent voice.

Consider Building J in Monte Albán's main plaza. It has a distinctive plan and orientation. Its carved slabs include groups commonly interpreted as records of places brought under Monte Albán's influence. Some scholars emphasize the building's possible astronomical alignments, while others focus on its political program; the subjects are not mutually exclusive. Yet the word conquest needs care. A public monument can declare domination even where control was incomplete, temporary, or negotiated through local leaders. It records the capital's view of a relationship. It does not give us testimony from each community named or represented on the walls.

Archaeological survey gives the stone texts a landscape. The Valley of Oaxaca contained smaller centers with their own ceremonial buildings and residential areas. Research at Cerro Tilcajete and other sites shows similarities in elite objects and architecture, but also local differences. The region was not a grid of identical administrative offices operated from the hilltop. Alliances, rivalry, shared rituals, and coercion could all help link settlements. When inscriptions seem to describe a large political realm, excavated buildings can test how its institutions actually reached the countryside. Sometimes they support a story of integration. Sometimes they reveal uneven authority.

Monte Albán had artisans, farmers, builders, priests, families, and people who prepared food for ceremonies. Urban life required work that is rarely named on the monuments. Terraces, paths, public stairways, and the maintenance of buildings were sustained by households below and around the civic core. A successful capital could draw offerings and labor, but it also depended on rainfall, land, harvests, and the choices of surrounding communities. A carved ruler might commemorate a victory; the city could not survive on commemorations. That gap between public claims and material requirements is one reason archaeology and epigraphy are stronger together.

Burial archaeology shows another scale of history. Tombs were not reserved exclusively for a separate cemetery beyond the city. Some were built into residential units, while other people were buried in pits or reused domestic spaces. Wealthy tombs could contain painted walls, decorated vessels, and offerings arranged to connect the living household to the dead. Tomb 105, for example, preserves a painted scene with richly dressed figures and calendrical signs interpreted as names. These images were not neutral family portraits. They selected ancestors and ceremonies that surviving families wanted remembered. Even so, they show a kind of history different from the speeches carved on a public staircase.

Craft and exchange linked Oaxaca to the wider Mesoamerican world. Archaeologists find objects associated with central Mexico, and a Zapotec neighborhood has been identified at Teotihuacan far to the north. These connections imply travel, exchange, and changes in where people made their homes. They do not automatically show that one city conquered the other. A ceramic style can move because people migrate, because goods travel, or because local makers adopt techniques and imagery. Material resemblance is a clue about contact; it is not a substitute for investigating the terms on which contact happened.

Monte Albán's political importance changed over the centuries. By the late Classic period, power in the valleys was being reorganized, and the hilltop capital no longer governed the region as it once had. That did not mean every settlement was destroyed or every person moved away at once. Communities continued, and later Mixtec elites used parts of the site in different ways. Histories that end at the moment a capital declines confuse the history of a monumental institution with the history of a people. A city's diminished authority can coexist with ongoing households, languages, and networks of memory.

The carved signs also have a history of modern interpretation. Early explorers, museum labels, archaeologists, and present-day Zapotec scholars do not always approach them with the same questions. A photograph removes a stone from its architectural context; a drawing can make a worn mark seem sharper than it is; a catalogue may preserve an old reading that specialists later revise. Responsible interpretation starts with the surface, the provenience, and the alternative readings. There is no shame in saying that a sign is undeciphered or that two plausible histories fit the same monument. The purpose of research is to explain uncertainty, not disguise it.

What made Monte Albán an early state? The answer cannot be a pyramid by itself, or the presence of violence, or even the invention of writing. It was the sustained coordination of many settlements, the construction of large common spaces, political claims that reached beyond a single household, and institutions capable of organizing people and resources across the valleys. Writing helped rulers present that arrangement as legitimate. It also preserved their rivalries in ways that would not survive through architecture alone. The question is not whether the state had a ceremonial image. It is how that image related to the people and land it claimed.

The carved slab at San José Mogote and the signs on Building J bookend a crucial transformation. Public images of defeated rivals became longer claims about territory and rulers. Meanwhile, the city grew through the labor of countless people whose names were seldom inscribed. Reading Monte Albán means keeping all these forms of evidence in view: stone, houses, burials, fields, and the surviving communities of Oaxaca. The monuments tell us how leaders wanted power remembered. The archaeological landscape lets us ask what that power required, where it reached, and where the stone's claim may have exceeded the reality.
