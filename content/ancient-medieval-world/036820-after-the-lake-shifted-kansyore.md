# After the Lake Shifted: Kansyore Fishing Communities and Changing Neighbors

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

A lakeshore can be an abundant home, and still be a risky one. Fish gather at particular times and places. Water levels rise and fall. A river mouth that works well for one generation can become shallow or distant for another. Around Lake Victoria, communities made decorated pottery, fished, harvested shellfish, and lived in changing relationships with the land long before the better-known medieval towns of East Africa. Archaeologists often call part of this record Kansyore. The name is useful, but we have to be careful with it. It refers first to recognizable pottery and archaeological contexts, not to one people whose language, politics, and way of life remained unchanged for thousands of years.

Lake Victoria is one of Africa's great inland water systems, surrounded by land now within Uganda, Kenya, and Tanzania. Its shores, islands, rivers, and nearby forests create many different habitats. In portions of the northern and eastern basin, archaeologists have identified shell-rich deposits associated with pottery and fish remains. Some sites appear suited to repeated seasonal visits, including rapids where fish runs could be exploited. The persistence of pottery-making and fishing does not make a community static. People can specialize, exchange, move, and alter their routines while continuing familiar technologies.

Earlier accounts often pictured Kansyore communities as highly specialized fisher-foragers, and for some excavated localities that remains a useful description. But a description established on one shoreline cannot be carried unchanged around the entire lake. Western Uganda's Ndali Crater Lakes region, to the east of the Rwenzori Mountains, had different soils, forests, and settlement histories. Research published in 2024 by Peter Schmidt, Jonathan Walz, Jackline Besigye, and a large team argues that groups using Kansyore-related ceramics there engaged substantially in farming and herding alongside other resource gathering. The authors' regional reconstruction differs from the better-known lakeshore model in western Kenya.

How could archaeologists distinguish these histories? First by location and chronology. A surface pot can show decoration, but the ground layer in which it was found, materials beside it, and radiocarbon dates help establish when it was used. Then come animal bones, plant remains, traces of iron production, and changes in vegetation reconstructed from environmental evidence. Together these can show that people acted within a changing landscape. Each item has limits. A piece of cattle bone might come from someone's own herd or from food obtained through exchange. A pollen signal indicates vegetation change, but it may not identify exactly who cleared the land.

In western Kenya, work on sites such as Wadh Long'o and Gogo Falls has explored fishing, seasonal movement, and contacts with herders. At Wadh Long'o, interpretations of domestic animal remains suggest that acquiring livestock or animal products could be incorporated into older foraging lifeways without requiring every household to become a pastoralist. The dates and their associations are still matters of study, and this does not describe all Kansyore sites. It is one reason the notion of an abrupt replacement, with fishers departing as soon as herders arrived, has lost much of its simplicity.

A 2022 study by Mica Jones and Ruth Tibesasa revisited a difficult chronological gap in the northeastern lake record. Their new evidence from Namundiri A in eastern Uganda, considered with dates and fauna from western Kenya, supports a picture of changing use of lakeshore and riverine settings during a drier period several thousand years ago. The relationship between aridity and settlement should not be treated as a single cause that forced everyone into the same response. People may have changed fishing locations, relied on different parts of the watershed, or used networks outside the excavated area. The record is discontinuous, and the excavations cannot follow one family through every season.

The western Uganda research raises another historical problem: what happens when groups with different practices live near one another for centuries? The excavations in the Ndali Crater Lakes region recovered locally distinctive pottery, iron-age wares, and evidence of settlement activities across a long chronological span. The researchers argue that contacts among people making Kansyore ceramics and contemporaries associated with early ironworking reshaped practices in both directions. Ceramic traditions can overlap or borrow features, rather than lining up neatly in the order of an imagined migration map. This is an interpretation supported by multiple site contexts, not a direct label attached to each ancient household.

One striking case involves a pottery type called Boudiné, with substantial vessels and decorative features that have been hard to classify. At some excavated locations, large pots were associated with human burials, including an infant burial. Schmidt and colleagues connect elements of this tradition with what they term Transitional Urewe, proposing a fifth-century common-era context in the wider region. The funerary use of a pot is evidence of a purposeful social practice, even if the words said around the burial are lost. It also warns us against treating a ceramic category as if it recorded only a cooking style or a population replacement.

The study draws on historical linguistics to consider interaction among populations that may have spoken different languages. Linguistic reconstruction asks what related languages tell us about earlier vocabularies and movements; archaeology asks what material practices and dates survive at specific places. The two bodies of evidence can inform each other but do not align one-to-one. No decoration on a Kansyore vessel tells us the language its maker spoke. Nor can the appearance of a new ceramic style by itself prove that the earlier residents moved away. Such assertions would need additional grounds.

Environmental histories require the same care. The 2024 researchers ask how people contributed to a shift from forested conditions toward forest-and-savannah mosaics in parts of western Uganda. Farming, fire, grazing, and settlement may all affect vegetation, just as rainfall variations can change which activities are possible. A landscape that looks natural to a later observer might have been shaped by generations of choices. Yet we cannot assign every environmental change to one named community without adequate dated evidence. The question is not whether people had an influence, but how to identify its timing, scale, and mechanisms.

There is a human history in these technical distinctions. A fishing family did not need to surrender its expertise merely because cattle became available somewhere nearby. A farmer could eat fish. A maker of pottery could adopt a new vessel form from a neighbor while continuing older practices in other parts of life. Communities need not be pure representatives of an archaeological category. The names of their members are largely unknown to us, but their ability to combine resources, adapt to a changing lake, and create enduring objects remains visible.

The Kansyore record therefore changes the question we bring to early East African history. We should not ask only when food producers arrived, as though there were an empty landscape waiting for them. We should ask who already lived along the waters, what they knew, how resources were shared or contested, and how practices changed at different sites. A shell mound, a decorated bowl, a cow bone, and a pot burial answer different parts of those questions. None is a full history by itself. Together, critically dated, they make the lake's long human history more than a prologue to farming.

## Evidence and reading

- Peter R. Schmidt, Jonathan R. Walz, Jackline N. Besigye, and collaborators, **[Remaking the Late Holocene Environment of Western Uganda: Archaeological Perspectives on Kansyore and Later Settlers](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10437-024-09583-8)**, *African Archaeological Review* (2024). Open multidisciplinary study of Ndali Crater Lakes settlements, ceramic traditions, and environmental changes. Interpretations of language and social interaction are those of the authors.

Jones and Ruth Tibesasa, **[Kansyore fisher-hunter-gatherers abandoned the northeastern Lake Victoria shoreline during an arid period in the middle Holocene](https://www.arch.ox.ac.uk/publication/1232953/ora-hyrax)**, *Journal of African Archaeology* (2022). Institutional abstract and bibliographic record of the Namundiri A and neighboring-site chronological reassessment; a shoreline-use argument, not a region-wide disappearance.

Goldstein and colleagues, **[Cultural, economic, and settlement shifts over the last 9,000 years at Kakapel Rockshelter, Western Kenya](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0328805)**, *PLOS ONE* (2025). Open excavation and survey report; useful independent example of changed subsistence without complete discontinuity.

## Evidence boundary

The northern/eastern lakeshore seasonal-fishing model does not describe all Kansyore-related sites. The western Uganda research proposes different and more mixed food economies, with material and linguistic interpretations that remain model-dependent. Ceramic contact is not a one-to-one register of ethnic, linguistic, or genetic identity, and pollen records do not assign environmental change to a named settlement by themselves.
