# Gardens among the Stones: Rapa Nui before European Arrival

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

On Rapa Nui, an island in the southeastern Pacific, stone figures called moai stand among the most recognizable monuments in the world. It is easy to begin history with the largest statues and stop there, as if the people who made them existed chiefly to solve a puzzle about stone transport. But the island also preserves evidence of gardens, houses, quarries, and coastal activities. Every household had to secure food and maintain relations with neighbors. The stone figures belong to that working society. To understand Rapa Nui before Europeans arrived in 1722, we need to examine how people cultivated difficult land, organized labor, and kept their communities alive through changing conditions.

The first sustained Polynesian settlement of Rapa Nui is generally placed around the thirteenth century, although exact dating of early traces has long been debated. An influential review of radiocarbon measurements found that secure dates from short-lived material supported a relatively late settlement of eastern Polynesian islands. The choice of material matters. A piece of old charcoal may give an age that predates human burning, while a short-lived plant or a directly associated food remain may be a better indication of an occupation. The lesson is not that an ancient visitor's date has finally been discovered. It is that explanations of later monuments depend on first establishing when settled communities existed.

Rapa Nui's environment placed real constraints on cultivation. Soils, rainfall, wind, and available freshwater differ across the island. One important agricultural technique mixed or placed rock within growing plots, often called lithic mulch or rock gardening. Stone changes how soil holds moisture and experiences temperature, and may help protect crops under certain conditions. In the archaeological record, rocks moved into a field can look similar from above to naturally stony ground or material displaced by erosion. A field system therefore needs careful mapping and ground verification. The existence of rock gardens is not in doubt; their full extent and productive capacity have been disputed.

In 2024, Dylan Davis, Robert DiNapoli, Gina Pakarati, Terry Hunt, and Carl Lipo published a new island-wide assessment using shortwave-infrared satellite imagery and machine learning. They estimated that about three quarters of a square kilometer could be identified as rock gardening, much less land than earlier estimates had suggested. Their conclusion challenged demographic arguments that assumed vast gardens supplied a very large population before an abrupt collapse. Yet the measurement is an estimate produced by a particular classification method. It is not a complete inventory of all foods, nor an observed population count. Other sources of nutrition and varied land uses also mattered.

The famous collapse story has often presented the islanders as having destroyed their environment, run out of resources, and suffered social breakdown before European contact. Historical ecology is more difficult than that story allows. Forest change, soil use, bird populations, and the effort needed to construct monuments all deserve study. But even if people transformed vegetation, it does not follow automatically that their society suffered the severe population crash often imagined. The agricultural reappraisal does not deny human environmental impact. It questions whether the mapped food system supports the large pre-collapse population assumed in some models. That is a narrower and more testable conclusion.

A different body of evidence comes from ancient genomes. A 2024 study analyzed fifteen people whose remains were dated to periods between the seventeenth and twentieth centuries. The researchers tested demographic scenarios and found that the genetic data did not support a major population bottleneck in the sixteen hundreds of the kind required by the strongest collapse hypothesis. This does not establish an exact population series for every century. Effective population size is not the same as a headcount, and the samples come from a small set of individuals living long after first settlement. It does offer an independent challenge to a story that had sometimes been presented as certain.

The same genetic study reported ancestry connected to Indigenous populations of the Americas, with a modeled mixing date broadly between the thirteenth and fifteenth centuries. This matters because it is evidence for contact across a part of the Pacific often drawn as an impassable divide. Genetics, however, does not identify which crew sailed from which shore, where the people first met, or what goods and knowledge were exchanged. We cannot replace those missing details with a romantic story about a single first encounter. The conclusion is both substantial and limited: human relationships reached farther than the political maps of later European visitors might suggest.

The moai and their stone platforms are another form of evidence for sustained skill and organized labor. Quarrying, shaping, moving, and erecting large stone figures required planning and the practical knowledge of artisans and workers. But monument scale does not alone prove that one island-wide ruler directed every carving. A 2025 mapping study, requested and authorized by the Rapa Nui Indigenous community Maʻu Henua, identified thirty quarrying foci at Rano Raraku, with varying carving methods. The authors interpreted these patterns as consistent with coordination among multiple community groups rather than one overarching authority. This is a tested interpretation of workshops, not the name or political allegiance of every artisan. It is the relationship among sites, not just the height of a single statue, that can illuminate political life.

The people whose history is being studied are not an extinct puzzle population. Rapanui communities persisted through pre-European changes and faced grave later disruptions, including raids, disease, and colonial interventions. The 2024 genetic research included discussions with Rapanui representatives, who identified repatriation of ancestral remains as an important goal. Those later events cannot be erased by a tale that places the decisive catastrophe several centuries earlier. Nor does a critique of the collapse story require claiming that life on the island was always harmonious. Histories of disagreement, land use, cooperation, and adaptation are compatible with resilience. The difficult question is which changes we can demonstrate from particular evidence, and which are narratives imposed on the island to teach an outsider's moral lesson.

A more complete pre-1500 history therefore begins with arrival, turns to food systems and communities, and only then considers great monuments. The revised garden map and genomic data do not agree about every feature of ancient life because they answer different questions. Together they show why simple population and moral-collapse narratives require testing. Rapa Nui was an inhabited and changing society whose people practiced forms of agriculture adapted to an island landscape, raised monuments of enduring significance, and maintained connections not entirely contained by the horizon. The work of understanding this history belongs as much to evidence and Rapanui scholarship as to the statues that made the island internationally famous.

## Evidence boundary

The 2024 agricultural map estimates the area of detected rock gardens, not total food production or an exact precontact headcount. The 2024 genomic study samples fifteen people living centuries after initial settlement; its demographic simulations reject one severe-bottleneck model but do not prove the absence of past environmental damage or conflict. Admixture estimates identify contact, not the route or direction of a specific voyage.
