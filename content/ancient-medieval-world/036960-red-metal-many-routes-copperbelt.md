# Red Metal, Many Routes: Copper and Exchange in Central Africa

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

In the ground of the region now called the Copperbelt, across northern Zambia and the southern Democratic Republic of the Congo, lies a mineral resource with an immense later history. Modern mines can make its earlier past difficult to imagine. Yet long before industrial extraction, people mined, smelted, worked, and exchanged copper here. The archaeological record contains traces of small-scale production and objects that traveled far beyond the ore-bearing country. Among the most recognizable are pieces cast in cross-like shapes, which specialists call croisettes. Their story is one of skilled work and networks of exchange, not merely a rehearsal for modern mineral industries.

Copper is unlike iron in ways that change its history. Iron smelting typically produces a solid bloom that needs further working. Copper ores can, under suitable conditions, be reduced to metal that melts and can be cast into prepared forms. Ores, fuel, furnaces, molds, and the knowledge to control heat all mattered. The color of finished copper and the way it could be shaped made it attractive for ornaments and other objects. But an ancient copper bracelet tells us little on its own about where ore was dug. To reconstruct production, archaeologists also search for old mine workings, slag, crucibles, casting molds, and relationships between finished artifacts and known deposits.

David Phillipson's survey of the Zambian Copperbelt described the Chondwe ceramic group, identifying pottery at nineteen open sites and five rock shelters. That distribution provided evidence for widespread occupation in the region during the Early Iron Age. His research also argued that prehistoric copper mining had been undertaken there on a small scale by early iron-using communities. These conclusions were based on the surveys and collections then available. Chondwe is a ceramic and archaeological classification, not the name of a recorded dynasty, language community, or organization of miners. Copper production and pottery distribution need to be demonstrated at each site rather than assumed to coincide everywhere.

There is a long interval between early local extraction and the later wide circulation of standardized metal forms. In a 2017 study, Nicolas Nikis and Alexandre Livingstone Smith reported that production in the Copperbelt reaches back to roughly the fourth through seventh centuries of the common era, while extensive distribution is more clearly documented from around the ninth century. These dates describe categories of evidence from a broad region; they do not mean that one mine operated continuously for a thousand years. Workshops might open and cease, routes might shift, and users could melt down older copper into new shapes.

One particular form, often called the H I H type of croisette, circulated between approximately the ninth and fourteenth centuries. Cross-like ingots have been found over a region extending from the Copperbelt toward what is now Zimbabwe, including the area of Great Zimbabwe, and north toward the Upemba Depression. Researchers have also found evidence of manufacture in more than one location. This is decisive for how we interpret the map. A similar ingot found at two far-apart sites does not prove that people from those settlements traded directly with each other. Metal, molds, and forms could travel in stages; local specialists could cast a widely recognized shape using ore from a different source.

What was a cross-shaped ingot worth? The answer is not as simple as its weight. Some pieces might have served as raw material that could be cut or recast. Others occur in contexts suggesting display, status, or exchange. Later, some small and standardized examples may have functioned in transactions much like a currency, but that does not mean every earlier croisette was a coin with a fixed official value. We have no surviving price list from a ninth-century Copperbelt market to settle the question. Its shape, place of discovery, association with other objects, and period all bear on the inference.

The Upemba Depression offers evidence that value could change as copper entered a different social setting. In burials from the period archaeologists call Kabambian A, roughly the thirteenth to mid-fifteenth centuries, some large cross-shaped ingots accompanied the dead. Finds of copper in graves can register distinctions in wealth and ceremonial display; they do not establish the deceased person's precise office or the procedure by which the object was acquired. Nikis and Livingstone Smith emphasize that particular shapes cluster geographically and chronologically. A change in that distribution may indicate a reorganized trade route, a difference in political influence, a shift in demand, or some combination of these.

By the later medieval centuries, the pattern had changed. Different forms, labeled H X, H H, and H X R by archaeologists, appeared in areas that no longer shared precisely the same distributions. In the northern zone, smaller H-shaped objects became common in certain later funerary deposits. Some of their most striking archaeological patterns belong after 1500, beyond the main chronological limit of this series. We can discuss them briefly to make the earlier change intelligible, but should not pretend they were all features of the tenth century. An artifact typology is also a timeline; omitting the dates turns a long transformation into a static map.

Copper did not move through empty space. Goods went between places occupied by people who needed food, fuel, containers, and safe passage, and whose political arrangements were rarely recorded in writing. Production required access to ore and wood. Exchange could involve intermediaries far from the mine and other goods that leave few durable traces, such as cloth or dried foods. A copper object may survive while the basket, fishing net, or meal exchanged for it has vanished. The imbalance in the archaeological record makes metal seem more central to economic life than it necessarily was.

The geography of the Copperbelt and its connections also resists the idea that all ancient African trade was directed toward the Indian Ocean. Inland goods could move toward wetland communities, the Zambezi system, and southern plateaus, as well as through chains that ultimately connected to coastal exchange. Archaeologists compare metal chemistry, mold evidence, stratigraphy, and distribution maps. Even if a route linked two distant regions indirectly, the people along it were participants with their own economic purposes. A coast-to-interior diagram is only one view of a much larger network.

These findings raise political questions without yielding instant answers. Who controlled a mine? Could many households cast ingots, or did specialists work for wealthy patrons? Did changes in object distribution reflect political boundaries? Nikis and Livingstone Smith investigate the last question, arguing that some geographic patterns may mark shifting spheres of influence. But much of that argument is an interpretation from objects, not a treaty or tax record. We can recognize coordination and regional interdependence without naming the ruler of a particular furnace from a mold left in the soil.

The history of copper before 1500 is thus about both production and translation. Ore becomes metal; metal becomes an ingot; an ingot may become an ornament, an exchange medium, or a funerary offering. At each stage its use and meaning can change. The same shape crossing great distances need not carry a single price or meaning with it. Studying those differences allows the archaeological record of central Africa to speak about labor, connections, and emerging social distinctions without treating a line on a trade map as the complete history of everyone along it.

## Evidence boundary

Chondwe pottery is not a directly known copper-mining ethnic group. Not every copper object is proof of local production; nor does finding the same ingot form at two places prove face-to-face exchange. Earliest mining dates, long-distance distributions, later monetary uses, and suggested political spheres describe different time periods and levels of inference. Some forms became especially prominent after 1500, and are included only for chronological context.
