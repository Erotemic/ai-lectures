The temporal boundary between Deep Time and political history is not a universal date: agriculture, writing, urbanism, and state formation developed at different times in different parts of the world. Our approximate bridge is **3500 BCE–1500 CE**, not a claim that all regions entered the same historical stage together.

## Targeted coverage added in this installment

- **Central Asia:** 051000–052000 distinguish steppe pastoral empires from Sogdian urban and family histories. The Sogdian lecture names the ancient-language letters, their findspot, the modern translator Nicholas Sims-Williams and the limits of preserving one lost packet. Other Central Asian languages and periods still need treatment.
- **Indigenous North America:** 053000–054000 cover Cahokia's urban and agricultural networks and Chaco's regional great-house system. They explicitly distinguish archaeology, working hypotheses, and descendant community histories. Many North American regions remain unaddressed.
- **Indigenous Caribbean:** 055000 treats maritime movement, ceremonial landscapes, Indigenous arts, pre-contact genetics and the biases of early colonial writing. This is a case study, not a survey of every island.
- **African social and regional history:** 056000 tests the centralized-state assumption using Djenné-Jeno's excavated urban complex; 057000 considers Lake Chad and Kanem–Bornu using regional archaeological questions as well as the Arabic geographers. Neither substitutes for a full history of women, kinship, or rural eastern African polities.
- **Southeast Asian countryside:** 058000 investigates Angkor's dispersed settlement and agriculture through lidar, Khmer inscriptions, population models and excavation rather than monuments alone. Comparative work beyond the Khmer heartland remains needed.

The initial fifty-lecture chronological sequence is unchanged. The additional regional lectures are deep dives, not new chronological eras and not proof that each geographic region is now covered comprehensively.

## Second group of regional and documentary deep dives (059000–066000)

- **Great Lakes and Northeast:** 059000 distinguishes indigenous copperworking, changing lakeshores, fishery networks, and regionally different transitions toward maize and village life. 060000 separates physical longhouses, Haudenosaunee constitutional knowledge, Wendat archaeology, and the disputed academic chronology of confederation. These are cases, not a comprehensive map of living nations or their complete precolonial histories.
- **Northwest Coast:** 061000 connects Kwakwaka'wakw transmitted clam-garden knowledge with archaeological dating and recent fish-trap research; the age of one studied garden cannot be generalized to every community. Relations of access and authority require Indigenous historical interpretation.
- **Nubian families and women:** 062000 uses identified Old Nubian texts, modern translations, and Maryam's Arabic court dossier to examine legal capacity and property. The most visible actors are disproportionately those documented by institutions; legal capacity cannot be extrapolated equally to every person.
- **Swahili households:** 063000 examines Songo Mnara's rooms, open spaces, plants, coins, and foodways through specialist site archaeology, distinct from the broader port-and-trade narrative in 036000.
- **South and Southeast Asian local systems:** 064000 investigates Bagan's donations, inscriptions, labor and landscape; 065000 examines Java's religious monuments, sima land/tax-status texts and Trowulan settlement remains. Archaeological and literary sources are kept distinct.
- **Central Asian documentary life:** 066000 follows secular and religious texts in Turfan, separating manuscript languages, difficult dates, and the provenance of modern collections. This does not replace region-wide histories of other Central Asian societies.

The new content does not claim equal research depth across periods: some evidence is based on public abstracts of specialist books and articles, while other references give accessible primary translations or full peer-reviewed methods and field results. Source records identify these differences. For future deep dives, prioritize material where direct field reports or primary-language editions can be read and checked in full.

## Cross-cutting deficits to investigate

**History of ordinary people.** Household organization, farmers' and craftspeople's work, bondage and unfreedom, gender and family, migration and displaced populations should be part of political history rather than appended after rulers and battles.

**Evidence and attribution.** Identify whose voices survive and why: funerary inscriptions, tax documents, missionary and traveler accounts, excavated settlement patterns, oral traditions, literary works, translated chronicles. Distinguish original evidence, later reconstruction, and modern historical debates. Where available, add original discoverers, document editors, translators, and field archaeologists to source records rather than attributing findings only to a museum or book publisher.

**Regional balance without quotas.** Add the regions required to explain consequential historical developments, including Africa, South and Southeast Asia, East Asia, Central Asia, the Americas, and the Pacific. Do not treat Europe as the default setting or assume contact where none is established.

**Chronological intersections.** Readers should understand which developments occurred simultaneously and which were causally connected. Parallel chronology is not the same as a single unified global economy.

**Existing material should be reused conceptually.** Where the World History collection already contains a full lecture on Gilgamesh, Fu Hao, Hatshepsut, Mansa Musa, or early archaeological evidence, a bridging lecture should give the necessary historical setting and refer readers to the specialist work rather than duplicate the whole account.

**Specific source limitations:** Monte Albán glyphs are only partially understood, and monuments overstate rulers' viewpoint. ## Eleventh regional-depth campaign — East African early settlement (November 2026)

The pack now has **132 indexed lectures**: the 50 introductory chronological units and 82 inserted regional, material, institutional, and documentary studies. The five new entries `036300`–`036700` clarify the relationships of early East African coastal towns to smaller villages, household production, inland material traditions, changing Muslim institutions, and southern routes. ## Twelfth regional-depth campaign — East African food systems and inland landscapes (November 2026)

**138 indexed lectures** now include six new material-history studies (`036800`–`036850`). Unlike the previously added coastal-household and commerce lectures, these examine the movement and maintenance of herds, regional fisher-forager economies, preserved crops, craft specialization and the infrastructure of field construction. All existing 132 manuscript texts and ID mappings remain unchanged.

**Evidence and method limits:** Lothagam's 580-plus burial estimate does not mean every grave was excavated or prove absolute social equality. Luxmanda's 2026 plant residues document plant processing rather than domesticated cereal crops; only the publisher abstract/highlights were available for this study. Kansyore and Boudiné pottery do not identify a single language or homogenous economy. Kakapel's cowpea and later grain dates are dates of excavated remains, not necessarily regional first introductions; the early pea is not directly dated. Iron technologies at Kasigau date specific deposits, not individual undated terraces; ivory-hunting interpretations are attributed to their authors. Engaruka's terrace landscape spans later 14th/15th to at least 18th century and must not be compressed to pre-1500; alleged environmental mismanagement is not established by surviving empty fields.

The next depth campaign should be anchored in peer-reviewed site archaeology for East African early agriculture, particularly where ceramics are frequently used as shorthand for speech, livelihood and identity.

## Thirteenth regional-depth campaign — Great Lakes ceramics, metallurgy, contact and power (November 2026)

**144 indexed lectures** now include six new studies (`036860`–`036910`) that connect first-millennium Urewe material cultures and regional iron-smelting evidence to later Great Lakes settlements, while keeping archaeological categories distinct from linguistic and genetic history. The six manuscripts are new text only; no binary assets or server changes.

**Research limits:** The 2026 Mara paper is a preliminary survey with incomplete absolute dating and a disputed isolated ceramic sherd. Ceri Ashley's ceramic-to-authority interpretation is a hypothesis; 2007 Lane and 2022 Kyazike are accessible primarily by abstract. Craddock et al. verify slag phases but do not establish purposeful addition of flux from the abstract alone. ## Fourteenth regional-depth campaign — Tanganyika to the Upemba wetlands (November 2026)

The track contains **150 indexed lectures** with six new source-mapped manuscripts (`036920`–`036970`). Their 68 spoken paragraphs each have a non-Wikipedia source registry reference and individualized evidence limitation. The new work targets first- and second-millennium Malawi, Zambia, and southern DRC, without equating archaeological ceramic groups with language communities or speaking for unrecorded families.

**Important evidence boundaries:** Kalambo Falls has multiple highly separated occupational phases; 2023 wood finds at ~476,000 years are not part of early first-millennium CE ironworking. Phillipson's Nakapapula painting date is associative, not a direct pigment date, and the full stratigraphy is not newly analyzed. Nkope/Kapeni/Namaso sequence uses publisher chapter/extract and Namaso abstract; lake-level change is contextual, not a sole cause. Zambia's Muteteshi sorghum was documented in earlier archaeology (reported by Goldstein 2021), whereas the four collagen samples examined by Janzen et al. Chondwe copper mining is publisher-abstract evidence and is not proof of state control. HIH, HH, HX and HXR ingot types have distinct time ranges; currency and political-boundary interpretations are hypotheses. Upemba's nearly three hundred graves document funerary action, not a domestic census; isotope differences are not crop-species identification; later Luba government is not directly attested by early Kisalian deposits.
