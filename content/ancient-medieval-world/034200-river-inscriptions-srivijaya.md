# A Kingdom in the River Mouths: Srivijaya, Oaths, and the Straits

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

Imagine standing beside a tidal river in southern Sumatra, where fresh water meets a route into the South China Sea. Boats bring people and goods from inland waterways, neighboring islands, and foreign ports. Somewhere in this landscape, an authority orders words cut into stone. A king records a journey; another inscription invokes the welfare of living beings; other texts warn against disloyalty. These records are among our earliest direct witnesses to a political world commonly called Srivijaya. They reveal movement, ambition, and religious patronage, but they do not draw us a simple map of an empire.

The earliest Old Malay inscriptions associated with this polity are concentrated around the late seventh century. One, found at Kedukan Bukit near Palembang, dates to the 680s of the Common Era. Specialists have differed in converting its date and in interpreting the movement it commemorates. The inscription connects a ruler known by the title Dapunta Hyang with a journey, a force of followers, and the success of a siddhayatra, often rendered as an auspicious or successful expedition. Its language is ceremonial. It cannot be read as a neutral ship's log recording every day of an expedition.

Palembang is a useful geographic anchor, but it was not an inland capital isolated from the world. Tidal rivers connected its neighborhood to forests, wetlands, inhabited settlements, and maritime passages. From the straits between Sumatra and the Malay Peninsula, vessels could move between the Indian Ocean and the seas leading toward China. That position made exchange important, but location alone does not create a political system. People must load ships, maintain river access, negotiate payments, secure alliances, and move provisions. Control over a harbor also differs from control over all the land behind it.

Another inscription, Talang Tuwo, dated 684, invokes a garden or park and the benefits its creation should bring. Its vocabulary has been read in connection with Buddhist ideas of merit and well-being. A royal religious aspiration is not evidence that everyone enjoyed the prosperity being requested. Yet it matters that public authority could represent itself through a project associated with living beings, plants, and spiritual merit, not only through conquest. The inscription shows how a ruler wanted an action understood. Archaeology must then ask what the physical setting and labor behind such a project might have been.

Other inscriptions take a harsher tone. The Telaga Batu oath inscription, generally assigned to this early world though not securely dated to a single year, is associated with curses on people who betrayed obligations to authority. Scholars discuss its wording in relation to a range of officeholders and dependents. An oath is evidence of a political relationship being demanded, not proof that obedience was universal. Indeed, the need to impose an oath suggests the possibility of dissent. Royal declarations expose the anxieties of government as well as its ideology.

The Kota Kapur inscription, found on Bangka Island and dated 686, extends the picture beyond one river system. It invokes Srivijaya and an expedition or hostile action against a place written in a form often translated as the land of Java. Specialists have debated what territory that expression designated. We should not turn one damaged or difficult phrase into a claim that a modern island's entire population was conquered. What we can say is that authorities associated with Srivijaya tried to project power across watery distances, and that their language framed this power as politically consequential.

A Chinese Buddhist traveler known as Yijing visited this region in the late seventh century while making journeys between China and India. His writing is often invoked because he describes the value of studying Buddhist learning and language in the maritime world before proceeding to Indian centers. A traveler's account has a different purpose from a royal inscription. Yijing wanted to help religious students make practical choices. He was not compiling a complete tax register of Sumatra. Read together, the sources suggest that Buddhism connected local patronage to circuits of scholarship and travel that crossed many political boundaries.

Religious movement was not a one-way flow from India to passive recipients. Buddhist teachings, Sanskrit terminology, and ritual practices were adapted in places whose inhabitants spoke multiple languages and participated in their own political traditions. Old Malay on stone is part of that story. The use of South Indian-related scripts and Sanskrit religious vocabulary did not require the governing society to be a branch office of an Indian kingdom. A borrowed form could become a locally authoritative document. The inscriptions help us see a maritime culture producing records for its own purposes.

The terms empire and thalassocracy, or sea power, can both be useful and misleading. Srivijaya appears in the evidence as a network of centers capable of assembling followers, honoring Buddhist institutions, and pressuring rivals. It does not appear as a modern territorial state with uniform boundaries and an administration present in every village. The surviving record comes from select places and moments. A river settlement might be allied one decade, resistant the next, and economically important without being politically subordinate at all times. Historians must reconstruct changing relationships, rather than color the same large area on a map for centuries.

Consider commerce from the perspective of producers rather than rulers. Traders could seek forest products, aromatic resins, metals, textiles, ceramics, and food. Each commodity had a different route and different workers behind it. Some goods passed through several hands before reaching an ocean-going vessel. The people who collected resin or grew rice did not necessarily share the language of a court inscription. Neither trade nor taxation could function without their labor, yet they are harder to locate in the surviving texts. Material remains and environmental histories are therefore essential companions to royal declarations.

During the following centuries, southern Sumatra and neighboring regions participated in changing trade and political relationships. Contacts with Chinese dynasties, Indian Ocean merchants, and Southeast Asian polities were not constant in scale or direction. In 1025, a Chola expedition from South India attacked centers associated with Srivijaya, according to inscriptions that themselves celebrate Chola achievements. It was a major episode of interregional violence, but a victory claim is not proof that the entire maritime network ceased to exist. Ports, ruling families, merchants, and religious communities did not all necessarily share the same fate.

There are also significant archaeological questions. Large settlements in humid river environments may leave fewer surviving stone monuments than some inland temple regions. The absence of a surviving palace complex does not mean political life was absent. Excavations, ceramics, settlement patterns, inscriptions, and evidence from places such as the broader Jambi region provide different angles on the history, but linking each find to a fixed seventh-century administrative system requires argument. Some identifications remain disputed. Later names and later states cannot be projected backward without checking the chronology.

Why, then, do the river inscriptions matter so much? They show local power expressing itself in words that could be seen, recited, feared, or remembered. One inscription celebrates a journey; another imagines benefit for living things; another binds people to authority. These are not three separate civilizations. They are different claims within a connected political and religious world. Their coexistence warns us not to describe Buddhist patronage as proof of gentle government or military organization as proof of uninterrupted conquest.

Srivijaya helps us understand an important alternative to history organized around land capitals. Some states built their power by cultivating relations among river mouths, ports, ships, inland producers, foreign pilgrims, and neighboring rulers. Their boundaries were difficult to draw because their relationships were constantly being made and renegotiated. The stones do not give a finished map. They give us a place to begin asking who could make an oath, who could enforce it, whose labor sustained a harbor, and how authority traveled with people across water.
