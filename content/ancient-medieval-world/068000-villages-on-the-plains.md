# The Plains before the Horse: Bison, Fields, and Earthlodge Villages

Picture the northern Plains long before horses became central to the histories most often shown in paintings and films. Along the Missouri River, families lived in substantial settlements, grew crops, stored harvests, traded stone and food, and traveled onto the grasslands for bison. The contrast between a stationary farmer and a wandering hunter does not describe these societies. Their knowledge lay partly in combining ways of life across environments and seasons. The archaeology of the Middle Missouri, together with Mandan, Hidatsa, and Arikara histories, lets us examine a Plains world with long continuities and substantial changes before 1500.

The Missouri River offered something the open prairie could not always provide: sheltered bottomlands, reliable water, wood, fertile soils, and transport routes. Houses could stand on terraces above spring floods while fields occupied lower ground. Beyond the river lay grasslands where large animals moved seasonally. Settlements needed people able to work across these spaces. The same family community might cultivate crops, prepare stores for winter, join a hunting party, and travel to meet trading partners. That flexibility was a sophisticated economic system, not a transitional stage toward another way of life.

Archaeologists use the term Plains Village tradition for several related settlement and farming patterns in the region. The Middle Missouri tradition, beginning around the first millennium CE and developing over subsequent centuries, included earth-sheltered houses, sometimes defended villages, cultivation, and bison hunting. Houses in some earlier Middle Missouri settlements were rectangular and partly dug into the ground; round earthlodges became especially prominent in later communities. We should not collapse every excavated dwelling into the round structures familiar from nineteenth-century descriptions. Building forms changed, as did the locations and political connections of settlements.

What does a house reveal? A depression in the earth can mark a floor; postholes can record where timber supported a roof; hearths, storage pits, broken pottery, and discarded bone can show repeated activity. A village may preserve patterns of rebuilding after fire or abandonment. Rows of houses, a ditch, and a stockade can tell us that residents invested in protecting a place. But these finds do not reconstruct every kinship rule. To understand social relationships, archaeologists compare the houses with burials, food remains, tools, and the oral histories of communities whose ancestors lived along the river.

Later Hidatsa villages near the Knife River offer a particularly well-described continuation of riverine farming. The National Park Service describes women cultivating maize, beans, squash, and sunflowers in bottomlands, and households occupying substantial earthlodges above them. Among Hidatsa communities documented in the contact era, women held major responsibilities for earthlodge construction and ownership. These details are important, but their documentation is often considerably later than the earliest Plains Village sites. It would be a mistake to assign every seventeenth- or nineteenth-century practice, unchanged, to a settlement in the year 1000.

The stored harvest was a form of resilience. Maize could be dried, carried, and kept against winter shortages. It also involved risk: late frost, drought, floods, or crop loss could strain a community. Hunting bison was another way to obtain food and raw materials, but large animals moved, and a hunt could fail. The combination of river farming and prairie hunting spread some risks across different sources. Archaeological remains show bones and plant traces; a particular drought's effect on a named village may be harder to establish. Environmental pressure mattered, but communities responded through movement, alliances, storage, and changes in settlement.

Bison provided more than meat. Hides could be worked for clothing and shelter; bone and horn supplied tools; fat and other products could be preserved or used in many ways. Hunting required planning and coordination, whether by small parties or larger groups. In the long record of the Plains there were many strategies for approaching and killing bison before the introduction of horses. To describe these people as incapable of extensive prairie travel without mounted transport would be to confuse one historically specific technology with the much older knowledge of the land.

Likewise, maize and other domestic crops did not appear on the Plains in a single wave that transformed everyone in the same way. Agricultural traditions were adopted, adapted, and sometimes reduced according to local ecology and social choices. Different river valleys supported different settlements. Some communities placed more emphasis on farming, others on mobility or particular game resources. Exchange linked them. Knife River flint, for example, was valued far beyond its source area. A stone artifact recovered far away can show a network of movement, even when it cannot tell whether the stone passed through two hands or twenty.

The words Mandan, Hidatsa, and Arikara name living peoples with distinct histories. They should not be treated as three labels for the same archaeological culture. National Park Service accounts combine the archaeological record with the groups' migration and origin traditions. The historical villages preserved at Knife River are mainly from after 1500, so they are valuable for exploring later forms and for understanding continuities, but cannot directly document every detail of the preceding centuries. Researchers distinguish earlier possible ancestors and regional movements from later settlements whose residents are more securely identified.

Political life had to operate at more than one scale. A household organized fieldwork and food storage. A village coordinated defenses, ceremonial activity, and relations with neighbors. Communities negotiated access to river bottoms and hunting territories, and carried obligations to relatives elsewhere. Conflict was possible; so were alliances and exchange. Archaeological ditches and burned buildings may point to danger, but rarely identify the parties involved. A later oral tradition may help explain a migration without supplying the exact calendar date of every archaeological episode. Political history here is made of local choices and changing connections, not only kings and treaties.

Try to follow a single seed through this system. Someone saves it from a previous harvest, plants it in ground that must be cleared and tended, protects it from animals and weather, then dries the mature ear and reserves part for another year. Other grains may be traded or served at gatherings. An archaeologist finds a charred kernel, perhaps preserved after a house fire, and uses its context to reconstruct part of that work. The kernel can be dated and identified. It cannot say who made the decision to save it, what that person hoped for, or what family rule governed the storehouse. The material remains let us ask better questions without turning reconstruction into fiction.

After 1500, changes intensified. Epidemics, new trade goods, shifting military relations, the spread of horses among some Plains societies, and European and later United States expansion radically altered regional life. In the nineteenth century the Mandan, Hidatsa, and Arikara suffered devastating disease and dispossession. Those events belong to their longer history, but they should not be projected backward as the defining features of the medieval Plains. The farming communities of the earlier centuries had their own opportunities, conflicts, and ways of making a living.

Much of the popular image of the Plains is drawn from the horse era, which covers only a part of its history. Before that period, the grasslands and rivers supported multiple connected economies: hunters, farmers, traders, and people whose activities crossed those categories. Villages were not signs that Plains peoples had stopped moving, and hunting did not mean that they lacked agriculture. To understand the region before 1500, begin with a river terrace, a store of maize, an earth-sheltered home, and a path out onto the prairie. Together they give us a more accurate world than either stereotype alone.
