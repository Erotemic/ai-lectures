# At the Falls Again: Kalambo and the Many Ages of a Place

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

At Kalambo Falls, close to the southern end of Lake Tanganyika, the landscape has preserved traces of human and earlier hominin activity separated by astonishing intervals of time. A river runs toward a waterfall, and the same broad basin has been visited, abandoned, and used again under changing conditions. In 2023, researchers announced a wooden structure at least 476,000 years old. But our subject is a much younger set of occupations, within the last two thousand years, when people used iron and made pottery in the same general landscape. The two histories belong to Kalambo. They must not be mistaken for one continuous settlement.

The difficulty is partly a gift of preservation. The Kalambo River deposited layers of sand, gravel, silt, and sometimes waterlogged material. People who worked near the river in widely different ages left things that could eventually become buried. Excavations directed by J. Desmond Clark in the mid-twentieth century made Kalambo central to the archaeological history of Africa. Later investigators reexamined some of the old interpretations by studying how water moved deposits and whether an object was still in the layer where it had originally been left. An excavation can expose a sequence, but a sequence is not automatically a story of uninterrupted inheritance from the oldest layer to the youngest.

By the first millennium of the common era, communities in what is now northern Zambia were making vessels and using iron in settings that archaeologists classify as Early Iron Age. David Phillipson distinguished a Kalambo group of pottery among several regional Zambian traditions. The categories depend principally on combinations of vessel form and decoration found at related sites, and their dates rest on the circumstances of excavation and radiocarbon samples. The phrase Kalambo group does not name an ancient nation. It is a way of comparing finds at the falls with fragments elsewhere in the woodland and along approaches to the lake.

For the occupations at Kalambo Falls themselves, older archaeological syntheses placed much of the relevant Iron Age sequence between approximately the fourth and eleventh or twelfth centuries of the common era. Those centuries overlap but are not identical with the time ranges sometimes given for the wider ceramic group. Some sherds came from open sites, some from shelters, and some from disturbed deposits. A date obtained from charcoal in a layer may support a phase of occupation; it cannot certify that every pottery fragment found near it was created at that moment. The absence of a secure sample in another layer is not proof that no one lived there.

Think of what the word settlement might mean under these conditions. A family could return repeatedly to a place where there was water, fishing, plants, or access to paths across the escarpment. Iron implements required ore, fuel, and labor somewhere in the regional economy; the mere presence of a finished iron blade does not prove that the metal was smelted beside the house where it was found. Pottery could be used to heat food, store water, or carry supplies, but most broken pieces cannot reveal precisely which task they performed. We can identify technological possibilities and archaeological patterns without inventing a single village plan that has not been excavated.

The larger region complicates a simple account of migration. Archaeologists have identified several different early pottery groupings in Zambia: Kalambo in the north, Chondwe farther toward the Copperbelt, and still others nearer Lusaka or the southern plateau. Similarities among their artifacts suggest contacts and historical relationships, but differences in manufacture may reflect local choices or encounters rather than successive ethnic frontiers. In a 1968 article, Phillipson explicitly warned that pottery and linguistic arguments could not yet be linked directly to prove which languages these communities spoke. That warning continues to matter. A collection of pots cannot be translated as a sentence in an ancient language.

There are clues about work and diet, but they vary in strength. Grinding equipment can indicate that people processed plant foods. It does not, without seeds, phytoliths, or other corroborating evidence, establish that they cultivated particular cereals. Ironworking debris can show operations that a finished tool alone cannot. Animal remains, when preserved and identified securely, might distinguish fishing, hunting, or herding. The absence of a particular object in one excavated area can reflect preservation, sampling, and ancient choices as well as genuine absence. Kalambo should not be made to stand for a standard package of iron, farming, cattle, and village life that necessarily traveled together.

The 2023 wooden-structure discovery is worth recalling for a methodological reason. It came from deep deposits studied with luminescence dating of sediments; the reported construction predates our species' best-established fossils. The Early Iron Age occupations were investigated through a quite different combination of ceramic comparisons, associated material, and radiocarbon chronology. Researchers may work at the same named site while answering questions separated by hundreds of thousands of years. They must keep findspots, dating methods, and stratigraphic relations separate. An old picture caption or a map pin cannot do that work for them.

The historical importance of this location is therefore not that a single people lived beside the waterfall for half a million years. Kalambo is a place repeatedly incorporated into changing forms of life. In one time people shaped wood with large stone tools; at a much later time pottery and iron became part of the local archaeological record. Between those episodes lie changes in environment and technology that require many independent lines of evidence. A deep place history offers several stories, not one ancestor list.

Seen from northern Zambia, the spread of iron and pottery is not simply the last leg of an arrow drawn south from the Great Lakes. The evidence includes different regional traditions, varied sites, and a long survival of stone technologies in neighboring places. People moved, ideas traveled, and objects were exchanged; archaeology alone seldom permits us to count those movements separately. What can be demonstrated is a landscape of overlapping practices across many centuries. Kalambo teaches us to distinguish the history of a place from the history of one community, and the evidence for a technology from an imagined biography of everyone who used it.

## Evidence boundary

Kalambo's approximately 476,000-year-old wood is not evidence of continuous habitation up to the first-millennium Iron Age. Early Iron Age dates and ceramic group definitions are reconstructed from uneven contexts; objects are not direct evidence for languages, exact domestic organization, or a single migrating population. The older survey and Cambridge synthesis are historical scholarship, not the latest excavated site-by-site chronology.
