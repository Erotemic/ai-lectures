# What the Slag Remembers: Early Iron Furnaces in Rwanda and Burundi

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

A furnace disappears differently from a house. Its clay walls may collapse, its fuel burn away, and the iron made inside it leave the site in someone's hands. Yet the process also creates stubborn waste: lumps and masses of slag, the stony material produced as ore, fuel, and furnace lining react at high heat. Archaeologists studying early ironworking in Rwanda and Burundi can sometimes reconstruct a technical operation from those discarded remains. They are not reading a smith's instruction manual. They are solving an old physical problem with traces that the heat itself helped preserve.

Iron in the ground is usually not a ready-made metal blade. Much of it is chemically bound in ore. A smelter wants to separate enough iron from those compounds to obtain a workable, porous mass often called a bloom. Charcoal supplies heat and helps create the reducing conditions needed for this transformation. Air must reach the fire, sometimes through clay pipes called tuyeres. Temperature, the chemistry of the ore, the furnace wall, and the movement of air all matter. Too little heat or badly controlled reactions can leave much of the iron in the waste. A bloom is then repeatedly worked to expel trapped slag and consolidate metal.

In parts of the African Great Lakes region, early smelting remains include so-called slag-pit furnaces. A pit beneath the working chamber collected molten or semi-molten waste while the furnace operated. Researchers have recovered pieces of clay structure, tuyeres, charcoal, and the slags themselves. These fragments can reveal that high-temperature smelting occurred at a particular location. They do not, by themselves, give us the name of a workshop owner, prove how many people attended the fire, or establish that all communities who used related pottery possessed exactly the same metallurgical knowledge.

Paul Craddock, Ian Freestone, Andrew Middleton, and Marie-Claude Van Grunderbeek studied early smelting debris from Rwanda and Burundi in research published in *Historical Metallurgy*. By examining the chemical and mineral makeup of slag and surviving furnace clay, they could ask what reactions had taken place. Their analysis identified minerals including fayalite, hercynite, and wustite in the waste. Those unfamiliar names point to a familiar principle: heated materials do not merely melt and separate. They react, and the products can retain a chemical history of what entered the furnace.

One of their findings complicates the assumption that a successful furnace required some extraordinarily fireproof clay. The preserved furnace and tuyere clays were not particularly refractory, meaning they were not exceptionally resistant to high temperatures. The slag composition suggested either that clay was deliberately added as a flux, a material helping unwanted components combine and separate, or that significant clay entered the slag from the furnace structure itself. Those alternatives are not the same. In one, the operator chooses a material for a specific effect. In the other, the furnace lining contributes as the process runs. The surviving slag cannot always tell which occurred.

There were also differences among slag specimens, but the researchers argued that modest changes in composition could account for important-looking variations in mineral makeup. This is a warning against turning each mineralogical difference into a different "people" or an invented revolution in technique. Laboratory analysis can narrow how a furnace worked, and can sometimes reveal the limits of a proposed reconstruction. It rarely turns a fragment of waste into a complete account of workshop organization, ritual authority, commercial demand, or the social status of the smelter.

Dating these early operations requires a different chain of reasoning. Charcoal from a securely excavated smelting context can be measured by radiocarbon techniques, but the carbon date may still carry uncertainty about calibration and association. Did the charcoal belong to the smelting episode? Could old wood have been burned? Was the context disturbed? Archaeologists have reported ironworking in East Africa during the first millennium before the common era, but proposed very early dates and grand theories about the first invention of iron have long attracted scrutiny. Establishing that a furnace operated at one place is not the same task as proving that iron smelting was invented there before anywhere else.

Urewe pottery is often discussed alongside this early technology. In Rwanda and Burundi, the histories of ceramics, settlement, agriculture, and furnaces overlap in important ways. But the things we find can travel through different channels. Someone might make a pot without operating a furnace; an iron blade might pass from a smelter to a farming household; an apprentice might learn the craft from a neighbor whose language differed. The connections between potters, smelters, and cultivators are historical questions requiring evidence, not consequences automatically supplied by the label "Early Iron Age."

What might it have taken to operate such a furnace? Ore had to be obtained and prepared; fuel gathered and burned to charcoal; clay formed into a structure that could stand long enough to perform; air supplied; the bloom removed and worked. These are descriptions of the technical tasks, not scenes witnessed by archaeologists at a particular first-millennium workshop. Each task also had a landscape cost and a possible social organization. Did the fuel come from nearby woodland? Was the work seasonal? Was the iron used close to home or exchanged? We can investigate those matters with charcoal analysis, settlement archaeology, tool distributions, and comparative ethnography. Slag alone does not answer them all.

The distinction between smelting and forging is worth keeping. Smelting transforms ore into metal. Forging reshapes an existing piece of iron by heating and hammering it. A site with iron objects is therefore not automatically a smelting site. A site with smithing debris need not have produced its iron from ore. These stages may have occurred in different places and involved different workers. Archaeological maps of "iron production" become more informative when each excavated operation is identified separately.

Early furnaces in Rwanda and Burundi demonstrate technical knowledge that is part of the region's own archaeological history. Their evidence is more substantial than an old claim that iron technology must have arrived from some distant empire, but it also does not license a new myth in which the first furnace can be assigned a single heroic inventor. What survives is a chain of material decisions, reconstructed by excavators and analysts who can test some possibilities and leave others open. The waste of a furnace can reveal chemistry, craftsmanship, and chronology. Understanding the people around that furnace requires asking the wider historical questions as carefully as the metallurgical ones.

## Evidence boundary

The study's accessible journal abstract identifies mineral phases and alternative explanations of clay contributions; it does not prove deliberate fluxing by every smelter. Early radiocarbon ages need secure contexts and calibration. Iron objects, forging, and ore smelting are distinct archaeological evidence classes. The question of iron metallurgy's first invention in Africa remains separate from these confirmed Great Lakes production sites.
