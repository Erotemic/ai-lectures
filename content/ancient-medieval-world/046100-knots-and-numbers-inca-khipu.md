# Counting without a Page: Khipu and Knowledge in the Andes

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

Imagine being responsible for a storehouse. Bags of maize arrive from one community; cloth is issued to another; a group of workers is assigned to repair a road. A record is needed, but it may not take the form of ink on paper. In the Andes, people developed systems of knotted cords that could preserve numerical and perhaps other kinds of information. These devices are usually called khipu, or quipu, from a Quechua term associated with knots. They offer a different route into the history of knowledge: what happens when a record can be tied, carried, checked, and interpreted, but cannot simply be read like a modern letter?

A khipu may have a main cord, with many hanging cords, and sometimes further branches. Its features can include the placement and type of knots, the color of thread, the direction of twist, and the way cords are attached. Some arrangements are plainly systematic. The Inca used many such devices to support government and accounting across the Andes. But the material form matters. A cord can be damaged, separated, or rehung. A surviving object may once have belonged to a larger set or been accompanied by a spoken explanation that no longer survives.

The best-understood Inca khipu records use a decimal place-value system. Knot forms and positions along a pendant cord can represent ones, tens, hundreds, and higher units. This is not merely decorative stringwork. It shows deliberate representation of quantity, with rules that permit different amounts to be recorded and compared. Modern researchers can often reconstruct numerical values, but a number without a label is an incomplete historical claim. Knowing that a cord records seventy-four does not necessarily tell us whether it counted sacks, households, labor days, or animals. Context and relationships among cords matter as much as arithmetic.

Inca administration did not operate in isolation from local communities. The imperial state required labor, provisions, transport, and information from territories incorporated through different means. Officials needed ways to organize obligations and compare reports. Khipu belonged in a world of roads, storage buildings, local authorities, and political coercion as well as skilled recordkeeping. A numerical device can serve people responsible for distributing food, but it can also make taxes and labor demands legible to rulers. Technical ingenuity and unequal power are not opposites.

Researchers Gary Urton and Carrie Brezine studied a group of twenty-one khipu associated with Puruchuco, an Inca administrative center on Peru's central coast. Their 2005 analysis argued that relationships among the records could reflect levels of aggregation, as information moved upward within an administrative system. That is a concrete way to test an idea about bureaucracy without having a Spanish-language translation of the cords. If the same quantities are combined or reorganized across several objects, the pattern may reveal the work records performed. Yet interpretation still depends on assumptions about what the groups and repeated values represent.

The work also raises a human question. Who knew how to tie and interpret these records? Colonial-era descriptions refer to specialists often known as khipukamayuq, people trained in making and reading khipu. Such testimony is important, but it was recorded after Spanish conquest under changed political conditions. We cannot assume every recorded job title or procedure worked identically in every Inca province before the conquest. We can, however, infer that specialized knowledge was needed to maintain consistent systems across distances. Records alone do not teach themselves to every passerby.

Khipu traditions were older than the Inca Empire. Researchers have identified cord-recording artifacts associated with the Wari or Huari Middle Horizon centuries before the great Inca expansion. Some early examples show patterns that differ from the later decimal system, including interpretations of groups of five. A study of Middle Horizon specimens used radiocarbon dating to clarify part of this history. The result does not prove that the Inca copied one entire system unchanged. It demonstrates a longer Andean record of experimenting with cords as durable carriers of structured information.

That history matters because the word Inca can hide generations of earlier Andean work. Techniques of farming, road building, textile production, and recordkeeping developed in multiple communities and political systems. The imperial government drew upon existing capacities while reorganizing them for its own purposes. When a khipu was made, its fiber had to be gathered or produced, spun, dyed or left undyed, assembled, and knotted. The cord is an administrative instrument and a product of material labor. Its history begins before a keeper makes the final knot.

Could khipu preserve stories rather than only numbers? This remains a major research question. Colonial writers reported uses that went beyond straightforward tallies, and some surviving devices have structures not readily reducible to decimal accounting. Researchers study color, knot direction, attachment, and sequence as possible bearers of categories or more complex information. No single accepted decipherment turns all known khipu into readable sentences. It would be as misleading to claim that every knot is a word as to insist that only numbers could ever have been remembered with cords.

The language of writing can itself mislead us. If writing is defined narrowly as marks that represent the sounds of words, a khipu's status may be debated. If the question is how a society stores and communicates structured knowledge across time and distance, the devices plainly belong in the conversation. Historians need not force them into the exact categories invented for alphabetic or logographic scripts. A cord record may work partly through physical arrangement and partly through training, speech, and context. This is not a lesser form of knowledge merely because its decoding remains difficult for modern researchers.

Conquest damaged the systems within which these records made sense. Spanish administrators sometimes tried to use khipu specialists' information while imposing new accounting practices and institutions. Other colonial policies and social disruptions undermined the transmission of knowledge. A surviving cord might later be collected as a curiosity, losing information about the storehouse, person, or community where it had functioned. Museum collections have preserved many objects, but acquisition can separate material records from the networks of interpretation that once gave them meaning. The history of collection is part of the history of what we can now read.

Living Andean communities have also maintained diverse forms of knotting and record-related practice, although continuity must be investigated rather than assumed to be identical everywhere. A contemporary practice cannot automatically supply the key to a particular Inca administrative bundle. At the same time, dismissing local knowledge in favor of an outside researcher's diagrams would be a serious methodological error. Archaeology, mathematical analysis, documentary history, and community-held knowledge can address different questions and sometimes correct one another.

Try again to picture the storehouse. A keeper may receive reports, compare quantities, and tie a record that someone else will interpret. The khipu could help maintain trust between offices, or help enforce an unwanted obligation. The arithmetic system is one part of its significance. The other part is that people created institutions in which information traveled without needing to resemble a European page. The fact that modern scholars can decipher some numerical structures but not every category is a limitation of present evidence, not proof that the original users had only a fragmentary system.

Khipu change how we describe Inca history. Roads, terraces, and stone buildings show the scale of material organization; knotted cords show a way of managing and communicating information within it. Their fibers carried decisions and counts even when no alphabetic document was made. By studying what the cords demonstrably encode, and being cautious about what remains undeciphered, we can recognize a sophisticated tradition of recordkeeping without inventing a translation. The knots have survived. Our responsibility is to explain what can be read from them, what remains unknown, and why both facts matter.
