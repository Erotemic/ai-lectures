# Rivers to the Sea: Scandinavians, Rus, and the North Atlantic

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

A small silver coin made far south of Scandinavia has been buried in northern Europe. Its Arabic writing points to one history; the soil where it was found points to another. Someone carried the coin, exchanged it, kept it, or concealed it, but we do not know that person's name. Objects like this are keys to the connected worlds of the eighth through twelfth centuries. Scandinavian raiders and traders reached many shores, while people associated with the Rus moved goods and armies along eastern European rivers. Farther west, families settled Atlantic islands and crossed to North America. These histories were related, but they were not one expedition or the beginning of today's nation-states.

The label Viking is useful but limited. It can refer to seaborne raiding and voyaging by people from Scandinavian societies, rather than a single nation with one ruler. Scandinavia itself contained diverse local communities, political rivalries, farmers, traders, craftspeople, and enslaved workers. From the late eighth century, raiding parties attacked monasteries, towns, and settlements around the North Sea and beyond. Other journeys involved trade, service in foreign armies, or colonization. The same networks could carry merchants and captives; violence and commerce were not opposites. To describe them all as brave explorers would erase the communities whose property, lives, and freedom were taken.

Ships made much of this possible. Scandinavian-built vessels could move along coasts and navigable rivers, combining sailing with rowing where needed. Their construction required timber, iron, textile production, specialized knowledge, and coordinated labor. Navigation depended on practical observation and local experience. The ships also made it possible to establish and maintain communities in places like the British Isles, the North Atlantic islands, and the shores of the Baltic. But mobility did not imply that every traveler enjoyed the same freedom. Enslaved people were among those forcibly transported in early medieval northern European markets, and their labor helped sustain households and exchange systems.

In the British Isles, Scandinavian activity included raids, military campaigns, settlement, and negotiation with existing rulers. The political map changed repeatedly. Some areas came under Scandinavian leadership, while marriage, language contact, agriculture, and local bargaining transformed everyday life. The term Danelaw, often used for areas of England with legal and settlement traditions influenced by Scandinavian rule, can hide variations among communities. Archaeology and place names illuminate patterns of settlement, but neither a Scandinavian-style brooch nor a place name reveals precisely how one family identified itself. Political and cultural boundaries were not always the same.

Along the eastern rivers, routes linked the Baltic Sea to the Black and Caspian seas. Traveling them involved portages, difficult currents, local guides, and negotiations with communities who already lived along the waterways. Silver coin hoards, including Islamic dirhams, help document exchange with lands to the south and east. Fur, wax, textiles, and enslaved people also moved through these networks. The history of the Rus emerged in contact among Scandinavian, Slavic, Finnic, Baltic, and steppe populations; it cannot responsibly be reduced to a single foreign founding group. Nor should it be rewritten as the inevitable birth of any modern state.

The Rus established or came to dominate centers along major routes, including places associated with Novgorod and Kyiv. By the tenth century, rulers connected to Kyiv negotiated and fought with Byzantium and other neighbors. Treaties, tribute demands, and market access mattered alongside military campaigns. Much of the narrative evidence comes from later chronicles whose authors had political and religious reasons for arranging the past. Archaeology and contemporary external sources can confirm parts of the story while questioning others. Even the word Rus changed meaning across sources and centuries, making it important to specify which people and institutions we are describing.

Religion helped reshape these networks. Scandinavian communities gradually adopted Christianity at different times and through different political choices; neither a single royal baptism nor the dedication of a church completed the process everywhere. Rus rulers and elites developed relationships with Orthodox Christian institutions, especially following the conversion associated in tradition with Vladimir of Kyiv in the late tenth century. The conventional date 988 is useful but does not settle every detail of the conversion narratives. Churches, texts, and liturgical forms entered local settings where earlier practices continued or changed unevenly. Religious institutions were part of diplomacy and governance, not simply a private change of belief.

To the northwest, Scandinavian-speaking settlers established communities in Iceland from the late ninth century. Iceland's Althing, conventionally dated to 930, became a recurring assembly for law and dispute settlement among free members of society. It is sometimes called a parliament, but that modern word can obscure its social exclusions and different political form. Households depended on agriculture, animal husbandry, fishing, and regional exchange. Settlers brought animals and altered environments; they also brought social hierarchies. The assembly did not mean that everyone in Iceland held equal voice or that disputes always ended peacefully.

Voyages continued to Greenland, where Norse settlements developed from around the late tenth century. These communities relied on difficult combinations of livestock keeping, hunting, trade, and cooperation in a demanding environment. Greenland was not uninhabited across its history: Indigenous peoples had occupied parts of the Arctic before Norse arrivals, though settlement and contact chronologies vary. Norse travelers also went farther west. At L'Anse aux Meadows in Newfoundland, excavated turf-and-timber buildings and evidence of ironworking demonstrate a short-lived Norse presence. This evidence does not make the visitors the first inhabitants of North America, whose Indigenous histories extend back many millennia.

A study published in 2021 used a radiocarbon signal from a known cosmic-ray event and annual tree rings to date Norse-cut wood at L'Anse aux Meadows to the year 1021. This is unusually precise evidence for activity at the site. It does not prove the date of every voyage reported in later Icelandic sagas, or how long every expedition remained. The sagas are valuable literary and historical traditions, but they were written down long after the events they recount. Their stories of conflict and encounter need to be studied alongside archaeology rather than used as complete firsthand reports of Indigenous people and Norse visitors.

By the eleventh and twelfth centuries, changing kingdoms and churches increasingly shaped Scandinavian politics. Routes remained active, but political organization and relations with neighboring peoples shifted. In eastern Europe, Rus principalities and their neighbors developed different regional histories. In the Atlantic, settlements followed their own trajectories. Later historians sometimes gather these changes into a single story of national origins; such stories are particularly tempting where modern political borders are contested. Yet a medieval river network cannot be made to validate a modern territorial claim simply because a later ruler used a familiar name.

There are multiple kinds of evidence to keep in balance: coins, ships, burials, rural sites, charters, outside chronicles, and sagas composed from remembered stories. Coins can establish circulation without revealing whether their final owner was a trader, raider, or captive. A burial assemblage can show ritual choices without providing a fixed ethnicity. A chronicle may preserve a political tradition while arranging events to support the legitimacy of its author's patrons. Exact dates from scientific analyses can establish an activity at a site but not an entire migration narrative. Each method answers a different question, and their combination gives us a stronger history.

Return to the buried coin. It traveled through overlapping worlds that included Muslim markets, steppe societies, river communities, Scandinavian settlements, and northern European courts. The people who moved it were not merely precursors of modern nations. They were making decisions about work, security, trade, belief, and migration amid relationships that could be cooperative or violent. The North Atlantic and the rivers of eastern Europe reveal how a seemingly peripheral region could take part in broad interregional exchange. And they remind us that to understand the past, we need to keep the many communities already living along those routes in the story.
