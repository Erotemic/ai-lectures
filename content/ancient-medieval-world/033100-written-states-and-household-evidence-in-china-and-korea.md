# Written states and household evidence in China and Korea

States make households visible through records, but they do not record every part of household life. Song Chinese examinations and legal decisions will give us one set of examples; earlier Korean village records and the history of Balhae will give us another. We will mark the change of period rather than treating East Asia as one administrative system.

## Exams, Paper, and Families: The Song State’s Printed World

For a family in Song-dynasty China, deciding whether a son should spend years studying for the civil service examinations was a consequential choice. They may need books, teachers, time away from farming or commerce, and relationships with other families who value scholarly learning. Success could open a path toward government office and social influence, yet most candidates could not expect to pass at the highest level. The examination hall is therefore a place where state institutions meet household finances. To understand its history, we need to follow printing presses, local schools, marriage strategies, and unequal opportunities—not simply praise an ideal of selection by merit.

The Song dynasty ruled much of China from 960 to 1279, though its territory and political circumstances changed significantly across that period. Officials were recruited through a growing examination system grounded in classical learning and the skills expected of literate administrators. Earlier dynasties had held examinations; the Song did not invent the idea from nothing. What changed was the scale and significance of the process within the state's system of recruitment. It helped form a recognizable class of educated men whose status could depend on learning, networks, landholding, and state recognition.

Examinations promised a way to compete for office beyond simple inheritance of a post. That promise mattered and should not be dismissed. But a family's ability to prepare a candidate was distributed unequally. Access to instructors, copies of classical texts, living costs, and leisure time shaped who could study. The result was not an anonymous contest in which everyone began from the same place. Scholarship on Song society traces growing advantages for some wealthy and commercially active regions, even as examinations reduced the exclusive hold of older capital-centered elites.

Printing helped change the economics of learning. Woodblock techniques had been used in China for centuries before the Song, and the period saw major growth in the production and circulation of printed texts. A carved block could reproduce pages for multiple readers. Printers, merchants, schools, and households created demand for books useful to examination candidates and to many other audiences. Printing required paper, ink, wood, skilled carving, correction, and distribution. A printed book was not an effortless duplicate; it was the outcome of organized labor and commercial calculation.

One surviving catalogue record illustrates the chain from one edition to another. A historical work associated with a Song printing house in Meishan is known through later printed material using blocks associated with an edition of the 1190s. The catalogue preserves a notice about the printer and a restriction on unauthorized duplication. Such evidence shows that publishers could attach commercial claims to books. But the surviving object is not automatically a pristine sheet printed in the first year a block was cut. Bibliography depends on identifying editions, blocks, later impressions, and the institutions that preserved them.

The relationship between print and the examinations ran in both directions. More candidates wanted reliable editions of texts that might shape their careers, encouraging wider production. More accessible texts could support the spread of educated communities outside the court. But the ability to buy a book did not guarantee success. Teachers could disagree over interpretations, and a family's local networks helped determine whether study was socially and financially sustainable. Literacy was not a single switch turned on by a technology. Its usefulness varied with institutions, expectations, and the kinds of work people hoped to do.

Education also formed a political geography. A prefecture with commercially successful families could invest in schools and lineages that prepared successive generations for office. A poorer or less connected region might send comparatively few successful candidates. Scholars have shown that some coastal regions gained disproportionate representation in higher examinations over time. This creates a paradox: a system designed to recruit talent across the realm could redistribute advantage among already prosperous groups. It could open routes for social mobility while also rewarding the places best equipped to exploit them.

Women were excluded from the official examination route, but they were not irrelevant to its success. Song writings and later scholarship describe mothers, wives, daughters, and other relatives participating in education, household management, property arrangements, and marriage alliances. Some women read and wrote, and educational opportunities differed sharply by class, region, and family. Their contributions might make it possible for a candidate to study, travel, or seek office. When a genealogy celebrates a successful male examination graduate, it may conceal the household work and resources that supported him.

The sources for educated women have biases of their own. Epitaphs often praise conduct according to elite ideals, and stories about gifted daughters can serve moral or literary purposes. They do not provide a statistical measure of how many girls received formal education in any village. Yet the evidence is enough to challenge a picture in which all learning was confined to men sitting in examination halls. Knowledge circulated within families through reading, memory, instruction, and work. The absence of women from an official list of degree holders tells us about institutional barriers, not the limits of women's intelligence or curiosity.

The examination system did not govern every aspect of local society. Tax collection, irrigation, public works, dispute resolution, and community relief involved administrators as well as local families, merchants, religious institutions, and lineage organizations. Educated men could claim authority through state office while also building influence through marriage ties and landholding. The categories state and local society were therefore closely connected. A magistrate's order depended on people who could supply information, labor, and cooperation, and local elites might use their learning to advance their interests.

The question of what candidates studied also changed. Classical texts were valued not just for memorization but for the moral and administrative capacities that examiners believed scholarship should cultivate. Different intellectual and political movements disagreed about interpretation, and educational reform could be connected to struggles over policy. We should avoid portraying the examination as a mechanically objective test. Human readers judged writing according to standards shaped by institutions and controversies. Knowledge of a respected canon could demonstrate training, but it also marked membership in a cultural world that excluded many forms of expertise.

A craftsperson, a farmer, and a woman managing a commercial household might possess highly developed knowledge without receiving a civil service degree. The state's preferred qualifications were not a universal measure of learning. Nor were all educated men officials. Some taught, wrote, managed estates, or remained unsuccessful candidates. The prominence of examinations nevertheless affected the cultural environment around them: families planned for candidates, printers responded to demand, and communities valued degrees as signs of status. Public institutions altered private choices in ways that did not require every family to participate directly.

The survival of books creates another problem for historians. Printed collections from elite libraries may be numerous in modern catalogues, while everyday school exercises and discarded merchant records have survived unevenly. Sometimes a later edition preserves a lost earlier text; sometimes editorial changes are difficult to trace. Paintings of scholars and literary descriptions of study reveal ideals, not photographs of typical homes. By comparing publication records, examination histories, family writing, and local evidence, historians can reconstruct patterns without claiming that one famous book or one successful candidate represented all Song society.

The Song educational world mattered because it linked a large state's demand for literate officials to an expanding economy of books and schools. It offered new ways for some families to seek power and recognition, but those opportunities depended on money, geography, gender, and networks. The image of a solitary candidate bent over a page is therefore incomplete. Around him stand printers, teachers, relatives, workers, and administrators. The examination produced a result for one person; the institution drew on a much wider society. Its history is about both ambition and the unequal conditions that made ambition possible.

## When Property Went to Court: Women and Judgment in Song China

The examination system was one way the Song state entered family life. Property disputes reveal another, with different consequences for the people who appeared before a court.

Imagine a woman in Southern Song China bringing a complaint over property. She does not appear before a court as an abstract category called women. She appears as a person with a family history, a marriage or remarriage, documents, and a claim that a magistrate must evaluate. Surviving case collections show that such disputes existed and that officials had to decide them. The decisions can be sharp, moralizing, or protective in different ways. This is why it is difficult to summarize Song property law in one sentence. The question is not simply whether women possessed rights. It is when a claim was recognized, under what conditions, and by whom.

The Song dynasty governed much of China from 960 to 1279. Across those centuries, cities grew, markets expanded, and contracts helped households manage land, credit, and inheritance. Family property moved through marriages, dowries, wills, gifts, and claims by descendants. Land was not just a source of income; it helped support ancestors, households, and the social position of kin groups. A legal document might protect one person's interest while competing with another relative's expectations. Those tensions became more important in a world where assets could move through active markets and family arrangements were subject to debate.

Scholars disagree about how far Southern Song law allowed daughters to inherit or control property. Some evidence has been read as supporting a daughter's share when parents died, particularly in certain household circumstances. Bettine Birge traces significant transfers to women and changing protection of their property. A study by Yoshirō Takahashi and Matthew Fraleigh warns against turning exceptional or conditional rules into a fundamental equality with sons. These views are not reconciled by choosing whichever summary sounds more modern. The dispute concerns definitions, source selection, and the difference between a particular allowable transfer and an unrestricted legal entitlement.

The Qingming Ji, a collection of judgments associated with well-known officials, preserves legal reasoning from the Southern Song. One modern study follows the case of a remarried woman, Zhao Zongji. It shows that remarriage could complicate property disputes and expose a litigant to moral criticism. Yet some magistrates still treated a remarried woman's complaint as a matter requiring judgment rather than dismissing it altogether. The case does not tell us how often such claims succeeded. Its value is that it forces a historian to move beyond two simple stories: that remarriage erased every claim, or that courts treated women and men on equal terms.

A legal case is a record shaped by procedure. Someone chose which facts to report, an official selected relevant law, and an editor later decided the case was worth preserving. We rarely possess the complete set of petitions, conversations, witnesses, and negotiations behind a published judgment. The most vivid case may have been preserved precisely because it illustrated a difficult principle. It cannot be counted as a random sample of all Song disputes. Nevertheless, it makes visible the strategies people used when they believed an official might hear them. Litigation was not confined to abstract scholars debating ideals.

What would a property conflict involve? A daughter might have received assets through marriage. A widow might seek to control household resources. A relative might contest a transfer by invoking obligations to a patrilineal family. A remarried woman might be judged against expectations of loyalty to a deceased husband, even while presenting an enforceable claim. Each situation asks a different question. Owning, using, managing, passing on, and inheriting property are not identical acts. Historians need to know which of them a rule addressed before declaring how much economic agency a woman had.

Song legal administration also dealt with deaths, injuries, and questions about whether a crime had occurred. In 1247, the official Song Ci completed a work commonly translated as The Washing Away of Wrongs, a systematic treatment of examination practices in cases involving death. It prescribed procedures for officials and examiners rather than anticipating modern laboratory science. The text belongs to the history of bureaucratic responsibility for evidence: signs on a body, the written record of an examination, and the problem of officials reaching a conclusion. Its existence does not tell us that every village inquiry followed its directions.

A magistrate's work might combine functions that modern governments assign to different institutions. An official could receive complaints, direct investigations, examine documentary claims, and communicate a ruling. That concentration made legal expertise important, but it also tied decisions to a hierarchy of state authority. Song Ci's manual concerns avoiding errors in death investigations; case collections show disputes over household affairs. Neither is a transparent window onto the lives of people without records. Together they reveal what officials considered dangerous mistakes and what conduct they wanted other officials to imitate.

The legal ideals of family continuity had material consequences. Keeping land within a male descent line could conflict with support owed to daughters, widows, or other dependents. Where property was divided, the timing of a death or marriage could change the claims. Where property was held jointly, a person's use of land might be real even without the status of an independent owner in every legal sense. We should not translate medieval Chinese kinship into the assumptions of a modern nuclear family. But we can ask practical questions recognizable across time: who could live on land, receive income from it, or prevent its sale?

Regional and class inequalities also mattered. Wealthy households had more property worth litigating and more ability to preserve documents or hire assistance. Literacy and familiarity with official procedures were uneven. A court record may give a voice to a person whose complaint reached the magistrate, while leaving quiet the people whose conflicts were settled within kin groups or never pursued. A historian who studies only the judgments might exaggerate the reach of courts. A historian who studies only moral literature might overlook the actual cases in which women and other family members acted against those expectations.

This tension helps explain why the scholarship remains active. Law changes across the Northern and Southern Song periods, surviving examples vary in genre, and the same term may look different when applied to a statute, a household agreement, or an individual ruling. Responsible work distinguishes an officially stated entitlement from an arrangement negotiated by kin and from a magistrate's interpretation in an exceptional case. Comparative claims about whether Song women were more independent than women in some other civilization can obscure these distinctions. It is more informative to follow what particular assets and relationships were at issue.

The Song state invested in trained officials, printed texts, and means of making judgments travel beyond one courtroom. A collection of precedent-like decisions could advise future administrators; a manual on death inquiries could standardize how facts were recorded. But these devices did not eliminate discretion or local pressures. The authority of paper depended on someone writing it, someone accepting it, and a government able to make the decision consequential. A law recorded in a book is not identical to a right exercised in a village. The distance between them is part of the history we want to recover.

We can end where we began, with a person bringing a claim. The surviving case does not reduce its litigant to an illustration of a general law. It reminds us that legal categories were tested by families, money, remarriage, illness, death, and disagreement. Southern Song courts left enough records for historians to see women pursuing property claims and officials debating how to decide them, but not enough to produce a simple verdict about equality. The enduring question is how a state converted social conflicts into written decisions, and how the people living under that state sought room to act within its rules.

## Counting Trees and Households: The Silla Village Registers

For comparison, step back several centuries and cross to the Korean peninsula. Village registers preserve a different administrative encounter with households.

Somewhere on the Korean peninsula, an official or scribe compiled information about four villages: their households, fields, cattle, horses, and useful trees. The lists included such ordinary things as mulberry trees and the changes in population recorded over several years. They were not written to entertain a later historian. They were administrative tools, likely used in assessing resources and obligations. Yet these fragments are among the richest surviving witnesses to rural life under the Silla kingdom. Instead of starting with a king's battle or a Buddhist temple, we can begin with a question that officials had to answer: what was present in each village, and how could it be counted?

Silla grew from a kingdom in southeastern Korea into a dominant power over much of the peninsula during the seventh century. Its capital, Gyeongju, became a major political and religious center. But a capital did not feed itself. People in villages cultivated grain, raised animals, planted useful trees, and performed work that supported local and royal institutions. Other states and communities, including Balhae in the north, had their own political histories. Calling the period unified Silla can obscure that geographic and political complexity. The surviving village records let us look at the workings of a state beyond its monumental capital.

The documents are known as the Silla village registers, sometimes called the Silla village documents or civil administration registers. They survive on fragments recovered in 1933 during conservation work involving material held in the Shosoin repository at Todaiji in Nara, Japan. Their survival is remarkable. Paper written for one administrative purpose was reused in a Buddhist textual setting and crossed a sea; the original context of recordkeeping was not preserved intact. Historians must reconstruct the documents' early life from the writing itself and from the later history of the material on which it survived.

What was counted? The surviving entries describe areas of fields, household populations, animals, and trees such as mulberry, pine nut, and walnut. Some categories concern people by age and household status. References to changes across three-year intervals suggest a process of repeated updating. These facts reveal an administration attentive to resources. A tree is not merely scenery when its leaves, nuts, fiber, or wood are useful; a field can be the basis for a tax or work obligation. Listing a resource does not prove that the state successfully collected every anticipated payment, but it shows what officials considered worth recording.

One must be cautious with the date. Many summaries describe the registers as belonging to the middle of the eighth century, often around 755. Other scholars have argued for 695 or 815, using features of writing, calendar terminology, and the circumstances under which the reused paper reached Japan. A Korean scholarly study links the shape of a particular character to a proposed date of 695. Another line of research has argued for a later composition. The disagreement is not a minor footnote. If the records were produced in different generations, they belong to different stages in the development of Silla administration. The safe historical claim is that they are early medieval records whose precise date remains debated.

The writing's language and material form also matter. Officials used written categories shaped by a learned administrative culture shared, with differences, across East Asia. That does not mean everyday villagers spoke the language of the register at home. Nor does the presence of familiar bureaucratic vocabulary prove that Silla copied every institution directly from Tang China. Terms can travel while local obligations change. The question is what the register does in its Korean setting: count people, classify resources, and create a record intelligible to those responsible for governing or collecting dues.

A household in a register is not automatically the same thing as a family. A recent study by historian Lee Hyun-ju distinguishes kin and marriage relationships from administrative units constructed for assessment and taxation. People who shared blood ties might be registered differently, while a recorded household could group dependents for fiscal reasons. This is a general problem in the history of censuses. A document that records household size may tell us how an administration classified people, not exactly how every child experienced home, kinship, or residence. We should resist translating an administrative box into a complete social biography.

Age categories are another clue to state interests. Differentiating people by age could help officials estimate work capacity or other obligations. But we need not invent a precise tax rate or service schedule for every category when the fragment does not provide one. Scholarship on Silla age classifications remains active, with historians comparing terms and trying to understand the social organization behind them. The names of categories make visible a desire to organize populations; the actual experience of people subject to those categories requires further evidence. Some may have negotiated, evaded, or suffered under demands that are not recorded in these surviving pages.

The trees provide an unexpectedly rich example. Mulberry trees could support silk production, while nuts offered foods and other useful materials. Historians and archaeobotanists can compare lists of cultivated or managed trees with physical remains recovered from settlements. A Korean archaeological review notes both the register's reference to pine nut and walnut trees and the difficulty of matching ancient textual names to identified plant remains. A species written on paper is evidence that a category existed. It does not locate every tree, establish a region-wide yield, or show how much food reached an official storehouse.

It is tempting to imagine an efficient centralized state directing every field. The survival of an official list proves something narrower and more interesting: people developed forms of accounting that could carry information from villages to authorities. The success of such administration depended on local intermediaries who knew the terrain, households, crops, and reputations. Recording changes over several years would require revisiting or revising entries. Such work creates opportunities for accuracy, mistakes, pressure, and local bargaining. Documents are part of government, not proof that government achieved everything it intended.

The fact that the paper ended up in Japan makes its history cross-regional without making the original villagers participants in a single direct international journey. Manuscripts and religious texts moved among the Korean peninsula, the Japanese islands, and continental East Asia. Reuse could turn a provincial administrative record into material associated with a Buddhist text. Only much later did conservators notice its historical value. A fragment can survive through routes unrelated to the reason it was first produced. That history of preservation should be acknowledged whenever the source is used to reconstruct the past.

We also need to ask whose lives are not measured adequately. A register might count an enslaved or dependent person according to a legal category while leaving the person's work, resistance, relationships, and aspirations invisible. It might count a woman as a member of a household without recording her management of land or her role in negotiations. Such absences should not lead us to dismiss the document. They tell us how to use it: as evidence of administrative priorities, a partial view of social structure, and a prompt to compare material and textual sources rather than a complete portrait of rural Korea.

Four villages cannot stand for the entire peninsula. They do not tell us directly about fishing communities on distant coasts, mountain settlements, or the households of every neighboring state. The problem of scale is fundamental to all evidence-based history. A uniquely preserved document often becomes famous precisely because little else survived, and fame can encourage researchers to make it bear more conclusions than it can support. We should use the registers to teach how a particular administration made villages legible, while preserving uncertainty about dates, local practice, and broader representativeness.

Return to the list of trees. Each entry likely referred to something a local person knew well: a tree at a field edge, a patch of land, an animal requiring feed, a household where children grew older. In the register, those relationships became counts and categories. This transformation was an exercise of political power, but also of recordkeeping skill and local knowledge. The document survives because a later chain of reuse and preservation carried it to us. It reveals an early Korean state through the ordinary resources on which it depended and reminds us that reading an archive involves understanding why it was made, what it omitted, and how it survived.

## Beyond a Border on a Map: Balhae, Its Cities, and Its People

Another early medieval East Asian state, Balhae, complicates histories organized only by the borders of present-day countries.

A stone inscription commemorating a princess may look like the story of a single royal household. In the history of Balhae, a kingdom that flourished from the late seventh to the early tenth century, such stones also help us examine language, political memory, and the limits of a state-centered account. The epitaphs of Princess Jeonghye and Princess Jeonghyo are among the best-known surviving texts connected with Balhae's court. They preserve names and dates through deliberate acts of commemoration. Yet they do not tell us how most villagers lived, or settle every modern argument about the kingdom's ancestry and identity. Their precision makes them valuable; their selectivity makes them demanding.

Balhae, also called Bohai in Chinese-language scholarship, emerged in 698 in the borderlands of the Korean peninsula and what is now northeastern China and the Russian Far East. Its foundation is linked in surviving historical traditions to Dae Jo-yeong after the destruction of Goguryeo and the movements of people in the region. The state drew on populations with different histories, including groups often called Mohe in Chinese records as well as people tied to the older Goguryeo world. The names used by later historians do not translate directly into modern national citizenship. A mixed and shifting frontier polity should not be squeezed into the borders of a present-day state.

The court established and moved political centers, among them sites called Sanggyeong, Junggyeong, and Donggyeong. Archaeology preserves traces of planned urban layouts, roof tiles, brickwork, Buddhist images, ceramics, and official objects. The National Museum of Korea emphasizes these finds in its Balhae display. They show architectural organization and the resources available to courts and temples. They cannot by themselves give us a census of a city's workers or establish that every settlement in the surrounding countryside followed the same plan. City walls and a capital name are only part of the map of how a state functioned.

In the museum display, a replica of a fish-shaped identification tag illustrates an object associated with officials. Such a small object directs attention from the grandeur of a capital toward the routines of authority: distinguishing office holders, conveying authorization, and maintaining administrative relationships. An object of this kind is evidence of institutional practices, even when the precise details of every use are reconstructed by comparison. Royal states do not govern solely by proclamations. They require messengers, scribes, storehouses, road travel, and people who can recognize the legitimacy of an official. Their surviving material tokens give us fragments of that less visible world.

The inscriptions commemorating Princess Jeonghye and Princess Jeonghyo offer another kind of official voice. The princesses died in the late eighth century, and their epitaphs record royal mourning and identity through learned written forms. Scholars have examined the inscriptions' calligraphy, comparing their styles with earlier northern Chinese traditions rather than assuming all Balhae court art was simply a copy of Tang fashion. Such comparisons are technical historical arguments based on strokes, formats, and scripts. The text and the stone were cultural choices; neither was an unmediated statement from an entire population.

Royal women's epitaphs can reveal names and family relationships unavailable in terse chronicles. But they were constructed to honor the deceased according to elite conventions. A recent scholarly comparison of four Balhae royal women's epitaphs finds repeated moral language and distinctive ways of describing kinship. This does not make the women imaginary. It means the surviving biography is filtered through the institutional purposes of the monument. When an epitaph praises virtue, we know what the writers thought it appropriate to commemorate. We know less about a woman's daily experience, choices, and private relationships than the polished formal inscription can seem to promise.

Buddhism connected Balhae's court and communities to other East Asian networks. Images and temple traces show religious patronage; diplomatic exchanges linked the kingdom with Tang China, neighboring Silla, and Japan. Yet interchange is not the same as cultural disappearance. Envoys and imported styles could be put to locally meaningful uses. A monument's script might be intelligible across several states without giving all its inhabitants a common spoken language. Learned Chinese writing, for example, could be a diplomatic or commemorative medium while households used other languages. The contrast between the language of an archive and the speech of a settlement is a recurrent problem in early medieval history.

What did households outside the palace do? Farming, herding, fishing, craft production, and local exchange must have supported the capitals and their institutions. But their proportions, regional variation, and forms of obligation are difficult to recover from elite monuments. Food residues, habitation remains, burials, and regional archaeology can begin to answer these questions. We should resist filling gaps with a fictional typical Balhae village. Different environmental zones and communities may have participated in the state in different ways. The fact that elite records are better preserved than ordinary accounts does not make the latter lives historically secondary.

Balhae fell to the Khitan Liao in 926. Later sources record displaced people and movements toward other polities, including Goryeo. A defeat in a chronicle can look instantaneous; for households it could involve flight, changed taxes, altered loyalty, or life under a new administration. Archaeologists studying burial practices have identified possible continuities and transformations among people associated with Balhae after the kingdom's collapse. But burial similarity does not supply a personal ethnic declaration from each deceased person. The archaeological evidence can make displacement visible while leaving room for changes in identity and practice across generations.

Present-day national histories sometimes argue over whether Balhae should be considered primarily Korean or Chinese. The argument can become misleading when it asks ancient people to choose between categories they did not possess in their current form. There are meaningful historical questions about succession from Goguryeo, the roles of Mohe and other groups, links with Tang, relations with Silla, and the later memory of Balhae in Goryeo. Those can be discussed through evidence. But the act of assigning a single modern nation to every inhabitant would conceal the social and linguistic complexity that the evidence is supposed to illuminate.

Different sources were created for different audiences. A Chinese dynastic history might classify neighbors through diplomatic categories; a Japanese record might attend to an embassy; a royal epitaph might commemorate the dead; a building plan can show where a palace stood. Each source presents a partial geography of the same world. Comparing them can expose contradictions and fill particular gaps, but more sources do not automatically produce a single uncontested map. Even basic details such as the preferred spelling of a name depend on conventions of transliteration and the language through which it entered scholarship.

The most productive way to study Balhae is to move between scales. The royal household and capital matter because governments made institutional decisions there. Roads, envoys, and religious centers matter because they connected people who did not live near the court. Burial archaeology matters because political defeat did not end the histories of communities on the ground. Looking across these scales helps us understand why the polity could participate in East Asian diplomacy while remaining locally complex. It also makes clear that a culture can adapt foreign forms without being reducible to a foreign imitation.

Return, finally, to the epitaph. It preserves the name of a woman who died more than twelve centuries ago, written in a formal script shaped by earlier traditions. We can study the date, the genealogy, the monument, and the work of modern epigraphists who interpret it. We cannot ask the stone to speak for every farmer, craftsperson, traveler, or displaced family in Balhae. Their histories require other evidence and, often, an admission of what is missing. That admission opens the past rather than closing it. Balhae was an inhabited world as well as a kingdom, and the difference is the beginning of its history.
