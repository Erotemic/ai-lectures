# Across the Red Sea: Aksum, Adulis, and a Connected African World

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

Near the Ethiopian town of Aksum, immense carved stone stelae rise above ancient burial areas. Some imitate buildings in stone, complete with false doors, windows, and architectural details. They were placed there by people whose rulers, languages, and economic connections belonged to an African kingdom with a history reaching across the Red Sea. Nearby, inscriptions and coins offer traces of kings who made political claims in more than one language. This is a different entrance to late antiquity from Constantinople or Ctesiphon. It reminds us that the history of the Red Sea was not just a corridor between empires elsewhere. Communities in the Horn of Africa were active makers of its politics, trade, and religious life.

The kingdom of Aksum flourished across parts of what are now northern Ethiopia and Eritrea, with its inland political center on the highlands and maritime connections through the port of Adulis on the coast of modern Eritrea. Its greatest territorial reach changed over time, and the area colored for Aksum on a modern map should not be read as permanently governed in the same way. The kingdom emerged from earlier local histories and developed state institutions, monumental building, and long-distance exchange. The highland setting made agriculture and inland routes as important to power as access to the sea.

Aksum's position linked it to networks reaching Egypt, Arabia, the Mediterranean, and the western Indian Ocean. Travelers, merchants, and political envoys moved along maritime routes made possible by seasonal winds and the skills of sailors. Goods could include ivory, gold, aromatics, textiles, metalwork, wine, and ceramics at different times and places. Not all these goods were produced in the same district. Traders and local intermediaries assembled them from wider hinterlands. Maritime exchange was not a self-sufficient economy floating above land: it depended on farmers, craft producers, port workers, caravan leaders, and political authorities able to manage or contest access to routes.

Coins give us unusually informative evidence. From around the third century CE, Aksumite rulers minted coinage, including gold issues with inscriptions intended to circulate beyond local markets. Greek legends appeared on some coins, while Ge'ez and other scripts were used in Aksumite written culture. A coin can identify royal titles, religious symbols, and the reach of particular forms of monetary exchange. But it should not be turned into a census of ordinary transactions. People could pay, exchange, or fulfill obligations in other ways. The circulation of gold coinage makes visible one part of a much larger economy.

The stelae of Aksum are another kind of record. Their carved architectural forms and association with elite burials point to a political culture in which funerary display mattered. Raising such monuments required quarrying, moving heavy stones, and organizing labor. We should not treat the monuments as merely mysterious feats of engineering. Their existence implies specialists, resources, and people whose work is largely absent from surviving written sources. The large stelae belong mainly to the centuries before or around the kingdom's fourth-century Christian transformation. They are not simply medieval church towers in another form.

In the fourth century, King Ezana is associated with the adoption of Christianity at the Aksumite court. Royal coinage changed: Christian crosses appear where earlier designs had other sacred symbols. Written and later narrative accounts connect the conversion with a figure called Frumentius, also known in Ethiopian tradition as Abba Salama. The story of his arrival and role comes through church histories written from particular perspectives; its details should not be accepted without examining the sources. The coinage and inscriptions give independent evidence for change in royal religious self-presentation. They do not prove that every household converted at once.

Aksumite Christianity took shape through connections with the church of Alexandria and other Christian communities. Ge'ez became an important language of Ethiopian Christian literature and worship, alongside older uses in royal and other inscriptions. The relationship between Ethiopian Christianity and developments elsewhere was never identical to rule by a Mediterranean emperor. Local religious institutions, sacred texts, monastic communities, and artistic practices developed their own histories. Religious change could happen through patronage, travel, teaching, and family networks over generations. The continued importance of Ethiopian Christianity long after Aksum's political transformation shows why dynastic chronology and religious continuity cannot be treated as the same thing.

Archaeology at Adulis helps us see the process at a different scale. Excavations of early Christian churches, including recent radiocarbon work, have identified sixth- and early seventh-century building phases. The physical evidence permits questions about worship spaces, construction techniques, changing liturgical use, and continuities in local materials. It cannot by itself settle every theological belief of those who attended. Adulis was a cosmopolitan port, and a building's history must be read alongside the movement of goods, people, and ideas that made a maritime town possible. A sixth-century church is thus a record of community investment, not just a sign on a map labeled Christian.

Aksum also took part in the political history of southern Arabia. Across the Red Sea, Himyarite rulers and other communities had their own histories of religion, inscription, and competition. In the sixth century, conflict involving a Jewish Himyarite ruler, Christian communities including Najran, Aksumite rulers, and wider diplomatic interests drew forces across the sea. Aksumite intervention under King Kaleb is commonly placed in the 520s. Later accounts of persecution, conquest, and heroic leaders are important, but their details require comparison with inscriptions and material evidence. We cannot reduce events to a timeless religious feud or imagine that the Arabian coast had no politics of its own.

In the decades following Aksumite intervention, southern Arabia saw changing rulers and power struggles, including the career of Abraha, a leader associated with Aksumite-linked rule in Yemen. His own inscriptions and later traditions do not always tell the same story in the same language. Political control across the Red Sea was costly and variable. The episode shows that African rulers projected power beyond the continent, negotiated with local groups, and competed within a wider world that included Byzantium and Iran. Yet it should not obscure the people who cultivated southern Arabian terraces or sustained coastal towns during these struggles.

There are also accounts in Muslim tradition of early followers of Muhammad taking refuge with a Christian ruler across the Red Sea. These stories are important to Islamic historical memory and to later ideas about relations between communities. Their precise chronology and individual details are difficult to confirm from contemporary Aksumite records. They should be identified as transmitted tradition, not used as a substitute for independent documentation of a royal audience. In any case, the story's setting reminds us that the Horn of Africa was part of the world within which early Muslim communities imagined movement, safety, and diplomacy.

From the seventh century onward, Aksum's political and economic landscape changed. Some older explanations put the kingdom's decline entirely down to the rise of Muslim control over Red Sea traffic. But trade routes, political centers, settlement patterns, environmental conditions, and regional competition all changed, and scholars debate their relative importance. A phrase like “the port was cut off” is too simple unless we can establish when, by whom, and with what effects. Local communities and Christian institutions continued. The historical transformation was not the sudden disappearance of the population that had built the stelae and churches.

The evidence is varied and uneven: coins, multilingual royal inscriptions, stelae, burials, imported pottery, harbor archaeology, religious buildings, and narratives written in other lands. Archaeological finds reveal connections without always naming the people who used the objects. Royal inscriptions proclaim victories and piety but rarely record the views of defeated communities. Church histories preserve religious memory while selecting the deeds they consider important. Later Ethiopian traditions must be studied on their own terms and compared carefully with earlier evidence. This variety is not a defect in the history. It is the condition that lets historians ask more than one kind of question.

Aksum and Adulis show what it means to write late antiquity across regions without turning Africa into an appendix to someone else's empire. Farmers supported inland institutions; merchants moved goods by sea; artisans raised monuments; rulers made claims in several languages; religious communities adapted Christianity within African settings; and armies crossed the Red Sea in pursuit of political aims. The stone buildings imitated by Aksum's stelae have largely vanished, but their images in stone survive. They remind us that powerful institutions rest on the work of many people—and that the histories connecting Africa, Arabia, and the Mediterranean cannot be understood from a single imperial capital.
