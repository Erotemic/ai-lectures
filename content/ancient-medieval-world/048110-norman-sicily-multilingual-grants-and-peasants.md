# Boundaries in Three Languages: Land and Labor in Norman Sicily

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

Imagine a boundary between two fields on an island where an administrator can describe it in Greek, another can write it in Arabic, and a third may record a later grant in Latin. The fields have not moved. Their owners and cultivators may know the same stream, stone, or old path. But the order in which the clerk names the boundaries can differ. In twelfth-century Sicily, this was not an abstract problem in translation. It was part of the practical machinery of government, landholding, and rural work. The documents of the Norman kingdom reveal a society where several writing traditions met within the same administration.

Norman forces conquered Sicily during the eleventh century. The island had long been connected to Mediterranean Islamic, Byzantine, and Latin Christian worlds. Its inhabitants included Arabic- and Greek-speaking communities, Muslims, Christians of different traditions, Jews, and people whose identities cannot be captured by one of these labels alone. Roger the Second became king in 1130. A conquest changed political authority, but it did not instantly erase the languages, legal habits, or knowledge of landscapes on which government depended. New rulers needed people who could identify lands, collect revenue, draft grants, and recognize the names that farmers used for their own settlements.

The royal administration is often called the diwan, a term associated with Arabic writing and state offices. Jeremy Johns's study reconstructs registers of people and land from a small surviving set of charters and references to lost administrative books. Many of the working registers themselves have not survived. This is a crucial limitation. We possess documents that refer to a larger bureaucracy, but that does not mean we can reconstruct the entire archive or assume every local procedure looked the same. What survives, however, shows rulers directing resources through registers whose forms reflected several administrative traditions.

Among the most revealing records are six documents relating to the Greek monastery of Saint George of Tròccoli near Caltabellotta, published and studied by Vera von Falkenhausen, Nadia Jamil, and Jeremy Johns. Three documents are bilingual in Greek and Arabic, and three are Arabic. They deal with the monastery's lands and with Muslim peasants connected to them. A Christian religious foundation appears in paperwork written in languages associated with several communities and administrative traditions. This is not a paradox. A monastery could own land cultivated by people of another faith, while officials employed whatever written practices were recognized and operationally useful.

An 1141 register connected to the monastery records 115 peasant households on the specified properties. It makes a region of rural Sicily suddenly countable in a way that a royal chronicle cannot. Yet even this number needs a label. It is a record of households within the scope of the grant, not a count of every person or of all the landowners in the district. The scholars who edited the documents use it to examine settlement density and the practical concerns of the royal administration. For us the most important lesson may be how the administrative record identifies which families had claims, duties, and relationships recognized by the monastery and the crown.

The texts also contain names that help scholars discuss the backgrounds of some peasant families. There are signs that people from North Africa reached Sicily and entered relationships of dependence with the monastery. It would be easy to turn this into a confident tale of desperate refugees fleeing one famous famine. But the chronology complicates that account. The editors point out that some people already appear before the famine of the early 1140s usually cited as an explanation. Hard conditions in North Africa and opportunities in Sicily are plausible contexts, yet each recorded family is not accompanied by a testimony stating why it moved.

A register of peasants is also a record of unequal power. The people listed were described through categories of labor and dependence meaningful to the crown and monastery. Calling them villeins does not give us a modern description of their wishes or explain all the legal distinctions involved. Some had crossed the sea, perhaps seeking better conditions; their decision to enter a dependent relationship would not make that relationship free of constraint. Their names survive because an institution needed to know who had obligations tied to its estates. A charter can preserve evidence of migration and limited choices at the same time.

Land descriptions reveal another kind of interaction. Alex Metcalfe compared boundary clauses in medieval Mediterranean documents written in Latin, Greek, and Arabic. In many Romano-Byzantine examples, descriptions began with the eastern boundary; many Arabic legal traditions favored the southern boundary. The difference was not an innate property of a language. It was a convention maintained through schooling, scribal forms, and learned practice. When one government used several documentary traditions, its boundary formulas could retain traces of those different institutional histories.

The Sicilian evidence becomes even more revealing when we distinguish who was issuing the document. Metcalfe found that private Arabic documents often followed a south-first convention, while Arabic descriptions produced for royal landholding could follow an eastern-first pattern closer to Romano-Byzantine practice. An Arabic sentence might thus participate in a particular state procedure shaped by other scribal traditions. This undermines the convenient equation of language with an administration's ethnic or religious origin. A change in who commissioned the document could matter as much as the language in which it was written.

How did officials know where an estate began? They depended on people with local knowledge, on landmarks, and on ways of validating a grant. A formula was not a substitute for walking or recognizing a boundary. Legal words gave a description an authority that could be cited if claims later conflicted. But geographical knowledge was still distributed among villagers, estate managers, religious foundations, and officials. The multilingual charter is therefore not just an accomplishment of translators in a royal office. It is an instrument joining the ruler's command to the locality where someone would have to recognize or enforce it.

These sources also expose the danger of celebrating medieval Sicily only as a perfectly harmonious meeting of cultures. The same documents that show several languages functioning together show dependency and unequal ownership. A Greek monastery held estates with Muslim cultivators; royal authorities confirmed rights; a person on the register was part of a labor system managed by others. Political power shaped which names were written and which transactions survived. Cultural exchange was real, but it did not abolish coercion or erase the conquest that had placed new rulers above older communities.

One further caution concerns the fate of the archive. Documents survived because monasteries and later owners had reasons to keep proof of property. Lost charters could radically change our sense of how widespread a practice was. We therefore cannot count the handful of bilingual records and turn that count into the percentage of Sicily that used a particular language. Nor can we assume that an individual peasant was able to read the document that recorded his family's obligations. Written administration was influential because it translated local relationships into durable claims held by powerful institutions.

Return to the boundary between the fields. There is an eastern edge, a southern edge, and a person whose livelihood may depend on how the grant is interpreted. Greek and Arabic may describe the same place in different orders, while the people who work there live with the consequences of decisions made in a distant office. This is a history of a Mediterranean state built not only by soldiers or kings but through lists, translations, measurements, and negotiated obligations. The paperwork contains an extraordinary amount of life, provided we remember how much of that life it leaves unwritten.

## Evidence boundary

The Tròccoli documents are a surviving small dossier, not a complete Sicilian census. The 1141 register counts 115 households for specified estates, not every inhabitant of the island. Scholars suggest that some Muslim migrants arrived from North Africa, but the register predates the mid-century famine often invoked to explain their departure. Arabic, Greek and Latin documentary traditions differed, and language in a document does not map simply to religion or ethnicity.
