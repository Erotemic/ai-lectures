# Houses on a Moving Shore: The Iron Age beside Lake Malawi

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

Imagine setting a bowl near the edge of a great lake and returning many generations later. The waterline has moved. The old settlement may now stand farther from the shore, or it may have been covered by rising water. At the southern end of Lake Malawi, archaeologists have had to reconstruct the history of villages against a shifting geography. Pottery fragments are a large part of that work. But the central story is not simply a sequence of decorated pots. It is how people made homes, found food, used tools, and maintained connections while the lake and the surrounding landscape changed.

Lake Malawi lies in the East African Rift. Its waters drain through the Shire River, while its shores include bays, flood-prone ground, hills, and pathways toward the Zambezi basin. Lake levels have varied over time. A site situated on a raised shore may preserve evidence of settlement during a time when water was higher, but a position on a modern map is not an ancient coastline. Dating the occupation and reconstructing its contemporary environment are separate tasks. Researchers compare sediments, archaeological contexts, and measurements of earlier shoreline positions, aware that local erosion and later building may have removed portions of the record.

By around the third century of the common era, communities in southern Malawi were using iron and pottery in settings archaeologists call Early Iron Age. Yusuf Juwayeyi's study of the southern Lake Malawi region describes the extensive excavation campaigns that developed this chronology. One widely recognized ceramic tradition is called Nkope, after a site near the southern lakeshore. Typical Nkope vessels include globular pots and bowls with distinctive rim treatments and combinations of incised or impressed decoration. Such details allow excavators to compare deposits even when no complete pot survives. They do not label the ethnicity of everyone who made or used a vessel.

An archaeologist building a timeline might first ask where a fragment was found. Was it inside a hearth, at the floor of a dwelling, in a pit, or washed into a later deposit? Is the radiocarbon sample associated securely with that pottery? Then comes a second question: do vessels in neighboring areas share forms and decorative habits, or do the similarities end at one motif? Finally, a third: can differences be understood as time passing, as neighboring styles, or as different tasks within one household? These steps help prevent a circle in which a pot is dated because of its style and the style is dated because of the pot.

The ceramic sequence is not a simple chain in which one named group vanished the morning another appeared. Kapeni pottery, recognizable by different combinations of form and incised decoration, occurs in deposits that can overlap with late Nkope phases. Dates differ from one excavated locality to another. Juwayeyi's review explains why radiocarbon samples, and not a rigid typological ladder, are necessary for establishing the extent of that overlap. The presence of a Kapeni sherd in one district does not prove that an entire Nkope population left. People could alter vessel-making habits while maintaining local families and food traditions, or exchange pots made elsewhere.

Spana Davison's research at Namaso Bay provides a particularly revealing case. He defined a Namaso archaeological phase during the late eighth to early eleventh centuries of the common era, using well-dated settlement evidence on the southeastern arm of the lake. Those dates place Namaso in a period that had not been represented adequately in earlier local classifications. The associated settlement lay in a landscape of unusually high lake levels. Researchers found that the material record reflected many local resources, while also showing connections to wider exchange networks. A site that once appeared to fill a chronological gap became a means of questioning how the whole regional sequence was constructed.

At Namaso, the evidence is not a story in which everyone suddenly depended on imported iron. Davison discusses local materials including stone and shell, and contacts that brought or circulated goods such as copper and cowries. Objects made from different substances can be useful in different tasks. Stone need not disappear when iron is present. A small shell or copper object may reveal a connection, yet it cannot tell us by itself how many journeys or intermediaries lay between the shore and its source. The materials point to mixed economic activities, not a tidy split between isolated self-sufficient villages and cosmopolitan trading towns.

There is a reason to ask about ordinary work as well as technology. Living beside a lake could involve fishing, cultivating nearby land, gathering shore and wetland resources, maintaining households, collecting clay, gathering firewood, and tending objects that could not survive archaeologically. Different sites could emphasize these activities differently. A grinding implement shows food processing, but not necessarily the cultivation of a particular grain. A pot near fish bones might have been used for many kinds of food. The reconstruction is strongest when several lines of evidence agree and weakest when a single artifact is made to stand for a whole economy.

Some pottery traditions in the region extend into the later medieval centuries and beyond the approximate year 1500, the chronological endpoint of this course. Mawudzu and still later Nkhudzi ceramics appear in archaeological schemes for the later part of Malawi's Iron Age. These names help researchers sort collections and ask when settlements changed. They should not be projected backward to create continuous, unbroken political identities reaching into the first millennium. The later histories of Chewa-speaking communities, important as they are, require oral traditions, comparative linguistics, and historical inquiry in their own right; pottery cannot replace those sources.

Why do historians pay so much attention to a changing lakeshore? Because its movements affect where food, settlement ground, and travel routes could be found, and also which traces survive for excavation. A rise in water might coincide with different settlement choices; it does not automatically cause every cultural change observed in the same centuries. The dates might not align closely enough. Families could respond in different ways. Ecological evidence should be used to enlarge the possibilities we consider, not to turn every political or economic development into a simple response to climate.

The history of southern Lake Malawi is therefore made from repeated acts of comparison: one vessel with another, one buried surface with the present shore, one set of tool materials with the things missing from a deposit, and one chronology with new dates that challenge it. Nkope, Kapeni, and Namaso are useful labels for parts of that record. The people who made homes there had histories more complicated than those labels. As the shoreline changed, their tools, settlements, and connections changed too, sometimes together and sometimes on different schedules. Reconstructing those schedules is the work of archaeology.

## Evidence boundary

The Nkope–Kapeni–Namaso reconstruction depends on excavation contexts and radiocarbon dating, not on assuming one pottery style equals one population. Davison's Namaso dates and lake-level association are based on a scholarly abstract rather than a direct review of the complete excavation tables. Shifts in lake level are not claimed to be singular causes of settlement or political change. Later Chewa histories are not inferred directly from first-millennium sherds.
