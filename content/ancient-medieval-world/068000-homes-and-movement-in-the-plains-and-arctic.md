# Homes and movement in the Plains and Arctic

Homes can connect a community to seasonal movement rather than oppose it. The Plains and Arctic offer very different environments in which to study that relationship. We will compare food, buildings, travel, and social ties without making one region explain the other.

## The Plains before the Horse: Bison, Fields, and Earthlodge Villages

Picture the northern Plains long before horses became central to the histories most often shown in paintings and films. Along the Missouri River, families lived in substantial settlements, grew crops, stored harvests, traded stone and food, and traveled onto the grasslands for bison. The contrast between a stationary farmer and a wandering hunter does not describe these societies. Their knowledge lay partly in combining ways of life across environments and seasons. The archaeology of the Middle Missouri, together with Mandan, Hidatsa, and Arikara histories, lets us examine a Plains world with long continuities and substantial changes before 1500.

The Missouri River offered something the open prairie could not always provide: sheltered bottomlands, reliable water, wood, fertile soils, and transport routes. Houses could stand on terraces above spring floods while fields occupied lower ground. Beyond the river lay grasslands where large animals moved seasonally. Settlements needed people able to work across these spaces. The same family community might cultivate crops, prepare stores for winter, join a hunting party, and travel to meet trading partners. That flexibility was a sophisticated economic system, not a transitional stage toward another way of life.

Archaeologists use the term Plains Village tradition for several related settlement and farming patterns in the region. The Middle Missouri tradition, beginning around the first millennium CE and developing over subsequent centuries, included earth-sheltered houses, sometimes defended villages, cultivation, and bison hunting. Houses in some earlier Middle Missouri settlements were rectangular and partly dug into the ground; round earthlodges became especially prominent in later communities. We should not collapse every excavated dwelling into the round structures familiar from nineteenth-century descriptions. Building forms changed, as did the locations and political connections of settlements.

What does a house reveal? A depression in the earth can mark a floor; postholes can record where timber supported a roof; hearths, storage pits, broken pottery, and discarded bone can show repeated activity. A village may preserve patterns of rebuilding after fire or abandonment. Rows of houses, a ditch, and a stockade can tell us that residents invested in protecting a place. But these finds do not reconstruct every kinship rule. To understand social relationships, archaeologists compare the houses with burials, food remains, tools, and the oral histories of communities whose ancestors lived along the river.

Later Hidatsa villages near the Knife River offer a particularly well-described continuation of riverine farming. The National Park Service describes women cultivating maize, beans, squash, and sunflowers in bottomlands, and households occupying substantial earthlodges above them. Among Hidatsa communities documented in the contact era, women held major responsibilities for earthlodge construction and ownership. These details are important, but their documentation is often considerably later than the earliest Plains Village sites. It would be a mistake to assign every seventeenth- or nineteenth-century practice, unchanged, to a settlement in the year 1000.

The stored harvest was a form of resilience. Maize could be dried, carried, and kept against winter shortages. It also involved risk: late frost, drought, floods, or crop loss could strain a community. Hunting bison was another way to obtain food and raw materials, but large animals moved, and a hunt could fail. The combination of river farming and prairie hunting spread some risks across different sources. Archaeological remains show bones and plant traces; a particular drought's effect on a named village may be harder to establish. Environmental pressure mattered, but communities responded through movement, alliances, storage, and changes in settlement.

Bison provided more than meat. Hides could be worked for clothing and shelter; bone and horn supplied tools; fat and other products could be preserved or used in many ways. Hunting required planning and coordination, whether by small parties or larger groups. In the long record of the Plains there were many strategies for approaching and killing bison before the introduction of horses. To describe these people as incapable of extensive prairie travel without mounted transport would be to confuse one historically specific technology with the much older knowledge of the land.

Likewise, maize and other domestic crops did not appear on the Plains in a single wave that transformed everyone in the same way. Agricultural traditions were adopted, adapted, and sometimes reduced according to local ecology and social choices. Different river valleys supported different settlements. Some communities placed more emphasis on farming, others on mobility or particular game resources. Exchange linked them. Knife River flint, for example, was valued far beyond its source area. A stone artifact recovered far away can show a network of movement, even when it cannot tell whether the stone passed through two hands or twenty.

The words Mandan, Hidatsa, and Arikara name living peoples with distinct histories. They should not be treated as three labels for the same archaeological culture. National Park Service accounts combine the archaeological record with the groups' migration and origin traditions. The historical villages preserved at Knife River are mainly from after 1500, so they are valuable for exploring later forms and for understanding continuities, but cannot directly document every detail of the preceding centuries. Researchers distinguish earlier possible ancestors and regional movements from later settlements whose residents are more securely identified.

Political life had to operate at more than one scale. A household organized fieldwork and food storage. A village coordinated defenses, ceremonial activity, and relations with neighbors. Communities negotiated access to river bottoms and hunting territories, and carried obligations to relatives elsewhere. Conflict was possible; so were alliances and exchange. Archaeological ditches and burned buildings may point to danger, but rarely identify the parties involved. A later oral tradition may help explain a migration without supplying the exact calendar date of every archaeological episode. Political history here is made of local choices and changing connections, not only kings and treaties.

Try to follow a single seed through this system. Someone saves it from a previous harvest, plants it in ground that must be cleared and tended, protects it from animals and weather, then dries the mature ear and reserves part for another year. Other grains may be traded or served at gatherings. An archaeologist finds a charred kernel, perhaps preserved after a house fire, and uses its context to reconstruct part of that work. The kernel can be dated and identified. It cannot say who made the decision to save it, what that person hoped for, or what family rule governed the storehouse. The material remains let us ask better questions without turning reconstruction into fiction.

After 1500, changes intensified. Epidemics, new trade goods, shifting military relations, the spread of horses among some Plains societies, and European and later United States expansion radically altered regional life. In the nineteenth century the Mandan, Hidatsa, and Arikara suffered devastating disease and dispossession. Those events belong to their longer history, but they should not be projected backward as the defining features of the medieval Plains. The farming communities of the earlier centuries had their own opportunities, conflicts, and ways of making a living.

Much of the popular image of the Plains is drawn from the horse era, which covers only a part of its history. Before that period, the grasslands and rivers supported multiple connected economies: hunters, farmers, traders, and people whose activities crossed those categories. Villages were not signs that Plains peoples had stopped moving, and hunting did not mean that they lacked agriculture. To understand the region before 1500, begin with a river terrace, a store of maize, an earth-sheltered home, and a path out onto the prairie. Together they give us a more accurate world than either stereotype alone.

## Homes along the Ice: Inuit Ancestors and the Medieval Arctic

Far to the north, ancestral Inuit communities joined homes and seasonal movement in a very different environment.

A winter dwelling built partly into the ground could be warm, durable, and closely fitted to an Arctic coast. Its walls might use stone, sod, and whalebone; its entrance could be lower than the living space; a stone lamp could supply heat and light from oil. Outside, the sea was not an empty barrier. Ice, open water, currents, animal migrations, and weather formed a landscape that knowledgeable travelers could read. Between about 1000 and 1500, ancestral Inuit communities developed and transformed ways of living across much of the North American Arctic and Greenland. Their history is a history of expertise, movement, family work, and change.

Archaeologists frequently call one major ancestral tradition Thule, after a place name used in the history of Arctic research. Modern Inuit are descendants of people associated with this tradition, but the archaeological label is not a substitute for the names and histories of living Inuit communities. Earlier northern peoples, often grouped under archaeological labels such as Dorset, had inhabited parts of the Canadian Arctic for centuries before the eastward spread of ancestral Inuit populations from Alaska. The relation between these groups, and the circumstances of displacement or interaction, differed across places and remain under investigation.

The spread from Alaska across the Canadian Arctic toward Greenland was rapid by archaeological standards, with settlements expanding during the late first and early second millennia CE. Parks Canada identifies arrivals in parts of the eastern Arctic around the end of the eleventh century and widespread Thule-associated sites by the thirteenth. These dates are not a schedule for a single migration column. Families took different routes, explored coasts, established seasonal settlements, and adjusted to local conditions. Archaeological chronology is inferred from house forms, artifacts, stratigraphy, and dating; community histories add perspectives that an excavated floor cannot supply alone.

Travel technology mattered. Large skin-covered boats, umiaks, could carry people and cargo. Kayaks supported individual travel and hunting. Dogs and sleds offered possibilities for crossing snow and ice. Each system demanded skilled construction and maintenance. A boat needed frames, prepared hides, careful stitching, and knowledge of where to launch safely. A sled required materials and training; an oil lamp required suitable stone, fuel, and tending. These technologies were not simply responses to a hostile environment. They were cultural achievements that let people move among resources and maintain relationships over great distances.

Animals shaped settlement seasons, but no single species fed everyone everywhere. Ringed seals could be hunted in winter and throughout the year. Caribou supplied meat and clothing materials. Fish, birds, and eggs mattered in particular seasons. Bowhead whales were enormously important in some early communities, where successful hunting could support substantial stores and gatherings. Walrus and beluga were central in other regions. An archaeologist can examine bones in a midden and the tools found beside them, while remembering that preservation and disposal practices differ. One large whalebone house does not prove that every Arctic settlement depended on large whales.

House construction gives evidence of planning across seasons. In parts of the eastern Arctic, early winter houses had semisubterranean rooms and roofs supported partly by whalebone. An entrance tunnel helped separate the living space from outside temperatures. Platforms supported sleep and work; lamps provided warmth and light. These were environments in which people repaired equipment, prepared skins, cared for children, stored food, and shared knowledge. A house was not simply shelter from cold. It was a productive workspace and a place where relationships were maintained through long winters.

The location of a village depended on more than proximity to animals. People needed landing places, access to materials, predictable ice conditions, and neighbors with whom they could coordinate travel or share resources. At some sites, several substantial houses show people gathering seasonally or repeatedly in one place. A settlement might later become smaller or be occupied differently as resources and sea ice changed. Long-term stability and mobility were not opposites: returning to a well-known site year after year could be part of a sophisticated mobile system.

Trade and exchange extended across the Arctic. Stone, native copper, iron, animal products, and other materials moved among communities. Some objects were imported from far beyond their eventual findspot. Metal was useful for particular edges and tools, but a metal artifact alone does not explain how it arrived. It might have passed between relatives, through multiple exchanges, or through intermittent encounters with outsiders. Nor should every artifact of unfamiliar origin be credited automatically to Norse contact. Archaeologists compare composition, context, and chronology before making such claims.

People of Norse ancestry lived in Greenland from the late tenth century into the later Middle Ages. Their chronology overlaps with the movement of Inuit ancestors into Greenland. The history of encounters is therefore a real question, but evidence for particular meetings, trade events, or violence must be assessed separately. It is tempting to stage the entire Greenlandic past as two cultures approaching one another across empty ice. In fact, both histories were embedded in different family networks and economic decisions. The Norse record is also uneven and filtered through written sources that do not speak for Inuit communities.

Parks Canada's accounts of the Beaufort Sea describe changes around the thirteenth century, including important developments in fishing and communal beluga hunting. Such regional adaptations remind us that Inuit history was not one unchanging set of tools transported intact across the continent. Techniques could develop locally. Some communities made greater use of driftwood; some hunted in environments with recurring openings in the ice. The boundaries and timing of these practices vary, and there is no reason to imagine the entire Arctic changing in synchrony.

Climate entered these choices without controlling them mechanically. Sea-ice conditions influence the access to whales, seals, and travel routes, but human responses depended on knowledge, equipment, cooperation, and alternative resources. A scholarly review of Arctic archaeology emphasizes that some areas experienced significant reorganization during colder centuries, while other places retained substantial settlements by switching toward locally available resources such as walrus. Climate history can establish patterns of temperature or ice, but cannot by itself reconstruct the political or emotional reasons why particular families moved.

Researchers also confront a history of collecting. Archaeological sites can be damaged by coastal erosion; objects have been removed from ancestral places; and older museum categories sometimes treated communities as vanished types. Inuit knowledge holders and research partners can help identify meaningful places, terms, and relationships among material objects. Collaboration matters when deciding not only how to interpret a find, but how to care for it and who should have access to it. The archaeological record is a source of evidence and a set of responsibilities.

An Inuit ancestral history is not a story that ends with the spread of a cultural tradition. People adapted through different environments, maintained knowledge, and developed traditions that continue in living communities. The label Thule helps historians organize a set of archaeological observations; it should not flatten those lives into one uniform society. The most important facts are practical and social: how to build a boat, where ice becomes unsafe, how to share a successful harvest, which materials to carry, and when to return to a winter home. These skills made the Arctic inhabited, connected, and historically dynamic long before modern national borders crossed it.
