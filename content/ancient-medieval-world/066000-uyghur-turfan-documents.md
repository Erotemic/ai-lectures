# Papers from the Oasis: Everyday Life in Medieval Turfan

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

A contract preserved on a scrap of paper can outlast the people whose agreement it records. In the oases around Turfan, in what is now the Xinjiang region of China, manuscripts survived in dry conditions long enough for modern scholars to discover a world of monasteries, merchants, scribes, farmers, and officials. Medieval Central Asia is often introduced as a road between empires. But an oasis is more than a stopping point on someone else's journey. Water must be managed, land worked, disputes settled, and religious communities supported. The documents from Turfan allow us to ask about those lives directly, even though many pages are damaged and many personal stories remain inaccessible.

The oases sit amid arid landscapes where settlement depends on reliable access to water and cultivated land. Travelers crossed mountains and deserts to reach markets and religious centers, but residents faced repeated local demands: repairing irrigation works, producing food, acquiring fuel, maintaining houses, and organizing relationships among families. A caravan could not move through a place that had no people sustaining it. The familiar phrase Silk Road can help orient a map, but it can also conceal a network made of many branches and numerous locally governed communities. Turfan was one inhabited center among several, connected to but not defined by distant courts.

The political history of the region changed when Uyghur groups moved toward the eastern Tian Shan and Gansu regions following the breakdown of their steppe empire in the ninth century. The resulting oasis polities were not simple continuations of a mobile steppe state. They interacted with peoples already living in the region and took part in existing traditions of administration, trade, and religion. The written records include Old Uyghur as well as other languages and scripts. Such variety is evidence of contact and cultural production, but language found on paper is not a census of everyone's speech. A person could use one language for a prayer and another for business.

The Berlin Turfan Collection preserves thousands of manuscript fragments and printed pieces gathered during expeditions in the early twentieth century. Simone-Christiane Raschmann's account for the Berlin State Library explains their diverse scripts, their religious and secular contents, and the difficult task of dating many pieces. The expeditions removed materials from Central Asia into German institutions, an event with its own colonial-era research history. Cataloguing and conservation now make study possible, while the dispersal of finds shapes what can be reconstructed. An archive's modern location is not evidence that a text originated with the people who collected it.

A large share of the surviving written material is religious. Old Uyghur Buddhists copied and translated texts associated with several language traditions. Manichaean communities also produced important texts; smaller bodies of Christian material survive, along with other religious and scholarly writing. This diversity matters because an oasis could connect people through institutions that were not all governed by one creed. It does not establish that every period was uniformly tolerant or that every religion enjoyed equal support. A manuscript may survive because it was deposited in a monastery, discarded in a particular room, or removed from a damaged building. Its survival can reflect a narrow setting within a larger society.

Religious manuscripts are also evidence of craftsmanship and intellectual labor. Someone prepared paper, copied characters, corrected mistakes, illustrated a page, or sponsored the production of a text. The form of a manuscript can suggest whether it was a scroll, a bound book, or a printed item. Scholars studying Old Uyghur book forms have reconstructed relationships among fragments now kept in different collections. The work is painstaking: a small piece of script may need to be compared with parallel passages, a scribal hand, or traces of a former page. This is why the attribution of a religious text involves editors, conservators, and specialists in several languages.

The secular documents take us closer to ordinary transactions. Researchers have identified contracts, letters, and administrative texts among the Turfan finds. Some concern sales and loans, while others help scholars examine property, obligations, and relationships with officials. The existence of a contract is direct evidence that at least some transactions were formally documented. But a contract does not show every bargain, especially the exchanges too small, too familiar, or too constrained by power to be written down. Nor should we invent the voices of buyers and sellers when a fragment preserves only a formula and part of a name. Document study works by distinguishing what is written from what must be inferred.

Dates can be difficult. The Berlin State Library notes that many Turfan texts are undated, and some dated pieces use a twelve-year animal cycle. Without additional context, that style of dating can point to more than one possible calendar year. Paleography, language, references to officials, archaeological findspots, and comparison with other documents help narrow the possibilities. A catalogue may date a group broadly to the tenth through thirteenth centuries rather than to an exact year. It would be irresponsible to turn that range into a precise narrative simply because a lecture sounds smoother with a single date.

A legal document also opens the question of inequality. Some Turfan contracts record people or labor in circumstances that scholars connect with unfreedom. The contracts can reveal the claims recognized by a writer or a legal system without explaining how those involved experienced the arrangement. When interpreting such terms, a historian must distinguish between sale of land, transfer of a service obligation, debt, and the sale of a person. The documents' fragmentary condition and translated vocabulary can make those distinctions difficult. It is important neither to sanitize coercion as an ordinary commodity exchange nor to imply that every household lived under identical constraints.

The relationships among land, monasteries, and officials are another recurring problem. Religious institutions needed resources to support residents and ritual activity. A farm or garden might contribute produce, payments, or work. Local authorities could intervene in property and tax matters, while political overlords changed with broader Central Asian history. But a monk's manuscript and a land contract represent different participants and different institutional aims. They cannot be combined into an imagined universal system without further evidence. The most productive question is how particular arrangements worked for named places and people, then whether wider comparisons are warranted.

We also need to ask whose writing has been preserved. Manuscripts associated with religious centers can overrepresent literate specialists. Commercial and legal papers can overrepresent people with property or reasons to document debts. Farmers, laborers, and travelers may appear in records produced by others, if at all. Women may be present in particular contracts and letters without leaving a continuous record of their own. Historians should not convert a silence in the archive into a claim that a group was absent from oasis society. They should record what the surviving genre was designed to document and what it omitted.

The scripts themselves carry a history of transmission. The Old Uyghur script developed from Sogdian writing traditions, one sign of the region's complex links with earlier Central Asian communities. Buddhist works were translated from Chinese, Sanskrit, Tocharian, and other languages. A translation was not a passive pipeline; scribes and scholars made decisions about terminology, form, and explanation. The Berlin-Brandenburg Academy's Turfan Studies project continues editing these materials, with catalogues of religious works and economic documents. Naming the editors matters because medieval texts are seldom available to us without modern reconstruction and interpretation.

Political developments beyond the oases eventually affected local life. Turfan's rulers negotiated or submitted to changing regional powers, including the Mongol world in the thirteenth century. Yet the arrival of an imperial overlord does not mean every contract was suddenly rewritten or that every household changed its language. Scholars need local dates and records to identify which institutions persisted, changed, or disappeared. The diversity of manuscripts across centuries suggests continuities and transformations, not one uninterrupted golden age. Empires can provide a chronological frame, but they rarely supply the whole history of an oasis.

There is another reason to keep the evidence precise. The region's modern political situation affects how its past is presented, collected, and claimed. Historical Uyghur states, medieval languages, and modern Uyghur communities belong to related but not identical historical questions. A text in Old Uyghur should not be forced into a contemporary national border or used as proof that an entire medieval population had one modern identity. At the same time, the history of local language and scholarship should not vanish beneath the phrase foreign influence. The oases were places where people produced documents and culture, not just places where armies and caravans passed.

What would we know about Turfan if the paper had not survived? We might see ruins of temples and canals, but many acts of buying, borrowing, translating, and corresponding would be far harder to recover. The manuscripts do not eliminate uncertainty. Their dates are sometimes broad, their surfaces broken, and their voices socially selective. They do something more valuable than filling every gap: they change the questions we can ask. Central Asian history becomes a history of households, institutions, literacy, and contested resources, alongside the history of imperial borders. The oasis was not an empty interval between great powers. It was a society with its own records and decisions.
