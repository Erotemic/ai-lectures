# Kingdoms between the Empires: Kush, Aksum, Arabia, and Iran

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

Imagine a map of the ancient world that gives Rome a bright color, draws a broad shape for Han China, and places everything between them in pale gray. Such a map is convenient for comparing two famous imperial courts, but it hides many governments and communities that organized trade, farming, religious life, and diplomacy on their own terms. Between about 300 BCE and 300 CE, the Nile valley south of Egypt, the Ethiopian and Eritrean highlands, the Arabian Peninsula, Mesopotamia, and the Iranian plateau were not empty corridors between more important places. They contained states with their own sources of wealth, forms of rule, and struggles over power.

South of Egypt, the Kingdom of Kush had a long history connected to, but not reducible to, Egyptian history. In the later centuries BCE and the first centuries CE, Meroe, in what is now Sudan, was a major political and cultural center. Its rulers commissioned temples and pyramids, and its artisans made ceramics, metalwork, and jewelry. Caravans and Nile routes linked communities inside the kingdom and beyond it. The city did not exist merely because Romans wished to buy African goods. Royal power relied on local production and labor, while regional environments placed demands on water supply and agriculture. Archaeological sites at Meroe, Naqa, and Musawwarat es-Sufra reveal activity beyond a single royal palace.

Kushite relations with Rome included both conflict and negotiation. After Rome absorbed Egypt in 30 BCE, forces from the south and Roman armies fought along the frontier. Ancient accounts associate a Kushite ruler, often identified as the kandake Amanirenas, with military resistance in the late first century BCE. The title kandake was connected with royal women; it should not automatically be interpreted through a later European idea of a queen. The details of the campaigns reach us partly through Roman writers who had their own assumptions about foreign rulers. An eventual settlement stabilized relations for a time. Neither side's narrative makes the communities of the frontier invisible, although too often we tell the story as if only commanders crossed that landscape.

The Meroitic writing system is a reminder of the limits of textual evidence. Scholars can identify the sounds of its signs to a significant extent, but the language remains only partially understood. A surviving royal inscription is therefore not like a modern report waiting to be translated word for word. Its location, arrangement, titles, and archaeological context can still reveal important things. Combined with buildings, graves, and imported objects, inscriptions support histories of ritual, power, and exchange. They do not yet furnish a continuous narrative of what every ruler thought or how every district was managed. It would be equally wrong to treat this partial decipherment as proof that the people had no literature or political ideas.

Farther southeast, a different state developed around Aksum in the highlands of the northern Horn of Africa. The Aksumite kingdom's rise is most visible from the early centuries of the common era, although its roots reach further back into regional societies. Highland agriculture and political centers were linked to Red Sea routes through places such as Adulis. Later Aksumite rulers issued coins and monuments, and some of their inscriptions provide named evidence for royal acts. We must be careful with chronology: features famous from the fourth century onward, including Aksum's adoption of Christianity, should not be projected wholesale onto the kingdom's first-century beginnings. At this stage, its commercial and political world already had multiple ties around the Red Sea.

Across that sea, Arabian communities were participants, not passive recipients, in regional commerce. Southern Arabian polities drew resources from agriculture, caravan networks, and traffic through ports and inland routes. Aromatic resins such as frankincense were valuable in religious and domestic settings around the wider region. Yet the familiar picture of a single Incense Road is too simple. Routes changed, traders negotiated with local powers, and the value of commodities depended on many separate markets. In northwestern Arabia and the southern Levant, the Nabataeans developed a kingdom centered in part on Petra, where engineering and water management supported settlement in a dry landscape. Their political and cultural history extends well beyond a picturesque image of rock-cut facades.

The Nabataean kingdom was annexed by Rome in 106 CE and became part of the Roman province of Arabia. That administrative event did not cause local languages and legal habits to disappear. Documents from the Dead Sea region, including legal papyri associated with a woman called Babatha in the second century CE, demonstrate how property, marriage, litigation, and multilingual documents shaped provincial life. They also remind us that women could take part in economic transactions under specific local legal circumstances; we cannot infer the condition of all women in Arabia from one archive. State boundaries changed, but family obligations, property disputes, and the need to present a claim before officials continued.

Look east toward the Iranian plateau and Mesopotamia, and another large political formation comes into view. The Arsacid, or Parthian, dynasty ruled extensive territories between roughly the third century BCE and 224 CE, though the degree of central control varied. Iranian ruling houses, cities, and regional elites negotiated power, and the political landscape shifted through conflict. Rome and Parthia fought repeatedly over contested zones, especially Armenia and Mesopotamia. In 53 BCE a Roman expedition under Crassus suffered a major defeat at Carrhae. The event is famous in Roman political memory, but from the perspective of Parthian communities, the Roman army was one threat among several in a much wider system of regional rivalries.

Parthian authority rested partly on political relationships with local dynasties and powerful families, rather than a uniformly imposed model of provincial administration. In Mesopotamian cities, different languages, religious communities, and commercial practices persisted. Ctesiphon, on the Tigris, grew into a major royal center. Roman incursions captured or attacked it at different times, and war affected the population of the region far more directly than a simple list of exchanged territories suggests. In 224 CE the Sasanian dynasty displaced the Arsacid rulers. That transition belongs to the long history of Iranian political power, not to a tale in which Persia vanished after Alexander and reappeared only as Rome's enemy.

Between Iranian and northern Indian worlds, the Kushan rulers created another connected realm in the early centuries CE. Its political roots lay among Central Asian peoples who moved west amid earlier conflicts and migrations. Kushan rulers came to command portions of what are now Afghanistan, Pakistan, and northern India. Their coinage, inscriptions, and art show contacts with several cultural traditions, including Iranian, Indian, and Greek-derived forms. In Gandhara, Buddhist institutions attracted patrons and donations, while trade routes connected cities and monasteries. A Buddhist image in a Greek-influenced style is evidence of artistic exchange, not evidence that the region had lost its own religious or social history.

These worlds were linked but not merged. The Nile, the Red Sea, the Arabian Sea, the Tigris, and Central Asian land routes carried different sets of travelers and goods. A merchant handling ivory in an African port, a trader selling textiles in Mesopotamia, and a pilgrim supporting a Gandharan religious center did not necessarily share one commercial network or know the same rulers. States attempted to tax and protect traffic, but routes also depended on independent guides, caravan organizers, sailors, farmers, and port workers. Economic connection often crossed political borders without eliminating them.

The sources are uneven. Egyptian and Roman writings say more about some frontier encounters than we can recover from surviving Kushite texts. Monumental Aksumite evidence is stronger for certain later centuries than for the kingdom's beginnings. The Nabataean and Parthian worlds survive through inscriptions, buildings, coins, and texts produced by outsiders as well as local communities. Historians gain confidence by bringing these sources together while refusing to make silence into absence. Archaeology can show a town that a court historian never thought worth naming, and a legal papyrus can disclose a household whose existence a royal monument would never acknowledge.

There was no empty middle between Rome and Han China. Nor did Africa and Arabia serve only as transit zones for someone else's commerce. Kush, Aksum, Nabataea, Parthian Iran, and the Kushan realm demonstrate that ancient political life was organized at different scales, with different institutions and histories. Their rulers could fight empires, negotiate with them, or become powerful centers of exchange themselves. Their subjects cultivated fields, made objects, sought justice, practiced religion, and endured the demands of government. To restore these lives to our picture, we have to color in the spaces that imperial maps so often leave blank.
