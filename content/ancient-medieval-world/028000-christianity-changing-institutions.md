# Many Churches, Changing Empires: Christianity in Late Antiquity

In Ravenna, two baptisteries from late antiquity preserve mosaics of the baptism of Jesus. A visitor can admire both without necessarily noticing that their commissioners belonged to Christian communities with sharply different understandings of the relationship between Jesus and God. These buildings were made in a world where rulers, bishops, monks, and ordinary worshippers debated matters they considered central to salvation and communal life. There was no single afternoon when “the church” was invented, defined for everyone, and left unchanged. Christianity developed through texts, ritual, disputes, political patronage, local communities, and institutions stretching far beyond the Roman imperial frontier.

Christianity began among Jewish communities in the eastern Mediterranean in the first century CE, in response to the life and teachings of Jesus of Nazareth and his followers' convictions about his death and resurrection. Those convictions are religious beliefs held by Christians; historians study how they were expressed and transmitted rather than independently confirming supernatural events. The letters associated with Paul and the writings that later formed the New Testament preserve some of the earliest surviving Christian testimony. They also show disagreements about communal rules, identity, worship, and authority. Christian groups existed long before an emperor decided to support them.

During the second and third centuries, communities spread across cities and regions of the Roman Empire and beyond it. They organized gatherings, support for members, rituals, and teaching. Bishops and other leaders gradually acquired authority that could be challenged by rivals. Surviving texts often present one interpretation as the rightful faith and others as errors. Such works are evidence of the debates, not a substitute for independent description of all participating groups. The existence of competing gospel traditions, interpretations, and local practices makes the early Christian world historically rich and difficult to summarize with a single set of beliefs.

Roman policies toward Christians varied by place and time. Some periods brought local hostility or episodic persecution; other years allowed communities to grow openly. The persecutions associated with Diocletian in the early fourth century were serious, but they did not affect every province identically. Constantine's rise brought major changes. In 313, the arrangements traditionally associated with Constantine and Licinius provided for religious toleration and restitution of confiscated Christian property. Constantine patronized church institutions and participated in disputes. Yet the legalization and privileging of Christianity should not be confused with the immediate disappearance of other religious traditions or with full agreement among Christians.

The Council of Nicaea in 325 gathered bishops to address disputes including the relationship between the Father and the Son. It produced a creed and decisions that became important within many later Christian traditions. But a council is an event in a continuing argument. Theological language changed, and rival interpretations persisted despite imperial support for one position. Bishops could be exiled or recalled as rulers and councils changed policies. To know what an imperial council declared is not to know what every person in a Syrian village, Egyptian monastery, or western Roman town believed the following morning.

By the end of the fourth century, imperial law increasingly endorsed forms of Christianity regarded as orthodox by the court. Theodosius the First's legislation was significant, but laws and social practice did not always align. Local churches gained property and influence, and clerical offices became important parts of civic life. Bishops might negotiate with armies, distribute charity, settle disputes, and sponsor buildings. They could also participate in coercion against rival religious communities. Support for the poor could coexist with the accumulation of institutional wealth. The church's growth therefore belongs to social and political history as well as to the history of ideas.

Monastic movements took distinctive forms in Egypt, Syria, Palestine, and elsewhere. Some individuals pursued solitary lives of discipline and prayer. Others joined organized communities with rules about work, worship, property, and leadership. Pachomian communities in Egypt and the wider tradition associated with Basil in the Greek-speaking east contributed to different forms of collective religious life. Monasteries copied texts and sometimes held land; they also depended on labor, gifts, and local relationships. Monasticism was neither completely outside society nor simply another arm of imperial administration. Its practitioners could criticize the priorities of wealthy patrons while relying on material support.

Debates became particularly consequential in the fifth century. The Council of Ephesus in 431 and the Council of Chalcedon in 451 addressed disputes about how to describe Jesus's divine and human reality, as well as questions of episcopal authority. Chalcedon's formulation of Christ as one person in two natures gained official support in the eastern Roman Empire. Other churches developed different theological languages and rejected the council's authority. It is misleading to describe their beliefs only by hostile labels assigned by opponents. For example, many churches now called Oriental Orthodox describe their teaching as miaphysite, affirming a united divine-human nature rather than simply denying Christ's humanity.

The differences were not confined to Constantinople. Egyptian Christian traditions developed in Coptic alongside Greek. Syriac-speaking communities in western Asia produced theology, poetry, biblical translation, and histories that cannot be reduced to the Byzantine court's agenda. Churches of the East developed important institutions in Sasanian territory, with their own administrative and theological history. Armenia and the kingdom of Aksum also formed Christian communities connected to but not ruled in daily religious matters by every Roman emperor. The movement of bishops, merchants, pilgrims, books, and artisans connected these worlds without making them institutionally identical.

Architecture and images provide another kind of evidence. The mosaics of Ravenna, the churches of Constantinople, and the church buildings of Aksum or Egypt testify to patronage and ritual space. Their decoration may celebrate rulers, saints, and theological claims. A building can tell us something about the resources available to a community and the kinds of objects it deemed appropriate for worship. It cannot tell us by itself whether every worshipper understood the same doctrinal arguments. Excavation may reveal changes in a site's use that written accounts ignore, but archaeology also depends on what has survived and been investigated.

Texts were copied, taught, translated, and disputed. Greek and Latin Christian literature circulated alongside Syriac, Coptic, Armenian, Ge'ez, and other traditions. Some texts survive because powerful churches preserved them; other voices are represented mainly through quotations by their opponents. This archive is not neutral. A historian who reads only later declarations of orthodoxy will miss disagreements and local experiences. Manuscripts must be dated, attributed, and compared. The exact wording of an ancient text may depend on a chain of scribes and translators rather than the author's original manuscript lying before us.

Religion also shaped relations with other communities. Christians and Jews lived alongside one another across the Mediterranean and western Asia, sometimes sharing spaces or intellectual traditions, sometimes experiencing conflict and discriminatory policy. Zoroastrian, Manichaean, and other religious traditions had their own institutions. In some cities, rulers' attempts to impose a religious boundary brought violence; in others, practical coexistence endured. The simple sentence “the empire became Christian” cannot describe all these relations. It describes a change in public authority and patronage more readily than a single universal change of personal belief.

In the seventh century, war and the growth of Muslim-ruled states changed the political environment of Christian communities in Syria, Palestine, Egypt, and Mesopotamia. Some churches found themselves outside direct Byzantine rule while continuing their liturgy, learning, and local administration. Greek, Syriac, Coptic, and increasingly Arabic became languages through which these communities negotiated new circumstances. Later histories sometimes portray this transformation as the immediate end of a Christian Near East. Evidence instead shows Christian communities persisting, adapting, and debating across many centuries. The timing of change differed significantly from place to place.

Christianity's late antique history is therefore a story of institutions as much as ideas: who could appoint a bishop, build a church, resolve a dispute, protect a manuscript, collect revenue, or define the boundaries of a community. It is also a story in which sincere beliefs matter to people, even when historians do not claim to adjudicate their truth. The two baptisteries at Ravenna remain witnesses to different Christian worlds occupying the same city. Their coexistence reminds us that a religion can have shared texts and sacred figures while developing through complex, sometimes bitter arguments about what those things mean.
