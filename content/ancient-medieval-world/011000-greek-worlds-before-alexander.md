# Greek Worlds before Alexander: Citizenship, Conflict, and the Polis

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

A person arriving in ancient Athens might find a busy harbor, a market, sanctuaries, workshops, and public spaces where political speeches were heard. If that visitor had asked who governed, the answer would have depended on the period. It would also have depended on whom one meant by the people. In the fifth century BCE, Athens allowed a body of male citizens to make consequential decisions directly. Women, enslaved people, and resident foreigners lived and worked in the city but were excluded from that political body. To understand Greek government, we must hold both facts in view: a striking experiment in citizen participation and a society with sharply unequal rights.

The Greek-speaking world was not one country. It included numerous communities on the Aegean islands, the mainland, the coast of Anatolia, southern Italy, Sicily, and around the Black Sea. Some were large and powerful; others were small. Each polis, or city-state, had its own institutions and ways of defining membership. The term polis refers not just to an urban place but to a political community, often with surrounding farmland and dependent settlements. The histories of Athens and Sparta are important, but neither can stand in for all Greeks. A city with different offices, alliances, or patterns of landholding could be recognizably Greek without adopting Athenian democracy.

During the eighth and seventh centuries BCE, Greek-speaking groups established new settlements across the Mediterranean and Black Sea regions. Older accounts often call these colonies, but that word needs care. Some new communities developed close relations with a founding city; others made their own laws and identities. Settlement brought Greeks into relationships with Indigenous peoples who already inhabited those places. Trade, intermarriage, cooperation, conflict, and dispossession varied by location. It is a mistake to draw arrows outward from Greece on a map and treat the receiving coasts as empty. The sea network was a meeting of societies with their own previous histories.

The new settlements moved grain, pottery, metal, wine, oil, and ideas. Households on the mainland relied on fields and livestock, but maritime exchange could help cities reach materials they lacked. Artisan workshops produced pottery and metalwork whose surviving forms now let archaeologists trace contacts. A cup found far from where it was manufactured is evidence of movement, not automatically proof that its owner was Greek. Material culture travels through trade, gifts, imitation, and people. The careful historian uses those possibilities to avoid making every object on the map an ethnic flag.

Greek institutions changed through political struggle. Aristocratic families held influence in many cities, while indebtedness, military service, conflicts over land, and demands from other citizens created pressure for reform. In Athens, Solon is associated with early sixth-century measures addressing debt and social conflict. Later the city passed through the rule of the Peisistratid tyrants. The word tyrant in this setting originally described a form of personal rule outside established arrangements, not always a checklist of personal cruelty. Around 508 and 507 BCE, reforms associated with Cleisthenes reorganized citizen participation and helped create the foundations of Athenian democracy.

In classical Athens, the assembly allowed eligible male citizens to debate and vote. Councils and many offices were filled by lot, while some positions, notably military commands, were elected. Large citizen juries heard cases. This was not representative government in the modern parliamentary sense, although not every person attended every meeting. The opportunity to speak and vote did not mean that wealth, reputation, rhetorical skill, and patronage ceased to matter. Nor did one policy remain fixed for centuries. Institutions were revised, sometimes interrupted, and tested by war. Democracy was a historical practice with rules and conflicts, not an abstract idea perfectly realized.

Participation had boundaries. The citizen body excluded women from formal assembly and jury participation. Enslaved people labored in households, agriculture, workshops, and other settings, with very unequal circumstances. Resident foreigners, often called metics in the classical period, contributed to economic and cultural life without citizen voting rights. The historical development of the metic category itself is debated; we should not simply project the fully formed fifth-century distinction into every earlier century. Restricting membership made the democracy possible in its specific form, even while much of the city's work was done by people outside its political definition of the people.

Sparta organized power and social life differently. Its institutions included kings, councils, and a restricted body of full citizens, and its military arrangements acquired a durable reputation. Much agricultural production depended on helots, a subordinated population especially associated with Messenia and Laconia. The risk of revolt and the need to maintain domination shaped political and military priorities. Ancient descriptions of Sparta often come from outsiders, some of whom admired its discipline or criticized its way of life. We should ask how far a surviving portrayal describes actual institutions and how far it turns Sparta into an argument about the virtues or dangers of a particular kind of government.

Wars between Greek states were part of the political story. The Persian Wars in the early fifth century have often been narrated as a defense of Greek liberty against an eastern empire. But Greek communities had conflicting interests, some supported Persia, and Persia was itself a complex collection of peoples. After those wars, Athens led a maritime alliance that increasingly became an empire funded by contributions from allies and subjects. At the same time that Athenians participated in self-government at home, they used force to constrain other cities. The tension is not a modern accusation imposed on the past; it is visible in the political history of the alliance itself.

The long conflict between Athens and Sparta, known as the Peloponnesian War, began in 431 BCE and involved many other communities. Thucydides wrote a history of it that remains central to political thought. He claimed to investigate causes and carefully distinguished some speeches he reconstructed from the events he reported. His work is not a recording device. It is a deliberate literary and analytical interpretation by a participant in a contested world. War brought disease, displacement, sieges, and the disruption of farms and trade. To read only the strategies of generals is to miss the households and allied communities that paid for those decisions.

Greek intellectual life also took shape within these institutions. Philosophers asked what justice was, who should rule, and whether laws expressed knowledge or convention. Dramatists staged questions of family duty, gender, violence, and civic authority. Poets and artists produced works that later societies would preserve and reinterpret. Women appear as authors, performers, religious participants, and patrons in particular contexts, though the archive is uneven; the poet Sappho is one prominent case. The achievements of this world should not be reduced to a short list of famous men, nor should their institutions be mistaken for the lives of all their inhabitants.

By the fourth century BCE, Greek states continued to contest power, and the kingdom of Macedon under Philip II became increasingly influential. His son Alexander would lead campaigns that radically altered the eastern Mediterranean and much of western Asia. But Greek history before Alexander is not merely the preparation for one conqueror. It is a history of many experiments in political membership, of unequal labor and rights, of cultural exchange, and of the consequences when communities tried to govern themselves while competing to govern others. The central question is not whether the polis anticipated the modern nation. It is what its citizens and noncitizens were able, and compelled, to do.

## Companion: different populations, different constitutions

Aristotle's later political analysis, Thucydides' composed history, and other Greek texts illuminate Greek institutions but represent particular authors. Archaeology and inscriptional evidence help constrain economic and chronological claims. The meaning and timing of metic status is debated, and the Athenian model must not be generalized to all Greek settlements or to modern inclusive democracy. Ancient literary descriptions of Sparta often reflect outsiders' interests.
