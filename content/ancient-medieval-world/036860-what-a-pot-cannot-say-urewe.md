# What a Pot Cannot Say: Urewe across the Great Lakes

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

A pot survives where a person's name does not. Its rim may be broken, its base missing, and its contents long gone. Yet the clay can preserve the pressure of fingers and the marks of a tool. Around Africa's Great Lakes, archaeologists find vessels with an especially recognizable combination of shapes and incised decoration. They call the related pottery Urewe, after a site in western Kenya. For a long time, these objects seemed to offer a tidy answer to a difficult question: who first brought farming and iron to these landscapes? The more closely researchers study the pots, the less tidy that answer becomes.

The Great Lakes are not a single ecological zone. Lake Victoria has fishing shores, islands, wetlands, and the slopes of surrounding hills. Farther west, the highlands of present-day Rwanda and Burundi rise between other lakes and river systems. A community could find quite different opportunities even a modest journey away: pasture, forest products, cultivable land, clay suitable for pots, and places to obtain ore or charcoal. When a type of pottery appears across this region, it does not follow that all its users lived in the same way. The geography alone warns against a map with one color and one kind of household.

A diagnostic Urewe vessel may have an indented, or dimpled, base, a carefully shaped rim, and arrangements of incised lines or impressed marks. Archaeologists compare these features in excavated fragments, often together with the vessel's fabric, thickness, and methods of construction. Not every fragment displays every feature. A small sherd with a decorated band may permit a confident comparison, while an undecorated body fragment may tell us little. When researchers classify a collection, they are assembling a pattern from incomplete objects, not reading a manufacturer's label with a date printed on it.

The chronology stretches across many centuries, with early occurrences in parts of the western lake region during the first millennium before the common era and later versions continuing into the first millennium of the common era. Different sites are not necessarily contemporary. Dates depend on the material sampled and the security of its association with the pottery. A charcoal sample from a hearth can help date the deposit in which sherds were found, but it does not date the moment when every vessel in that deposit was made. Broad labels such as Early Urewe and Transitional Urewe are working archaeological classifications rather than a clock that ran identically around every lake.

At Kakapel Rockshelter in western Kenya, the details become tangible. Excavators found a small Urewe-bearing area with rims, incised and impressed decoration, and a characteristic dimpled fragment. In the same long archaeological sequence, researchers documented food remains, changing occupation practices, and eventually directly dated crops. A Urewe pot there belongs to a history of actual meals and work, but it cannot by itself tell us the language of the person who cooked with it. Nor does a crop found at Kakapel prove that the people who shaped every Urewe pot elsewhere grew that same crop.

In 2026, a team led by Musa Mwitondi published a preliminary investigation of the Mara region southeast of Lake Victoria. At Balima, Mkirira, and Nyabekwabi, their work brought together stone tools, pottery, animal remains, beads, ochre, and other traces. The survey also recorded Urewe and other Iron Age pottery traditions in the wider region. One sherd from Nyabekwabi resembled manufacturing practices known at some Kansyore sites, but the authors specifically declined to assign it confidently to that tradition. Their restraint matters: the next excavation could change the classification, and the sampled sites still require firmer chronological work.

Why was Urewe once made into a larger historical story? The spread of Bantu languages into much of central, eastern, and southern Africa is a real and important subject of historical research. Linguists compare related words and grammatical features to reconstruct earlier connections; archaeologists identify changing pottery, settlements, farming, and metallurgy. Since early Urewe often appears in settings associated with iron or food production, it was tempting to make the three lines of evidence stand for one migrating population. But a person may learn a pottery style from neighbors, acquire a vessel through exchange, adopt a crop, or change language without carrying all of the other practices along.

Ceri Ashley's study of first- and second-millennium ceramics examined pots not only as ethnic labels or chronological markers but as things people used. Size, wear, form, and cooking context can raise questions about what was prepared, who used the vessel, and whether some communities specialized in fishing or other lakeshore work. Ashley argued for continuities from Urewe into later ceramic traditions, alongside changing social relationships. This is an interpretation built from comparative evidence, not an engraved record naming the owners of each cooking pot. It nevertheless shows why a vessel's practical life can be more revealing than a simple cultural name.

Consider an imaginary collection assembled in a museum drawer. One Urewe sherd comes from a household hearth; another lies beside iron-smelting debris; a third was recovered from a small lakeside deposit containing fishing equipment. Similarity in decoration may justify placing them within a broad ceramic tradition. It does not force the hearth to belong to an ironworker, or the fishing settlement to be founded by newcomers. Before making a claim about livelihood, we would inspect the animals, seeds, furnaces, tools, spatial distribution, and dates actually found at each place. The collection is hypothetical; the archaeological distinction is real.

There is a related danger in the phrase "arrival of the Iron Age." Iron, farming, pottery, and stone technology did not always arrive together or replace earlier practices overnight. Some communities used iron tools while continuing to work stone. Some acquired domestic plants and animals while retaining fishing or gathering. Some pots of one tradition occur beside objects that an older classification scheme would put in a different period. These combinations do not indicate that the excavation is necessarily confused. They can be evidence of long contact between neighbors with different skills, preferences, and resources.

The word Urewe is still useful. Archaeologists need names for recurring material patterns, and comparing pottery across sites helps build regional chronology. But the object of history is not the label. It is the people who dug clay, formed vessels, fetched water, kindled fires, cooked meals, passed on skills, and exchanged goods across an uneven landscape. Pottery can make those actions visible in fragments. It cannot identify a single ancestry, language, or political system for everyone who left such fragments behind. The strongest history begins with that difference between what the pot tells us and what we still have to ask.

## Evidence boundary

A ceramic style is not a language, ethnicity, or securely dated migration event. The 2026 Mara excavations are preliminary; the Nyabekwabi sherd is explicitly not firmly assigned. Regional chronologies, dates for particular archaeological contexts, and individual vessels must not be collapsed into one dated cultural frontier.
