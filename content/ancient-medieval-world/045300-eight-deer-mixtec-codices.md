# Eight Deer and the Painted History: Reading Mixtec Books

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

Open a painted Mixtec screenfold and the story may begin from a direction unfamiliar to a reader of modern books. The Codex Zouche-Nuttall is made from joined, prepared animal skin, painted and folded into a compact object. Images and signs flow across its surfaces; the reader follows characters, calendrical names, places and paths between scenes. It is not illustrated prose with captions removed. It is an intellectual technology for telling history, especially the history of ruling families, whose forms of record keeping did not depend on the alphabet brought by Europeans.

One side records histories and genealogies of important Mixtec centers. The other includes the political biography of a ruler conventionally known as Eight Deer Jaguar Claw. Names such as Eight Deer use the day of birth in a ritual calendar; an additional identifying name distinguishes a person from others who might share the same day sign. The fact that a person can be named in a calendar does not mean a text merely lists dates. Images of journeys, meetings, marriages, ritual acts and warfare turn chronology into an argument about legitimate power.

The book preserves stories centered on Oaxaca's highlands and coast. Tilantongo in the Mixteca Alta and the coastal center of Tututepec became linked in the remembered career of Eight Deer. Yet a connection in a manuscript need not mean that both places were administered as one continuous kingdom. The historian asks which scene portrays a ritual claim, which a political alliance, and which a conquest, then checks what excavations show about towns, routes and settlement growth. A painted history can be precise about genealogies while remaining selective about the experiences of ordinary households.

Eight Deer is presented as a ruler whose career involved travel, military alliances and claims to legitimate authority in several centers. In some interpretations, the stories connect the founding of Tututepec with his actions and trace a later assertion of power in Tilantongo. They also include ritual acts that later historians cannot translate into only secular diplomacy. A place could be claimed through kinship, ceremonies, alliances and war. Turning every painted meeting into a treaty or every procession into a battle would flatten the record and erase the institutions through which leaders made their authority meaningful.

Archaeologists working in the lower Río Verde valley have investigated the history of Tututepec through settlement survey and excavation. The archaeological picture supports its emergence as an important Late Postclassic center and shows substantial later expansion. This does not prove that every chapter of Eight Deer's life occurred exactly as one version presents it. Conversely, gaps in excavation do not automatically make an Indigenous account fictional. The best comparison asks whether the scale, date and location of settlement changes are compatible with the political patterns remembered in a manuscript.

Other books supply versions of overlapping history. The Codex Colombino, now held in Mexico and digitized through a Library of Congress catalog, also records the political and ritual deeds associated with Eight Deer and other rulers. It is connected materially and historically with the Becker I fragment. The survival of more than one source is valuable precisely because versions can disagree. A historian can compare which ancestors are given prominence, which alliances are remembered, and which settings are emphasized. A difference is not simply an error to be corrected: it may reveal a community's interest in one lineage or claim.

Calendrical notation made a historical biography more than an undated legend. A person’s day-name, a year sign, and sequences of events could place claims within a remembered chronology. Modern scholars develop correlations among calendrical cycles and compare different books. But the symbols are not read as though they form one universal Mesoamerican language. Mixtec pictorial writing had conventions and readers of its own. The skill lies in understanding relationships among iconographic forms, lineages, geography and chronology, including cases in which the interpretation of one sign remains disputed.

Women appear in these genealogies because marriages and descent were central to political history. A marriage could bring claims to a place or join ruling lines whose authority lay in different communities. Yet the narrative usually gives priority to the dynastic work a woman performed, not the whole range of her choices or experiences. We should not mistake a political genealogy for evidence that women outside the court were inactive, or that all households experienced political change in the same way. A manuscript preserves named actors selected for a particular argument about rule.

The manuscript's later journey is part of its history. By the nineteenth century the Zouche-Nuttall appeared in a Dominican monastery in Florence and then passed through a British aristocratic collection. The British Museum eventually acquired it, and a facsimile associated with Zelia Nuttall's scholarship helped researchers study its images. Those collecting histories do not tell us precisely who made the book or how it left Oaxaca. They do remind us that surviving knowledge was shaped by colonial disruption, private collections and the long effort to identify and publish material that was no longer in its place of origin.

Scholarly editions now divide the screenfold into episodes and sections, but those divisions are tools for readers. The original painter did not necessarily conceive of the work as chapters in a modern biography. A physical turn in an accordion book, a change of direction, or a repeated ancestral figure can organize the meaning. Reproduction helps many more people consult the imagery, but a reproduction also mediates the object's scale and color. Conservators and readers of the original must attend to pigment, folding and damage, not simply the translated sequence of names.

A painted history like the Zouche-Nuttall challenges a common boundary between archaeology and history. We have the names and political claims of people who lived long before European contact, but the source is neither a transparent chronicle nor a list of rulers for a museum label. Its evidence comes from an Indigenous way of organizing time, lineage and political legitimacy. Read beside Tututepec archaeology and other codices, the book helps reconstruct a contested world of regional power. Read alone, it is already evidence of a sustained practice of historical thought.

## Evidence boundary

Mixtec painted books encode dates, lineages, alliances and political claims but are not phonetic transcripts or independent eyewitness accounts of every event. Codex Zouche-Nuttall survives as a preconquest manuscript recording much earlier histories; narratives differ across codices. Birth-year and accession reconstructions vary in accessible guides, so exact dates are omitted when they are not needed.
