# Courts across the Water: Korea and Japan in Medieval East Asia

Imagine a Buddhist text traveling across the sea, accompanied by a monk, a merchant, or an envoy. On the Korean peninsula, it might be studied in a temple supported by aristocratic patrons. In the Japanese archipelago, it could enter a courtly world that was developing new literary forms. The text may have come through a network connected to China, yet its meaning was not fixed by the place it started. Between roughly 750 and 1200, Korean and Japanese societies transformed inherited institutions and created new ones. Their histories belong within East Asian connections, but neither society should be described as a copy of a Chinese original.

At the beginning of this period, the Silla kingdom dominated much of the Korean peninsula. Its rise followed long struggles involving Baekje, Goguryeo, and alliances and conflicts with Tang China. The term Unified Silla is conventional, but it should not be confused with control over all regions historically inhabited by Korean-speaking communities or peoples tied to earlier northern kingdoms. To the north, the state known as Balhae, or Parhae, developed from the late seventh century and lasted until 926. It maintained its own relations with neighboring states. A peninsula-centered map that ignores Balhae leaves out an important part of the period's regional political landscape.

Gyeongju, the Silla capital, was a center of elite culture, government, and Buddhism. Religious patronage produced buildings and sculptural programs through which rulers and wealthy families expressed their aspirations. The eighth-century Seokguram grotto and Bulguksa temple complex demonstrate the skill and resources involved. Their carved figures and stonework are not evidence that every inhabitant lived like a court patron. They also depended on quarrying, transport, specialized labor, food production, and the organization of donations. The buildings are therefore sources for institutional and economic history as well as for Buddhist art. Their later restoration histories must be distinguished from their original construction.

Silla society contained influential aristocratic families whose status affected officeholding and marriage. Economic and political competition helped weaken the court from the later ninth century. Local commanders and rival polities grew stronger; what historians call the Later Three Kingdoms period brought new wars and political claims. Wang Geon founded the Goryeo dynasty in 918. Goryeo consolidated authority over much of the peninsula by 936, while also facing frontier challenges and maintaining relationships with other East Asian states. This was not simply the replacement of one national dynasty by another. It involved regional alignments, military power, elite negotiation, and changes in the institutions used to govern.

Under Goryeo, Buddhist monasteries and court patronage remained important. Temples could hold land, commission images, and influence social life. Artistic production, including ceramics, later became especially associated with this dynasty, but neither a beautiful vessel nor a court-sponsored ritual describes the whole of Korean society. Farming households, artisans, traders, and communities in border regions lived under different pressures. Goryeo rulers also developed administrative practices drawing on several models, adapted to local circumstances. External connections to Song, Liao, and other neighboring states were sometimes commercial, sometimes diplomatic, and sometimes hostile. A simplified 'tributary' label can conceal this range of relationships.

Across the Korea Strait, rulers of the Japanese archipelago had long engaged with Korean and Chinese states, Buddhist institutions, and immigrants who brought specialized knowledge. The Nara period preceded the establishment of a new capital at Heian-kyo, now Kyoto, in 794. Its street plan reflected continental models, yet the court subsequently developed distinctive practices of governance and literary expression. The early Heian court combined an emperor, powerful families, officials, religious institutions, and provincial interests. Political authority was not a single object that one person could simply possess. It was continually negotiated among households, ritual responsibilities, land claims, and service relationships.

The Fujiwara family's position in Heian politics illustrates the importance of marriage and kinship. Members of the family arranged marriages with the imperial house and often exercised power through regencies or access to rulers. Courtly prestige involved the performance of poetry, calligraphy, ceremony, and cultivated judgment. These practices were not merely decoration around politics; they helped determine who belonged within influential circles and how people communicated claims to status. The elaborate world described in court literature was real for a narrow section of society, but most residents of the archipelago lived far from the capital's ceremonial routines.

Written Japanese culture changed through the development and increasing use of kana scripts alongside Chinese characters. Kana made it possible to write Japanese literary forms in ways that were particularly significant for courtly poetry, diaries, and prose. Murasaki Shikibu's Tale of Genji, usually dated to the early eleventh century, emerged from this world. It portrays the emotional and political relationships of an elite court through a sophisticated work of fiction. Its literary power does not make it an eyewitness record of every event it describes. But it is a valuable source for the social expectations, aesthetic practices, and imagined interior lives of courtly circles, especially when read alongside other texts and documents.

Buddhism continued to develop in Japan through institutions and practices that had different regional and social audiences. Monasteries and temples owned land, received patronage, and took part in debates over ritual and doctrine. Religious specialists moved through networks linking Japan to the Korean peninsula and China, while local sacred sites and traditions influenced how teachings were understood. Political authority and religious life were deeply entangled. A change in an imperial decree did not necessarily produce an immediate change in village worship. As in Korea, surviving sculpture and temple records provide detailed evidence for patrons and specialists, but less for the people who maintained the sites through daily labor.

From the later Heian period, provincial military households became increasingly influential. Local land disputes and rival claims to authority brought armed groups into politics. In the twelfth century, competition between the Taira and Minamoto families culminated in the conflict conventionally called the Genpei War, ending in 1185 with Minamoto victory. These events contributed to the rise of warrior government at Kamakura, although the gradual shift of political authority cannot be reduced to one battle. Heian court institutions continued to matter, while military families drew new power from landholding and regional alliances. The transition is better understood as a rearrangement of institutions than as the total replacement of court culture by warriors.

There were many points of connection across the water, but no simple hierarchy explains them all. Korean envoys and artisans took part in exchanges with Japan; Japanese monks studied foreign religious traditions; merchants moved goods through ports; and both Korean and Japanese rulers negotiated relations with neighboring states. War and diplomacy did not prevent cultural exchange. Nor should a shared Buddhist image or writing practice be taken as proof of shared political identity. Each community selected, adapted, and gave different meanings to what moved across the sea. Local choices and inherited traditions mattered as much as distant models.

Our sources are uneven. Korean dynastic records were often compiled in later periods, and court documents focus on administrators and elites. Japanese diaries and literature preserve detailed perspectives but generally from privileged social circles. Architecture and sculpture document skills and patronage without directly recording every worker's voice. Archaeological evidence from settlements and graves adds other dimensions, as do inscriptions and commercial objects. Comparisons across Korea and Japan are fruitful only if we retain differences in dates, institutions, social structures, and the survival of evidence. The label medieval East Asia is a framework for investigation, not a claim that one story happened everywhere.

Return to the traveling Buddhist text. By the time it was read in a Korean temple or a Japanese court, its meaning had been shaped by translators, teachers, patrons, and local practice. The worlds of Silla, Goryeo, and Heian Japan were active participants in this circulation. They developed political strategies and artistic forms whose significance cannot be measured solely by their proximity to a Chinese capital. Their history is at once regional and connected: the work of officials, writers, monks, soldiers, farmers, and artisans, living in places where seas were both barriers and paths.
