# Central African exchange, chronologies, and gaps in the archaeological record

Copper exchange can connect places across Central Africa, but it cannot supply a complete chronology of the region. Burials, pottery, and gaps in dated settlement evidence add different pieces. Our task is to keep their scales and uncertainties visible while asking how people made and maintained relationships.

## Red Metal, Many Routes: Copper and Exchange in Central Africa

In the ground of the region now called the Copperbelt, across northern Zambia and the southern Democratic Republic of the Congo, lies a mineral resource with an immense later history. Modern mines can make its earlier past difficult to imagine. Yet long before industrial extraction, people mined, smelted, worked, and exchanged copper here. The archaeological record contains traces of small-scale production and objects that traveled far beyond the ore-bearing country. Among the most recognizable are pieces cast in cross-like shapes, which specialists call croisettes. Their story is one of skilled work and networks of exchange, not merely a rehearsal for modern mineral industries.

Copper is unlike iron in ways that change its history. Iron smelting typically produces a solid bloom that needs further working. Copper ores can, under suitable conditions, be reduced to metal that melts and can be cast into prepared forms. Ores, fuel, furnaces, molds, and the knowledge to control heat all mattered. The color of finished copper and the way it could be shaped made it attractive for ornaments and other objects. But an ancient copper bracelet tells us little on its own about where ore was dug. To reconstruct production, archaeologists also search for old mine workings, slag, crucibles, casting molds, and relationships between finished artifacts and known deposits.

David Phillipson's survey of the Zambian Copperbelt described the Chondwe ceramic group, identifying pottery at nineteen open sites and five rock shelters. That distribution provided evidence for widespread occupation in the region during the Early Iron Age. His research also argued that prehistoric copper mining had been undertaken there on a small scale by early iron-using communities. These conclusions were based on the surveys and collections then available. Chondwe is a ceramic and archaeological classification, not the name of a recorded dynasty, language community, or organization of miners. Copper production and pottery distribution need to be demonstrated at each site rather than assumed to coincide everywhere.

There is a long interval between early local extraction and the later wide circulation of standardized metal forms. In a 2017 study, Nicolas Nikis and Alexandre Livingstone Smith reported that production in the Copperbelt reaches back to roughly the fourth through seventh centuries of the common era, while extensive distribution is more clearly documented from around the ninth century. These dates describe categories of evidence from a broad region; they do not mean that one mine operated continuously for a thousand years. Workshops might open and cease, routes might shift, and users could melt down older copper into new shapes.

One particular form, often called the H I H type of croisette, circulated between approximately the ninth and fourteenth centuries. Cross-like ingots have been found over a region extending from the Copperbelt toward what is now Zimbabwe, including the area of Great Zimbabwe, and north toward the Upemba Depression. Researchers have also found evidence of manufacture in more than one location. This is decisive for how we interpret the map. A similar ingot found at two far-apart sites does not prove that people from those settlements traded directly with each other. Metal, molds, and forms could travel in stages; local specialists could cast a widely recognized shape using ore from a different source.

What was a cross-shaped ingot worth? The answer is not as simple as its weight. Some pieces might have served as raw material that could be cut or recast. Others occur in contexts suggesting display, status, or exchange. Later, some small and standardized examples may have functioned in transactions much like a currency, but that does not mean every earlier croisette was a coin with a fixed official value. We have no surviving price list from a ninth-century Copperbelt market to settle the question. Its shape, place of discovery, association with other objects, and period all bear on the inference.

The Upemba Depression offers evidence that value could change as copper entered a different social setting. In burials from the period archaeologists call Kabambian A, roughly the thirteenth to mid-fifteenth centuries, some large cross-shaped ingots accompanied the dead. Finds of copper in graves can register distinctions in wealth and ceremonial display; they do not establish the deceased person's precise office or the procedure by which the object was acquired. Nikis and Livingstone Smith emphasize that particular shapes cluster geographically and chronologically. A change in that distribution may indicate a reorganized trade route, a difference in political influence, a shift in demand, or some combination of these.

By the later medieval centuries, the pattern had changed. Different forms, labeled H X, H H, and H X R by archaeologists, appeared in areas that no longer shared precisely the same distributions. In the northern zone, smaller H-shaped objects became common in certain later funerary deposits. Some of their most striking archaeological patterns belong after 1500, beyond the main chronological limit of this series. We can discuss them briefly to make the earlier change intelligible, but should not pretend they were all features of the tenth century. An artifact typology is also a timeline; omitting the dates turns a long transformation into a static map.

Copper did not move through empty space. Goods went between places occupied by people who needed food, fuel, containers, and safe passage, and whose political arrangements were rarely recorded in writing. Production required access to ore and wood. Exchange could involve intermediaries far from the mine and other goods that leave few durable traces, such as cloth or dried foods. A copper object may survive while the basket, fishing net, or meal exchanged for it has vanished. The imbalance in the archaeological record makes metal seem more central to economic life than it necessarily was.

The geography of the Copperbelt and its connections also resists the idea that all ancient African trade was directed toward the Indian Ocean. Inland goods could move toward wetland communities, the Zambezi system, and southern plateaus, as well as through chains that ultimately connected to coastal exchange. Archaeologists compare metal chemistry, mold evidence, stratigraphy, and distribution maps. Even if a route linked two distant regions indirectly, the people along it were participants with their own economic purposes. A coast-to-interior diagram is only one view of a much larger network.

These findings raise political questions without yielding instant answers. Who controlled a mine? Could many households cast ingots, or did specialists work for wealthy patrons? Did changes in object distribution reflect political boundaries? Nikis and Livingstone Smith investigate the last question, arguing that some geographic patterns may mark shifting spheres of influence. But much of that argument is an interpretation from objects, not a treaty or tax record. We can recognize coordination and regional interdependence without naming the ruler of a particular furnace from a mold left in the soil.

The history of copper before 1500 is thus about both production and translation. Ore becomes metal; metal becomes an ingot; an ingot may become an ornament, an exchange medium, or a funerary offering. At each stage its use and meaning can change. The same shape crossing great distances need not carry a single price or meaning with it. Studying those differences allows the archaeological record of central Africa to speak about labor, connections, and emerging social distinctions without treating a line on a trade map as the complete history of everyone along it.

## Burials beside the Lualaba: Work, Wealth, and the Upemba Wetlands

In the Upemba wetlands, burials give copper objects a different context from a mine or workshop.

A burial can tell us how a person was laid to rest. It may also tell us what objects others chose to place beside them. But a burial does not show an entire society. In the Upemba Depression, a great wetland in what is now the southeastern Democratic Republic of the Congo, archaeologists have excavated an exceptional series of graves spanning more than a thousand years. Pots, iron tools, copper ornaments, fishing gear, and later cross-shaped ingots make it possible to study changing forms of work, exchange, and social distinction. The challenge is to remember that these are records of burial decisions, not a census of every living household.

The depression lies within the upper Congo drainage, crossed by the river also known as the Lualaba. Lakes, marshes, higher ground, and channels create a patchwork of habitats. Fishing would offer one set of opportunities, cultivation on suitable ground another. Much of the oldest archaeological sequence is known through excavations at places such as Sanga, Kamilamba, Kikulu, Malemba-Nkulu, Katongo, and Katoto. Nicolas Nikis's 2021 synthesis describes roughly fifty identified sites and almost three hundred excavated graves. Those are substantial collections, yet associated settlements are poorly understood. Long occupation and disturbance have made domestic layers difficult to expose over large areas.

In the earliest clearly identified iron-using archaeological phase, called Kamilambian, sites in the northern part of the depression show occupation around the sixth through eighth centuries of the common era. Iron tools include implements that could be used in cultivation and fishing; a grinding-stone fragment and certain pottery features add to the picture. These traces suggest people who exploited water and land, but the remains do not provide a complete farming calendar. Nor is it certain whether the Late Stone Age practices visible in older layers ended abruptly. A ceramic phase name gives us a starting classification, not a complete set of social and economic habits.

The following Early Kisalian phase, roughly the eighth and ninth centuries, is associated with different burial goods. Nikis describes objects such as iron tools and, in some graves, items interpreted as symbols of authority, including a ceremonial axe or an anvil. An anvil could be a practical metalworking tool in one setting and an emblem in another. The burial context may suggest high standing, but it does not supply the deceased person's name or office. It does, however, provide an important counterweight to histories that search only for stone capitals: social difference and organized specialist work can become archaeologically visible without monumental buildings or a surviving royal chronicle.

Copper then becomes increasingly significant. It is not the chief mineral resource of the Upemba wetlands themselves; much of the metal appears to have come through links to the Copperbelt farther south. When archaeologists find copper among graves along the upper Congo, they are looking at one section of a much longer network of mining, casting, exchange, and use. Similarities in pottery with other regions and the appearance of imported materials can suggest connections, but a graveside bracelet does not disclose which intermediary delivered it. Some other goods, especially foods and textiles, would survive less reliably than metal and ceramics.

The Classic Kisalian phase, approximately the tenth through thirteenth centuries in Nikis's broad chronology, produced some elaborate burials. Graves can contain many vessels, iron implements, copper ornaments, hooks and harpoons, and objects that required specialized manufacture. Those goods show that the lives represented were connected to both watery environments and wider material exchange. They also suggest differences in access to valued things. Still, a rich grave is not a household account book. The amount placed with a dead person depends on ritual decisions and relationships among survivors, not necessarily on an exact proportion of wealth possessed during life.

There is a striking aquatic dimension to this history. The Upemba wetlands supplied fish, and some graves contain equipment used in fishing or objects that specialists connect with boat-based food preparation. Dried fish may have been exchanged for goods from farther away. That possibility fits the ecology and later histories of exchange, but preserved dried fish is not available in the relevant graves to certify the full route. It is reasonable to reconstruct a strongly water-based economy from multiple traces. It would be unreasonable to treat every hook as evidence of a state monopoly on fishing or every vessel as a receipt for a market transaction.

Burials have also made it possible to ask about diet in a more direct, though still partial, way. In 2016, Nonhlanhla Dlamini, Alan Morris, and Judith Sealy published measurements of carbon isotopes in human tooth enamel, together with evidence of dental cavities. Individuals buried at Sanga showed stronger signatures of foods associated with a category of plants called C-four, while the Katoto sample indicated more C-three resources. One possible explanation is variation in the consumption of cereals or other vegetation, but an isotope value does not identify a specific crop by itself. Dental decay also reflects many aspects of diet and oral environment. These measurements demonstrate regional differences rather than one uniform Upemba menu.

The comparison between Sanga and Katoto is especially useful because the two are not interchangeable descriptions of the same community. Researchers give the southern Katoto burial tradition a separate archaeological label, Katotian, overlapping some of the centuries of the Classic Kisalian but less fully understood. A region with many lakes and settlements can contain more than one material and dietary tradition at a time. If we collapse those records into a single people with a single food system, we erase precisely the differences the archaeological evidence preserves.

A further transition is marked by what archaeologists call Kabambian A, approximately the thirteenth to mid-fifteenth centuries in the northern Upemba sequence. The range and quantity of some metal grave goods changed, while particularly rich graves contained substantial collections of copper crosses and other objects. The pattern has been interpreted as a reorganization of authority and exchange networks. Yet the appearance of a more sharply distinguished set of wealthy graves does not determine whether a ruler imposed new taxes, a local lineage gained prominence, or ritual priorities changed. These are hypotheses to test alongside settlement and trade evidence, not meanings permanently stamped into copper.

Later Kabambian phases and the eventual integration of parts of the region into the Luba kingdom belong largely to centuries after the fifteenth. Archaeologists have traced some continuity in vessel-making across these later transformations, but we must not read the structure of a much later kingdom backward into every eighth- or eleventh-century grave. The political organizations of earlier Upemba communities may have differed substantially. Later traditions of Luba power can enrich the questions we ask, but they cannot serve as eyewitness testimony for the people buried centuries before those traditions were recorded.

The history of the excavations is also part of the history of knowledge. Research campaigns in the twentieth century assembled collections now held both in the Democratic Republic of the Congo, including at the museum in Lubumbashi, and in Belgian institutions. The archaeological categories familiar from books grew from those particular projects and their available sites. Future excavation of homes, workshops, fields, or harbors could change our picture of the people who produced the goods discovered in graves. A burial record is powerful evidence of action around death, but everyday labor often remains just outside its frame.

What, then, does Upemba contribute to the wider story of pre-1500 Africa? It shows long-term occupation and changing communities in wetland landscapes that were neither remote from history nor simply minor outposts of coastal states. Fishing, food production, metalworking, copper exchange, and ritual distinctions developed over many centuries. Archaeologists can trace some of those changes through pottery, tools, grave goods, and chemical signatures in human remains. They cannot transform those objects into a complete list of offices, languages, or laws. The task is to build an account that is both substantial and proportional to what survives.

## When the Copper Crosses Changed: Upemba and the Kabambian Centuries

Following those burial sequences through time shows that the form and use of copper crosses also changed.

A cluster of copper crosses lies in a grave in the wetlands of southeastern Congo. They are not Christian crosses, and the person buried with them cannot tell us what the metal meant. These carefully cast, cross-shaped ingots belong to a long tradition of copper exchange in southern Central Africa. Archaeologists found them among the remains of people whose communities lived beside the Upemba lakes and the upper Lualaba. The important question is not simply how much metal lay there. It is why the objects, the burials, and the wider networks changed between the thirteenth century and the centuries of the later Luba kingdoms.

Upemba is not a single village. It is a long floodplain with lakes, marshes, channels, and higher places suitable for settlements. Excavations between the nineteen fifties and the nineteen eighties yielded nearly three hundred graves from several sites, but the associated homes remain much less well studied. Before the Kabambian phase, the Kisalian funerary sequence, especially between about the ninth and thirteenth centuries, often included fine ironwork and copper ornaments. Some graves were much richer than others. The burials show enduring craft skill and social distinction without recording a named kingdom or an administrative hierarchy.

The transition archaeologists call Kabambian A began around the thirteenth or fourteenth century and lasted into the middle of the fifteenth. Pottery features in some deposits bridge the earlier Kisalian and later Kabambian classifications. There was continuity, but not simple repetition. The range of objects placed in graves became less uniform from place to place. Many burials contain relatively few metal goods; a small number stand out sharply. Calling one group Kisalian and another Kabambian is a shorthand for classifying excavated material. It is not a record of two named peoples replacing each other on a particular day.

At Malemba-Nkulu, two particularly rich Kabambian A burials held hundreds of iron ornaments and many copper crosses. Together they accounted for more than half of the metal recovered in the Kabambian A funerary sample described by Nicolas Nikis. The concentration is striking, especially against the more modest neighboring burials. It invites questions about inherited privilege, ritual spending, and access to regional supply. But the excavators did not recover a tax ledger. We cannot decide from the distribution alone whether copper was accumulated by a ruler, a wealthy family, a ritual association, or several kinds of authority operating together.

The shape and size of the copper pieces are important. Large cross-shaped ingots of the type often called HIH were produced in the Copperbelt during part of the first and early second millennia and appear in early Kabambian burials. Other, smaller cross-shaped ingots, commonly labeled HH, became numerous in later contexts. There were changes in size, quantity, and sometimes metal composition. Archaeologists compare these traits with objects found far beyond Upemba. A particular form can mark a period of circulation, but finding matching shapes in different places does not prove that two owners met or that every ingot had an agreed monetary value.

Kabambian B begins around the middle of the fifteenth century and extends well beyond the year fifteen hundred in the archaeological sequence. The graves generally become even less furnished with varied iron tools, while small copper crosses can still occur in groups of dozens or hundreds. A change in what went into a grave is not automatically the same as a fall in iron production or a loss of skill. It can mark changing funerary obligations, available materials, social preferences, or political relationships. We have far less evidence from workshops and houses with which to test these alternatives.

In the same later centuries, ceramics changed in ways that researchers have connected with practices documented among more recent Luba potters. The techniques show continuities as well as transformations. They offer one of the reasons scholars see historical relationships between the medieval Upemba sequence and the much later Luba political world. But continuity in clay-making is not an unbroken royal genealogy. A vessel form can persist when rulers change, and it can move when potters trade, migrate, marry, or teach. Material similarity opens a historical question; it cannot supply every missing link in a dynasty.

It is tempting to put a famous name at the center of the rich medieval burials. Later histories tell of the Luba king, his council, and an influential circle of affiliated leaders. Yet the identifiable dynastic kingdom becomes clearer in later sources around the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries. Giacomo Macola describes earlier political experimentation around the Upemba floodplain and the territories between the upper Congo tributaries. That is a historically plausible connection between different eras. It is not a license to give a thirteenth-century grave a royal title taken from a nineteenth-century account.

Upemba's wetland resources shape the problem: productive fishing grounds and cultivable land lay apart from southern copper deposits. Upemba offered productive fishing grounds and cultivable places, while copper deposits lay farther south. Communities had reasons to organize labor and to maintain relations beyond a single shore. A scholar may suggest that competing groups developed institutions for managing resources or controlling trade. We must distinguish that argument from documented irrigation works, written property rules, or an identified toll post. None is supplied by the funerary assemblages themselves. The marshland was part of a political and material history long before anyone wrote its royal history down.

The larger history of Upemba is a history of change on several scales. A pot can carry a craft tradition across centuries; copper crosses can register an altered economic sphere; a burial can express the standing of a family. None of these records alone proves the structure of the state that came later. Archaeologists have demonstrated substantial social development in central Africa well before European records of its dynastic kingdoms. To connect these eras responsibly, we must keep the documentary and archaeological clocks running separately, even when both point to the same landscape.

## A Pot before the Ironworker: Luani, Nkar, and the Kwilu-Kasai Interior

We now step back several thousand years and move west toward the Kwilu-Kasai interior. A pottery burial there cannot be folded into the later copper chronology.

A nearly whole pottery vessel was placed with a person whose body was cremated near the Kwilu River about four thousand four hundred years ago. This is Luani, in the western part of the Democratic Republic of the Congo. The find is important because ceramics in the region were once too easily associated with an assumed arrival of Bantu-speaking farmers. At Luani, the pottery was present in a much earlier setting, among the material traces of people who also used stone tools. A pot has a date, a findspot, and a human context. It does not come with a label telling us the language its makers spoke.

Researchers working on the BantuFirst project investigated the Kwilu-Kasai region through river surveys, inland fieldwork, excavation, and studies of ancient environments. Their synthesis describes one hundred fifty-two newly identified archaeological sites with evidence ranging back tens of thousands of years. This large effort changes the picture of a landscape once represented by widely separated discoveries. Yet the map is not complete. Riverbank searches make some settlements easier to detect than others. Erosion, flood burial, later use, and the chance of finding a datable sample all influence which histories become visible.

At Luani the striking feature is a cremation with associated objects, including beads, flaked stone artifacts, and pottery. The archaeological report also describes plant food residues connected with the funerary feature, possibly involving yam, palm, and arrowroot. The people who gathered those foods knew varied river and forest environments. This is a history with particular human practices, not just an arrow on a migration map. The researchers also distinguish the much earlier burial from later pottery-bearing occupations in the same area. One riverside place can preserve traces of people from very different times.

The vessel was found among Late Stone Age material. It may have been manufactured by the people living nearby, or acquired through exchange. That uncertainty matters. An object can arrive without its craft tradition being adopted everywhere, and a person can learn a technology without changing language. The team notes that the earliest evidence of pottery here is much older than previously known pottery horizons in much of western Central Africa. Therefore the old shortcut, pottery means Bantu speaker, is especially unsafe. Archaeology classifies practices; historical linguistics reconstructs relationships among languages. The two kinds of evidence can be compared, not substituted for one another.

Centuries later the record changes again. Around the fourth century before the common era, new pottery-bearing occupations appeared along the Kwilu, including a later phase at Luani and activity upstream near Lukombe. Other sites emerged along this river and the Kasai in the centuries that followed. But the pots are not copies of one universal style, and the settlements occupied different settings. Some lay near forests, others near more open land. The combined evidence favors a region of varying small communities rather than a neat line of identical villages moving steadily in one direction.

At Nkar, in the highland Idiofa region, researchers found evidence for ironworking in deposits dating to approximately the second century before the common era, often summarized as around one hundred forty-six BCE. These are among the earliest secure signs of iron production south of the Congo rainforest. The local industry was accompanied by ceramics and flaked stone artifacts. Stone did not stop being useful simply because iron existed. Someone might still use sharp flakes for tasks they suited, while metal tools demanded access to ore, fuel, furnaces, and specialists. A change in available technologies is not necessarily a sudden replacement of every older skill.

The environmental setting at Nkar also corrects a simple narrative. The study reports that forest degradation in the area had begun before the earliest dated local ironworkers arrived. Palaeoecological data can come from tiny remains of plants, from charcoal, and from chemical signatures in soils. These reveal vegetation mosaics rather than a single forest line marching uniformly across a map. People may have selected sites in relation to water, land clearance, hunting, or crop opportunities, but the exact choices of the first Nkar households were not written down. Correlation with changing climate is a question to explain, not an excuse to erase human decisions.

The team notes several contemporary sites with pots but without direct evidence of local iron production. Even where similar pottery decoration occurs, the distribution of iron smelting is not continuous. Later Kay Ladio pottery from the Lower Congo shares traits with early Idiofa ceramics, but resemblance does not prove a single migration route. There may have been exchange, knowledge transfer, or common inherited craft habits. The same material can serve several different histories. Each possible explanation needs other dated evidence before one can be favored confidently.

Consider how differently the same object is read by three kinds of researcher. An archaeologist asks which layer held it and what lay beside it. A specialist in plant remains asks whether food preparation left starch, seeds, or microscopic silica structures. A historical linguist asks when particular words for metalworking or cultivated plants may have spread or changed meaning. Each method has a different time resolution and different assumptions. Their results can strengthen or challenge each other, but none is a substitute for all the others. Luani and Nkar are important precisely because the finds resist an easy one-word account of an entire region.

The western Congo interior was inhabited long before metalworking became common there. People gathered foods, made things from stone, exchanged or produced pottery, built relationships to river corridors, and later developed several kinds of iron-using community. The archaeological sequence is uneven, but its major lesson is firm: technologies can overlap, archaeological groups are not speech communities, and the earliest dated appearance of a craft in one excavated site is only the earliest currently known there. A careful regional history has to preserve the diversity that the new excavations have brought into view.

## The Centuries with Few Dates: Settlement Gaps in the Congo Basin

Widening the scale again reveals gaps as well as finds. Sparse dates in the Congo Basin demand a different argument from a continuous cemetery sequence.

Imagine making a history from a map in which the points suddenly grow scarce. In the Congo rainforest and adjacent southern lands, archaeological radiocarbon dates are plentiful in some centuries and far fewer in others. Is this a record of fewer people, fewer places excavated, different ways of building, or a combination? The question has become central to reconstructing precolonial Central Africa. A modern language map cannot settle whether communities inhabited an entire region continuously or in successive phases, nor whether their languages and relationships changed over centuries.

Dirk Seidensticker and colleagues assembled more than one thousand radiocarbon dates linked to archaeological sites across the rainforest and nearby areas. Their 2021 study reports high human activity during the early Iron Age, followed by a strong decline beginning about four hundred to six hundred of the common era. The modeled regional pattern remains comparatively low between about six hundred and one thousand, before another phase of expansion. This is not a claim that every settlement vanished at once. Some dated sites remained, and the apparent size of the decline depends partly on how available archaeological dates were screened and modeled.

In the Kwilu-Kasai region south of the rainforest, the chronology is different in detail. Peter Coutros and his colleagues describe a low-activity period lasting roughly from seven hundred fifty until fourteen hundred fifty. Earlier pottery-producing sites are far more numerous in the excavated sample; later sites grow common again. The two histories overlap, but they do not share identical boundaries. That difference is a reason to investigate local conditions and survey coverage rather than flatten a large basin into one timeline. Rivers, forest patches, soil conditions, and settlement practices did not behave identically across thousands of kilometers.

The apparent gap also has exceptions. In the Idiofa area, Okwon and Esal have contexts dated within the eighth to tenth centuries. Along the Kasai, a later phase of Ntswo is dated to the twelfth or thirteenth centuries. Other pottery similarities suggest additional occupation, though some of those parallels lack direct dates of their own. These are small pieces of an important correction: a low point on a regional chart does not mean the land was empty. It means the particular kinds of remains archaeologists can presently date are scarce relative to other periods.

There are several ways a settlement could become hard to find. People might move from the places archaeologists usually survey, especially visible riverbanks, to sites now buried or eroded. Habits of burying pots or making charcoal might change. River levels might cover older banks. Archaeological teams may have sampled some areas much more thoroughly than others. Each possibility could alter the number of dates available without requiring the same proportional change in the number of living people. Yet it would also be wrong to dismiss the pattern as mere sampling. Independent regions show substantial declines in records of activity.

Climate has been considered carefully. Parts of Central Africa experienced shifting rainfall, forest recovery, and later drying at different times. In the Kwilu-Kasai study, wetter conditions preceded or accompanied the start of the local low-activity interval, while a marked later return to aridity occurred within that interval. If people were adapting to particular mixes of riverbank forest and open woodland, changing vegetation could have mattered. But climate alone cannot be accepted as the cause. Some areas with limited environmental change also show low activity, and the dates are not precise enough to show a single environmental trigger causing the disappearance of particular villages.

Disease has also been proposed. The 2021 rainforest study raised the possibility of a prolonged epidemic during the time of demographic decline. That is a testable hypothesis, not an identified outbreak in a Central African burial pit. Without human skeletal evidence of a specific pathogen or contemporary written testimony for these communities, it would be irresponsible to name a plague as the explanation. Conflict, altered trade networks, the departure of families, or a mixture of processes may also be considered. The existence of several explanations is not the same as evidence that they all happened.

The sparse centuries change how we read the history of languages. A map of languages spoken today cannot simply be traced backward to one first-wave settlement. Seidensticker and colleagues use the phrase spread over spread to describe a process in which later settlement episodes may have reconfigured earlier ones. Archaeology supports repeated changes in the distribution of material traditions. Historical linguistics and genetics bring different kinds of evidence about population relationships. But the history of each modern language does not correspond automatically to one pot type, and neither a language nor an ancestry profile can be assigned to the people at an individual undated site.

Around the fifteenth and sixteenth centuries, the Kwilu-Kasai region shows new clusters of settlements. Pottery forms share traits across some groups of sites, but distinct local interaction spheres remain recognizable. Researchers identify examples around Kikundi, Idiofa, and other river corridors. Similar pots can suggest that people met, exchanged, or learned from one another. They do not by themselves tell us whether a single government directed their movements. The new density of sites is important without requiring a story of one returning tribe or an empire that archaeology has not located.

A pause in the archaeological record can be a historical problem rather than a blank page. It asks us to think about the difference between people and the traces that preserve them, between regional trends and local persistence, and between an observed sequence and a proposed cause. The Congo Basin was not frozen between its better-dated occupations. The new chronology makes those centuries more worth investigating, not less. It reminds us that a responsible history can say with confidence that a major change occurred while leaving open what combination of migration, livelihood, environment, and chance produced it.

#### Evidence boundary

Almost three hundred excavated graves provide a strong burial sequence but a weak direct census of homes and farming. Iron tools, fish remains, pottery, and copper goods support several economic inferences; grave wealth does not establish named offices or particular redistribution laws. Sanga–Katoto isotope differences do not identify individual cereals. The chronology of later Luba political formation must not be projected onto Early or Classic Kisalian phases.
