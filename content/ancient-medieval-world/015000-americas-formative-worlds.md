# Several American Worlds: Ceremonial Centers, Settlements, and Exchange

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

Around 500 BCE, the people living in the Valley of Oaxaca in what is now Mexico were participating in a transformation of regional settlement. A hilltop center called Monte Alban began to develop above the valley. Around the same broad era, people in the high Andes traveled to the ceremonial center now called Chavin de Huantar. Farther north, communities in the Ohio valley built earthworks and maintained ceremonial traditions. None of these places existed because events in Persia, Greece, or China had reached them. They are part of the same world chronology, but their histories were made through local decisions, environments, and regional connections of their own.

The earlier centers often grouped under the archaeological label Olmec had already developed monumental architecture and sculpture along the Gulf Coast of Mexico. La Venta was an important center during much of the first millennium BCE, with earthen constructions and stone monuments that required skilled labor. Some famous sculptures portray figures whose rank or precise identity remains debated. Around 400 BCE, La Venta lost population and political importance, yet neighboring traditions and later communities continued. A change at a monumental center is not the extinction of everyone who used its art or lived within its sphere of exchange.

The Gulf Coast materials have been found or imitated in wider Mesoamerican contexts. Jade and greenstone objects, iconography, and shared artistic ideas traveled through relationships that were not necessarily governed by one state. The older claim that a single mother culture invented everything later recognized as Mesoamerican civilization is too simple. People in different areas contributed to long-term exchanges and developed their own practices. Similar imagery can be evidence of contact, emulation, or linked beliefs, but not every resemblance proves conquest. Archaeologists seek more specific evidence before assigning direction or political control to the movement of a design.

Monte Alban was founded on a hilltop around 500 BCE and became a central place in the Valley of Oaxaca. Building on high ground required organization, but the motives for the settlement's location and early political arrangements remain matters of interpretation. Its growth coincided with changes in nearby valley settlements, including a reduction in population at some older centers. The archaeological record includes public architecture, carved stones, domestic remains, and signs of social hierarchy. Scholars connect it with the development of Zapotec state traditions, while recognizing that the precise language and identity of each early inhabitant cannot be read directly from the stones.

Writing and calendar systems developed in parts of Mesoamerica across the first millennium BCE. Some early carved monuments may carry names, dates, or political claims, but interpreting them is difficult where signs are incompletely understood. Unlike in a modern archive, finding an inscription does not guarantee agreement on its reading. Evidence for systems of counting time and making political statements tells us that people developed ways to memorialize events beyond oral tradition. Still, the limits of decipherment are part of the history. We must not transform a tentative sign identification into a certain list of rulers.

In the central Mexican highlands, large ceremonial centers also developed through long local processes. Cuicuilco, south of the Basin of Mexico, became an important community with monumental architecture. The city of Teotihuacan, famous for the great pyramids that survive today, belongs primarily to a later phase of growth, beginning around the end of the first millennium BCE and expanding dramatically during the first centuries of the common era. Historians sometimes project its later size backward when describing earlier Mesoamerica. Chronology matters: these are not interchangeable cities, and architectural resemblance does not make them one political tradition.

Travel south into the Andes and the conditions change. Narrow coastal valleys, high mountains, different growing seasons, and steep routes shaped how communities organized food and exchange. At Chavin de Huantar in present-day Peru, a major ceremonial complex developed from about 900 BCE. Stone structures, galleries, plazas, and intricate carvings made it a place of ritual importance. Objects and artistic conventions associated with Chavin appear across multiple regions. A site with far-reaching religious influence was not necessarily the capital of an empire that directly administered all the places where its art appeared.

A visitor entering Chavin's stone galleries would have encountered architecture that controlled movement, light, and sound. It is possible to study the built spaces and their acoustic properties, but historians cannot recover exactly what each ancient participant felt or believed. Sculptures combine human and animal forms in ways that suggest sophisticated religious imagery. The experience was made by architects, stoneworkers, and communities who supported the center. Religious power was not only an idea; it depended on transport, construction, food, and people who invested time in journeys to a meaningful place.

On Peru's southern coast, communities later grouped under the label Paracas developed elaborate textile traditions, ceramics, and burial practices, particularly from around 700 BCE onward. Some of their finest surviving cloth comes from funerary contexts. The objects provide evidence of skilled fiber work, iconography, and access to materials. They do not tell us how every person dressed every day. Burial goods are shaped by choices made for particular people and rituals, and excavated collections have themselves been affected by looting and collection histories. A history of textile art has to include both the creators and the circumstances of survival.

In the eastern woodlands of North America, people developed distinct ceremonial and settlement patterns. Archaeologists use the name Adena for some earthwork and burial traditions of the Ohio valley from the first millennium BCE. Mounds and enclosures involved shared labor and knowledge of landscape. These communities did not reproduce the urban forms of Monte Alban or Chavin, and they should not be assessed against those sites as failed versions of a city. The surviving earthworks raise questions about ceremony, kinship, seasonal gathering, and material exchange. They cannot be reduced to a ranking on a single scale of complexity.

Along the Bering Strait and Arctic coasts, communities developed maritime hunting knowledge and durable technologies suited to their environments. In parts of the American Southwest, settlement, pottery, and subsistence changed at different times. These regional histories resist being squeezed into one continental stage. Archaeologists piece them together from tools, dwellings, food remains, landscape alteration, and later oral histories. Indigenous communities also maintain knowledge about places and traditions, though oral history cannot be treated as if it were an archaeological date that requires no interpretation. Each source has its strengths and its responsibilities.

The Americas during the first millennium BCE were neither one isolated civilization nor a waiting room for European contact. They contained many societies that exchanged goods and ideas within particular networks, and others whose connections ran along very different routes. Monumental centers became important and sometimes declined; communities reorganized and carried practices forward. To place these histories beside the Persian Empire or the Greek city-states does not require inventing contact. It requires a broader sense of what simultaneous history means: many peoples making consequential worlds in different places, leaving different sorts of evidence for us to understand.

## Companion: chronologies without false equivalence

Distinguish La Venta's decline around 400 BCE from the continuation of Gulf Coast traditions; Monte Alban's foundation around 500 BCE from its later growth; and Chavin's roughly 900-200 BCE ceremonial phases from a demonstrated territorial empire. Paracas textiles disproportionately survive from burials. Eastern Woodlands mound chronology and Arctic/Southwest developments are region-specific. No intercontinental contact with contemporary Eurasian states is assumed.
