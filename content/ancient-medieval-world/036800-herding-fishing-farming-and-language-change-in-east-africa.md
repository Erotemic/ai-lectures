# Herding, fishing, farming, and language change in East Africa

Herding, fishing, farming, and language change did not arrive everywhere as one package. Across eastern Africa, sites separated by long distances and long spans of time let us ask which changes can be demonstrated and which are being inferred. No single object can stand in for an entire population.

## Stones for the Living: Lake Turkana's First Herders and a Monument for Many

A great stone monument is easy to mistake for the work of a king. Someone must have organized the people who carried the stone, chosen the place, settled disagreements, and kept the project going. In many familiar histories, our next question would be the ruler's name. But near Lake Turkana in northern Kenya, a monument built about five thousand years ago leads us in a different direction. Beneath a broad mound at Lothagam North, archaeologists have found a communal burial place used by generations of people. The excavated evidence does not reveal a separate royal tomb. It points instead toward a shared place made by herding communities whose movements and relationships mattered more than a palace.

Lake Turkana lies in the Rift Valley, in a landscape whose water, vegetation, and shorelines have changed repeatedly. At the end of the African Humid Period, the lake receded substantially and conditions became more variable. Across northeastern and eastern Africa, people who had long relied on fishing, hunting, and gathering encountered cattle, sheep, and goats, and over time some communities adopted livestock keeping. This was neither a single migration at a single date nor the immediate disappearance of older ways of life. A household could maintain animals, use wild foods, and exchange knowledge with neighbors living differently. The early herding frontier was a region of encounters.

A pastoralist is a person whose livelihood depends substantially on managed herds. That definition is about work, not a fixed rule that everyone must move constantly. Animals need water, grass, shelter, protection, and attention when they are sick or giving birth. Families may divide tasks, shift pastures with the seasons, and depend on people in other places for access and information. Such connections become especially valuable when one stretch of country receives rain and another does not. The archaeological question is how these relationships become visible when houses, conversations, and paths have disappeared.

Lothagam North provides an answer that is not primarily about houses. It contains a platform of stones and sediment, standing and fallen pillars, smaller stone circles, and cairns. Excavation and ground-penetrating radar revealed a large burial cavity cut into the underlying sandstone and then repeatedly filled with human remains. Elisabeth Hildebrand, Katherine Grillo, and their collaborators reported the investigation in 2018. From sampled portions of the cavity they estimated that more than five hundred and eighty people had been placed there. This is an estimate for the whole cavity, not a count of every burial individually exposed by excavation.

The dead included children and adults, and people identified osteologically as female and male. Many were accompanied by objects of adornment, including stone beads, ostrich-eggshell beads, and other ornaments. The arrangements were dense and layered; bodies were not laid out as a neat set of individual plots that could be visited like a modern cemetery. The researchers did not find a systematic separation in which rich or conspicuous burials belonged to a small ruling class and everyone else was set apart. That negative result has limits: different sorts of status can leave little trace, and a burial sample never records every difference between the living. But the monumental labor here cannot simply be read as evidence that a powerful chief commanded a population.

Consider what construction required. People excavated a substantial cavity in rock, shaped and filled a platform, transported stones, and maintained a recognizable place over a long period. The 2018 radiocarbon evidence indicates several phases across centuries. A few calibrated dates do not tell us how often communities gathered or exactly how long any one phase lasted. They do show that the site was not the product of a single afternoon or the burial of one celebrated person. Building a place for many people can itself be a reason to coordinate. It can also make cooperation easier the next time a group meets.

There is no surviving account of a funeral at Lothagam North, and we should not invent speeches, chants, or the beliefs of mourners. Yet the pattern suggests questions about social ties. Who carried food when people traveled there? Did distant families recognize one another through the same ornaments or shared work? Was the gathering an occasion to arrange future access to grazing or water? Hildebrand and colleagues propose that communal burial and monument making could have reinforced networks at a time when herding was spreading through a changing landscape. That is a persuasive interpretation of the setting and architecture, not a direct recording of what the builders intended.

This is also an important lesson in how archaeology evaluates inequality. A pyramid with one central royal burial and rich offerings would support one set of inferences. A large collective cavity with different ages, shared burial space, and widespread ornamentation supports another. Even the second cannot establish that everyone had equal authority or identical resources. It asks us to describe the observable distribution before choosing a social model. Monumental architecture is evidence of organized effort. The form of that organization remains a question to investigate.

The ornaments are not minor details. An ostrich eggshell bead requires obtaining shell, drilling it, shaping it, and sometimes joining many beads into a wearable object. Other materials may have circulated through exchanges. A single bead cannot map an entire trade network, but repeated choices about adornment show skill, attention, and social expression. These people did not live as anonymous herds moving through empty space. They made objects and monuments that mattered to them, although we cannot recover all the meanings they attached to them.

After many centuries, the cemetery ceased to be used as it had been. There is no reason to assume that the people of the region simply vanished. Archaeological places can fall out of use while families change their movements, ritual practices, or ways of making a living. The monument itself might remain visible long after its original social function changed. What survives for an archaeologist is a sequence of deposits and features, not a continuous diary of the community.

Lothagam North belongs in the history of food production because it corrects an expectation about what the first herders should have built. Mobility did not prevent substantial architecture. A collective monument did not require a city. Early livestock keeping was not merely a new method of getting calories; it altered risks, alliances, exchanges, and obligations across a broad landscape. The dead make one part of that history visible. The living people who carried stones, fed animals, made beads, and maintained relationships are the subject of the history we are trying to reconstruct.

## A Herd and Grinding Stones: Working Life at Luxmanda

To compare another early herding setting, move south from the Turkana region to Luxmanda in Tanzania. Similar questions about food do not establish a single migrating community.

A large grinding stone seems to explain itself. You imagine grain being reduced to flour and a household preparing a meal. But the stone does not tell us which plant was ground, whether it was cultivated, or even whether food was always its main use. These are the questions archaeologists have asked at Luxmanda in northern Tanzania, a settlement of livestock-keeping people occupied roughly three thousand years ago. Here, many of the most consequential finds are not monumental buildings. They are concentrations of broken animal bones, pottery, tools, dung-derived deposits, and unusually large grinding stones. From them a history of domestic work begins to emerge.

Luxmanda lies near Mount Hanang in the Mbulu highlands, farther south than many earlier excavated Pastoral Neolithic settlements. The term Pastoral Neolithic is an archaeological label for several long-standing traditions of livestock use, stone-tool production, pottery making, and exchange in parts of Kenya and Tanzania. It does not identify a single kingdom or language. Research led by Katherine Grillo and Mary Prendergast described Luxmanda as an open-air settlement with substantial occupation deposits. Radiocarbon dates place its major early occupation around three thousand calibrated years before the present, or approximately a thousand years before the common era.

The animals were central to life there. Identified bones and teeth include large numbers of cattle, sheep, and goats; wild species are present in small amounts in the analyzed faunal sample. Many bones bear evidence of processing and breaking. These remains point to the repeated tasks of acquiring, preparing, sharing, and discarding food, but the shape of the discarded bones alone cannot give us a household recipe. Archaeologists have also found ash, pottery, and other domestic refuse. Such deposits are the cumulative record of people returning to activities again and again, not a photograph of one dinner.

The settlement extends across roughly three hectares, a useful corrective to an image of early herders who owned only what they could carry and left no substantial residential place. Archaeological teams combined surface survey, excavation, and geophysical measurements to explore the organization of activity areas. Some deposits may derive from the gathering of livestock and accumulation of dung, while other features reflect hearths or refuse disposal. Finding a dwelling floor or an enclosure is rarely as simple as seeing an intact wall. Researchers must distinguish an ancient activity from material moved there later by erosion, animals, roots, or people.

Then there are the stones. At Luxmanda, excavators encountered a concentration of twelve large lower grinding stones in a feature together with handstones, pottery, stone-tool debris, and dung-derived material. Some lower stones were heavy enough to be difficult to carry from one seasonal camp to another. In a 2020 report, Grillo and colleagues asked whether features like these require a more flexible account of pastoral mobility and gatherings. The stones suggest repeated investment in particular places. They do not tell us that every member of the community lived there permanently or that every stone was in use at the same moment.

What were people processing? For decades, researchers debated whether the Pastoral Neolithic involved cereal cultivation. Grinding equipment, stone axes, historical linguistic arguments, and analogies with recent pastoral livelihoods made agriculture plausible to some scholars. Others emphasized that the region lacked directly identified crop remains from the relevant sites. Grinding stones could have processed wild grass seeds, tubers, animal products, or non-food materials. Their wear reveals work but cannot, by itself, establish that a domesticated crop grew nearby.

A study published in 2026 gives us more specific evidence. Carolina Jiménez-Arteaga and colleagues examined microscopic plant remains on Luxmanda grinding stones, combining starch analysis with the identification of silica bodies, called phytoliths, and samples of surrounding sediments. Their study reports evidence of processed local grasses, including panicoid grasses, legumes, and underground plant storage organs. The researchers also found indications of wild rice and a broad range of plant parts. This establishes that plants were actively worked into the material life of a pastoralist settlement. It does not establish that people at Luxmanda cultivated domesticated cereal fields.

We should distinguish plant processing from the domestication question. A domesticated plant is one shaped through cultivation and selection over generations, sometimes acquiring traits that make it dependent on human harvest or planting. A person gathering a wild grass and grinding its seed is doing important food-producing work, but that activity need not involve sowing the grass. Conversely, plant use might have served medicine, craft, or animal care, not only human meals. The 2026 authors themselves caution that their microscopic evidence does not turn every grinding feature into a cereal-dehusking workshop. The details of the residues constrain the story; they do not fill every gap in it.

The value of this result extends beyond a botanical inventory. If livestock bones preserve readily while seeds and plant tissues often decay, then archaeological narratives can become overly centered on animals simply because animals leave better traces at some sites. A grinding stone offers a different archive. Combining animal remains, residues, soils, and tool positions produces a more balanced picture of what occupied people's time. Herding requires labor; so does collecting and processing plants, repairing vessels, maintaining tools, and making decisions about when to move or stay. We cannot assign those tasks to a particular sex or age group without evidence.

Luxmanda also points toward contacts beyond one settlement. Stone tools made from obsidian have been linked to distant sources in Kenya's central Rift Valley, and its material traditions resemble those found at other sites to the north. This suggests connections across considerable distances. A shared stone source does not document the exact journeys of a named trader or herder, and similar pots do not by themselves identify a people's language. But such evidence does show that this household economy belonged to a broader social geography, rather than existing in isolation below Mount Hanang.

Our understanding will continue to change as research methods improve. The same site can be read through a carefully dated bone, microscopic starch, an animal's tooth, or the distribution of material across an excavation. Each method has its own errors and silences. Luxmanda's importance is not that it finally supplies a fixed recipe for the first pastoralists. It shows what happens when we make ordinary work the object of historical investigation. Alongside the cattle and goats were people making meals and materials from plants. A herd did not eliminate other forms of knowledge, and a grinding stone does not need to prove agriculture to tell us something important.

## After the Lake Shifted: Kansyore Fishing Communities and Changing Neighbors

At lakeside sites associated with Kansyore traditions, fishing and other practices followed their own long histories, overlapping unevenly with herding and cultivation.

A lakeshore can be an abundant home, and still be a risky one. Fish gather at particular times and places. Water levels rise and fall. A river mouth that works well for one generation can become shallow or distant for another. Around Lake Victoria, communities made decorated pottery, fished, harvested shellfish, and lived in changing relationships with the land long before the better-known medieval towns of East Africa. Archaeologists often call part of this record Kansyore. The name is useful, but we have to be careful with it. It refers first to recognizable pottery and archaeological contexts, not to one people whose language, politics, and way of life remained unchanged for thousands of years.

Lake Victoria is one of Africa's great inland water systems, surrounded by land now within Uganda, Kenya, and Tanzania. Its shores, islands, rivers, and nearby forests create many different habitats. In portions of the northern and eastern basin, archaeologists have identified shell-rich deposits associated with pottery and fish remains. Some sites appear suited to repeated seasonal visits, including rapids where fish runs could be exploited. The persistence of pottery-making and fishing does not make a community static. People can specialize, exchange, move, and alter their routines while continuing familiar technologies.

Earlier accounts often pictured Kansyore communities as highly specialized fisher-foragers, and for some excavated localities that remains a useful description. But a description established on one shoreline cannot be carried unchanged around the entire lake. Western Uganda's Ndali Crater Lakes region, to the east of the Rwenzori Mountains, had different soils, forests, and settlement histories. Research published in 2024 by Peter Schmidt, Jonathan Walz, Jackline Besigye, and a large team argues that groups using Kansyore-related ceramics there engaged substantially in farming and herding alongside other resource gathering. The authors' regional reconstruction differs from the better-known lakeshore model in western Kenya.

How could archaeologists distinguish these histories? First by location and chronology. A surface pot can show decoration, but the ground layer in which it was found, materials beside it, and radiocarbon dates help establish when it was used. Then come animal bones, plant remains, traces of iron production, and changes in vegetation reconstructed from environmental evidence. Together these can show that people acted within a changing landscape. Each item has limits. A piece of cattle bone might come from someone's own herd or from food obtained through exchange. A pollen signal indicates vegetation change, but it may not identify exactly who cleared the land.

In western Kenya, work on sites such as Wadh Long'o and Gogo Falls has explored fishing, seasonal movement, and contacts with herders. At Wadh Long'o, interpretations of domestic animal remains suggest that acquiring livestock or animal products could be incorporated into older foraging lifeways without requiring every household to become a pastoralist. The dates and their associations are still matters of study, and this does not describe all Kansyore sites. It is one reason the notion of an abrupt replacement, with fishers departing as soon as herders arrived, has lost much of its simplicity.

A 2022 study by Mica Jones and Ruth Tibesasa revisited a difficult chronological gap in the northeastern lake record. Their new evidence from Namundiri A in eastern Uganda, considered with dates and fauna from western Kenya, supports a picture of changing use of lakeshore and riverine settings during a drier period several thousand years ago. The relationship between aridity and settlement should not be treated as a single cause that forced everyone into the same response. People may have changed fishing locations, relied on different parts of the watershed, or used networks outside the excavated area. The record is discontinuous, and the excavations cannot follow one family through every season.

The western Uganda research raises another historical problem: what happens when groups with different practices live near one another for centuries? The excavations in the Ndali Crater Lakes region recovered locally distinctive pottery, iron-age wares, and evidence of settlement activities across a long chronological span. The researchers argue that contacts among people making Kansyore ceramics and contemporaries associated with early ironworking reshaped practices in both directions. Ceramic traditions can overlap or borrow features, rather than lining up neatly in the order of an imagined migration map. This is an interpretation supported by multiple site contexts, not a direct label attached to each ancient household.

One striking case involves a pottery type called Boudiné, with substantial vessels and decorative features that have been hard to classify. At some excavated locations, large pots were associated with human burials, including an infant burial. Schmidt and colleagues connect elements of this tradition with what they term Transitional Urewe, proposing a fifth-century common-era context in the wider region. The funerary use of a pot is evidence of a purposeful social practice, even if the words said around the burial are lost. It also warns us against treating a ceramic category as if it recorded only a cooking style or a population replacement.

The study draws on historical linguistics to consider interaction among populations that may have spoken different languages. Linguistic reconstruction asks what related languages tell us about earlier vocabularies and movements; archaeology asks what material practices and dates survive at specific places. The two bodies of evidence can inform each other but do not align one-to-one. No decoration on a Kansyore vessel tells us the language its maker spoke. Nor can the appearance of a new ceramic style by itself prove that the earlier residents moved away. Such assertions would need additional grounds.

Environmental histories require the same care. The 2024 researchers ask how people contributed to a shift from forested conditions toward forest-and-savannah mosaics in parts of western Uganda. Farming, fire, grazing, and settlement may all affect vegetation, just as rainfall variations can change which activities are possible. A landscape that looks natural to a later observer might have been shaped by generations of choices. Yet we cannot assign every environmental change to one named community without adequate dated evidence. The question is not whether people had an influence, but how to identify its timing, scale, and mechanisms.

There is a human history in these technical distinctions. A fishing family did not need to surrender its expertise merely because cattle became available somewhere nearby. A farmer could eat fish. A maker of pottery could adopt a new vessel form from a neighbor while continuing older practices in other parts of life. Communities need not be pure representatives of an archaeological category. The names of their members are largely unknown to us, but their ability to combine resources, adapt to a changing lake, and create enduring objects remains visible.

The Kansyore record therefore changes the question we bring to early East African history. We should not ask only when food producers arrived, as though there were an empty landscape waiting for them. We should ask who already lived along the waters, what they knew, how resources were shared or contested, and how practices changed at different sites. A shell mound, a decorated bowl, a cow bone, and a pot burial answer different parts of those questions. None is a full history by itself. Together, critically dated, they make the lake's long human history more than a prologue to farming.

## A Seed in the Hearth: Kakapel and the Crops of Eastern Africa

Farther west in Kenya, plant remains at Kakapel let us follow changes in crops at a much finer scale.

What can a burned seed tell us that a thousand decorated pots cannot? A pot may show that its maker had a familiar technique or belonged to a network of exchanging ideas. A seed can sometimes identify a plant that someone actually handled. If the seed was charred, remained in a secure archaeological context, and was directly radiocarbon dated, it can help establish when a crop was present. At Kakapel Rockshelter in western Kenya, that difference has transformed the history of farming around Lake Victoria. It shows not one agricultural package arriving fully formed, but a long sequence in which different crops and animals entered local lives at different times.

Kakapel is a shallow shelter against a rocky hill in Busia County, near the lower slopes of Mount Elgon. The place has rock art and traces of repeated occupation. It is not a medieval capital, and its walls contain no royal charter listing agricultural taxes. Yet its deposits hold a lengthy sequence, reaching back roughly nine thousand years. Excavations, radiocarbon dating, survey, and careful recovery of plant remains allow researchers to compare early fishing-and-foraging occupations with later communities that grew crops, kept some domestic animals, and continued using wild resources.

To recover a charred seed, archaeologists may use flotation: soil samples are agitated in water so that light carbonized remains can be separated from heavier sediment and examined. Identifying a seed requires comparing its shape and anatomy with reference collections, sometimes under magnification. Charred remains are a partial record because some plant foods preserve more readily than others, and a crop may be prepared far away from the spot where soil was sampled. But when a seed is directly dated, the chronology of that seed is stronger than a claim based solely on the date of a nearby potsherd or an assumed cultural sequence.

In a 2024 study, Steven Goldstein, Natalie Mueller, and collaborators reported directly dated cowpea remains at Kakapel from about twenty-three hundred years ago. Cowpea is a legume whose domestication history has important connections with western Africa, although questions of local evolution and additional domestication histories remain under discussion. The Kakapel find came from a well-defined hearth feature associated with the Early Iron Age and pottery of the Urewe tradition. The same broader archaeological phase also provides evidence of domestic cattle. The result is not a claim that one person introduced every crop and every animal in a single journey. It is evidence that an early mixed food system was present at a particular place by that time.

The date matters. A charred cowpea from a hearth about twenty-three hundred years old tells us that the crop was there then, not precisely when its first seed crossed into the region. It could have arrived earlier without leaving a recovered sample. There is also a difference between a crop consumed at a site and fields cultivated beside it. Archaeologists combine plant remains with landscape, settlement, and other evidence before reconstructing agriculture at scale. Even a well-dated food can have been obtained from someone else. The stronger historical claim concerns access to, and use of, that crop at Kakapel.

Another find shows how fragile a simple migration story can be. The same early hearth contained a pea that might belong to one of two closely related cultivated forms, including a crop associated with northeastern Africa. The researchers did not directly date that individual pea, and its diagnostic preservation was incomplete. They therefore treated an early introduction as a possibility rather than a certainty. This distinction is worth hearing. A confident paper does not mean that every object it reports is equally well dated or equally easy to identify.

Then the plant assemblage changes. In the later Iron Age deposits, directly dated finger millet appears by about one thousand and fifty calibrated years before the present; sorghum appears later, by roughly nine hundred calibrated years before the present. Finger millet, domesticated in eastern Africa, is much more common than sorghum in the analyzed plant assemblage from this phase. These plants belong to different historical trajectories from cowpea, and their differing presence at one site reveals selective changes in diet and cultivation. It would be wrong to say that every farmer in the region adopted these grains on Kakapel's schedule.

In 2025, Goldstein and colleagues published a companion analysis of settlement, material culture, and site use across the same long sequence. They described early agropastoral activity by about twenty-four hundred years ago, followed much later by denser regional settlement and more sustained use of the landscape, associated with the expansion of finger-millet and sorghum economies. This does not prove that millet alone made larger villages possible. It connects changing foods with broader patterns that require explanation: more frequent occupations, different households, contacts, and population movements.

The researchers also compare ancient DNA evidence from individuals buried in the region with other archaeological records. Such evidence may show connections among populations and change through time. It cannot directly tell us which words someone spoke, what meal a household preferred, or how one farmer learned to sow a plant. Pottery, genes, and seeds describe different kinds of history. Their conjunction can strengthen a reconstruction; their differences should stop us from describing a migration as if it automatically transported a complete, unchanging menu.

A common textbook picture divides history into hunters, then herders, then farmers, with each stage making the previous one obsolete. Kakapel does not fit that neat succession. Wild animals and gathered plants remained important even after cultivated foods appeared. Domestic animals were present without every occupation being dominated by herding. Over time, people combined different resources depending on what they could grow, obtain, or preserve. The name agropastoralism is useful for some mixtures of farming and livestock keeping, but no single term explains the balance of work at every site.

These results also alter how we imagine the relationship between western and eastern Africa. Crops did not all originate in the same place or travel in a convoy that arrived once. Cowpea points toward one set of connections; finger millet and sorghum point toward other histories of domestication and exchange. Communities in the Lake Victoria region were not merely recipients of a finished agricultural system. They made practical choices about which crops to cultivate or obtain, how to fit them into local soils and rainfall, and how to combine them with fishing, gathering, and livestock.

There are still large unanswered questions: how widely these crops were planted, who controlled access to seed, what social obligations surrounded harvests, and how practices differed in nearby valleys. Kakapel cannot resolve them all. But it provides something foundational: real botanical remains tied to a measured sequence. The burned cowpea is small, yet it moves the history of farming from the presumed date of a migration to a physical event in a particular hearth. When we follow the later millet and sorghum remains through the same layers, we see agriculture as a changing social history rather than an invention that simply arrived.

## Words, Genes, and Moving Families: What the Bantu Expansion Explains

Language and genetic evidence widen that scale again. They can illuminate long movements, but neither can simply assign a language to every excavated pot or hearth.

A map of the spread of Bantu languages can look like a single enormous arrow leaving western Africa and sweeping across much of the continent. It is a striking image, and it represents a real historical question. Hundreds of related languages are now spoken across central, eastern, and southern Africa. How did that come about? But no one witnessed the whole process, and the arrow conceals thousands of choices, encounters, births, marriages, exchanges, and changes of speech. A language family is not a migrating army. To understand the history behind the map, we need several kinds of evidence that can support one another without being interchangeable.

Bantu languages form a major branch of the Niger-Congo family. Historical linguists compare systematic correspondences among sounds, words, and grammatical structures in related languages. If a regular pattern recurs across many words, it may help reconstruct an earlier form or relationship. This is different from noticing that two languages have a similar word that could have been borrowed in trade. Linguists also investigate changes that are shared by some branches but not others. Such evidence helps ask which speech communities were connected in the past, although it does not provide a literal travel diary of each family or clan.

Most broad models locate the early expansion of Bantu languages in or near the region of present-day Cameroon and Nigeria, followed over long periods by movements, contacts, and language spread into the great central African forest, the Great Lakes, and lands farther south. These are hypotheses about processes occurring over millennia, not one dated expedition. Linguistic ancestry and individual genetic ancestry also differ. A person can inherit genes from many ancestors while speaking one language learned at home or in a wider community. Entire communities can shift language through changing alliances, marriage, migration, or political influence without every family being physically replaced.

Archaeology offers another perspective. Pottery styles, ironworking remains, animal bones, food plants, and settlement patterns can be dated and compared across regions. Urewe ceramics in the Great Lakes are frequently placed in explanations of early eastern Bantu-speaking communities. Yet the pot has no vocabulary written on it. A vessel may travel as a traded object, a style may be copied, and technology can spread between groups that speak different languages. In some places, a material tradition may reflect substantial population movement; in others, it may reflect contact, borrowing, or both. The inference must be demonstrated rather than placed in the name of the pot.

Genetics brings questions about biological descent and interaction. In a major study first published online in 2023 and appearing in *Nature* in 2024, Cesar Fortes-Lima and colleagues analyzed genome-wide information from 1,763 living participants, including 1,526 Bantu-language speakers from 147 populations in fourteen African countries. They also generated sequences from twelve Late Iron Age individuals from parts of south-central and southern Africa. The investigators found patterns of genetic diversity consistent with movements outward from western Africa and identified substantial gene flow from communities encountered along those routes. The ancient sample does not include every region or every century, so the result is a broad population-history model rather than a family tree for an Urewe potter.

One proposed mechanism is serial founder migration. Imagine a population in which a smaller group establishes a new settlement; some of its descendants later establish another. Each movement may carry only part of the genetic variation found in earlier communities. Over many such events, genetic diversity can diminish with distance from a broad source region. That is a model with observable predictions, not a rule that every move happened the same way. Later mixing, population growth, disease, and local history can complicate the picture. The Fortes-Lima team combined genetic, geographical, and linguistic information to test such a scenario, while also finding evidence that the spreading communities mixed with people already living in the regions they entered.

There are limits to what DNA says. A genome does not record a person's spoken language or whether a household made pottery of a particular type. A pattern among living populations cannot be transferred unchanged onto people two thousand years ago. Ancient genomes provide valuable chronological anchors but are scarce, often unevenly preserved, and subject to decisions about which burials can be sampled. Scholars try to separate biological ancestry from culture and to describe uncertainty in their statistical models. We should not use a genetic cluster as an ancestral tribal name or turn population-level estimates into a claim about the identity of one excavated individual.

Even the linguistic family tree is an active area of debate. In a 2025 review, Igor Yanovich challenged the confidence with which some reconstructions join linguistic branches to archaeological and genetic scenarios. He argued that competing assumptions can yield deceptively settled narratives, and that the details of the family's historical structure require more careful evaluation. Other researchers, including Koen Bostoen, have defended and refined cross-disciplinary models of a broad expansion. This is not a choice between believing that Bantu languages spread and believing that they did not. It is a debate about dates, branching relationships, rates of change, pathways, and how strongly different kinds of evidence can be aligned.

Consider how a family could change its language without moving very far. Suppose people lived beside a politically influential settlement, married into its households, and began raising children in a language that helped them participate in wider networks. Over generations their speech might shift while many ancestors remained local. The example is hypothetical, not the recovered biography of a named Great Lakes family. It illustrates why the spread of a language can be partly demographic and partly social. Another settlement might have been established by people moving considerable distances, whose descendants mixed with neighbors. A historical explanation need not demand the same mechanism everywhere.

This also changes what we mean by the "arrival of agriculture." Crops have histories of domestication and exchange that are not identical to the history of language. At Kakapel in western Kenya, dated cowpea remains and later millet and sorghum horizons show that agricultural practices changed in stages. Around the same lakes, fishing, foraging, and pastoral activities could persist or combine with cultivation. Ironworking had its own social networks and technical requirements. Treating Bantu language spread, the first Urewe pot, the first iron furnace, and the first crop as a single timestamp would discard the very differences that make the evidence informative.

The people encountered by expanding language communities were not a blank background on the map. Some had long histories of fishing, gathering, herding, crafts, and exchange. Contact could involve new technologies, intermarriage, competition over resources, shared settlements, and changes in what languages were learned. Archaeologists may recover signs of interaction without discovering what the participants called themselves. Linguistic reconstruction may reveal a borrowed word without identifying the settlement where it was first heard. Population genetics may recover mixing without determining which social relationship produced it. These are complementary partial views, not complete substitutes for a missing archive.

Bantu languages spread widely, and population movement was part of that history. But languages, crop varieties, pottery, and ancestry did not all travel together or arrive everywhere at once. In some places the evidence points toward new settlements; elsewhere it records exchange, mixing, and practices continuing through change. Linguistics can trace relationships among speech communities, archaeology can date objects and activities, and genetics can illuminate ancestry. Kept distinct and then read together, these traces describe encounters among people already living on the land, not a single arrow sweeping across an empty map.

#### Wikipedia orientation (supplementary, not evidence for historical claims)

- [Urewe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Urewe) — ceramic traditions and research history.
- [Mount Elgon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mount_Elgon) — Kakapel's geographic setting.
- [Lake Victoria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lake_Victoria) — region whose agricultural histories are being compared.

#### Evidence boundary

A directly dated plant remain demonstrates that a plant was present and handled, not necessarily that it was cultivated directly beside the excavated shelter. The early pea lacks a direct date and has a taxonomic ambiguity; it must not be presented as a secured proof of an early northeastern African import. Later finger-millet and sorghum remains are not a timeless regional crop calendar. Site-specific patterns must not be converted into universal migrations or assigned to a language solely from pottery or genetics.

### Words, Genes, and Moving Families: What the Bantu Expansion Explains

#### Evidence and reading

- Steven T. Goldstein and collaborators, **[Early agriculture and crop transitions at Kakapel Rockshelter](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11335020/)**, *Proceedings of the Royal Society B* (2024). Direct plant evidence for staged agricultural change in the Lake Victoria region.

#### Wikipedia orientation (supplementary, not a historical authority)

- [Bantu languages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bantu_languages) — linguistic family overview.
- [Bantu expansion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bantu_expansion) — overview of models and related debates.
- [Urewe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Urewe) — ceramic evidence often connected to these discussions.

#### Evidence boundary

Genetic clustering is not a language test or an individual ethnic identity. The ancient genomes in Fortes-Lima et al. come from selected southern and south-central African sites, not a comprehensive early Great Lakes sample. Yanovich's criticism should be attributed as a substantive methodological intervention rather than presented as universal scholarly agreement. The broad spread of Bantu languages is established; the exact branching routes, dates, and relations to each ceramic and crop tradition remain debated.
