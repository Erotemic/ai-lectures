# Arid North America: water, fields, towns, and migration

Arid North America contains wetlands as well as deserts, and movement as well as enduring places. Farming and water engineering are part of that history; so are gathering, fishing, exchange, and migration. We will follow these different practices without treating one as a failed version of another.

## Where the Rivers Disappear: Wetlands, Farming, and the Great Basin

In Nevada's Great Basin, rivers flow into lakes and sinks rather than reaching the sea. Their shores have shifted dramatically over thousands of years. Along some wetlands, people made nets, harvested seeds, caught fish and birds, and stored carefully shaped tools. At Lovelock Cave, archaeologists recovered woven objects, baskets, and striking duck decoys made from tule. The objects are useful evidence of technologies suited to wetland life. They also expose a problem in how history is commonly told. A society that does not leave a capital, palace, or permanent masonry town can still possess deep environmental knowledge, skilled production, and enduring social relations.

The Great Basin is not one unchanging desert. It encompasses mountain ranges, valleys, springs, rivers, and shifting wetlands across what are now parts of Nevada, Utah, and neighboring states. Water availability can change over short distances and across seasons. Foragers made decisions about where food and water were available, how far groups should travel, which plants could be stored, and how people maintained relations among places. Some communities returned to particular shelters and lake margins over many generations. An archaeological sequence that contains seasonal occupations is not evidence of people wandering without a plan; it may record a sophisticated schedule of movement.

The state's historic preservation office summarizes long human use of the Humboldt and Carson sinks, while the Bureau of Land Management describes Lovelock Cave and its discoveries. Woven netting, fishhooks, dried fish, waterbird remains, and stored plant materials make sense as parts of a particular food economy. They show production and use, not merely decorative skill. A woven net can take sustained labor and specialized knowledge, and a wooden or tule decoy is part of a working technique for attracting birds. We should not invent the maker's name or a dramatic hunting scene from the surviving object alone.

The site also has a history of excavation and collecting that affects what we know. Early twentieth-century investigations removed enormous quantities of material. Museum displays, archaeological catalogs, and repatriation work have continued to change the relationship between collections and living Native communities. Lovelock Cave remains significant to Northern Paiute people. Accounts of its importance cannot be reduced to a label attached by the first archaeological expedition, just as archaeologists cannot use a story collected centuries later as a literal date for every ancient object. Different forms of historical knowledge should be identified and examined with care.

A few hundred miles east, other archaeological patterns appear. In central Utah and neighboring areas, archaeologists use the label Fremont for a set of material traditions prominent during the first and early second millennia of the Common Era. The label was taken from a river, not from a self-designation preserved in a text. Pottery, distinctive basketry, footwear, rock art, storage structures, and traces of fields form part of the evidence. The National Park Service describes both cultivated crops and the collection of wild resources. These communities should not be imagined as a failed attempt to become the better-known Pueblo societies farther south.

Fremont sites are diverse. Some were occupied by groups using pit houses and storage facilities, while other places show shelter use and seasonal movement. Corn, beans, and squash were cultivated in some settings; hunting and gathering remained important. A human-shaped figure painted or carved on rock may tell us that an image mattered to its makers, but it does not grant us a reliable translation of a religion. Specialists can compare styles and archaeological contexts, while recognizing that later viewers may not know the meanings attached to particular motifs. A picture is evidence of a choice to make a mark and of the techniques used to make it. Interpretation of its significance demands caution.

Across the thirteenth and fourteenth centuries, some Fremont-associated farming and settlement patterns become less visible in the archaeological record. Earlier narratives sometimes treated their disappearance as the disappearance of a people. That conclusion is not justified simply by changing pottery or house forms. Drier climatic episodes, altered farming opportunities, shifts in mobility, and changing relationships among communities are all relevant possibilities. Archaeologists disagree about their relative weight. A change in the material pattern used to define a culture can occur when descendants reorganize their lives, join other groups, or adopt different practices. Material discontinuity is not proof of biological extinction.

Modern Great Basin nations include Northern Paiute, Western Shoshone, Goshute, and Ute communities, among others. Their histories are not interchangeable, and their relationships to archaeological categories cannot be written as a single line of descent without supporting evidence. The National Park Service notes that interpretations of Fremont and Numic-speaking populations remain contested. A language family spreading across a region is not a perfect proxy for population replacement, and labels developed by scholars do not supersede the names communities use for themselves.

To understand why a change in farming mattered, consider the difference between a field and a wetland. A field can promise a concentrated harvest, but it may require more predictable moisture and a different schedule of work. A wetland may offer fish, birds, roots, and reeds during particular months; its productivity changes with water levels. Families could combine these strategies rather than choose permanently between farmer and forager. That flexibility explains some material variation, although it does not reveal the decisions of every household. The presence of maize does not automatically mean year-round residence or centralized authority.

The artistic and practical evidence can be read together. A basket made by a recognizable coiling technique may help archaeologists trace learned skills. A storage pit can show investment in returning to a place. A painted figure can indicate an enduring visual tradition. A crop remain, when securely dated, can show cultivation or exchange. None of these facts by itself establishes who held political authority, whether a place was sacred, or what language its inhabitants spoke. Their combination gives a richer reconstruction while leaving essential questions open.

The Great Basin also helps correct a broad habit in world history: measuring all societies by whether they built cities. The archaeological record of wetlands, mobile households, farming villages, and storage places can reveal coordination on many scales. Access to fishing locations and seasonal harvests may have been governed by relationships we cannot recover from a broken basket. Care for children, agreements between families, conflict, trade, and ritual all existed whether or not they produced monuments. The absence of an imperial archive is a limitation of our evidence, not a license to imagine the absence of social complexity.

Return to the duck decoy. Its maker understood birds, plant fibers, shape, and the place where the object would work. We can know that much from the object and its context, even if we cannot identify the person who held it. At another site, pottery and an irrigated field show a different but equally situated way of making a living. The Great Basin was never merely an empty corridor between other civilizations. Its history is written in water, movement, stored food, and durable knowledge, and continues in the communities for whom the region has remained home.

## Water before the City: Canals and Communities of the Sonoran Desert

South of the Great Basin, canal communities in the Sonoran Desert confronted a different problem of coordinating water and work.

A canal in the Sonoran Desert must move water by gravity. Too steep a channel can damage its banks; too shallow a channel may fail to carry water where fields need it. Every long channel also needs attention when sediment builds up or an embankment breaks. Archaeologists have traced extensive ancient canals in the valleys of the Salt and Gila rivers, in what is now Arizona. Their presence reveals repeated engineering decisions, but also something less visible than a canal trench: people had to coordinate labor, maintenance, access, and the seasonal timing of crops. Irrigation was not simply a technology installed once. It was a continuing social arrangement.

Archaeologists have long used the label Hohokam for particular material traditions in the Sonoran region, especially red-on-buff pottery, irrigated settlements, and certain public structures. The National Park Service now often refers to the ancestral Sonoran Desert people. Its Casa Grande account is careful about names: Hohokam is an archaeological term, not a documented name that all inhabitants used for themselves. The Salt River Pima-Maricopa Indian Community explains that archaeologists adapted it from the O'odham word huhugam, whose meaning in community usage extends beyond the bounded archaeological period. O'odham people and other descendant communities understand this history through their own traditions and relationships to place. We can explain an archaeological pattern without treating a research classification as a vanished tribe.

Farming in the Sonoran Desert depended on more than canals. The Salt and Gila rivers provided water under variable conditions. Fields required soils capable of sustaining crops, labor for planting and harvesting, and knowledge of seasonal floods. Maize became part of the regional food system, while beans, squash, native plants, and hunted animals contributed to people's diets in differing proportions. A river valley was not one uniformly watered garden. Some households lived close to established channels; others used runoff or cultivated smaller plots. Archaeology allows comparison among locations, but a field's precise social rights may not be recoverable from its shape alone.

Canals are useful evidence because they create a physical record of planning. A main channel could carry water toward a network of smaller branches. Repair might require work well beyond one household. Maintenance offered opportunities for cooperation and also for dispute: the needs of an upstream field could conflict with those of someone downstream. It is reasonable to infer that channels required agreed arrangements, but less safe to describe a specific ancient water court, overseer, or election if no such record survives. The engineering establishes a problem that social institutions had to address. It does not tell us the exact words people used when addressing it.

The settlement pattern changed over time. The National Park Service describes early communities with public ball courts and later sites with larger rectangular platform mounds. Archaeologists discuss those shifts in relation to ritual activity, regional connections, and changing political organization. A ball court does not mean that every village belonged to a centrally ruled state, just as a platform mound does not prove that its occupants exercised identical authority everywhere. Buildings can express different forms of gathering, status, and ceremony. Their sequences can be dated and compared, but the meaning of each structure needs a local argument rather than a borrowed model from another region.

Casa Grande, whose monumental earth structures survive in present-day Arizona, is one of the places where visitors encounter this history. The Great House was built with caliche, a material made from the region's soils, and belongs to a broader settlement landscape rather than standing alone. The monument's preservation can make it seem that the large structure is the whole community. Most daily life occurred in less durable homes, fields, work areas, and paths. The people who carried building material, tended crops, raised children, prepared food, and repaired canals did not all leave names. Their labor was part of what made the impressive structure possible.

Trade mattered, but its traces need careful interpretation. Pottery styles, shells, ornaments, and other materials suggest interactions among Sonoran communities and areas beyond them. Finding a marine shell does not tell us whether its final owner crossed the desert to collect it, received it through several intermediaries, or inherited it. The object shows connection. The number of exchanges and the motives of participants require additional evidence. The region should not be reduced either to an isolated desert civilization or to a simple outpost of a more famous cultural center farther south.

Why did large settlements and many canal systems change during the centuries approaching 1500? Archaeologists have discussed flood damage, changes in river courses, climatic stresses, social conflict, and political reorganization. Different explanations may illuminate different places, and the timing was not identical everywhere. A major channel that fell out of use is evidence of changed water management, not necessarily evidence of total population disappearance. The National Park Service notes continued populations and relationships to modern O'odham communities. Describing the end of a particular archaeological period should never become a story in which living descendants are written out of the landscape.

The problem is partly how archaeological periods are named. A period boundary may be defined by styles of pottery, house form, or ceremonial architecture. People could change those things while remaining in the region, and some might move and join relatives elsewhere. A history of displacement or migration would require evidence about where people went, not only where one building type stopped appearing. Later oral histories and community knowledge preserve additional understandings of origins and continuity. These should be presented as living historical traditions rather than folded silently into an archaeologist's chronology.

The canal system also raises questions about distribution. If irrigation allowed a larger harvest, who received the extra food? Did households have comparable field sizes? Were some people expected to maintain channels without receiving proportional benefits? Monuments and earthworks may suggest organized leadership, but the precise institutions governing rights remain partly inaccessible. A historian should state that limitation plainly. It is better to teach the real relationship among engineering, production, inequality, and evidence than invent a detailed political constitution simply because a canal had to be governed somehow.

Modern restoration and archaeological research introduce another perspective. The channels have been disturbed by contemporary urban development, and in some places ancient routes lie under a modern metropolis. Maps produced from excavation and historical surveys are indispensable, but they also reflect where archaeologists were allowed or able to investigate. A surviving canal segment may be only a fraction of a system. Preservation decisions determine what future listeners will be able to see. People living in the Phoenix region today inhabit a landscape transformed repeatedly, not a desert that only recently became agricultural.

We can return to the practical task with which we began. Imagine an upstream branch filling with silt just before fields need water. Someone must notice the change, organize a response, and direct effort where it matters. That much follows from how the system functioned. The archaeology cannot tell us whether the work was voluntary, required, paid, or negotiated in every period. The power of the Sonoran evidence is that it brings those questions into the history of a place too often described only through monuments. A canal is at once a mathematical slope, a physical trench, a commitment to future crops, and the trace of relationships among people. Those relationships are the history we are trying to recover.

## Roads into the Canyon: Chaco and the Ancestral Pueblo World

Farther east on the Colorado Plateau, Chaco offers another form of connected settlement, with a different chronology and social setting.

A broad road runs across the landscape of the Colorado Plateau toward a canyon in present-day northwestern New Mexico. Its ancient builders left traces that can still be mapped, although not every stretch survives and we do not know every journey that used it. In the canyon stand the remains of great buildings, with carefully laid masonry and rooms stacked into multiple stories. These are among the material traces of Chaco, a regional center associated with ancestral Pueblo peoples between roughly 850 and 1150 CE. The buildings invite an obvious question: why did people devote so much labor to this place? The answer requires us to look beyond the walls, into food, ritual, routes, and the descendant communities that remember the landscape.

Chaco Canyon lies in a dry environment where rainfall and runoff vary sharply. People grew maize, beans, and squash, and used wild plants and animal resources. Such a livelihood required knowledge of soils, seasonal water, planting conditions, and storage. A large building did not create its own food. Families living in the canyon and in the wider region supported activities through farming, gathering, craftwork, and exchange. Some agricultural goods may have entered from outside the immediate canyon. Researchers debate how densely particular buildings were inhabited and how many visitors came for periodic events. The question of population cannot be answered simply by counting rooms.

Beginning in the ninth century, people constructed buildings now called great houses, including Pueblo Bonito, Una Vida, and Peñasco Blanco. At some sites, the massive walls used carefully shaped sandstone, timber beams, and repeated building episodes. Rooms and plazas were organized in forms distinct from many smaller domestic settlements. Great kivas, large circular structures, are among the features associated with public or ceremonial life. Their functions are inferred from architecture and comparisons; we should not assume every great kiva hosted the same ritual or that we know the precise words spoken in any gathering.

The labor was formidable. Sandstone had to be quarried and shaped, timber acquired from distant woodland areas in some cases, and construction coordinated over many years. Tree-ring dating provides unusually precise evidence for when some beams were cut, though the date of a tree's felling is not always exactly the date it was installed. Dendrochronology, the study of annual growth rings, gives archaeologists a way to build a detailed sequence of construction. It also exposes an error in popular retellings: the Chacoan world was not built all at once according to a single master plan that remained unchanged for centuries. Buildings were expanded, reworked, and used in different ways over time.

Roads and alignments connect the great houses to places beyond the canyon. Some road segments are broad and strikingly straight. Their purposes remain debated: they may have supported movement, procession, communication, and assertions of regional relationship, and different sections may have served different purposes. Roads are not automatically proof of a central empire capable of policing every mile. Likewise, the presence of comparable buildings at outlying communities shows a regional architectural and social connection without specifying exactly who commanded whom. We have evidence for an extensive Chacoan system; we lack a complete constitution for it.

The same distinction applies to exchange. Turquoise, exotic birds, distinctive pottery, and other finds show connections with distant regions, including parts of Mesoamerica in some cases. Such objects did not travel by themselves. People exchanged goods, moved between communities, and carried knowledge of routes and relationships. Yet the discovery of a macaw in a great house is not proof that one Chaco resident made the entire journey to the bird's original habitat. Intermediaries may have been involved. An artifact can establish that a connection existed while leaving its full human itinerary unknown. An account of regional networks must therefore distinguish proven contact from conjectured routes, intermediaries, and institutions.

Burials and household remains offer evidence of differences in wealth and status. At Pueblo Bonito, certain burials stand apart from most known burials in the canyon. Some interpretations emphasize powerful families or a concentrated ritual elite; others point to the diversity of institutions that a regional ceremonial center might support. The archaeological evidence invites questions about inherited position, obligations, and control over valued objects, but it does not resolve every debate about political hierarchy. If we called Chaco a kingdom and invented a ruler, we would be replacing the evidence with the government structure of another place.

The people of Chaco were not isolated from the surrounding Colorado Plateau. Great houses appear at locations beyond the canyon, linked with smaller habitation sites, roads, and local landscapes. Some nearby communities may have used the canyon as a ceremonial gathering place; others may have participated through exchange, marriage, or political relationships. A regional network could sustain shared architecture without every family living in one center. When archaeologists revise estimates of the number of permanent residents, this does not diminish the significance of the site. A place visited periodically by many communities can be socially important even if its year-round population was modest.

One challenge in narrating Chaco is the temptation to divide its history into magnificent construction followed by inexplicable disappearance. The evidence points instead to a changing regional system. Major building programs slowed after the early twelfth century; some people moved toward other parts of the San Juan Basin and the broader Southwest. By the thirteenth century, migration and settlement reorganization had reshaped communities throughout the region. Drought and resource stresses may have played roles, alongside political, religious, and social decisions. No surviving document lists a single cause for every household's move. The choices of families were likely varied and made across generations.

Descendant communities are vital to this history. Hopi and Pueblo peoples maintain traditions linking them to ancestral places across the Southwest, and Navajo communities also describe historical relationships with Chaco. The histories of particular clans and communities are not interchangeable. The National Park Service emphasizes that affiliation cannot be reduced to a neat one-to-one correspondence between an ancient building and one contemporary nation. Oral traditions and archaeological evidence have different forms and may emphasize different events. Responsible research does not dismiss Indigenous knowledge because it is not written in the style of a modern excavation report, nor does it force every oral tradition into a rigid archaeological timeline.

Language illustrates the problem. We do not possess a Chacoan written archive that names the inhabitants in their own words, records every building's purpose, or describes the decisions that led to settlement shifts. Modern terms such as Ancestral Pueblo and Chacoan system are useful historical descriptions, not exact translations of labels spoken in the eleventh century. Descendant communities have their own names and ways of describing ancestry and place. Choosing respectful terminology does not eliminate scholarly uncertainty; it helps us avoid confusing the categories of later researchers with the identities of earlier people.

Research methods continue to change what can be said. Tree rings date some construction events; ceramics and architecture help establish regional connections; plant remains and soils inform accounts of farming; excavation reveals domestic and public spaces. Remote sensing can identify roads or structures but must be checked against conditions on the ground. Each method has a particular resolution. A line in a landscape image does not automatically reveal how many travelers used a road or what ceremony accompanied their movement. Confidence grows when independent sources converge, while significant ambiguities should remain visible to the listener.

Preservation is part of the story, not an afterword. Chaco's buildings and landscape are places of continuing cultural significance. Excavation, tourism, and development decisions can affect ancestral sites and the people who identify with them. The responsibility of museums and heritage agencies therefore includes consultation and care, not merely the collection of interesting artifacts. Some historical information is held in forms or contexts that communities may not wish to make public. A commitment to understanding the past does not require treating every sacred place or practice as an unrestricted research resource.

Imagine returning to the road with which we began. Archaeology can map part of its course, estimate the labor that constructed it, and place it within a regional network of great houses and communities. It cannot tell us the thoughts of every person who walked it. That gap is a reason for precision, not for silence. Chaco teaches that an influential center can organize relations over a wide region without looking like a stone-walled imperial capital. It also teaches that the people who built and inherited this landscape did not disappear when construction slowed. Their histories continued beyond the canyon, and they continue to matter to living communities today.

## Leaving One Home, Making Another: Pueblo Migrations after Chaco

Following later movement beyond places such as Chaco changes the story from an abandoned center to the making of new homes.

In the thirteenth century, people in the Mesa Verde region of the American Southwest moved into larger villages, lived through serious local stresses, and then left the region in great numbers. A map showing an abandoned settlement around 1300 can make this look like an ending. But descendants of these communities live in Pueblo towns today, especially in parts of New Mexico and Arizona. The movement was a change in where communities lived, not the disappearance of a people. To understand it, we have to look beyond empty houses and ask how people chose, or were compelled, to leave familiar places and make new ones.

The preceding history includes Chaco Canyon, which already has a separate lecture in this series. Chaco's monumental great houses and roads belonged to an earlier phase of regional connections, and their history cannot explain every later settlement. The Mesa Verde region developed its own network of farms, villages, ceremonial architecture, and exchange. Over the twelfth and thirteenth centuries, many households reorganized into larger settlements. At places such as Sand Canyon Pueblo, archaeological surveys and excavations reveal a concentrated population living in a relatively short span. Larger villages could make cooperation easier and also place more demands on nearby fields and water.

Archaeologists can date wood used in buildings with tree rings, compare pottery styles, map household structures, and study plant and animal remains in excavated deposits. Each method has strengths and limits. A date from a roof beam may tell us when a tree was cut or the wood was used, not the exact day a family moved out. A pottery style may help trace social ties, not a guaranteed linguistic identity. The presence of a large communal structure may suggest forms of gathering without revealing every rule of political decision-making. Multiple types of evidence become more powerful when they agree, especially when the question is population movement over several decades.

Environmental stress is part of this story. Tree-ring reconstructions reveal variations in precipitation and periods of severe drought, including the late thirteenth century. Farming households faced difficult choices about planting locations and stored food. Yet a drought does not act as a single command that tells everyone to migrate. Regions differed in water availability, households in resources, and communities in their ability to negotiate support. People moved at different times and along different social connections. Climate evidence describes pressures; it does not identify the decisions of particular families or prove that every conflict had the same cause.

A particularly important excavation took place at Sand Canyon Pueblo. Archaeologist Kristin Kuckelman's analysis of the site's end, based on faunal and plant remains as well as human skeletal evidence, points to both food stress and a violent attack that contributed to abandonment there. The conclusion concerns a specific village with a particular sequence of deposits. It would be a mistake to project that attack onto every Pueblo settlement or to say that violence alone caused the entire regional migration. Other studies have emphasized social and religious developments, changing institutions, and networks connecting communities across the Southwest.

Donna Glowacki's social history of the Mesa Verde migrations asks how these pressures interacted. Rather than presenting the movement as a single collapse event, it examines differences among communities and the social relationships that made departure possible. Pottery exchanges and changes in public architecture offer clues about contacts beyond the places being abandoned. Households would need knowledge of destinations, access to fields, and relationships with people already living there. Archaeologists can identify patterns consistent with these connections, even when the personal conversations that guided a journey remain lost.

The destinations matter. Crow Canyon Archaeological Center describes movement into areas of present-day Arizona and New Mexico where other Pueblo communities were already established. People did not necessarily arrive in vacant land. They joined, negotiated, and sometimes transformed settlements with existing populations. New forms of community organization and ritual life emerged in later centuries. Migration can change the institutions of places people leave and places they enter. Calling the process abandonment without following the migrants creates a misleading break between Ancestral Pueblo history and Pueblo history.

Modern Pueblo traditions add indispensable perspectives. Communities maintain relationships to ancestral places, remember origins and journeys, and continue forms of cultural knowledge that cannot be reconstructed from pottery alone. These accounts are not interchangeable across all Pueblo nations. Hopi, Zuni, and the many Rio Grande Pueblo communities have distinct histories and traditions. Archaeologists need to work in dialogue with descendant communities while distinguishing an oral historical account from an excavated date or artifact distribution. Respect for living knowledge does not mean pretending that all categories of evidence answer the same question.

A house itself can be read at several scales. It records building techniques, room arrangement, food preparation, storage, and sometimes repairs. A village records how houses relate to plazas, ceremonial rooms, and defensive or practical features. A regional distribution of villages records shifts in settlement density. At every scale, archaeology records material outcomes of social decisions but not necessarily the full negotiations behind them. A family leaving a house might remove valuable tools and leave heavy pottery behind. These remnants need not mean that departure occurred in panic, though in some places violence is clearly documented.

The phrase collapse often confuses several different claims. A settlement can become empty, a political network can dissolve, a ritual tradition can transform, and people can survive while reorganizing their communities. These are not the same event. In the Mesa Verde case, the evidence strongly supports widespread migration by the late thirteenth century, with significant variation in local circumstances. That claim is compatible with the continued existence of Pueblo societies. The most important historical question is not how an isolated civilization vanished, but how families and institutions navigated a difficult transformation.

The research itself has a history that affects which questions have been asked and which sites have been studied. Early archaeological interpretations sometimes described ancient inhabitants as unrelated to the people now living nearby, or treated descendant traditions as decorative additions rather than knowledge relevant to interpretation. Contemporary site interpretation at Mesa Verde and Crow Canyon foregrounds continuity and community perspectives. Archaeological research still involves debates over chronology, causation, and the meaning of material patterns. Descendant collaboration helps change the questions asked and the responsibilities associated with ancient sites, including how human remains and ceremonial materials are treated.

To close, imagine not a dramatic final day but a period of decisions stretching across decades. A household might stay through another planting season, send relatives to a more secure community, or travel along a route already familiar through marriage and exchange. We cannot turn those possibilities into a fictional diary and call it evidence. What we can show is a regional change seen in building dates, settlement locations, environmental records, and the living histories of Pueblo people. The movement out of Mesa Verde was serious and consequential. It was also part of a longer story of adaptation, social relationships, and the making of homes that did not end when the old villages emptied.
