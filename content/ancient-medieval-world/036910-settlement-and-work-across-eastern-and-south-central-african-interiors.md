# Settlement and work across eastern and south-central African interiors

A mound, a shelter, and a lakeshore settlement do not preserve the same kinds of history. Traveling from Uganda toward the south-central African interior, we will compare what those places reveal about work, food, movement, and political change. We will also move backward and forward in time as the evidence requires.

## Mounds, Cattle, and Enclosures: Ntusi, Bigo, and Political Change in Uganda

In the grasslands of western and southwestern Uganda, some places remain visible because people piled up the debris of daily life; others because they cut great trenches into the earth. The settlement at Ntusi has substantial mounds of ancient refuse, bones, ash, and broken pottery. Not far away, at Bigo bya Mugenyi, earthworks define large spaces with banks and ditches. Both have been drawn into stories about the legendary Cwezi rulers and the beginnings of Great Lakes kingdoms. But a legend and a trench answer different historical questions. The challenge is to give proper attention to both without turning one into an automatic proof of the other.

Ntusi was occupied over several centuries, roughly from the eleventh through the fifteenth century of the common era in the chronology proposed by archaeologist J. E. G. Sutton. The site lies amid the Bwera grasslands and has deposits showing substantial activity, including the remains of cattle and the preparation or use of grain. An archaeological mound is not necessarily an enormous tomb or an earthen palace. Repeated disposal of food remains, ash, and broken objects can build layers that preserve a record of ordinary practices. The excavation of such a mound gives evidence of activity over time, even if it does not tell us how many households were present in every year.

The Ugandan heritage submission for Ntusi describes two major mounds, sometimes called the male and female mounds, and a broad basin known as Bwogero. Excavations have revealed deep deposits of pottery, cattle bone, ash, and stones. Large pots found in one area have been interpreted as evidence for storing food, potentially below ground. These finds invite questions about the quantity of food brought to the site, the animals consumed there, and relations with smaller settlements in the surrounding country. None of these questions requires us first to identify a king. A settlement could become important through work, concentration of people, exchange, and control of resources before anyone left a royal genealogy.

Cattle are particularly visible in Ntusi's faunal record. Large accumulations of their bones indicate repeated consumption and perhaps a wider system for supplying animals from surrounding pasture. Grain evidence points to cultivation and preparation alongside herding. We should resist imposing a later social division in which one separate class only kept cattle while another only worked the fields. Some of the archaeological evidence suggests that grain and animals belonged to interrelated households and economies, although exactly who owned herds, decided slaughter, and distributed food remains open to debate. Consumption at a central place is not the same as a surviving record of tax collection.

Bwogero gives another perspective. The term is applied to a striking basin or depression created or reshaped through human activity. Sutton interpreted this landscape feature in relation to the need for water for people and cattle. The physical scale suggests repeated labor and attention to local conditions. But it does not provide a schedule of who dug, how obligations were assigned, or what ceremonial meanings particular workers attached to the place. To move from the feature to institutions, historians have to compare settlement evidence, environment, and later traditions without assuming that all of those records describe the same generation.

Bigo bya Mugenyi has a different archaeological signature. The Ugandan nomination to UNESCO's tentative World Heritage list describes an extensive system of trenches and enclosures, including outer works connected to the landscape around the Katonga River and nested interior spaces. The tentative listing places them broadly in the fourteenth to sixteenth centuries. This is not the same as claiming that all trenches were dug at once. Dating such features is difficult, because a charcoal sample may date a deposit above or below construction rather than the exact day a ditch was cut. Earthworks can also be repaired and reused over many years.

What were the ditches for? Earlier interpretations often called them forts. Peter Robertshaw challenged treating that answer as self-evident. He suggested that functions could have overlapped: excluding elephants from cultivation, protecting a ruler or valuable property, regulating the movement of people and goods, enclosing animals, and demonstrating collective power. Some possibilities fit the shape of particular features better than others. Without a written account from the builders, the relative importance of defense, farming, ceremony, and political display remains contested. The scale of the work, however, establishes that substantial labor was mobilized, and that fact itself demands historical explanation.

Munsa offers a third comparison. This western Ugandan site, centered around the hill of Bikegete, has evidence for ironworking, cattle consumption, probably grain storage, burial, and other activities. Excavations indicate a long history of use, with some activity beginning well before the major encircling ditches. Robertshaw's dating work proposed that the earthworks at Munsa and Kibengo were probably made in the fifteenth or sixteenth centuries, with Bigo possibly comparable or somewhat earlier. A site occupied in the tenth or eleventh century need not have had its later trenches then. That distinction prevents us from assigning every find inside an enclosure to the century in which the enclosure was built.

Now consider the Cwezi traditions. In several later Great Lakes societies, oral histories tell of powerful rulers, ancestral figures, and the realm often called Kitara. These traditions matter as historical practices: they transmit accounts of legitimacy, descent, place, and the past, and they have been reinterpreted in different political circumstances. Some earlier scholarship tried to assign named Cwezi rulers to the earthworks as if the trenches were surviving royal signatures. Robertshaw argued that the archaeology did not establish a single ancient Cwezi empire, and proposed the possibility of distinct contemporary polities. Sutton likewise warned against literally matching legendary dynasties with excavated places. Neither position makes the oral histories meaningless; it changes the questions historians ask of them.

The evidence also allows us to ask why some settlements grew large. Grain cultivation, cattle herds, access to water, and connections to exchange networks could help support places like Ntusi. A central site might accumulate wealth by receiving animals, food, craft products, or visitors from smaller settlements. But the shape of a landscape does not tell us whether a ruler collected tribute, hosted feasts, organized seasonal work, or exercised coercion in exactly the same way as later kingdoms. Those possibilities need comparison with the distributions of materials, settlement sizes, and dated occupation phases. The existence of political complexity does not mean that one modern constitutional label fits every excavated community.

Environmental evidence adds another complication. Pollen, charcoal, and sediments from western Uganda record periods of changing vegetation and moisture. Researchers such as David Taylor, Peter Robertshaw, and colleagues have asked how agriculture, cattle, settlement concentration, and climate might have interacted. Forest reduction may reflect clearing as well as drying; apparent settlement decline could result from a movement toward dispersed households rather than a demographic catastrophe. An earthwork left without its earlier occupants need not mark a single dramatic conquest. The archaeological and environmental sequences can help constrain such explanations, but cannot make each one certain.

Our history ends at a chronological seam. Ntusi's earlier occupation and the beginning of some major earthworks lie before 1500, but much of the excavated evidence at Munsa and Bigo reaches into the sixteenth century and beyond. We should not squeeze those later phases backward simply to fit a course that leads toward the early modern world. Instead, the sites show processes that crossed that boundary: changing landscapes of cattle and grain, expanding capacity for collective labor, and political institutions whose later histories became part of powerful stories about origins. In the mounds and ditches, we have traces of those developments. In the traditions, we have histories people continued to tell about them. Responsible historical reconstruction keeps the two kinds of evidence in conversation without requiring them to say the same thing.

## At the Falls Again: Kalambo and the Many Ages of a Place

Kalambo Falls, farther south, preserves a far longer sequence than the Ugandan earthworks. We must separate its very ancient evidence from the later occupations relevant to this comparison.

At Kalambo Falls, close to the southern end of Lake Tanganyika, the landscape has preserved traces of human and earlier hominin activity separated by astonishing intervals of time. A river runs toward a waterfall, and the same broad basin has been visited, abandoned, and used again under changing conditions. In 2023, researchers announced a wooden structure at least 476,000 years old. But our subject is a much younger set of occupations, within the last two thousand years, when people used iron and made pottery in the same general landscape. The two histories belong to Kalambo. They must not be mistaken for one continuous settlement.

The difficulty is partly a gift of preservation. The Kalambo River deposited layers of sand, gravel, silt, and sometimes waterlogged material. People who worked near the river in widely different ages left things that could eventually become buried. Excavations directed by J. Desmond Clark in the mid-twentieth century made Kalambo central to the archaeological history of Africa. Later investigators reexamined some of the old interpretations by studying how water moved deposits and whether an object was still in the layer where it had originally been left. An excavation can expose a sequence, but a sequence is not automatically a story of uninterrupted inheritance from the oldest layer to the youngest.

By the first millennium of the common era, communities in what is now northern Zambia were making vessels and using iron in settings that archaeologists classify as Early Iron Age. David Phillipson distinguished a Kalambo group of pottery among several regional Zambian traditions. The categories depend principally on combinations of vessel form and decoration found at related sites, and their dates rest on the circumstances of excavation and radiocarbon samples. The phrase Kalambo group does not name an ancient nation. It is a way of comparing finds at the falls with fragments elsewhere in the woodland and along approaches to the lake.

For the occupations at Kalambo Falls themselves, older archaeological syntheses placed much of the relevant Iron Age sequence between approximately the fourth and eleventh or twelfth centuries of the common era. Those centuries overlap but are not identical with the time ranges sometimes given for the wider ceramic group. Some sherds came from open sites, some from shelters, and some from disturbed deposits. A date obtained from charcoal in a layer may support a phase of occupation; it cannot certify that every pottery fragment found near it was created at that moment. The absence of a secure sample in another layer is not proof that no one lived there.

Think of what the word settlement might mean under these conditions. A family could return repeatedly to a place where there was water, fishing, plants, or access to paths across the escarpment. Iron implements required ore, fuel, and labor somewhere in the regional economy; the mere presence of a finished iron blade does not prove that the metal was smelted beside the house where it was found. Pottery could be used to heat food, store water, or carry supplies, but most broken pieces cannot reveal precisely which task they performed. We can identify technological possibilities and archaeological patterns without inventing a single village plan that has not been excavated.

The larger region complicates a simple account of migration. Archaeologists have identified several different early pottery groupings in Zambia: Kalambo in the north, Chondwe farther toward the Copperbelt, and still others nearer Lusaka or the southern plateau. Similarities among their artifacts suggest contacts and historical relationships, but differences in manufacture may reflect local choices or encounters rather than successive ethnic frontiers. In a 1968 article, Phillipson explicitly warned that pottery and linguistic arguments could not yet be linked directly to prove which languages these communities spoke. That warning continues to matter. A collection of pots cannot be translated as a sentence in an ancient language.

There are clues about work and diet, but they vary in strength. Grinding equipment can indicate that people processed plant foods. It does not, without seeds, phytoliths, or other corroborating evidence, establish that they cultivated particular cereals. Ironworking debris can show operations that a finished tool alone cannot. Animal remains, when preserved and identified securely, might distinguish fishing, hunting, or herding. The absence of a particular object in one excavated area can reflect preservation, sampling, and ancient choices as well as genuine absence. Kalambo should not be made to stand for a standard package of iron, farming, cattle, and village life that necessarily traveled together.

The 2023 wooden-structure discovery is worth recalling for a methodological reason. It came from deep deposits studied with luminescence dating of sediments; the reported construction predates our species' best-established fossils. The Early Iron Age occupations were investigated through a quite different combination of ceramic comparisons, associated material, and radiocarbon chronology. Researchers may work at the same named site while answering questions separated by hundreds of thousands of years. They must keep findspots, dating methods, and stratigraphic relations separate. An old picture caption or a map pin cannot do that work for them.

The historical importance of this location is therefore not that a single people lived beside the waterfall for half a million years. Kalambo is a place repeatedly incorporated into changing forms of life. In one time people shaped wood with large stone tools; at a much later time pottery and iron became part of the local archaeological record. Between those episodes lie changes in environment and technology that require many independent lines of evidence. A deep place history offers several stories, not one ancestor list.

Seen from northern Zambia, the spread of iron and pottery is not simply the last leg of an arrow drawn south from the Great Lakes. The evidence includes different regional traditions, varied sites, and a long survival of stone technologies in neighboring places. People moved, ideas traveled, and objects were exchanged; archaeology alone seldom permits us to count those movements separately. What can be demonstrated is a landscape of overlapping practices across many centuries. Kalambo teaches us to distinguish the history of a place from the history of one community, and the evidence for a technology from an imagined biography of everyone who used it.

## The Shelter Was Not Empty: Nakapapula and Contact in Central Zambia

In central Zambia, a shelter provides a smaller and differently preserved record of contact and change.

A fragment of pottery appears among stone tools. What changed? It is tempting to say that farmers arrived, the hunters left, and the pottery records a new population. In a rockshelter called Nakapapula, in the Serenje district of Zambia, archaeologists encountered evidence that resists this simple succession. Stone-tool practices associated with the Late Stone Age persisted well after the earliest pottery of a type associated with iron-using communities entered the record. The shelter also offered evidence that forms of schematic rock painting were made during the period of contact. This is a history not merely of new technology but of people who could remain neighbors while using different things.

Begin with a rockshelter rather than a royal center. A sheltered rock face offers shade, a place to work, and an area that may gather many episodes of occupation. If people return over generations, stone flakes, charcoal, animal remains, fragments of vessels, and fallen paint can accumulate in separate layers. But layers can be mixed by water, animals, roots, and later digging. A pot fragment within a stone-tool layer raises an archaeological question, not an immediate declaration of who slept beside it. What was above and below it? How secure is the association? What materials could have been exchanged? Who else used the shelter at other times?

David Phillipson published the Nakapapula investigation in 1970, after earlier work on Zambia's Early Iron Age. The article described a relatively continuous sequence of stone tools of the Nachikufan tradition. That name is an archaeological term for recurring forms of Late Stone Age equipment in parts of central Africa. It is not a surviving community's self-designation. Phillipson emphasized that the stone-tool sequence extended into the second millennium of the common era, substantially later than the initial appearance of Early Iron Age pottery. In other words, a pottery-bearing horizon did not mark the instant disappearance of stoneworking at Nakapapula.

That fact changes the kinds of historical accounts we can test. One possibility is that people already making stone tools sometimes obtained pots from farming neighbors. Another is that families adopted potting while keeping some older tool practices. Different visitors may have used the same shelter at different seasons. Some fragments may have been carried into an older deposit by disturbance. Excavation can weigh these explanations, but the existence of stone tools and pottery together does not select only one. Contact is a reasonable research question; a precisely documented marketplace between two named groups would be an invention.

Nor should stone imply isolation. A cutting edge made from stone could be effective for a particular task even when an iron blade was available somewhere nearby. Stone raw materials, skill, and repair habits differ from the fuels, ores, and labor needed for forging iron. Technologies can coexist because households have different access to resources, because some objects are exchanged and others made locally, or simply because older techniques continue to work. Historians should not label one family's continuing use of stone as a failure to progress. The question is which tools met which needs and what the excavated contexts show.

Rock paintings add a second form of evidence. At Nakapapula, Phillipson related schematic painted motifs to the archaeology of the shelter, arguing that their production was contemporary with the Early Iron Age. A painted line or figure is not automatically a portrait of an ironworker, a declaration of a religious affiliation, or a text we can decode. Its dating depends on how pigment, rock surfaces, falling fragments, and archaeological layers relate. When a painting is not directly dated, a secure association with deposits may help, but the conclusion is weaker than a date measured from the painted material itself. The author proposed an important connection; the identity and intentions of the painters remain uncertain.

The original research was produced in a time when archaeologists often framed the transition as a encounter between incoming farmers and older hunters who were eventually displaced. The evidence is worth separating from that framing. Material differences do not in themselves specify ancestry. Household relationships might include trade, marriage, teaching, conflict, labor exchange, or a family's gradual change of technique. Those are possible social processes, not things that a few potsherds can count. More recent work elsewhere in Zambia has reinforced how diverse local sequences can be, and how careful we have to be about assuming that pottery, animals, crops, and iron all appeared in a single package.

Consider the calendar as well as the materials. People who hunted, collected plants, farmed, or kept livestock did not necessarily use every place for the whole year. A rockshelter could be a stopping point rather than a permanent village. Two deposits with similar objects might be decades apart; two groups with different equipment might be active in the same region during the same season. Radiocarbon dates provide ranges, not clock readings for meetings between individuals. Even when a site shows prolonged continuity, it does not establish that the exact same community occupied the shelter without interruption.

There is an additional lesson about the names we give things. Nachikufan classifies a stone-tool tradition. Early Iron Age usually refers to recurring combinations of ceramics, iron-related material, and chronology. The two terms were coined by specialists studying different artifact classes. Their coexistence in one sequence does not mean that two self-aware peoples named Nachikufan and Iron Age signed a treaty. We are observing classifications overlapping, and asking what human lives made that overlap possible.

Nakapapula does not solve the whole problem of farming and foraging contacts in central Africa. Its excavation is one site, and the original publication's arguments deserve review against current dating and methods. But it blocks a misleading question: on what date did all the people in Zambia stop using stone and begin using iron? No single date exists, and no single household story can serve as the regional answer. At Nakapapula, what matters is that the archaeological record holds practices once treated as successive within overlapping centuries. That opens a fuller history of coexistence, exchange, and choice.

## Houses on a Moving Shore: The Iron Age beside Lake Malawi

Along Lake Malawi, shifting shores create another problem: the landscape in which a settlement stood may no longer look the same.

Imagine setting a bowl near the edge of a great lake and returning many generations later. The waterline has moved. The old settlement may now stand farther from the shore, or it may have been covered by rising water. At the southern end of Lake Malawi, archaeologists have had to reconstruct the history of villages against a shifting geography. Pottery fragments are a large part of that work. But the central story is not simply a sequence of decorated pots. It is how people made homes, found food, used tools, and maintained connections while the lake and the surrounding landscape changed.

Lake Malawi lies in the East African Rift. Its waters drain through the Shire River, while its shores include bays, flood-prone ground, hills, and pathways toward the Zambezi basin. Lake levels have varied over time. A site situated on a raised shore may preserve evidence of settlement during a time when water was higher, but a position on a modern map is not an ancient coastline. Dating the occupation and reconstructing its contemporary environment are separate tasks. Researchers compare sediments, archaeological contexts, and measurements of earlier shoreline positions, aware that local erosion and later building may have removed portions of the record.

By around the third century of the common era, communities in southern Malawi were using iron and pottery in settings archaeologists call Early Iron Age. Yusuf Juwayeyi's study of the southern Lake Malawi region describes the extensive excavation campaigns that developed this chronology. One widely recognized ceramic tradition is called Nkope, after a site near the southern lakeshore. Typical Nkope vessels include globular pots and bowls with distinctive rim treatments and combinations of incised or impressed decoration. Such details allow excavators to compare deposits even when no complete pot survives. They do not label the ethnicity of everyone who made or used a vessel.

An archaeologist building a timeline might first ask where a fragment was found. Was it inside a hearth, at the floor of a dwelling, in a pit, or washed into a later deposit? Is the radiocarbon sample associated securely with that pottery? Then comes a second question: do vessels in neighboring areas share forms and decorative habits, or do the similarities end at one motif? Finally, a third: can differences be understood as time passing, as neighboring styles, or as different tasks within one household? These steps help prevent a circle in which a pot is dated because of its style and the style is dated because of the pot.

The ceramic sequence is not a simple chain in which one named group vanished the morning another appeared. Kapeni pottery, recognizable by different combinations of form and incised decoration, occurs in deposits that can overlap with late Nkope phases. Dates differ from one excavated locality to another. Juwayeyi's review explains why radiocarbon samples, and not a rigid typological ladder, are necessary for establishing the extent of that overlap. The presence of a Kapeni sherd in one district does not prove that an entire Nkope population left. People could alter vessel-making habits while maintaining local families and food traditions, or exchange pots made elsewhere.

Spana Davison's research at Namaso Bay provides a particularly revealing case. He defined a Namaso archaeological phase during the late eighth to early eleventh centuries of the common era, using well-dated settlement evidence on the southeastern arm of the lake. Those dates place Namaso in a period that had not been represented adequately in earlier local classifications. The associated settlement lay in a landscape of unusually high lake levels. Researchers found that the material record reflected many local resources, while also showing connections to wider exchange networks. A site that once appeared to fill a chronological gap became a means of questioning how the whole regional sequence was constructed.

At Namaso, the evidence is not a story in which everyone suddenly depended on imported iron. Davison discusses local materials including stone and shell, and contacts that brought or circulated goods such as copper and cowries. Objects made from different substances can be useful in different tasks. Stone need not disappear when iron is present. A small shell or copper object may reveal a connection, yet it cannot tell us by itself how many journeys or intermediaries lay between the shore and its source. The materials point to mixed economic activities, not a tidy split between isolated self-sufficient villages and cosmopolitan trading towns.

There is a reason to ask about ordinary work as well as technology. Living beside a lake could involve fishing, cultivating nearby land, gathering shore and wetland resources, maintaining households, collecting clay, gathering firewood, and tending objects that could not survive archaeologically. Different sites could emphasize these activities differently. A grinding implement shows food processing, but not necessarily the cultivation of a particular grain. A pot near fish bones might have been used for many kinds of food. The reconstruction is strongest when several lines of evidence agree and weakest when a single artifact is made to stand for a whole economy.

Some pottery traditions in the region extend into the later medieval centuries and beyond the approximate year 1500, the chronological endpoint of this course. Mawudzu and still later Nkhudzi ceramics appear in archaeological schemes for the later part of Malawi's Iron Age. These names help researchers sort collections and ask when settlements changed. They should not be projected backward to create continuous, unbroken political identities reaching into the first millennium. The later histories of Chewa-speaking communities, important as they are, require oral traditions, comparative linguistics, and historical inquiry in their own right; pottery cannot replace those sources.

Why do historians pay so much attention to a changing lakeshore? Because its movements affect where food, settlement ground, and travel routes could be found, and also which traces survive for excavation. A rise in water might coincide with different settlement choices; it does not automatically cause every cultural change observed in the same centuries. The dates might not align closely enough. Families could respond in different ways. Ecological evidence should be used to enlarge the possibilities we consider, not to turn every political or economic development into a simple response to climate.

The history of southern Lake Malawi is therefore made from repeated acts of comparison: one vessel with another, one buried surface with the present shore, one set of tool materials with the things missing from a deposit, and one chronology with new dates that challenge it. Nkope, Kapeni, and Namaso are useful labels for parts of that record. The people who made homes there had histories more complicated than those labels. As the shoreline changed, their tools, settlements, and connections changed too, sometimes together and sometimes on different schedules. Reconstructing those schedules is the work of archaeology.

## A Seed, a Bone, and a Village: Farming in the Zambian Interior

Turning inland again, evidence from Zambia tests the familiar picture in which crops, herds, pots, and iron all arrive together.

What did early farming actually look like in the Zambian interior? An easy answer would be a village of grain fields, cattle, iron tools, and clay pots. But an archaeologist arriving at such a site may find abundant sherds, only a few recognizable seeds, and bones shattered into pieces too small to identify by shape. Each category tells a different part of the story. Near the Mulungushi River, sites called Muteteshi and Fibobe the Second offer evidence of early crops and ironworking. Farther south and west, animal bones from other communities show how hunting continued alongside livestock. The difficulty is to hear these different records together without forcing them into one standardized village.

Muteteshi lies in the central Zambian region known to archaeologists as the Irumide Belt. Excavations by John Robertson and colleagues in the late 1970s established an Early Iron Age occupation, with radiocarbon measurements broadly around two thousand to sixteen hundred years before the middle of the twentieth century. Researchers reported early evidence of domesticated sorghum there. Sorghum is a grain capable of growing under many African rainfall conditions, but the discovery of its remains at a site is a local finding, not the date when all of Zambia began to farm. The crop identification comes from earlier archaeological work, summarized in later research, and it must be read in the context of that excavation.

At nearby Fibobe the Second, excavators documented traces of houses, areas associated with ironworking, and the cultivation of domesticated crops. The researchers who revisited the area reported a later Iron Age phase there, around fourteen hundred to twelve hundred years before the present in conventional radiocarbon language. In calendar terms, those fall mainly in the middle to later first millennium of the common era, depending on calibration and on which dates describe which structures. Hut traces reveal places where people built and lived; iron debris can reveal production activities; crop remains add evidence about cultivation. None of these, separately, identifies the language spoken in a dwelling or the precise division of labor within its household.

We should distinguish three kinds of plant evidence. A grinding stone can show that hard material was processed, including seeds or other plants, but not which plants without further evidence. A charred grain identified by its form may indicate a particular crop, provided it really belongs to the dated context. A direct date on that grain is stronger than a date on nearby long-lived charcoal, which might have burned long after the tree began growing. These are not arbitrary levels of skepticism. They separate a useful clue from a stronger determination of the time and substance of cultivation.

Animal evidence presents the opposite problem: there may be many fragments but few recognizable bones. Cattle and large antelope can leave similar-looking pieces, especially after cooking, trampling, weathering, and breakage. In 2021, a team led by Anneke Janzen published work applying a method called Zooarchaeology by Mass Spectrometry, usually shortened to ZooMS. Instead of asking only whether a fragment looks like a particular bone, the researchers examined surviving collagen proteins. Different animal lineages have identifiable combinations of peptide markers; those patterns can sometimes distinguish domestic cattle from wild bovids, even when the original shape is unrecognizable.

The team studied material from six archaeological sites in central, southern, and southwestern Zambia. These included Muteteshi, Fibobe the Second, Kapiri Mposhi A, Kalundu Mound, Salumano, and Jakobo West. They did not get identical results everywhere. Preservation was uneven, and the number of usable fragments varied drastically among sites. At Muteteshi, all four bones tested failed to yield sufficient collagen for useful ZooMS identifications. This is important: the 2021 collagen study cannot be cited as having newly identified cattle or wild game at Muteteshi. The site remains significant for its other archaeological evidence, but one line of analysis failed there.

Across other analyzed sites, the peptide identifications showed that domestic cattle were part of the animal economy and that people also consumed or otherwise used wild bovids. The researchers reported increased taxonomic diversity compared with identification by bone shape alone. This complicates the image of farming communities abandoning hunting once they kept livestock. An economy can include fields, herds, and wild animals simultaneously. But the team studied preserved and selected bone samples, not every meal eaten in the region. The proportion of fragments in a small excavation is not automatically the proportion of meat on a household's table.

Kalundu Mound, much farther south on the Batoka Plateau, shows why a new date can matter. Earlier charcoal measurements had suggested a broad chronological span for the mound. More recent direct dates on short-lived plant remains and bone, combined in a statistical model, concentrated the relevant occupation in approximately the thirteenth and fourteenth centuries. This later phase is associated with what archaeologists call the Kalomo tradition. Its history should not be used to backdate the Muteteshi crop evidence, or to suggest that cattle-keeping began across the whole country at Kalundu. They are different sites with different phases of occupation.

Kapiri Mposhi A supplies another caution. The 2021 research described a long material sequence with stone tools and later pottery but noted that the site had not yet been directly dated. Comparisons of artifacts suggested a broad timespan, and the investigators proposed contacts between stone-tool-using communities and iron-using farmers. A material resemblance is a useful preliminary clue; it is not a calendar date. In a region with many recurring forms of vessels and stone tools, dating the actual deposit matters before any detailed account of contact can be settled.

All of this changes the conventional phrase 'the arrival of agriculture.' Farming is not one object that lands in a new region. It includes selected crops, remembered knowledge about seasons and soils, ways of saving and preparing seed, permissions to use land, and work shared across households. Herding adds obligations for animals; ironworking requires fuel and specialist skill; hunting demands knowledge of other habitats. Pottery fragments preserve only some of these relationships. Archaeology can reveal their material foundations, but the social rules behind them are more difficult to recover. We should avoid replacing those missing rules with a universal script.

It is also worth asking who makes the evidence. Archaeologists excavate and identify objects, but the interpretation depends on field workers, collections, laboratory specialists, and access to dated samples. The ZooMS study included collaboration with the Livingstone Museum and the National Museums of Kenya. Its value is not that laboratory technology replaces excavation. Collagen spectra matter only when the sample's site, layer, and history are recorded. Conversely, a carefully excavated but badly preserved bone may not yield an animal identification. Different forms of evidence are complementary, not interchangeable.

So imagine placing three items on a table: a sorghum grain associated with Muteteshi's earlier farming evidence, an ironworking trace at Fibobe the Second, and an identifiable wild-animal fragment from another Zambian assemblage. Together they show possibilities that existed in the wider region across different times. They were not excavated from one household, and they must not be narrated as its menu or toolkit. The history we can defend is more interesting than the invented composite. Communities combined cultivation, craft production, livestock, and hunting differently across the Zambian interior, and each site asks us to discover that combination rather than assume it.

#### Evidence boundary

The excavated chronology of a settlement must not be equated with the age of its later ditches. Ntusi's earlier occupation and the fifteenth- to sixteenth-century earthworks overlap the course boundary at 1500. The UNESCO records are Uganda's tentative nominations, not World Heritage inscriptions. Cwezi/Kitara traditions are historically consequential, but no excavation cited here establishes a single empire ruled by the named legendary figures. Proposed earthwork functions remain interpretations.

#### Evidence boundary

Kalambo's approximately 476,000-year-old wood is not evidence of continuous habitation up to the first-millennium Iron Age. Early Iron Age dates and ceramic group definitions are reconstructed from uneven contexts; objects are not direct evidence for languages, exact domestic organization, or a single migrating population. The older survey and Cambridge synthesis are historical scholarship, not the latest excavated site-by-site chronology.

#### Evidence boundary

Muteteshi's sorghum evidence is reported from earlier excavations, not newly established by the 2021 ZooMS samples. All four Muteteshi specimens failed to yield useful collagen; the cattle/wild-bovid conclusions come from other sampled sites. Kalundu Mound's later dates do not date Muteteshi. A crop, hearth, or bone does not by itself establish a representative diet, domestic property rules, or the spoken language of a household.
