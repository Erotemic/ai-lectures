# Roads before the Empire: Andean States and the Rise of the Inca

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

A road crosses an Andean mountain slope. It climbs from one ecological zone toward another, sometimes near steep terrain where transport is difficult. When the Inca state expanded in the fifteenth century, it maintained and enlarged a remarkable network of such routes. Yet not every segment began with the Inca. People in the Andes had built paths, cultivated terraces, managed water, moved herds, and linked communities for centuries. To begin the story with an emperor ordering a road would mistake the last major extension of a system for its entire history. The political world of the Andes before 1500 contains older states, regional loyalties, and labor arrangements on which imperial expansion depended.

Andean geography encouraged diverse forms of production. Coastal valleys supported irrigated agriculture amid desert terrain. Highland communities used lands at different elevations for crops and pastoralism. Llamas and alpacas could supply wool, transport, and other resources, while fishing and coastal trade were important in different areas. Communities did not all solve environmental challenges in the same way. Cooperation among related households, exchange between zones, and the management of water were longstanding practices. The great states of the later Middle Ages did not invent Andean knowledge; they sought to mobilize and govern it.

On Peru's northern coast, the Chimú kingdom developed a powerful political center at Chan Chan. Its large adobe complexes, decorated walls, storage areas, and carefully organized spaces demonstrate sustained labor and planning. By the fourteenth and fifteenth centuries, Chimú power extended across several coastal valleys. Irrigation canals and agricultural lands made dense settlement possible, while workshops produced textiles, metal objects, and other goods. The surviving monumental compounds reveal political differentiation, but they do not mean everyone lived inside palace walls. Houses, farms, specialized workshops, and smaller settlements are necessary to understand the whole system.

The Chimú state was not a footnote to the later Inca. It had rulers, military expansion, skilled producers, and relationships with neighboring communities. Archaeologists document distinctive coastal styles and material connections, but some narratives of dynastic origins come through later oral or colonial transmission. We should not turn every archaeological change into the conquest of a named king. Metalworkers, builders, and agricultural workers can be visible through the products of their labor even when their personal names are lost. Chan Chan's layout suggests a highly stratified political society, but precise assignments of every compound require interpretation rather than certainty.

Farther south and in the highlands, communities such as the Wanka, Lupaca, and Qolla maintained different political structures. Religious centers including Pachacamac attracted visitors and patronage. Earlier Wari and Tiwanaku institutions had left architectural, road, and cultural legacies, yet later peoples were not simply replicas of those states. Their histories involved local alliances, rivalries, movement, and adaptation. One effect of telling Andean history only through the Inca is that the Inca's eventual subjects appear to have no history before subjection. The evidence shows the opposite: the empire grew through contact with places that already had durable institutions.

The Inca political center arose around Cusco. Later histories associate a major phase of imperial expansion with the ruler Pachacuti in the fifteenth century, conventionally beginning around 1438. These dates and individual deeds depend substantially on accounts recorded after the Spanish invasion, alongside archaeology and regional traditions. A useful chronology can be built from them, but exact narratives of each campaign and succession dispute should not be repeated without qualification. Expansion accelerated under Inca rulers during the fifteenth century, eventually creating a state known as Tawantinsuyu, or the realm of four parts. Its scale should not obscure how recently some territories had been incorporated.

Imperial government relied on relationships with local leaders, often known as kurakas. Authorities negotiated alliances, demanded labor, settled disputes, and sometimes relocated populations. Military force and threats of punishment were part of rule, but accommodation also mattered. Local communities might retain established leaders or practices while accepting demands from Cusco. That arrangement could shift if an uprising or a succession struggle changed the balance of power. The existence of an administrative system does not mean every province was governed identically or that all subjects experienced incorporation as beneficial.

The road network, known as the Qhapaq Ñan, helped integrate this varied landscape. Routes connected highland and coastal regions through bridges, stairways, rest stations, and storage facilities. Runners could carry messages, and officials and armies could move through places where rapid travel otherwise posed serious challenges. Roads also supported redistribution and communication. Their maintenance required labor from communities along the routes, while their alignments often incorporated older paths. A modern map may display a continuous network, but travelers had to deal with weather, elevation, local conditions, and the actual capacities of each section.

Labor obligation was central to imperial power. In systems often discussed under the term mit'a, communities supplied rotating labor for state projects or services. Work could involve agriculture, construction, transport, military duties, and other tasks. Imperial storage and redistribution were significant features of the system, and reciprocity could be expressed in political ideology. But labor owed to a conquering state was not identical to freely chosen cooperation. Nor should the Inca institution be confused without explanation with the much later colonial mita imposed by Spanish rulers. Similar words can describe arrangements with very different conditions and consequences.

Recording and administration did not require European-style alphabetic manuscripts. Khipus, arrangements of knotted cords, were used for recording information, often associated with quantities and administration; research into their broader functions continues. Their existence challenges the idea that societies without an alphabet had no sophisticated record-keeping. Yet the modern interpretation of particular khipus remains incomplete, and it would be equally misleading to pretend we can read each one as a fully translated narrative. Archaeological objects, colonial descriptions, and continuing Andean practices must be brought into conversation without collapsing their differences.

The empire also supported religious and ceremonial centers, integrating regional sacred places into wider institutions. State ceremonies, agriculture, relationships to ancestors, and local sacred landscapes all mattered. The Inca court promoted forms of worship associated with the Sun and royal ancestors, but regional traditions did not disappear when rulers asserted authority. Some shrines received new patronage or became part of imperial routes. The material record of temples and offerings documents practices, though personal belief is not directly recoverable from every artifact. An empire's official ideology is not an exhaustive statement of what its subjects thought.

The fifteenth-century transformation of the Andes therefore included both political concentration and continued local diversity. Coastal Chimú administration, highland community obligations, agricultural systems, and old roads became resources the expanding Inca state tried to govern. Resistance, negotiation, and continuity coexisted. After 1500 the consequences of Spanish campaigns and epidemics would alter this order, but they should not define the entire story retrospectively. Andean people had already built states and infrastructures that changed over generations. The road is evidence of their accumulated knowledge as much as of imperial power.

When we return to the mountain slope, imagine not one emperor but successive generations of travelers and workers: someone carrying provisions, someone repairing a bridge, someone recording contributions, someone moving an animal herd. The Inca imperial road gave these activities new political purposes and sometimes new constraints. Yet it inherited landscapes shaped by earlier societies. The story of the Andes before 1500 is about how a rapidly expanding state tried to govern a rich collection of existing histories, not about the sudden invention of civilization by a single dynasty.
