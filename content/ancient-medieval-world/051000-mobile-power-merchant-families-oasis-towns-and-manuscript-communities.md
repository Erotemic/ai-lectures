# Mobile power, merchant families, oasis towns, and manuscript communities

Central Asia was a place to live, not merely a route between other places. Steppe political arrangements, merchant letters, painted rooms, oasis towns, and paper contracts reveal different kinds of local life. Their periods overlap unevenly, so the movement among them will be thematic as well as chronological.

## Power That Moved: Pastoral Worlds and the Empires of the Eurasian Steppe

On a map, an empire is often a colored shape. Its borders enclose fields and cities, and its capital is marked with a dot. For the peoples of the Inner Asian grasslands, this familiar picture is misleading. Control could depend on which herding communities followed a leader, which valleys they used in different seasons, and which routes connected people and animals across great distances. A political center might be a mobile court rather than a fixed palace. To understand the early steppe empires, we have to ask what it means to govern people whose movements are both a livelihood and a source of power.

The Eurasian steppe is not a single endless pasture. From the forests of Mongolia and southern Siberia to drier grasslands and desert margins farther west, rainfall, snow, water, and the availability of winter grazing change sharply. Herding families needed detailed knowledge of those differences. Horses, sheep, goats, cattle, and sometimes camels provided transport, food, fiber, and goods for exchange; the composition of herds varied by place. Seasonal movements were choices made within ecological and political constraints. They required household work: tending animals, preparing food, producing felt and clothing, repairing gear, teaching children routes and skills, and negotiating access to pasture. This was a productive way of life, not an absence of settled civilization.

Our written evidence is uneven. Chinese court historians recorded northern peoples because rulers needed information about rivals, allies, and frontier problems. Their texts preserve names, negotiations, and military events, but they also organize foreign societies through the concerns of an imperial court. Archaeology supplies another view: burial grounds, camps, pottery, traces of cultivation, animal bones, and objects made or obtained far away. More recently, research on ancient DNA and isotopes has added evidence about mobility and biological relationships. None of these sources can be substituted for another. A burial can show that an object traveled; it cannot explain every political agreement behind the journey.

The Xiongnu confederation became a formidable power from the late third century before the Common Era. Chinese texts associate its consolidation with a leader called Modu, or Maodun, around 209 BCE. The imperial formation included communities across parts of present-day Mongolia and neighboring regions, and endured in changing forms for roughly three centuries. Its leaders could mobilize armed forces, negotiate with the Han court, and establish relationships among groups with different local histories. The label Xiongnu therefore names a political formation more reliably than it identifies one language, ancestry, or uniform culture. Its scale should not persuade us that every community within it lived in the same way.

From the perspective of Han officials, frontier conflict often took the form of raids, campaigns, gifts, hostages, marriage diplomacy, and arguments over access to markets. Peace agreements could authorize flows of silk or grain; conflict could interrupt them. From the steppe, however, these exchanges were not merely payments extracted from a wealthy neighbor. Alliances and access to goods could help leaders maintain relationships with subordinate communities. A ruler who redistributed coveted materials might strengthen loyalty. The balance changed repeatedly, and the difference between a diplomatic gift and tribute depended in part on whose account survived. The people who bore the costs of campaigns and frontier fortification rarely wrote the records we possess.

Pastoral mobility did not preclude cultivation. Archaeological surveys of the Egiin Gol valley in Mongolia have identified Xiongnu-period settlements, ceramics, and evidence for farming alongside herding. That evidence challenges a picture in which all steppe people lived permanently on horseback and never worked the soil. It does not mean the same combination existed everywhere. A winter settlement, a trading station, and a mobile camp could each be part of one wider regional economy. We learn more by asking where activities occurred and how they were connected than by trying to decide whether an entire population should be classified as nomadic or settled.

Graves reveal both inequality and cultural variety. Some Xiongnu-period burials contain elaborate structures and imported objects, while other burials are modest. Archaeological and genomic research has found considerable diversity among people associated with the empire. An object from far away may indicate trade, diplomacy, inheritance, or movement of people; its presence does not by itself identify the deceased person's language. Biological ancestry is likewise not equivalent to political membership. A confederation could incorporate people from different backgrounds, and a wealthy grave reflects one person's funerary treatment rather than a direct census of living social classes.

Consider the work of supporting a traveling political elite. Horses had to be raised and supplied; carts, saddles, weapons, and textiles had to be made or obtained; messengers had to carry information; winter feeding and water were constant concerns. Imperial politics therefore depended on craftspeople, herders, and intermediaries as well as warriors. Some material goods entered the steppe from China or farther across Eurasia. They could arrive by negotiation, commerce, seizure, or through several stages of exchange. Discovering imported glass or silk does not prove a single merchant traveled from its manufacturing center to the place where archaeologists found it.

The steppe was inhabited by many formations besides the Xiongnu. The Yuezhi, Saka, Xianbei, and later Turkic-speaking groups each appear in different sources and periods. Their histories intersected, sometimes through warfare and displacement, but no single migration story connects them all into a continuous people. It is particularly important not to treat the Xiongnu as simply identical with the much later Huns who entered European records. Scholars debate the possible relationships, but similarity in names or the presence of riding armies does not prove uninterrupted ethnic or political descent across centuries.

In the sixth century CE, rulers identified as Turks, or Türks, organized a powerful khaganate across extensive parts of Inner Asia. They negotiated and fought with Chinese states and interacted with Sogdian cities and other neighbors. Power could shift between eastern and western courts, leaders, and allied communities. The title khagan expressed a claim to leadership over more than a local tribe. A network of mobile people could support expansive authority, yet the same dependence on alliances made political unity fragile. The lands visible on a modern map do not show all the loyalties that had to be sustained to hold the khaganate together.

By the eighth century, some Turkic rulers spoke to posterity in their own written monuments. The Orkhon inscriptions associated with Köl Tegin, Bilge Khagan, and Tonyukuk celebrate leadership, campaigns, and the relationship between rulers and their people. These are among the earliest surviving substantial texts in Old Turkic. They provide a perspective different from a Chinese historian's account of frontier affairs. But they, too, are selective. An inscription erected for a ruler has reasons to praise victories, warn against disloyalty, and present past events as a lesson. It lets us examine political argument, not read a verbatim diary of every person under the khagan's authority.

The Orkhon valley also contains evidence for the Uighur polity that followed the earlier Turkic khaganate. The eighth- and ninth-century center at Khar Balgas shows that rulers with deep pastoral traditions could support substantial built settlements and administrative or ceremonial centers. It is wrong to imagine that urbanism belonged only to one side of a permanent wall between farmers and nomads. Political authorities could use different settlement forms for different purposes. A capital, a seasonal camp, and a distant oasis community might each contribute to a single imperial system without becoming identical places.

Women appear in the archaeological record and sometimes in written sources, but reconstructing their full range of work and authority remains difficult. Burials can offer evidence for status and individual lifeways; they do not tell us every task performed by women in ordinary households. The production of clothing, food, shelter materials, and animal products was indispensable to mobility, whatever formal titles royal histories chose to record. A history written only through male war leaders would leave out much of the labor that made steppe power possible. Yet imposing one universal model of steppe gender relations across a thousand years would be another way of replacing evidence with an expectation.

Environmental change mattered, but it cannot explain political change alone. Snow conditions could make winter pastures difficult, drought could alter migration strategies, and disease could affect herds. Conflict or a failed alliance might deny a community access to needed land at precisely the wrong moment. These forces interacted rather than operating as a single environmental switch. When an empire fragmented, people did not simply disappear from the grasslands. They formed new coalitions, moved into different relationships with neighboring states, retained cultural practices, and adapted economic routines. The breakup of a court is not the end of the society around it.

There is a larger lesson here. Writing from cities often made the steppe visible mainly at moments of war, when mounted forces threatened a frontier. Archaeology and self-authored monuments make a more complicated history possible: herding households, cultivated patches, local settlements, diverse communities, imperial diplomacy, and arguments about legitimate leadership. The governments of Inner Asia were not incomplete copies of agrarian states. They drew on a different set of skills and relationships, and their people helped build the networks connecting Eurasia. The next time a map leaves the grasslands blank, remember that a blank space on paper is not an absence of history.

## Letters That Never Arrived: Sogdian Families and the Cities of Central Asia

To see movement at a household scale, turn from political arrangements of the grasslands to the fourth-century letters of Sogdian merchants and their relatives.

Early in the fourth century CE, someone folded a letter, marked where it should go, and entrusted it to the long roads running west from the Chinese frontier. The letter did not reach its intended recipient. Centuries later, it was found in a ruined watchtower near Dunhuang, with other messages. This accident of preservation gives us something imperial chronicles rarely provide: the concerns of merchants and relatives separated by distance, money, and political danger. The letters were written in Sogdian, an Iranian language associated with the cities around Samarkand. Their discovery means that for a few moments we can hear individuals rather than a court speaking about the world they inhabited.

The letters are known today as the Sogdian Ancient Letters. Archaeologist and explorer Aurel Stein found them in 1907; they are held in the British Library. Nicholas Sims-Williams and other specialists have studied and translated them, explaining both their language and the difficulties of their dating. The surviving bundle is small and incomplete. It is not an archive of everyone who traveled between China and Central Asia. But these pages are enough to show that Sogdian people were established in several Chinese towns, and that families and commercial partnerships could stretch across thousands of kilometers.

Two of the letters were written by women. One writer, Miwnay, describes being left in Dunhuang by her husband and facing severe hardship. Another concerns her daughter Shayn. The details are available through translation, and the original phrasing matters: we must not invent a private scene or supply words that the manuscript does not preserve. Even without embellishment, the circumstances are stark. A person traveling or living far from her home community depended on promises, money, relatives, and intermediaries. When those obligations failed, the distance between cities became not a romantic adventure but an obstacle to survival.

These letters unsettle the familiar image of the Silk Road as a line of camels carrying luxury cloth. People needed places to stay, partners who would honor debts, relatives willing to receive messages, and arrangements for children and property. Merchants could profit, but family members might bear risks they did not choose. The women in the surviving correspondence were capable of expressing their situation in writing, yet that does not show that all Sogdian women had equal education or independence. Their letters survive because a particular packet was lost in a particular place. The historian must value their testimony without making the accident of survival representative of an entire society.

Another letter, written by the merchant Nanai-vandak, was addressed to associates in Samarkand. It concerns commercial affairs and political upheaval in China. Its writer reports the destruction of Luoyang in the turmoil of the early fourth century. The letter is especially useful for dating because the fall of Luoyang occurred in 311 CE, placing the report soon afterward, probably in 312 or 313. Here a private business communication preserves news of an event also found in Chinese historical sources. The two kinds of evidence can support each other without having identical purposes or describing identical experiences.

The material form of this correspondence matters. A letter wrapped in silk, then in coarser material, with delivery instructions, was built to travel through a succession of hands. Its survival suggests routes, packaging, literacy, and trust. It does not tell us how often every courier succeeded. We know the names of some goods in these letters, including precious metals, textiles, and aromatics, because merchants had reason to keep track of them. Grain and other ordinary materials also appear. Long-distance exchange was not one continuous trip made by each trader; goods and messages might pass through several cities and agents before reaching a destination.

Samarkand and neighboring Sogdian centers lay in what is now Uzbekistan and Tajikistan. Their inhabitants farmed irrigated land, worked in towns, and participated in commerce. The history of Sogdiana is therefore not a story of merchants floating between other people's empires. Cities had their own political authorities, surrounding villages, religious institutions, and local rivalries. The links eastward to China and westward toward Iranian and other Central Asian worlds enlarged their possibilities, but they also exposed communities to wars and changing taxes. A merchant partnership could succeed or fail for reasons far beyond the price of a length of silk.

By the sixth and seventh centuries, Sogdian commercial and cultural networks had become even more visible. Sogdian people lived in China, worked as interpreters, transported goods, and acted as intermediaries between courts and caravans. Some served as trainers and handlers of horses. It is tempting to call them the people who ran the Silk Road, but that would give a single group control over countless routes used by many others. Chinese, Turkic, Iranian, Indian, and other merchants and communities mattered. Sogdian traders were influential participants in intersecting regional systems, not owners of a transcontinental highway.

An extraordinary set of paintings found at Afrasiab, near Samarkand, opens a different window onto this world. Created in the middle of the seventh century, the murals show a richly dressed court and delegations or figures associated with several lands. An inscription mentions Varkhuman, a ruler of Samarkand. The pictures make claims about diplomatic reach and prestige. Yet parts of the murals are missing, and scholars disagree about who originally occupied the center of the most important scene. Art can document the court's chosen image of itself while leaving central political questions unresolved. The missing plaster is part of the history of our knowledge.

These images also remind us that a city was a place of ceremony as well as exchange. Processions and offerings connected public authority to ritual practice. The murals include figures presented in different styles of dress; they are evidence for what court artists considered worth depicting, not a complete register of foreign visitors. If a painting includes a person in Chinese attire, that does not establish the exact itinerary of the individual who posed for it—or even that a literal embassy appeared on the precise day portrayed. Read as political art, the murals are extraordinarily informative. Read as a photograph, they would mislead.

Religion in Sogdiana cannot be confined to one label. Archaeological and textual sources attest traditions related to Mazdaism and Zoroastrianism, and communities or individuals participating in Buddhism, Christianity, Manichaeism, and other religious worlds. The evidence belongs to different places and centuries, so a list of religions is not proof that every town welcomed every practice at every moment. Sogdian speakers also translated and transmitted religious texts. Language skills served worship as well as business, and writing crossed boundaries that armies sometimes tried to enforce. Religious objects, painted walls, and manuscripts illuminate different portions of this history.

The Sogdian script developed from forms related to Aramaic writing, but language and script changed over time. A text may preserve a word in a spelling convention inherited from an earlier written tradition. Specialists must recognize those conventions before they can offer a defensible translation. Many Sogdian manuscripts were found far from Sogdiana, especially in the oases of what is now western China. That distribution reflects both ancient movement and the places where fragile materials were eventually preserved and excavated. An archive's geography is not a simple map of the number of people who once spoke a language.

A source should also be followed through its modern history. Stein's 1907 expedition took place amid competitive collecting and imperial expeditions in Central Asia and western China. The documents entered European collections, where philologists painstakingly reconstructed their meaning. Naming the collector, present repository, editor, and translator helps us understand why we can read the letters today and what steps stand between an ancient writer and our modern interpretation. A translation is a scholarly achievement, not a transparent window with no human mediation. Where the original wording is uncertain, our interpretation must remain provisional.

We should not let the dramatic history of a lost mailbag overwhelm quieter evidence. Coins, pottery, paintings, tomb furnishings, domestic buildings, and administrative texts point to the labor sustaining Sogdian cities. Farmers managed water; craftspeople worked metal and wood; households prepared goods for use and sale. Political rulers and religious specialists made claims on these resources. Merchants depended on the products of people who might never leave their valley. The term trading people can illuminate an important feature of Sogdian history while concealing an equally important world of agricultural and urban work.

What finally makes the Ancient Letters remarkable is not that they prove commerce existed. Other evidence already tells us that. It is that they disclose the fragile human arrangements within commerce. A merchant attempts to inform partners; a wife tries to reach people who might help her; a child is drawn into obligations she did not create. War, delay, trust, family, and writing intersect on sheets of paper. When we say Central Asia connected Eurasia, those connections were not abstract arrows on a map. They were relationships maintained, and sometimes broken, by people whose names we can occasionally recover.

## The Painted Room: Households and Stories in Panjikent

Several centuries later and farther west, paintings in Panjikent let us enter a different kind of household record.

In an early eighth-century house in Panjikent, a person entering a reception room could find a story spread across the walls. Warriors confront monsters; a hero takes part in battles; brilliant blue paint turns the room into a stage. Modern archaeologists call one such space the Rustam Room because episodes resemble the Iranian hero's adventures that later became famous through epic literature. The paintings were not made for us to read. They were part of the life of a household that had guests to receive, relationships to display, and stories worth placing where people gathered. That setting gives us a different beginning for Sogdian history from the familiar picture of merchants and camels on the Silk Road.

Panjikent, in what is now Tajikistan, stood within Sogdiana, the historical region of Iranian-speaking towns and agricultural districts between major Central Asian rivers. Its inhabitants participated in exchange that reached China, the Iranian plateau, and neighboring steppe societies, but the town was also a place where people lived without constantly traveling. Its houses needed water and fuel. Its craftworkers needed customers. Parents raised children, hosts received visitors, and rituals marked life's passages. Archaeology recovers some of these routines by excavating the rooms of a city, rather than following only the names in travelers' accounts and diplomatic correspondence.

The city did not appear at once in its final form. Archaeologists identify an earlier Panjikent taking shape in late antiquity, with temples and relatively modest houses of mud brick or compacted earth. Across succeeding building phases, streets and domestic spaces became more elaborate. The Smithsonian exhibition's city reconstruction describes an enlarged urban core and richer multistory houses in the seventh and eighth centuries. These are stages of growth, not a record of every inhabitant becoming wealthier. A rebuilt wall can show a city's changing plan while still leaving us uncertain who moved away, who moved in, or what pressures led a family to rebuild its home.

Inside a wealthy reception hall, painted walls did more than make a room attractive. Low plastered benches around the perimeter offered places to sit. A large image of a deity could face the entrance, while additional registers of scenes carried episodes of myth, legend, or social ceremony. A guest could look across to the sacred figure, along a wall of stories, and toward the people sharing food or conversation. We must not claim to know exactly what any visitor thought. But the layout shows that architecture helped stage hospitality, religious identity, and the public life of a household. A prosperous home could be an important social institution without being the royal palace.

The Rustam cycle is particularly revealing because its scenes appear centuries before Ferdowsi completed the Shahnameh, the Persian Book of Kings, around the turn of the eleventh century. This does not mean the walls are illustrations copied from that surviving poem. Rather, related heroic stories circulated long before they took the literary shape familiar today. In Panjikent the painter selected moments that could communicate a narrative visually. The figures may be recognized by attributes, actions, and positions, but identifications depend on interpretation. A scholar can compare an image with later written stories without claiming a one-to-one correspondence or assuming the written version was already fixed.

Art in Panjikent was not all heroic combat. Temple walls depict worshippers, deities, donors, and ceremonies. Religious images display strikingly varied visual languages, some related to Iranian traditions and some drawing on iconography shared across neighboring regions. Scholars have identified a four-armed goddess in several contexts, while debating details of particular attributes and names. We should resist making an inventory of religious identities from a few images. To recognize a divine figure is not to determine what every resident believed, and a richly decorated shrine may tell us more about patrons' resources than about the private convictions of the population.

There were workshops too. As Panjikent grew, some wealthy householders developed small commercial rooms or shops along streets, spaces that could be used by craftspeople. This was a city of buildings that combined domestic wealth with other people's work. A painted reception hall could exist close to workshops where labor was repetitive, noisy, and less likely to leave a prestigious picture. That contrast is important. Murals survive most visibly from patrons who could afford them. If we call the whole settlement a painted city, we may inadvertently let the affluent represent everyone. The ordinary architecture uncovered by excavations helps correct that imbalance.

Beyond the walls stood funerary structures associated with ossuaries, containers for cleaned bones used in some Sogdian mortuary practices. These are another way to read the social landscape. A town was not only a market and a place of residence; it maintained relationships with its dead. The archaeology of a burial structure can reveal the form of a ritual, its materials, and sometimes its chronology. It cannot tell us precisely what a grieving person said at the ceremony. Family ties and the care of the dead may have persisted through wars and political changes that dominate narrative histories.

Panjikent also belonged to a dangerous political world. During the eighth century armies associated with the expanding Arab caliphate transformed the politics of Transoxiana. Political leaders, local alliances, and populations made different choices. Some documentary evidence survives from neighboring Sogdian settings, including records found at Mount Mugh, but it cannot be made into a full political diary of every Panjikent household. Fires, rebuilding, and changing uses of space complicate the archaeology. A destroyed building gives a dated disruption only if its destruction layer and associated material can be dated with care; its ruin does not automatically tell us who ordered an attack or what each resident did next.

The preserved colors tempt us to speak as though we can stand in a perfectly reconstructed room. That is not quite true. Paint was removed from walls in fragments; panels were restored and sometimes reassembled; museum light and photographs change how a surface is seen. Art historians use location, building sequence, comparison, and iconographic detail to make responsible identifications. A picture may be rich in information and still retain essential ambiguities. If a painted figure holds a vessel, is the scene a banquet, a ritual offering, or part of a longer epic? The answer depends on other surviving details, not our first impression.

The Sogdians are sometimes treated as a people defined by their ability to connect distant civilizations. That description notices their remarkable networks but misses the labor of producing a society at home. Panjikent's houses show the architecture of family status. Its workshops connect patrons to craftsmen. Its temples and burial structures reveal communal practices. Its stories show that people could recognize a cultural world reaching beyond any one king's court. The same town might be tied to international exchange while possessing strong local traditions. Neither role needs to be reduced to the other.

Return to the painted room. Its walls do not preserve the voice of the original storyteller, and the person who commissioned the paintings has not left us a modern autobiography. Yet the architecture tells us where listeners might sit, and the images tell us what themes patrons considered worthy of display. History does not always require a king's name. It can emerge from the distance between a household's public room and the city's working streets. Panjikent allows us to study a Central Asian city as a place that made stories, goods, religious meanings, and social differences, not merely as a place through which travelers passed.

## Cities beyond the Pass: Suyab and Navikat in the Chuy Valley

North and east across the mountain routes, towns in the Chuy Valley challenge the idea of Central Asia as empty space between famous centers.

On the way through the Chuy Valley, east of today's Bishkek in Kyrgyzstan, a traveler could find settlements rather than a gap between famous cities. One place later became known as Suyab, whose ruins are called Ak-Beshim. Another, Navikat, is associated with Krasnaya Rechka. In accounts of the Silk Road, such places can appear as dots on a route from one empire to another. But a dot on a map is not a town. It represents walls, homes, ritual buildings, local fields, and institutions through which visitors entered an inhabited world. The question is not simply what passed through the valley. It is how the valley's people made such passage possible.

The Chuy Valley lies along one of the northern corridors connecting Central Asia with places farther east and west. Towns expanded there in the sixth century and later medieval centuries, in a landscape of settled agriculture and mobile pastoral power. They were linked to the Western Turkic and other regional political formations, but also to Sogdian-speaking communities whose urban and commercial networks were strong across Central Asia. Different kinds of authority met here: a khan's court, a city community, a religious foundation, a market, and local agricultural households. There is no reason to expect a single uniform language or political identity at every stage.

The identification of Ak-Beshim with Suyab rests on a combination of historical records and excavated remains. Scholars have studied the city through several campaigns of excavation. It is not enough to find a ruined wall and simply attach an ancient name to it because a source mentions a city nearby. The names in written records, the location, the dated material, and the sequence of building phases need to fit together. Once that identification becomes persuasive, archaeologists can ask a new range of questions: which communities used the religious buildings, how long were they maintained, and what happened as the town's political connections changed?

Some of the most informative evidence comes from religious buildings. Excavations at Suyab have uncovered Buddhist structures, a Zoroastrian-associated cemetery, and Christian church buildings. An account in Encyclopaedia Iranica describes a tenth- to early-eleventh-century church with finds including Christian symbols and inscriptions. These remains do not mean every faith flourished at precisely the same moment or that the town had equal numbers of adherents. What they show is that different traditions established physical institutions in the valley across its history. A sanctuary is evidence of investment and use, not a census.

One pottery vessel discovered in a church context carries a Sogdian inscription that specialists interpret as a dedication to a person named Alan. Such a tiny text is tempting because it suddenly supplies a voice where most building stones are anonymous. Yet even here the possibilities are limited. The inscription records a presentation or dedication, not a full biography of Alan. Its language does not tell us which language every worshipper spoke, just as a Christian object does not turn an entire city into a single religious community. The artifact is a trace of connection between writing, gift, and institution, valuable precisely because it is specific.

Navikat offers another view. Archaeologists working at Krasnaya Rechka have found remains associated with Buddhist temple complexes and other traditions, as well as evidence of a settlement shaped by successive inhabitants and rebuilding. A Kyrgyzstan heritage nomination submitted to UNESCO describes these remains as part of a northern Silk Roads cultural landscape. That is useful orientation, but the nomination is not a neutral excavation diary. Its purpose is to describe why the sites should be preserved. Responsible history checks the kinds of buildings reported, then asks what the original finds, their dates, and independent archaeological publications permit us to conclude.

It is common to read about Sogdian merchants in China and imagine a single Sogdian commercial population moving along a road. The Chuy sites offer a less tidy picture. Sogdian writing, Turkic political influence, and religious forms linked to several parts of Eurasia intersected in towns that were continually rebuilt. A label such as Sogdian might describe a language used in a dedication or a family tradition, not every resident's ancestry. Similarly, an association with a Turkic khanate tells us something about political relationships but does not determine the form of every shop, farm, or household.

A town like Suyab needed food and materials even when its fame depended on routes reaching far beyond it. Agricultural production along a valley, pastoral exchange with people of surrounding grasslands, and local craftwork created the conditions in which a traveler might find supplies and shelter. It would be a mistake to imagine every bowl and coin there was an import. Archaeological objects become evidence of far-reaching links when their material, technique, form, or inscriptions can be connected through independent comparison. Ordinary ceramics may reveal more about local production than about travel. A city could be both a crossroads and a home.

Political change affected the valley repeatedly. Western Turkic, Türgesh, Chinese and later regional powers all interacted with the northern routes at different times. But a place name in a campaign narrative tells us little about whether the same merchant families occupied their shops through each episode. Occupation phases and renovations must be dated against those narratives, not forced into them. A military source may describe a ruler's arrival as the decisive event, while archaeology shows ongoing construction, abandonment in one quarter, or religious rebuilding elsewhere. The two kinds of evidence ask different questions, and neither makes the other unnecessary.

There is also a problem of historical visibility. A church with unusual inscriptions may receive more attention than an ordinary room. A Buddhist temple whose walls remain high appears more impressive than a workshop destroyed by later construction. The artifacts that survive, and the kinds of excavations undertaken, decide which activities are easiest to describe. These limitations do not weaken archaeological history. They tell us to avoid converting a list of spectacular objects into a ranking of what mattered most to the people who lived nearby.

One can understand the historical significance of the Chuy Valley without making its towns miniature copies of Rome, Persia, China, or any other imperial center. The evidence suggests settlements that combined local resources with writing systems, religious practices, and political relationships spanning an enormous region. The culture of a northern route was produced partly by the people who stayed in place. In Suyab and Navikat, they maintained buildings, gave gifts, used languages that crossed boundaries, and lived under changing forms of authority. The Silk Road was not a road laid out once and then traversed by identical caravans. It was a moving network sustained by inhabited places.

Imagine leaving the valley and looking back. The last thing you see might be an earthen mound, not the bustling town it once held. A map tells you where to put the mound; excavations tell you how some of its rooms and shrines were arranged; an inscription gives you a name and a word spoken or written by someone long gone. Together they yield a history more precise than the label crossroads. It is a history of institutions that made connections possible, and of communities whose lives cannot be reduced to the empire that happened to claim a route at a given moment.

## Papers from the Oasis: Everyday Life in Medieval Turfan

Returning toward the eastern oases, paper records at Turfan bring daily agreements and local institutions into focus.

A contract preserved on a scrap of paper can outlast the people whose agreement it records. In the oases around Turfan, in what is now the Xinjiang region of China, manuscripts survived in dry conditions long enough for modern scholars to discover a world of monasteries, merchants, scribes, farmers, and officials. Medieval Central Asia is often introduced as a road between empires. But an oasis is more than a stopping point on someone else's journey. Water must be managed, land worked, disputes settled, and religious communities supported. The documents from Turfan allow us to ask about those lives directly, even though many pages are damaged and many personal stories remain inaccessible.

The oases sit amid arid landscapes where settlement depends on reliable access to water and cultivated land. Travelers crossed mountains and deserts to reach markets and religious centers, but residents faced repeated local demands: repairing irrigation works, producing food, acquiring fuel, maintaining houses, and organizing relationships among families. A caravan could not move through a place that had no people sustaining it. The familiar phrase Silk Road can help orient a map, but it can also conceal a network made of many branches and numerous locally governed communities. Turfan was one inhabited center among several, connected to but not defined by distant courts.

The political history of the region changed when Uyghur groups moved toward the eastern Tian Shan and Gansu regions following the breakdown of their steppe empire in the ninth century. The resulting oasis polities were not simple continuations of a mobile steppe state. They interacted with peoples already living in the region and took part in existing traditions of administration, trade, and religion. The written records include Old Uyghur as well as other languages and scripts. Such variety is evidence of contact and cultural production, but language found on paper is not a census of everyone's speech. A person could use one language for a prayer and another for business.

The Berlin Turfan Collection preserves thousands of manuscript fragments and printed pieces gathered during expeditions in the early twentieth century. Simone-Christiane Raschmann's account for the Berlin State Library explains their diverse scripts, their religious and secular contents, and the difficult task of dating many pieces. The expeditions removed materials from Central Asia into German institutions, an event with its own colonial-era research history. Cataloguing and conservation now make study possible, while the dispersal of finds shapes what can be reconstructed. An archive's modern location is not evidence that a text originated with the people who collected it.

A large share of the surviving written material is religious. Old Uyghur Buddhists copied and translated texts associated with several language traditions. Manichaean communities also produced important texts; smaller bodies of Christian material survive, along with other religious and scholarly writing. This diversity matters because an oasis could connect people through institutions that were not all governed by one creed. It does not establish that every period was uniformly tolerant or that every religion enjoyed equal support. A manuscript may survive because it was deposited in a monastery, discarded in a particular room, or removed from a damaged building. Its survival can reflect a narrow setting within a larger society.

Religious manuscripts are also evidence of craftsmanship and intellectual labor. Someone prepared paper, copied characters, corrected mistakes, illustrated a page, or sponsored the production of a text. The form of a manuscript can suggest whether it was a scroll, a bound book, or a printed item. Scholars studying Old Uyghur book forms have reconstructed relationships among fragments now kept in different collections. The work is painstaking: a small piece of script may need to be compared with parallel passages, a scribal hand, or traces of a former page. This is why the attribution of a religious text involves editors, conservators, and specialists in several languages.

The secular documents take us closer to ordinary transactions. Researchers have identified contracts, letters, and administrative texts among the Turfan finds. Some concern sales and loans, while others help scholars examine property, obligations, and relationships with officials. The existence of a contract is direct evidence that at least some transactions were formally documented. But a contract does not show every bargain, especially the exchanges too small, too familiar, or too constrained by power to be written down. Nor should we invent the voices of buyers and sellers when a fragment preserves only a formula and part of a name. Document study works by distinguishing what is written from what must be inferred.

Dates can be difficult. The Berlin State Library notes that many Turfan texts are undated, and some dated pieces use a twelve-year animal cycle. Without additional context, that style of dating can point to more than one possible calendar year. Paleography, language, references to officials, archaeological findspots, and comparison with other documents help narrow the possibilities. A catalogue may date a group broadly to the tenth through thirteenth centuries rather than to an exact year. It would be irresponsible to turn that range into a precise narrative simply because a lecture sounds smoother with a single date.

A legal document also opens the question of inequality. Some Turfan contracts record people or labor in circumstances that scholars connect with unfreedom. The contracts can reveal the claims recognized by a writer or a legal system without explaining how those involved experienced the arrangement. When interpreting such terms, a historian must distinguish between sale of land, transfer of a service obligation, debt, and the sale of a person. The documents' fragmentary condition and translated vocabulary can make those distinctions difficult. It is important neither to sanitize coercion as an ordinary commodity exchange nor to imply that every household lived under identical constraints.

The relationships among land, monasteries, and officials are another recurring problem. Religious institutions needed resources to support residents and ritual activity. A farm or garden might contribute produce, payments, or work. Local authorities could intervene in property and tax matters, while political overlords changed with broader Central Asian history. But a monk's manuscript and a land contract represent different participants and different institutional aims. They cannot be combined into an imagined universal system without further evidence. The most productive question is how particular arrangements worked for named places and people, then whether wider comparisons are warranted.

We also need to ask whose writing has been preserved. Manuscripts associated with religious centers can overrepresent literate specialists. Commercial and legal papers can overrepresent people with property or reasons to document debts. Farmers, laborers, and travelers may appear in records produced by others, if at all. Women may be present in particular contracts and letters without leaving a continuous record of their own. Historians should not convert a silence in the archive into a claim that a group was absent from oasis society. They should record what the surviving genre was designed to document and what it omitted.

The scripts themselves carry a history of transmission. The Old Uyghur script developed from Sogdian writing traditions, one sign of the region's complex links with earlier Central Asian communities. Buddhist works were translated from Chinese, Sanskrit, Tocharian, and other languages. A translation was not a passive pipeline; scribes and scholars made decisions about terminology, form, and explanation. The Berlin-Brandenburg Academy's Turfan Studies project continues editing these materials, with catalogues of religious works and economic documents. Naming the editors matters because medieval texts are seldom available to us without modern reconstruction and interpretation.

Political developments beyond the oases eventually affected local life. Turfan's rulers negotiated or submitted to changing regional powers, including the Mongol world in the thirteenth century. Yet the arrival of an imperial overlord does not mean every contract was suddenly rewritten or that every household changed its language. Scholars need local dates and records to identify which institutions persisted, changed, or disappeared. The diversity of manuscripts across centuries suggests continuities and transformations, not one uninterrupted golden age. Empires can provide a chronological frame, but they rarely supply the whole history of an oasis.

There is another reason to keep the evidence precise. The region's modern political situation affects how its past is presented, collected, and claimed. Historical Uyghur states, medieval languages, and modern Uyghur communities belong to related but not identical historical questions. A text in Old Uyghur should not be forced into a contemporary national border or used as proof that an entire medieval population had one modern identity. At the same time, the history of local language and scholarship should not vanish beneath the phrase foreign influence. The oases were places where people produced documents and culture, not just places where armies and caravans passed.

What would we know about Turfan if the paper had not survived? We might see ruins of temples and canals, but many acts of buying, borrowing, translating, and corresponding would be far harder to recover. The manuscripts do not eliminate uncertainty. Their dates are sometimes broad, their surfaces broken, and their voices socially selective. They do something more valuable than filling every gap: they change the questions we can ask. Central Asian history becomes a history of households, institutions, literacy, and contested resources, alongside the history of imperial borders. The oasis was not an empty interval between great powers. It was a society with its own records and decisions.
