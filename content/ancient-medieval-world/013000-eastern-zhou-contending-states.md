# Eastern Zhou: Contending States and Arguments about Rule

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

Imagine a political adviser entering the court of a ruler in ancient China. One adviser recommends governing through inherited ritual and cultivated example. Another argues for standards that apply across families and states. A third emphasizes clear laws, rewards, and punishments. We do not need to fabricate a single historical meeting at which every school gathered. The surviving writings are enough to show that such arguments mattered. Across the centuries of Eastern Zhou rule, rulers competed for land and labor, while thinkers disputed the foundations of authority. These philosophical traditions developed in a world where political choices could change the fate of whole communities.

The Zhou ruling house had replaced the Shang around the middle of the eleventh century BCE. Its kings initially exercised significant power through relationships with regional lineages and settlements. In 771 BCE, the western royal center suffered military and political disaster; the court relocated eastward, and the beginning of Eastern Zhou is usually dated to 770. Royal authority continued to matter symbolically, but powerful regional states increasingly made their own policies. Modern historians divide the era into the Spring and Autumn period and the Warring States period. Those labels help organize a complex chronology, though political change did not wait for an exact line drawn between two dates.

An important part of this history survives in bronze vessels and their inscriptions. Such objects could commemorate gifts, lineage relationships, offices, and obligations. Their ritual settings show how status and memory were made durable in material form. The larger archaeological record reveals settlement, craft, burial, and regional diversity. Elite bronzes are disproportionately visible because they survived and were collected. Most farmers left no autobiographies in bronze. Historians therefore learn about hierarchy from the objects that rulers and wealthy families displayed while also looking for less celebrated evidence of production and ordinary life.

During the Spring and Autumn centuries, states such as Jin, Chu, Qi, and Qin competed, formed alliances, and fought wars. Nominal deference to the Zhou king could coexist with substantial independence. Regional cultures were not identical: material styles, languages, histories, and political relationships differed. Some states expanded by absorbing neighbors; others were divided or reorganized. Diplomacy mattered, but agreements could be broken and loyalties changed. Political geography did not consist of a single country waiting to be reunited. It was made and remade by many courts with different capacities and ambitions.

The later Warring States period intensified military competition and state organization. Large armies required agricultural surpluses, roads, records, equipment, and trained officers. Rulers sought ways to register households, measure land, mobilize labor, and reward service. Reforms associated with Shang Yang in the state of Qin made law and military achievement central to political advancement in later accounts. Such stories are partly preserved by texts with their own agendas, so the detail of every reform and its original author remains a problem for scholarship. What is clear is that warfare and administration became increasingly connected. A state capable of recording and taxing households could raise resources that a looser network of aristocratic obligations might not.

The experience of ordinary people helps explain the stakes. A cultivated field could become a taxable unit; a household might owe labor or military service. A family near a contested frontier could face danger, relocation, or new authorities. Agricultural tools and irrigation also changed possibilities for production in different regions. Technological improvement did not guarantee a more comfortable life if stronger states claimed a larger share of the result. Equally, a centralized administration could standardize some expectations and protect some forms of exchange. We should ask whose burdens changed and by what evidence, rather than praising or condemning bureaucracy in the abstract.

Confucius, traditionally dated from 551 to 479 BCE, became associated with an influential body of ethical and political thought. The Analects presents him teaching through conversation, often linking good government to the cultivation of character, ritual practice, and proper relationships. But the collection took shape through generations of students and later editors. The sayings do not all have the same evidentiary standing as direct utterances by one historical man. The tradition's central questions nevertheless remain clear. What gives a ruler authority beyond force? Can example change behavior? How should family responsibility and public obligation relate? Confucian thought was not a complete blueprint for a single historical government.

The Mohist tradition, named for Mozi, presented different priorities. Its surviving writings argued for standards of conduct that would benefit the wider world, criticized costly displays, and opposed aggressive warfare. A well-known idea is jian ai, often translated as inclusive care. It asks people to consider the welfare of those beyond their own immediate circle. The philosophical details and practical demands have been debated by later readers, and the text is layered. What matters historically is that thinkers could argue about whose interests a ruler ought to protect and whether a policy's value should be judged by its consequences for people.

Other traditions explored the relation between rule and order in different ways. Texts associated with the Daodejing questioned the pursuit of power through restless intervention and urged attention to the way processes unfold. Writings later labeled Legalist emphasized governing through institutions, clear standards, and enforceable rewards and penalties. These are retrospective categories placed over works composed across generations. The thinkers did not assemble neatly into the fixed philosophical schools found in modern textbook charts. Some borrowed from or responded to each other, and court advisers could combine practices from more than one tradition.

Consider how a philosophical manuscript differs from an administrative order. A text may declare that rulers should seek the welfare of all, yet a local record can show what resources a government actually demanded. Conversely, the absence of a surviving policy document does not prove that an ethical argument had no effects. Archaeological discoveries of bamboo manuscripts have revealed works and textual variants that differ from later received editions, reminding scholars how much was copied, selected, rearranged, or lost. The history of ideas is therefore also the history of writing materials, schools of transmission, and institutions that preserved particular texts.

By the late fourth and third centuries BCE, Qin had become a formidable contender. Its eventual conquest of the other major states in 221 BCE created a new imperial order. That outcome was not already decided when the Zhou court moved eastward centuries earlier. Rival states had their own paths, and their contests generated both suffering and significant experimentation in government, agriculture, crafts, and thought. The Eastern Zhou age deserves attention not merely because it ended in empire, but because its inhabitants wrestled with questions that empire did not settle: what counts as legitimate authority, who should bear the cost of rule, and what makes a society ordered rather than merely obedient?

## Companion: sources, philosophical schools and period labels

The Analects and Mozi are layered transmitted texts, not verbatim transcripts of a historical debate. Bronzes and excavated manuscripts provide distinct, often elite, material evidence. Spring and Autumn / Warring States boundaries are conventional chronological frames. Accounts of Qin reforms were transmitted and rewritten by later historians; attribution of every measure to Shang Yang should not be presumed. This lecture treats Qin unification in 221 BCE as a conclusion beyond the nominal 300 BCE unit boundary, not as the subject of a full Qin history.
