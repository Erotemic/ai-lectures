# Cities without the Roman Calendar: American Worlds across the Classical Centuries

Around the first century of the common era, monumental buildings were taking shape in central Mexico, artists were painting sacred narratives in the Maya lowlands, and people along the Peruvian coast were organizing water, agriculture, and ceremonial life in ways suited to sharply different environments. None of these developments needs a Roman empire, a Han dynasty, or a caravan crossing Central Asia to explain it. The chronology is shared only because historians use common dates to compare events. The histories themselves emerged from networks within the Americas, with their own traditions of urban planning, authority, art, and environmental knowledge.

In the Valley of Oaxaca, the hilltop center of Monte Alban had been developing since about 500 BCE. Its terraces, public structures, carved monuments, and connections with surrounding communities reveal a long process of political centralization. The city is associated with Zapotec history, but labels inherited from later periods do not let us recover every early resident's name or political allegiance. Some carved monuments contain signs that scholars examine as writing and political statements. Their interpretation is not always straightforward. Public art can show that rulers or institutions wished to display power without telling us that their subjects accepted every message or that the society was organized exactly as a later state.

In the Basin of Mexico, the city later known as Teotihuacan began to grow around the end of the first millennium BCE. Its great pyramids, broad avenues, and large residential compounds belong to a long sequence rather than to one moment of foundation. The monumental ceremonial core took major shape during roughly the first two and a half centuries CE, while many of the famous apartment compounds expanded especially after that time. It would be misleading to give the city its population and institutions at their fourth- or fifth-century peak when describing its earliest phases. Cities change; the ruins we see now contain buildings made at different times for different purposes.

The planners of Teotihuacan created a striking relationship between architecture, mountains, and public space. Archaeologists examine the orientations of its streets and monuments, the movement of water, workshops, offerings, and the layout of dwellings. Its later neighborhoods reveal migrants from other Mesoamerican regions living alongside people with local traditions. Yet the city leaves historians a challenging problem: its political system is not as directly legible as that of the better-documented Classic Maya dynasties. We should not invent a complete royal succession because we see an imposing pyramid. Nor should the absence of a familiar portrait of kings lead us to assume there were no important governing institutions.

In the Maya lowlands of present-day Guatemala and neighboring regions, settlements such as El Mirador and Nakbe developed large public constructions during the Preclassic period. Agricultural production, causeways, ceremonial architecture, and the management of landscapes supported concentrations of people. The term Maya names a broad collection of related histories and languages, not one kingdom extending uniformly across the region. Cities competed and connected, and their political forms changed over time. Recent landscape surveys have revealed networks of sites and constructed routes that are difficult to appreciate from the perspective of one excavated pyramid alone. These discoveries enlarge the questions scholars can ask; they do not yet settle every argument about centralized control.

A remarkable set of murals from San Bartolo in Guatemala offers a closer view of early Maya intellectual life. Paintings made around 100 BCE depict complex mythic and ceremonial imagery, and earlier fragments contain writing. Research on a calendar notation from the site has strengthened evidence for developed Maya recordkeeping well before the later famous stelae. We can describe preserved figures and the contexts in which the paintings were buried. Their interpretation also involves comparing images with much later indigenous texts, including traditions recorded after European conquest. Such comparisons can illuminate continuities, but they must never imply that a later text records an ancient ceremony without change across a thousand years.

The circulation of artistic forms, precious materials, and people connected the regions of Mesoamerica in different ways. Archaeologists find obsidian transported beyond the places where it was quarried, as well as conventions of sculpture, architecture, and pottery shared across neighboring areas. Movement can represent exchange, political pressure, intermarriage, migration, or imitation. A material's source can sometimes be studied scientifically, yet identifying the quarry does not tell us the conversation that accompanied a transaction. This is why the history of ancient Mesoamerican networks cannot be drawn as a series of arrows labeled conquest whenever two sites contain similar designs.

The Andean region presents another set of histories. Along the south coast of present-day Peru, communities associated with the Paracas and later Nasca traditions produced elaborate textiles and ceramics. The arid coast preserves some materials particularly well, but it also creates a biased record: surviving burial cloth may show what was prepared for a funeral rather than what people wore while farming. In valleys where rainfall was limited, fields depended on careful use of water descending from the Andes. People combined agriculture with coastal resources and long-standing highland contacts. Political life here cannot be understood solely through stone monuments, because textiles, pottery, irrigation, and burials carry so much of the evidence.

The enormous designs now called the Nasca and Palpa geoglyphs were made over many centuries, with different phases spanning the late centuries BCE and the first centuries CE. Their lines, geometric forms, and images were created through manipulation of the desert surface. The most responsible interpretation distinguishes observed form and date from explanation of purpose. Many researchers connect the geoglyphs with ritual movement, place, and environmental knowledge, while precise meanings remain debated. There is no need to supply an exotic mystery where the achievement of sustained local planning and labor is already substantial. These were made by people who lived in the region and knew its terrain.

On Peru's north coast, societies grouped under the label Moche developed prominent regional centers from the early centuries CE onward. River valleys supported irrigation in a dry coastal environment, while fishing and other maritime resources were important. Moche artists created modeled ceramics that portray animals, humans, ritual figures, and scenes of social life. The political organization behind shared styles remains debated: different valleys may not have belonged to a single uniformly administered state. Some images depict captives and violence; excavations reveal richly furnished burials alongside more ordinary settlements. These sources make social inequality visible, but cannot be read as illustrated diaries of every event.

Far to the north, in what is now the Ohio River valley, Indigenous communities built geometric earthworks and maintained exchange relationships across large portions of eastern North America. Archaeologists often use the term Hopewell for related traditions most prominent in the early centuries CE. The name comes from the modern history of excavation, not a self-designation preserved from the builders. Materials such as copper, shell, and mica reached ceremonial contexts through extensive contacts. Large earthworks required planning and coordinated labor, but their presence does not prove that the builders lived under an emperor or inhabited permanent cities in the same form as Teotihuacan. Different social institutions can produce monumental achievements.

We cannot reconstruct each region with the same kind of evidence. Maya inscriptions can sometimes be read as language and dates; some earlier signs are debated. Teotihuacan's painted walls provide rich visual information but do not offer a continuous narrative of named rulers. Nasca textiles and desert lines preserve kinds of evidence that written chronicles cannot. Hopewell earthworks and distributions of materials support inference about gatherings and exchange without telling us precisely how people named their communities. Archaeological interpretation depends on careful dating, context, and Indigenous knowledge, and on admitting when a beautiful object does not answer a political question.

A world history organized by the classical centuries should include all these societies without pretending they were in contact with Rome or Han China. Their histories help expose the limits of a definition of civilization built around a single imperial model. People could organize enormous constructions through many arrangements of authority; rich cultural practices could flourish without written court chronicles; and centers could grow or shrink without ending the lives of all surrounding communities. The right comparison is not which American society became most like an empire elsewhere. It is how different people created enduring forms of social life out of the places, resources, knowledge, and relationships available to them.
