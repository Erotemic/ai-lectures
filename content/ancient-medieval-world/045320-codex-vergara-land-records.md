# A Field Drawn and Measured: Indigenous Land Records after Conquest

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

In an early colonial book from the Valley of Mexico, an agricultural field can appear as a drawing with measurements along its sides. Other signs identify a household and its connections to land. The document known as the Codex Vergara is not a map of an empty landscape and not a European survey sheet with Indigenous decoration. It is a record of people's fields made with local pictorial conventions in a transformed political world. To read it, historians have to understand both what the painters measured and why information about household land had suddenly become so consequential.

The Codex Vergara and the closely related Códice de Santa María Asunción concern communities around Tepetlaoztoc, northeast of Texcoco. Barbara Williams and H. R. Harvey showed that these manuscripts record landholding and household composition across seventeen localities. They were likely assembled within a generation of the conquest, in the period around 1539 to 1543. That date is central. These are not photographs of life before 1519. They contain traditions of naming, measurement and pictorial documentation used by Indigenous people in a new colonial administrative and legal setting.

A household register could name a person and show relatives connected to that household. A parcel record could give the outline of a field, lengths of its edges, and descriptions of the land. Harvey identified distinct categories in the Santa María Asunción manuscript: a census by household, a record of parcels and their measurements, and a register of calculated field areas. The names and conventions are not merely curiosities for decipherment. They show that family composition, resources, and land entitlements were organized in ways that scribes could communicate and compare.

Why measure fields so carefully? Land was a productive resource, but it was also a basis for tribute obligations, rights, and disputes. In colonial Mexico, rulers, officials and local communities could contest what a household owed and what it controlled. A record might be used to count persons and parcels in the same administrative effort. Its precision was not a hobby detached from politics. But we should avoid claiming that each figure was invented by a Spanish official. The manuscripts preserve Indigenous graphic and numerical expertise while reflecting the pressure of a colonial system that could use records to extract payments.

The fields are not all rectangles, and their drawn shapes do not automatically yield areas by simple multiplication. In 2011, a team including María del Carmen Jorge, Barbara Williams, C. E. Garza-Hume and Arturo Olvera tested the accuracy of reported areas using mathematical comparisons to possible quadrilateral shapes. They found that, under the study's model, about three-quarters of the listed areas lay within five percent of the maximum possible area for the stated side lengths. This was strong evidence of competent quantitative practice, not merely picturesque diagrams.

Why does a maximum possible area matter? If the lengths of four sides are given, a quadrilateral can still bend into different shapes with different areas. The investigators compared the figures recorded by the Indigenous surveyors with geometric constraints derived from the side measurements. This is a problem of practical mathematics: how to represent a parcel that does not fit a simple square and still estimate its area fairly. The comparison shows systematic care, even though different procedures and assumptions can lead to different area estimates.

The results also teach a lesson in evaluating historical quantitative sources. A percentage describing close agreement is not the same as saying the land actually had that size in modern square meters. Erosion, changing boundaries, drawing conventions and errors in copying can matter. It is possible for a calculation to be internally consistent while the physical property measured has changed. Scholars therefore use mathematical analysis alongside manuscript history, place names and knowledge of local political institutions. The codex becomes more informative when its numbers are treated as evidence to test rather than figures to repeat uncritically.

Household entries matter as much as field areas. A lineage or marriage relationship could affect access to land, while a household's recorded composition mattered for assessing obligations. The registers reveal names and links often absent from royal monuments. Yet even a detailed census is selective. People could be omitted, households might be grouped for a particular administrative purpose, and changing colonial conditions shaped what counted as a relevant unit. We should not use the record to infer the private motives of the named people, or imagine that their arrangements were unchanged from a century earlier.

The physical survival of the books is itself historically consequential. Many preconquest and early colonial pictorial manuscripts were destroyed, dispersed or damaged. Libraries now preserve a limited, uneven sample, including books whose subjects range from dynastic lineages to tribute and land. The Bodleian's conservation work illustrates the care required to study pigments and supports without destroying them. Preservation is not merely about beautiful pictures. If a field-measurement register cannot be opened or examined safely, a major source for Indigenous mathematical and social history is harder to reconstruct.

These early colonial documents also help historians read preconquest archaeology. A field shape, house compound, or settlement zone found by excavation might be compared to later pictorial evidence for land and household categories. Such comparisons can generate useful hypotheses. But the documents were produced after demographic loss, political conquest and new legal demands. They cannot simply be moved fifty years backward as though nothing intervened. The correct use of continuity is to ask which practices can be shown to persist, which changed, and what additional evidence would distinguish one from the other.

The Codex Vergara gives us two kinds of history at once. It is evidence of Indigenous mathematicians and scribes organizing land information in the sixteenth century, and it offers a cautious window onto older traditions of household and field management. It also shows how colonial rule made certain kinds of record urgent and politically charged. A ledger of plots may look impersonal, but every line was connected to work, sustenance and authority. To study the Americas before 1500 responsibly, we sometimes have to read later sources closely without pretending they were written earlier.

## Evidence boundary

The Codex Vergara and Códice de Santa María Asunción were made circa 1539–1543 under Spanish colonial conditions and are not pre-1500 censuses. They record Indigenous households and field measures but were produced in a context of taxation, land and legal disputes. Mathematical comparisons assess geometric consistency under assumptions and do not prove the physical accuracy of every parcel or establish uninterrupted preconquest practice.
