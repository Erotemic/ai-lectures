# Timber Sewn to the Sea: The Boats and River Ports of Butuan

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

In the waterlogged ground around Butuan, in northeastern Mindanao, archaeologists found the remains of wooden boats. They were not miniature ceremonial models or images painted on a jar. Their planks had once carried people or cargo across water. Some survived with the technical details of their construction still visible: shaped timbers, pegs joining plank edges, and projecting wooden lugs that could be lashed to internal supports. They give us a way to study the maritime past of the Philippine islands through the knowledge of builders rather than through the voyages recorded by a foreign court.

The boats were found in and around an old river system. Excavations beginning in the 1970s exposed several vessels; further work examined additional remains. Not all were removed from the ground. Waterlogged timber can be preserved for centuries when conditions prevent ordinary decay, but recovering and treating a large vessel is costly and can destroy evidence if done badly. A plank lifted from mud must be documented and conserved, or it may deform as it dries. What museums display is therefore the outcome of archaeological work, decisions about preservation, and the survival of materials in one specific environment.

Dating the vessels takes care. Early reports circulated radiocarbon ages that have sometimes been converted into apparently exact calendar years, including dates far earlier than those emphasized by later museum summaries. Subsequent analyses and recalibrations have revised parts of the chronology. The National Museum of the Philippines commonly describes the principal Butuan boat remains as dating to the later first millennium, especially around the eighth through tenth centuries. Maritime archaeologist Ligaya Lacsina has documented the individual boats' excavation, dates, and museum histories, including disagreements among earlier reported figures. We should not compress different boats and different measurements into one date for all of Butuan.

The construction method is a stronger place to begin than an exact year. In an edge-joined plank boat, builders shape adjoining edges so the timbers can meet closely, then connect them with wooden pegs. Internal projections, often called lugs, help attach supports by lashings. This technique differs from a vessel whose planks are fastened primarily to a rigid skeleton with metal nails. It required careful knowledge of wood behavior, joinery, water pressure, and repair. A boat may be engineered for local waters while sharing technical principles with craft from distant parts of Southeast Asia. Similarity does not automatically prove a single workshop or a single migrating fleet.

Imagine the work without romanticizing it. Trees had to be selected, felled, moved, and shaped with tools. Builders needed to judge whether a plank would bend and how it would fit with its neighbors. Lashings had to be made from suitable fibers and checked over time. The hull had to survive currents, loading, and repeated beaching or mooring. Communities supported the builders with food, labor, and access to materials. People who could repair a seam or select a resilient timber were part of the infrastructure of trade as surely as merchants who negotiated the cargo.

Butuan's position matters. The Agusan River system connected interior communities to coastal movement and exchange. River ports were not isolated points on a map; their significance depended on waterways, seasonal conditions, settlements, and the movement of goods to and from inland producers. Archaeologists have recovered objects suggesting participation in wider Asian exchange, including ceramics and ornaments. Those imports show relationships across water but do not by themselves identify every trading partner or the exact route any given boat took. A boat found near Butuan need not have sailed directly to the place where an imported pot was made.

The vessels also tell us something about the limits of written history. Chinese records mention overseas polities and contacts, and later chroniclers describe Southeast Asian trade, but such accounts were written for particular political audiences. A foreign official might be more interested in tribute or diplomacy than in how a local boat was built. Archaeological timbers answer questions that those texts neglect. Conversely, the boat does not tell us the captain's name, the languages spoken aboard, or whether people traveled freely. Combining sources expands the picture without making any one of them complete.

A common story of early maritime Southeast Asia treats exchange as the movement of luxury goods among rulers. Yet boats also moved food, textiles, tools, and people. A river port depended on fishers, farmers, woodworkers, potters, pilots, and households who prepared supplies. Cargo could be redistributed many times before reaching its final consumer. Social hierarchy and coercion might shape who worked and who profited, but the surviving wood alone does not reveal the contractual status of a crew. We should ask about labor and inequality while keeping the limits of each artifact in view.

The word balangay is widely used to describe the Butuan vessels and appears in contemporary discussions of local maritime heritage. Its later associations with communities and political units should not be used to assume that each excavated boat carried one self-governing village or had an identical social meaning. Technologies have histories of borrowing and adaptation, and names can change their scope. Archaeologists can compare joinery, dimensions, and tool marks; linguists and historians can investigate words and later descriptions. Those different inquiries may illuminate one another without yielding a simple origin story for a nation.

The archive of the boats extends beyond the earth. Photographs, drawings, conservation notes, new measurements, and museum displays preserve evidence from excavations that cannot be repeated in precisely the same way. Lacsina's work on how the boats moved from archaeological sites into museums is therefore part of the research, not an administrative footnote. When older publications assign different dates to the same material, the correct response is to trace the sample and analytical method. Correcting a museum label may matter as much for understanding the past as finding another fragment of timber.

Butuan should also be compared with the copper document from Luzon dated to 900. The inscription provides a glimpse of named legal offices and obligations; the boats provide physical evidence of construction and maritime infrastructure. Both belong to Philippine histories before Spanish colonization, but they do not prove that one government ruled both places. The archipelago held communities connected by water, exchange, language, and political relationships of varying scale. We learn more by examining those differences than by forcing the evidence into a single kingdom with clear borders.

What remains is a vivid but disciplined picture. Boats built with skilled joinery worked within riverine and maritime worlds; their timbers survived because particular waters and mud protected them. The people who built them understood forces that historians can still study through wood and tool marks. Their ports linked inland labor to distant exchange. Their individual journeys are mostly lost. By starting with the workmanship of these vessels, we recover a history of technology and social cooperation that rulers' chronicles, however informative, could never tell on their own.
