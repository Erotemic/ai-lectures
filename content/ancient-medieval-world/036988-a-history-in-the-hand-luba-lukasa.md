# A History in the Hand: Luba Lukasa and the Work of Remembering

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

Imagine a wooden board small enough to hold in two hands. Its surface has beads, pegs, shells, or carved shapes, arranged in ways an untrained visitor may not understand. This is a lukasa, an object connected with the historical knowledge of the Luba peoples in central Africa. To someone who knows the relevant traditions, its features can help call forth stories of predecessors, relationships, sacred places, and royal authority. A lukasa is not a book whose written words can be sounded out by every reader. It is a material partner in an expert performance of memory.

The surviving boards often date to the nineteenth or twentieth century. That point matters. The Luba political world had developed over earlier centuries, and the Mbudye association helped preserve and interpret knowledge associated with its rulers. But a later dated wooden board cannot demonstrate the age of every rule and story performed with it. The date of an object, the date of a narrated event, and the time when a method of narration was developed are three different questions. An archive made for the living can have more than one historical clock.

A specialist who has learned the stories can touch the surface and use the placement of its features to guide a narrative. The performance may concern the origins of rulers, the relationships among named offices, or landscapes associated with past political events. It depends on memory that was taught, corrected, and interpreted in social settings. The board does not replace the knowledgeable person. Without that training, a modern museum visitor sees things that are visible but not their full historical significance. The difference is not between a society with a history and a society without one; it is between different ways of practicing historical authority.

One surviving object in the Smithsonian collections reveals both the richness and the limits of museum knowledge. It was accessioned in nineteen twenty-one and described as a wooden memory board associated with Luba communities. A later curatorial note reports a particularly elaborate form, including carved heads and a reptile-like figure. The record also preserves a frank admission: some informants could not, or would not, explain the symbolism to a researcher. This is not a failure of the object. It is evidence that access to specialist knowledge was itself part of the social world in which the object functioned.

In the museum account, the board features could mark ancestral kings, royal places, or rules of conduct, while the exact narration varied with the historian. This is a key feature of mnemonic practice. A carved form or a cluster of beads can remind a specialist of an episode and its relations, much as a place may prompt a remembered story. It is not necessary for the entire spoken account to be inscribed on the wood. The knowledge lives partly in a community of trained interpreters. Historians studying the object must investigate the people and institutions around it, not just count its beads.

Why did political communities value these methods? Luba dynastic histories mattered to the distribution of recognition, titles, and rights. The Mbudye association is described in scholarly and museum writing as a body of specialists who preserved and interpreted historical knowledge connected to kingship. Remembering could offer precedent, challenge a claim, or reinforce one. The board did not mechanically decide every dispute, and we should not imagine it as a secret constitution printed in code. Its significance depended on who was entitled to interpret it, who listened, and how a remembered past was made relevant in the present.

The objects of Luba memory extended beyond the lukasa. Museum collections preserve carved seats, bow stands, staffs, headrests, and iron emblems connected with royalty and ancestral authority. Many incorporate images of women and references to earlier rulers. The Metropolitan Museum of Art has objects dated to the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries that bear these forms. Artistic representation is not simply decoration added after the political work was done. It could be part of how claims to authority were visible and persuasive. But stylistic resemblance to medieval metalwork cannot by itself prove that every earlier object served the same purpose.

The study of the lukasa also has a history. Mary Nooter Roberts, Allen F. Roberts, and colleagues assembled the 1996 exhibition catalog Memory: Luba Art and the Making of History. Its chapters brought archaeology, historical scholarship, performance, and the study of objects into conversation. That collaboration offered a way to describe memory as something actively made and interpreted, not merely a container of fixed dates. In our own source records we must also be careful: a publicly available catalog description tells us the topic and authors, while a museum object record documents a particular accession. Neither should be passed off as a transcript of an initiation ceremony.

There is a broader historical lesson here. Written archives preserve some things exceptionally well but also contain gaps, official preferences, and deliberate omissions. Oral performances require living transmission, which can preserve relationships while adapting to political change. Objects can anchor that transmission without carrying everything on their surface. These differences shape which questions historians can ask. A date on a museum accession card is not a date for the origin of a kingdom; a remembered genealogy is not a radiocarbon measurement; a copper axe in a medieval grave is not itself a lukasa.

A lukasa helps us see why historical evidence cannot be reduced to documents on paper. People made the past present through trained recall, performance, crafted materials, and authority shared or contested within a community. The work for modern historians is to take those practices seriously while remaining honest about date and access. We can describe the collected board, the association that used objects of this kind, and the later institutions they served. We cannot turn them into an eyewitness account of the earliest Upemba settlers. The board is a record of the work of remembering, and that work has its own history.

## Evidence boundary

The collected lukasa discussed are nineteenth- or twentieth-century objects. Their use illustrates later Luba historical expertise, not the existence of an identical board in a tenth-century grave. A board is a mnemonic prompt, not an automatically decodable fixed text.
