# Cities beyond the Pass: Suyab and Navikat in the Chuy Valley

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

On the way through the Chuy Valley, east of today's Bishkek in Kyrgyzstan, a traveler could find settlements rather than a gap between famous cities. One place later became known as Suyab, whose ruins are called Ak-Beshim. Another, Navikat, is associated with Krasnaya Rechka. In accounts of the Silk Road, such places can appear as dots on a route from one empire to another. But a dot on a map is not a town. It represents walls, homes, ritual buildings, local fields, and institutions through which visitors entered an inhabited world. The question is not simply what passed through the valley. It is how the valley's people made such passage possible.

The Chuy Valley lies along one of the northern corridors connecting Central Asia with places farther east and west. Towns expanded there in the sixth century and later medieval centuries, in a landscape of settled agriculture and mobile pastoral power. They were linked to the Western Turkic and other regional political formations, but also to Sogdian-speaking communities whose urban and commercial networks were strong across Central Asia. Different kinds of authority met here: a khan's court, a city community, a religious foundation, a market, and local agricultural households. There is no reason to expect a single uniform language or political identity at every stage.

The identification of Ak-Beshim with Suyab rests on a combination of historical records and excavated remains. Scholars have studied the city through several campaigns of excavation. It is not enough to find a ruined wall and simply attach an ancient name to it because a source mentions a city nearby. The names in written records, the location, the dated material, and the sequence of building phases need to fit together. Once that identification becomes persuasive, archaeologists can ask a new range of questions: which communities used the religious buildings, how long were they maintained, and what happened as the town's political connections changed?

Some of the most informative evidence comes from religious buildings. Excavations at Suyab have uncovered Buddhist structures, a Zoroastrian-associated cemetery, and Christian church buildings. An account in Encyclopaedia Iranica describes a tenth- to early-eleventh-century church with finds including Christian symbols and inscriptions. These remains do not mean every faith flourished at precisely the same moment or that the town had equal numbers of adherents. What they show is that different traditions established physical institutions in the valley across its history. A sanctuary is evidence of investment and use, not a census.

One pottery vessel discovered in a church context carries a Sogdian inscription that specialists interpret as a dedication to a person named Alan. Such a tiny text is tempting because it suddenly supplies a voice where most building stones are anonymous. Yet even here the possibilities are limited. The inscription records a presentation or dedication, not a full biography of Alan. Its language does not tell us which language every worshipper spoke, just as a Christian object does not turn an entire city into a single religious community. The artifact is a trace of connection between writing, gift, and institution, valuable precisely because it is specific.

Navikat offers another view. Archaeologists working at Krasnaya Rechka have found remains associated with Buddhist temple complexes and other traditions, as well as evidence of a settlement shaped by successive inhabitants and rebuilding. A Kyrgyzstan heritage nomination submitted to UNESCO describes these remains as part of a northern Silk Roads cultural landscape. That is useful orientation, but the nomination is not a neutral excavation diary. Its purpose is to describe why the sites should be preserved. Responsible history checks the kinds of buildings reported, then asks what the original finds, their dates, and independent archaeological publications permit us to conclude.

It is common to read about Sogdian merchants in China and imagine a single Sogdian commercial population moving along a road. The Chuy sites offer a less tidy picture. Sogdian writing, Turkic political influence, and religious forms linked to several parts of Eurasia intersected in towns that were continually rebuilt. A label such as Sogdian might describe a language used in a dedication or a family tradition, not every resident's ancestry. Similarly, an association with a Turkic khanate tells us something about political relationships but does not determine the form of every shop, farm, or household.

A town like Suyab needed food and materials even when its fame depended on routes reaching far beyond it. Agricultural production along a valley, pastoral exchange with people of surrounding grasslands, and local craftwork created the conditions in which a traveler might find supplies and shelter. It would be a mistake to imagine every bowl and coin there was an import. Archaeological objects become evidence of far-reaching links when their material, technique, form, or inscriptions can be connected through independent comparison. Ordinary ceramics may reveal more about local production than about travel. A city could be both a crossroads and a home.

Political change affected the valley repeatedly. Western Turkic, Türgesh, Chinese and later regional powers all interacted with the northern routes at different times. But a place name in a campaign narrative tells us little about whether the same merchant families occupied their shops through each episode. Occupation phases and renovations must be dated against those narratives, not forced into them. A military source may describe a ruler's arrival as the decisive event, while archaeology shows ongoing construction, abandonment in one quarter, or religious rebuilding elsewhere. The two kinds of evidence ask different questions, and neither makes the other unnecessary.

There is also a problem of historical visibility. A church with unusual inscriptions may receive more attention than an ordinary room. A Buddhist temple whose walls remain high appears more impressive than a workshop destroyed by later construction. The artifacts that survive, and the kinds of excavations undertaken, decide which activities are easiest to describe. These limitations do not weaken archaeological history. They tell us to avoid converting a list of spectacular objects into a ranking of what mattered most to the people who lived nearby.

One can understand the historical significance of the Chuy Valley without making its towns miniature copies of Rome, Persia, China, or any other imperial center. The evidence suggests settlements that combined local resources with writing systems, religious practices, and political relationships spanning an enormous region. The culture of a northern route was produced partly by the people who stayed in place. In Suyab and Navikat, they maintained buildings, gave gifts, used languages that crossed boundaries, and lived under changing forms of authority. The Silk Road was not a road laid out once and then traversed by identical caravans. It was a moving network sustained by inhabited places.

Imagine leaving the valley and looking back. The last thing you see might be an earthen mound, not the bustling town it once held. A map tells you where to put the mound; excavations tell you how some of its rooms and shrines were arranged; an inscription gives you a name and a word spoken or written by someone long gone. Together they yield a history more precise than the label crossroads. It is a history of institutions that made connections possible, and of communities whose lives cannot be reduced to the empire that happened to claim a route at a given moment.

## Evidence boundary

The Chuy Valley sites were occupied and rebuilt across several centuries; individual churches, shrines and inscriptions must not be treated as simultaneous. State-submitted UNESCO nomination claims are identified as heritage summaries, not independent proofs of uninterrupted institutional continuity or of the religious proportions of the population. Sogdian and Turkic labels are linguistic/political descriptions, not clean archaeological ethnic categories.
