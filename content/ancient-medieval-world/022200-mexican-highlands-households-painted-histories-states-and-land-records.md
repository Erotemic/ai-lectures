# Mexican highlands: households, painted histories, states, and land records

In the Mexican highlands, houses, painted books, and measured fields preserve different histories of authority. We will follow those forms of evidence across distinct societies and then across the rupture of European conquest. Later documents may preserve earlier knowledge, but their new political setting matters.

## Signs above the Valley: Writing, Power, and Households at Monte Albán

The city of Monte Albán stands on a hill above the Valley of Oaxaca in southern Mexico. Building a city there was a decision about power as well as geography. The hill was not the richest agricultural land. Food came from fields below, while labor carried stone, earth, water, and offerings uphill. From the plaza, people could see the valleys whose villages supplied the city. Around five hundred years before the Common Era, several communities began concentrating their political and ceremonial lives there. Monte Albán grew into a center that endured for more than a millennium. Its history is not only written in pyramids. It is written on carved stone.

One of the earliest clues comes from San José Mogote, a settlement that preceded the great hilltop center. A carved slab found in a temple passage shows a body lying in apparent defeat, accompanied by a calendrical name. Archaeologists place the monument before Monte Albán's foundation. The stone was laid where people could walk across it. That placement matters: an image of a defeated enemy became part of the way a community entered a ceremonial building. Joyce Marcus and other specialists read this as political display. We cannot recover the captive's account of events, but we can see a claim that victory and public authority belonged together.

Early carvings at Monte Albán have long been called the Danzantes, or dancers, because some figures appear to twist across the stone. The label survives, but it is misleading. Many carved bodies show wounds, mutilation, or signs of subjection. They are more plausibly representations of captives and enemies than an assembly of celebratory dancers. The images formed part of major public construction. This is a warning about how names given by later observers can quietly become explanations. Before asking who is depicted, we should ask what the artist actually carved and where the image was displayed.

The Zapotec writing system is among the earliest known in Mesoamerica. It included calendrical names, signs for places, and combinations of glyphs that could make political relationships durable in stone. It was not simply an ornamental variant of later Maya writing. Different societies developed writing practices suited to different institutions. We can read some Zapotec signs with confidence, and specialists debate others. A sign that identifies a ruler by a day name is not a full royal biography. An apparent place name is not proof of an accurately surveyed border. The writing is essential evidence, but it does not speak with a single transparent voice.

Consider Building J in Monte Albán's main plaza. It has a distinctive plan and orientation. Its carved slabs include groups commonly interpreted as records of places brought under Monte Albán's influence. Some scholars emphasize the building's possible astronomical alignments, while others focus on its political program; the subjects are not mutually exclusive. Yet the word conquest needs care. A public monument can declare domination even where control was incomplete, temporary, or negotiated through local leaders. It records the capital's view of a relationship. It does not give us testimony from each community named or represented on the walls.

Archaeological survey gives the stone texts a landscape. The Valley of Oaxaca contained smaller centers with their own ceremonial buildings and residential areas. Research at Cerro Tilcajete and other sites shows similarities in elite objects and architecture, but also local differences. The region was not a grid of identical administrative offices operated from the hilltop. Alliances, rivalry, shared rituals, and coercion could all help link settlements. When inscriptions seem to describe a large political realm, excavated buildings can test how its institutions actually reached the countryside. Sometimes they support a story of integration. Sometimes they reveal uneven authority.

Monte Albán had artisans, farmers, builders, priests, families, and people who prepared food for ceremonies. Urban life required work that is rarely named on the monuments. Terraces, paths, public stairways, and the maintenance of buildings were sustained by households below and around the civic core. A successful capital could draw offerings and labor, but it also depended on rainfall, land, harvests, and the choices of surrounding communities. A carved ruler might commemorate a victory; the city could not survive on commemorations. That gap between public claims and material requirements is one reason archaeology and epigraphy are stronger together.

Burial archaeology shows another scale of history. Tombs were not reserved exclusively for a separate cemetery beyond the city. Some were built into residential units, while other people were buried in pits or reused domestic spaces. Wealthy tombs could contain painted walls, decorated vessels, and offerings arranged to connect the living household to the dead. Tomb 105, for example, preserves a painted scene with richly dressed figures and calendrical signs interpreted as names. These images were not neutral family portraits. They selected ancestors and ceremonies that surviving families wanted remembered. Even so, they show a kind of history different from the speeches carved on a public staircase.

Craft and exchange linked Oaxaca to the wider Mesoamerican world. Archaeologists find objects associated with central Mexico, and a Zapotec neighborhood has been identified at Teotihuacan far to the north. These connections imply travel, exchange, and changes in where people made their homes. They do not automatically show that one city conquered the other. A ceramic style can move because people migrate, because goods travel, or because local makers adopt techniques and imagery. Material resemblance is a clue about contact; it is not a substitute for investigating the terms on which contact happened.

Monte Albán's political importance changed over the centuries. By the late Classic period, power in the valleys was being reorganized, and the hilltop capital no longer governed the region as it once had. That did not mean every settlement was destroyed or every person moved away at once. Communities continued, and later Mixtec elites used parts of the site in different ways. Histories that end at the moment a capital declines confuse the history of a monumental institution with the history of a people. A city's diminished authority can coexist with ongoing households, languages, and networks of memory.

The carved signs also have a history of modern interpretation. Early explorers, museum labels, archaeologists, and present-day Zapotec scholars do not always approach them with the same questions. A photograph removes a stone from its architectural context; a drawing can make a worn mark seem sharper than it is; a catalogue may preserve an old reading that specialists later revise. Responsible interpretation starts with the surface, the provenience, and the alternative readings. There is no shame in saying that a sign is undeciphered or that two plausible histories fit the same monument. The purpose of research is to explain uncertainty, not disguise it.

What made Monte Albán an early state? The answer cannot be a pyramid by itself, or the presence of violence, or even the invention of writing. It was the sustained coordination of many settlements, the construction of large common spaces, political claims that reached beyond a single household, and institutions capable of organizing people and resources across the valleys. Writing helped rulers present that arrangement as legitimate. It also preserved their rivalries in ways that would not survive through architecture alone. The question is not whether the state had a ceremonial image. It is how that image related to the people and land it claimed.

The carved slab at San José Mogote and the signs on Building J bookend a crucial transformation. Public images of defeated rivals became longer claims about territory and rulers. Meanwhile, the city grew through the labor of countless people whose names were seldom inscribed. Reading Monte Albán means keeping all these forms of evidence in view: stone, houses, burials, fields, and the surviving communities of Oaxaca. The monuments tell us how leaders wanted power remembered. The archaeological landscape lets us ask what that power required, where it reached, and where the stone's claim may have exceeded the reality.

## The Work behind the Walls: Families and Crafts at El Palmillo

Elsewhere in Oaxaca, residential terraces bring the work of households closer to view than the monuments of a regional capital.

Imagine beginning the day on a residential terrace above the eastern Valley of Oaxaca. A wall keeps the slope from washing down. A hearth must be tended, fiber prepared, stone tools repaired, and provisions brought up from fields or exchanged with neighbors. At El Palmillo, archaeology makes those activities visible without supplying a diary. The settlement was built along a hill, not around a famous royal inscription. Its terraces let us study the people whose work sustained the cities and towns of Classic-period Oaxaca, roughly during the first millennium of the Common Era.

El Palmillo lies in the eastern Tlacolula arm of the Oaxaca valley. It was one among several substantial hilltop terrace settlements, while Monte Albán remained an important regional center in earlier parts of the Classic sequence. Surface mapping at El Palmillo, Guirún, and Mitla Fortress shows that a valley cannot be reconstructed by surveying only its largest plaza. Houses were spread across altered slopes; terraces had to be built and maintained. Regional settlement depended on many communities, each with its own access to fields, routes, craft workers and ceremonies.

Feinman, Nicholas, and Haines excavated residential terraces at El Palmillo. The floors, retaining walls, and modifications reveal occupation that lasted through more than one building episode. A terrace might be extended, a wall repaired, or domestic space reorganized. When historians call a settlement stable because its place-name lasts for centuries, this is what stability can mean materially: many decisions to invest again in the same location. The archaeology does not imply that one family remained on exactly the same floor for the entire occupation. It shows repeated commitments to a built landscape.

Stone artifacts offer a view of specialized work. Chipped-stone production leaves cores, flakes, finished blades and discarded pieces; their distribution across houses can distinguish tool use from intensive manufacture. The El Palmillo stone-tool study compared six excavated terraces. Some craft work appeared widely at different intensities, while other activities were concentrated in fewer homes. The distribution shows craft specialization spread among households rather than confined to a palace workshop. Neighbors could make different goods, trade with one another and depend upon networks reaching beyond their hill.

Another activity involved the maguey plant. Its leaves can supply strong fibers used to make cord, nets, and cloth, while parts of the plant also have food value. The excavators reported equipment connected with fiber processing, including spindle whorls and tools that could help prepare tougher leaves. The objects are not preserved shirts. A whorl tells us that fibers were twisted into thread, but it does not usually identify the hands of the spinner, the final buyer, or the full form of the finished product. In a warm climate, durable tools can outlast the work they enabled.

Measurements of whorls from eight El Palmillo residences suggest that households did not all spin identical material. Some equipment was suited to one range of fibers and some to others. The mix varied among residences. This is better evidence than assuming every domestic space produced the same goods simply because the same kind of artifact appears everywhere. But whorl size is not a label written by its maker. Researchers have to connect weight, form, wear and archaeological context to hypotheses about thread. Several materials can overlap in the same range of tools.

Households also differed in access to goods from elsewhere. The excavations found differences in nonlocal obsidian and semiprecious materials, but the overall contrast among the sampled terraces was not as sharp as a simple picture of rich and poor districts might predict. That leaves several explanations open. Some resources may have circulated through exchange, reciprocal relations, or different kinds of household connections. A rare object could have been acquired through several routes. An artifact inventory is evidence of what entered the archaeological deposit, not a bank account showing every family's lifetime wealth.

The combination of craft specialization and relatively modest contrasts in certain exotic goods suggests a web of horizontally connected households. One house could make more stone tools and another more fiber goods, with exchanges helping both. That does not mean the community had no hierarchy, obligations, or conflict. Labor might have been shaped by age, skill, status and kinship in ways that archaeology cannot assign to named individuals. The important discovery is a range of economic relations that cannot be reduced to centralized orders from a single ruler.

Domestic space was also a place of ritual and ancestry. Archaeological work at the site has documented offerings, hearths, and burials associated with living spaces. A house could therefore be where food was made, work was coordinated, and relationships to the dead were maintained. But the meaning of a particular offering cannot be recovered simply from its contents. A buried vessel does not tell us every word spoken over it. The strongest method compares placement, repeated practices, building history, and evidence from neighboring communities before proposing an interpretation.

A terraced neighborhood was never independent of the valley below. Cultivation needed land and water; fiber and stone products needed people who could use them. Routes between communities joined domestic producers to regional centers, perhaps through household exchanges, specialists, ritual occasions and political obligations. Rather than presenting the city as a separate machine that supplied a passive countryside, El Palmillo lets us see the region as a collection of producers and consumers. Archaeologists must still distinguish what is physically mapped from what is inferred about political organization or market institutions.

The study of El Palmillo has an interpretive advantage and a limitation. Its excavated households make women, children, artisans, and ordinary workers plausible subjects of history, but tools do not reliably reveal the social identity of their users. We cannot declare every spinner a woman, every stoneworker a man, or every high terrace an elite home without further evidence. Household members learned work over time; one activity could have been shared, another guarded as a specialized skill. Archaeology traces the patterned consequences of those choices more readily than the choices themselves.

When we return to Monte Albán's carved stones, the domestic terraces give the monuments a different meaning. Regional authority required more than inscriptions and ceremonial plazas. It rested in landscapes where people spent years maintaining walls, feeding families, preparing fibers, making tools and exchanging goods. At El Palmillo, the record is fragmentary but specific. It shows that economic complexity can emerge through households that perform different work while remaining connected. A history built from only kings and ceremonial centers would not see this part of Oaxaca at all.

## Eight Deer and the Painted History: Reading Mixtec Books

Moving to later painted histories also changes communities and political settings. A Mixtec book is not a transparent continuation of Monte Albán’s inscriptions.

Open a painted Mixtec screenfold and the story may begin from a direction unfamiliar to a reader of modern books. The Codex Zouche-Nuttall is made from joined, prepared animal skin, painted and folded into a compact object. Images and signs flow across its surfaces; the reader follows characters, calendrical names, places and paths between scenes. It is not illustrated prose with captions removed. It is an intellectual technology for telling history, especially the history of ruling families, whose forms of record keeping did not depend on the alphabet brought by Europeans.

One side records histories and genealogies of important Mixtec centers. The other includes the political biography of a ruler conventionally known as Eight Deer Jaguar Claw. Names such as Eight Deer use the day of birth in a ritual calendar; an additional identifying name distinguishes a person from others who might share the same day sign. The fact that a person can be named in a calendar does not mean a text merely lists dates. Images of journeys, meetings, marriages, ritual acts and warfare turn chronology into an argument about legitimate power.

The book preserves stories centered on Oaxaca's highlands and coast. Tilantongo in the Mixteca Alta and the coastal center of Tututepec became linked in the remembered career of Eight Deer. Yet a connection in a manuscript need not mean that both places were administered as one continuous kingdom. The historian asks which scene portrays a ritual claim, which a political alliance, and which a conquest, then checks what excavations show about towns, routes and settlement growth. A painted history can be precise about genealogies while remaining selective about the experiences of ordinary households.

Eight Deer is presented as a ruler whose career involved travel, military alliances and claims to legitimate authority in several centers. In some interpretations, the stories connect the founding of Tututepec with his actions and trace a later assertion of power in Tilantongo. They also include ritual acts that later historians cannot translate into only secular diplomacy. A place could be claimed through kinship, ceremonies, alliances and war. Turning every painted meeting into a treaty or every procession into a battle would flatten the record and erase the institutions through which leaders made their authority meaningful.

Archaeologists working in the lower Río Verde valley have investigated the history of Tututepec through settlement survey and excavation. The archaeological picture supports its emergence as an important Late Postclassic center and shows substantial later expansion. This does not prove that every chapter of Eight Deer's life occurred exactly as one version presents it. Conversely, gaps in excavation do not automatically make an Indigenous account fictional. The best comparison asks whether the scale, date and location of settlement changes are compatible with the political patterns remembered in a manuscript.

Other books supply versions of overlapping history. The Codex Colombino, now held in Mexico and digitized through a Library of Congress catalog, also records the political and ritual deeds associated with Eight Deer and other rulers. It is connected materially and historically with the Becker I fragment. The survival of more than one source is valuable precisely because versions can disagree. A historian can compare which ancestors are given prominence, which alliances are remembered, and which settings are emphasized. A difference is not simply an error to be corrected: it may reveal a community's interest in one lineage or claim.

Calendrical notation made a historical biography more than an undated legend. A person’s day-name, a year sign, and sequences of events could place claims within a remembered chronology. Modern scholars develop correlations among calendrical cycles and compare different books. But the symbols are not read as though they form one universal Mesoamerican language. Mixtec pictorial writing had conventions and readers of its own. The skill lies in understanding relationships among iconographic forms, lineages, geography and chronology, including cases in which the interpretation of one sign remains disputed.

Women appear in these genealogies because marriages and descent were central to political history. A marriage could bring claims to a place or join ruling lines whose authority lay in different communities. Yet the narrative usually gives priority to the dynastic work a woman performed, not the whole range of her choices or experiences. We should not mistake a political genealogy for evidence that women outside the court were inactive, or that all households experienced political change in the same way. A manuscript preserves named actors selected for a particular argument about rule.

The manuscript's later journey is part of its history. By the nineteenth century the Zouche-Nuttall appeared in a Dominican monastery in Florence and then passed through a British aristocratic collection. The British Museum eventually acquired it, and a facsimile associated with Zelia Nuttall's scholarship helped researchers study its images. Those collecting histories do not tell us precisely who made the book or how it left Oaxaca. They do remind us that surviving knowledge was shaped by colonial disruption, private collections and the long effort to identify and publish material that was no longer in its place of origin.

Scholarly editions now divide the screenfold into episodes and sections, but those divisions are tools for readers. The original painter did not necessarily conceive of the work as chapters in a modern biography. A physical turn in an accordion book, a change of direction, or a repeated ancestral figure can organize the meaning. Reproduction helps many more people consult the imagery, but a reproduction also mediates the object's scale and color. Conservators and readers of the original must attend to pigment, folding and damage, not simply the translated sequence of names.

A painted history like the Zouche-Nuttall challenges a common boundary between archaeology and history. We have the names and political claims of people who lived long before European contact, but the source is neither a transparent chronicle nor a list of rulers for a museum label. Its evidence comes from an Indigenous way of organizing time, lineage and political legitimacy. Read beside Tututepec archaeology and other codices, the book helps reconstruct a contested world of regional power. Read alone, it is already evidence of a sustained practice of historical thought.

## Lakes, Workshops, and a Court: The Purépecha State before 1500

Farther northwest, the Purépecha state developed around a different set of lakes, settlements, and institutions.

The political history of late precolonial Michoacán begins beside a changing lake. Around Lake Pátzcuaro, people lived on shorelines that shifted, farmed slopes and accessible land, fished, traveled by canoe, cut reeds and drew resources from surrounding forests. Over the later medieval centuries, powerful rulers brought much of this basin and neighboring regions into the state often called Tarascan in historical scholarship and Purépecha today. This was one of the major powers of Mesoamerica before Spanish conquest. To understand it, we should begin not with an enemy's account of warfare, but with communities adapting their work to a particular landscape.

The lake was not a permanent blue shape with fixed banks. Environmental and archaeological research documents changes in water level and in the places where people could farm and build. A settlement on an island in one period could have a different relation to shoreland later. Modern diagrams sometimes use the lake's sixteenth-century outline as though it had always been there. Researchers studying historical landscapes warn against this. A political center emerges within a shifting field of routes, arable land and resources. Maps can make that field legible while also concealing change through time.

During the Postclassic period, communities around the basin grew and reorganized. By the fourteenth and fifteenth centuries a powerful polity developed, ultimately with Tzintzuntzan as its principal court. Scholars debate the exact sequence through which central authority formed. Some interpretations emphasize competing lineages and the control of land; others examine negotiations among large settlements and the local choices visible in archaeological materials. Later histories describe the Uacúsecha ruling group, but no single colonial account can fix every step in state formation. Even the word empire names a process that developed over time, not an institution born complete.

At Tzintzuntzan, INAH documents monumental terraces, public spaces and distinctive raised structures called yácatas. Their platforms were important ceremonial markers, but the capital also needed housing, roads, crafts and food. A city built into a hillside tied a ruler's visible authority to daily supplies from surrounding communities. The surviving monumental complex is not a diagram of every social relation in the region. It can be interpreted alongside survey and excavation from smaller places, where farmers, potters and other producers made decisions that large buildings alone do not record.

Ceramics provide a particularly instructive test of the power of the emerging court. Researchers used neutron activation analysis, measuring elemental signatures in clay-rich objects, to compare pottery from four basin sites. They found eight compositional groups and long-lived local production recipes. The political formation of the state did not replace all those local traditions with one uniform imperial workshop system. Different communities participated differently in production and distribution. The objects still circulated in a political economy shaped by the state, but many potters continued to make vessels through localized technological traditions.

This result complicates a familiar picture of centralization. A ruler may command tribute, support ceremonies, and claim authority without standardizing every pot. Local craft economies can endure through changes in supreme political power. Production might remain in communities whose relationships to a court are negotiated or constrained in different ways. The relevant archaeological question is not simply whether a vessel looks Purépecha, but where its clay and technique place it, and how that pattern changes through time. Eight geochemical groups are analytical categories, not eight known ethnic communities.

Metalworking adds another dimension. The Tzintzuntzan museum's archaeological synthesis describes sophisticated working of copper, alongside gold, silver and alloys, using techniques such as casting and hammering. Metal could become tools, bells, tweezers and ornaments. Material sources linked the capital to regions beyond the lake basin, including Tierra Caliente. This does not mean every smith worked directly for a royal workshop. Smelting, transport, shaping and use can occur in different communities. The prominence of metal in Purépecha archaeology establishes technical skill, while the organization of the workforce must be investigated through production sites, debris and distribution.

The state also interacted with the growing Mexica political world to the east. Accounts of their rivalry often describe a stark frontier between two empires. But borderlands were inhabited by communities with mixed affiliations and histories of movement. Archaeological research in the Ucareo valley has identified varied settlement traditions and ceramic connections older than the late state frontier. A territorial line in a modern atlas summarizes power claims at a particular moment. It does not capture every exchange, alliance or household on the ground, and it should not be assumed to have existed unchanged since early Postclassic times.

The most detailed narrative of Purépecha dynastic origins survives in the Relación de Michoacán, assembled around 1539 to 1541 after the Spanish arrival. Indigenous noble informants and artists participated with a Franciscan writer in shaping it. Historian Angélica Jimena Afanador-Pujol has shown that the work emerged amid colonial conflicts over status, labor and land, and that its images had their own political work to do. The text therefore gives indispensable access to the histories people wished to preserve and assert, but it is not a tape recording of a fourteenth-century royal council.

The landscape offers a second correction to written narratives. Older accounts sometimes assumed that the growth of an empire necessarily ruined its soils by forcing ever more cultivation. Sediment and settlement studies of the Pátzcuaro basin find a more complicated sequence, with some intensive occupation and landscape stabilization, and severe changes associated with European conquest and colonial disruption. These findings do not prove that precontact land use caused no erosion. They show that environmental histories must be dated and separated, rather than assigned reflexively to a general story of imperial overpopulation.

By 1500, the Purépecha state connected fishing communities, agricultural land, craft production, ceremonial centers, and border settlements through a system of power extending well beyond its lake core. It could demand tribute and organize military force, while many local production traditions endured. That combination is historically important. A large state is not necessarily a single economy in which all decisions are made at the capital. The archaeology of pots, lake levels and buildings provides a check on later descriptions of political order and helps recover the labor of people the court's origin story did not name.

## A Field Drawn and Measured: Indigenous Land Records after Conquest

The next records cross the boundary of European conquest. Their measured fields preserve Indigenous knowledge within a new colonial setting.

In an early colonial book from the Valley of Mexico, an agricultural field can appear as a drawing with measurements along its sides. Other signs identify a household and its connections to land. The document known as the Codex Vergara is not a map of an empty landscape and not a European survey sheet with Indigenous decoration. It is a record of people's fields made with local pictorial conventions in a transformed political world. To read it, historians have to understand both what the painters measured and why information about household land had suddenly become so consequential.

The Codex Vergara and the closely related Códice de Santa María Asunción concern communities around Tepetlaoztoc, northeast of Texcoco. Barbara Williams and H. R. Harvey showed that these manuscripts record landholding and household composition across seventeen localities. They were likely assembled within a generation of the conquest, in the period around 1539 to 1543. That date is central. These are not photographs of life before 1519. They contain traditions of naming, measurement and pictorial documentation used by Indigenous people in a new colonial administrative and legal setting.

A household register could name a person and show relatives connected to that household. A parcel record could give the outline of a field, lengths of its edges, and descriptions of the land. Harvey identified distinct categories in the Santa María Asunción manuscript: a census by household, a record of parcels and their measurements, and a register of calculated field areas. The names and conventions are not merely curiosities for decipherment. They show that family composition, resources, and land entitlements were organized in ways that scribes could communicate and compare.

Why measure fields so carefully? Land was a productive resource, but it was also a basis for tribute obligations, rights, and disputes. In colonial Mexico, rulers, officials and local communities could contest what a household owed and what it controlled. A record might be used to count persons and parcels in the same administrative effort. Its precision was not a hobby detached from politics. But we should avoid claiming that each figure was invented by a Spanish official. The manuscripts preserve Indigenous graphic and numerical expertise while reflecting the pressure of a colonial system that could use records to extract payments.

The fields are not all rectangles, and their drawn shapes do not automatically yield areas by simple multiplication. In 2011, a team including María del Carmen Jorge, Barbara Williams, C. E. Garza-Hume and Arturo Olvera tested the accuracy of reported areas using mathematical comparisons to possible quadrilateral shapes. They found that, under the study's model, about three-quarters of the listed areas lay within five percent of the maximum possible area for the stated side lengths. This was strong evidence of competent quantitative practice, not merely picturesque diagrams.

Why does a maximum possible area matter? If the lengths of four sides are given, a quadrilateral can still bend into different shapes with different areas. The investigators compared the figures recorded by the Indigenous surveyors with geometric constraints derived from the side measurements. This is a problem of practical mathematics: how to represent a parcel that does not fit a simple square and still estimate its area fairly. The comparison shows systematic care, even though different procedures and assumptions can lead to different area estimates.

The results also teach a lesson in evaluating historical quantitative sources. A percentage describing close agreement is not the same as saying the land actually had that size in modern square meters. Erosion, changing boundaries, drawing conventions and errors in copying can matter. It is possible for a calculation to be internally consistent while the physical property measured has changed. Scholars therefore use mathematical analysis alongside manuscript history, place names and knowledge of local political institutions. The codex becomes more informative when its numbers are treated as evidence to test rather than figures to repeat uncritically.

Household entries matter as much as field areas. A lineage or marriage relationship could affect access to land, while a household's recorded composition mattered for assessing obligations. The registers reveal names and links often absent from royal monuments. Yet even a detailed census is selective. People could be omitted, households might be grouped for a particular administrative purpose, and changing colonial conditions shaped what counted as a relevant unit. We should not use the record to infer the private motives of the named people, or imagine that their arrangements were unchanged from a century earlier.

The physical survival of the books is itself historically consequential. Many preconquest and early colonial pictorial manuscripts were destroyed, dispersed or damaged. Libraries now preserve a limited, uneven sample, including books whose subjects range from dynastic lineages to tribute and land. The Bodleian's conservation work illustrates the care required to study pigments and supports without destroying them. Preservation is not merely about beautiful pictures. If a field-measurement register cannot be opened or examined safely, a major source for Indigenous mathematical and social history is harder to reconstruct.

These early colonial documents also help historians read preconquest archaeology. A field shape, house compound, or settlement zone found by excavation might be compared to later pictorial evidence for land and household categories. Such comparisons can generate useful hypotheses. But the documents were produced after demographic loss, political conquest and new legal demands. They cannot simply be moved fifty years backward as though nothing intervened. The correct use of continuity is to ask which practices can be shown to persist, which changed, and what additional evidence would distinguish one from the other.

The Codex Vergara gives us two kinds of history at once. It is evidence of Indigenous mathematicians and scribes organizing land information in the sixteenth century, and it offers a cautious window onto older traditions of household and field management. It also shows how colonial rule made certain kinds of record urgent and politically charged. A ledger of plots may look impersonal, but every line was connected to work, sustenance and authority. To study the Americas before 1500 responsibly, we sometimes have to read later sources closely without pretending they were written earlier.

###### Evidence boundary

The Codex Vergara and Códice de Santa María Asunción were made circa 1539–1543 under Spanish colonial conditions and are not pre-1500 censuses. They record Indigenous households and field measures but were produced in a context of taxation, land and legal disputes. Mathematical comparisons assess geometric consistency under assumptions and do not prove the physical accuracy of every parcel or establish uninterrupted preconquest practice.
