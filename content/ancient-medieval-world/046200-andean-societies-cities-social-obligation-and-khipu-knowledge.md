# Andean societies: cities, social obligation, and khipu knowledge

Andean power depended on relationships among communities, landscapes, labor, and knowledge. Earlier states, household obligations, and khipu records let us approach that problem from different directions. The Inca belong within this longer history, rather than at its beginning.

## Before the Inca: Wari, Tiwanaku, and Two Andean Ways of Power

The Inca built an empire in the Andes, but they did not invent the idea of connecting distant highlands and valleys through institutions, roads, ritual, and labor. Centuries earlier, two major societies flourished in the central and southern Andes: Wari, centered in the highlands of what is now Peru, and Tiwanaku, with an important center near Lake Titicaca in what is now Bolivia. Their objects sometimes share striking religious imagery. Yet shared images do not make them one state. They offer contrasting ways to ask how political authority could operate across varied landscapes long before Inca expansion.

Between approximately the sixth and eleventh centuries of the Common Era, both societies became influential across extensive regions. Their exact political reach varied across time and place. Historians use the term Middle Horizon for a broad Andean archaeological period, not as the name of a single unified kingdom. Material similarities in ceramics or textiles may indicate exchange, shared ritual ideas, mobility, emulation, or political affiliation. To reconstruct power, archaeologists compare the distribution of objects with settlement patterns, architecture, farming systems, burials, and local histories.

Wari developed a major center near Ayacucho and established settlements in other regions of Peru. Some sites show carefully planned compounds, walls, storage or administrative spaces, and an architectural style associated with the wider Wari world. Pikillacta, near Cusco, is especially important for its planned buildings and infrastructure. Its archaeological evidence has been studied in detail, but even there the exact functions of particular compounds are not always certain. A large planned settlement can demonstrate coordinated construction without revealing the complete chain of command behind every activity.

Building such a site required surveying, quarrying, carrying materials, preparing food, and maintaining water supplies. In the highlands, a road, canal, or walled enclosure was useful only if people kept it functioning. Wari influence may have combined conquest, administrative oversight, local alliances, and other relationships. It is difficult to assign every village to one model. Some communities adopted Wari-associated pottery or architecture while retaining older practices. The evidence asks us to reconstruct the variety of local encounters with an expanding power, rather than impose uniform control on a modern map.

Tiwanaku's ceremonial center stood in the Lake Titicaca basin. Monumental stone architecture, carved figures, courts, and ritual spaces gave it a distinctive public character. Archaeological survey has also revealed dense settlement and complex water management in and around the monumental core. Temples were not isolated from daily life. People lived, raised food, crafted objects, and sustained gathering places in a demanding highland environment. The visible stone buildings are among the strongest surviving evidence, but a political history must account for the agricultural and household world that made their construction possible.

Farmers around Lake Titicaca used raised fields and other techniques adapted to local soils and water conditions. The fields often called sukakollos were not just an ancient technological curiosity. They required construction, repair, and knowledge of climate and growing conditions. The relationship between raised-field systems and Tiwanaku political authority is a matter for archaeological investigation. We should not assume that the state owned or directly managed every agricultural plot. Farmers and communities had expertise that monuments do not record, and their decisions helped determine what could be grown.

Objects associated with Wari and Tiwanaku circulated over long distances. Highly accomplished textiles, ceramics, and carved images used recurring designs, including a staff-bearing figure whose interpretation has generated extensive scholarship. Some designs resemble one another across the two traditions, but resemblance is not proof of shared rulers. A maker might adapt an image for local ritual, political affiliation, or prestige. The art provides evidence for relationships of ideas and skilled production, while the mechanisms of coercion, diplomacy, and travel have to be investigated separately.

Both centers also reveal how religious and political power could overlap without becoming identical. A monumental gathering place might attract pilgrims, reinforce alliances, host ceremonies, or display elite resources. Such activities could sustain political authority, yet we cannot always distinguish a priestly order from a royal court through architectural remains alone. Modern categories like empire, kingdom, pilgrimage center, and state capture different aspects of evidence. Historians sometimes disagree about which best describes Tiwanaku's reach, and this disagreement should not be hidden behind a convenient label.

Environmental conditions mattered, but they did not mechanically determine events. Some scholars have argued that drought influenced Wari expansion and religious claims over water. That is an interpretation built from environmental and archaeological patterns, not a surviving statement of motive from a Wari ruler. Changing rainfall could create pressures, but communities responded through technology, migration, ritual, trade, or political action. When we say climate contributed to historical change, we still have to ask who chose a particular response and which groups bore its costs.

Both political networks changed markedly around the end of the first millennium and into the following centuries. Archaeological chronologies of abandonment, reuse, and regional transformations are not identical everywhere. Calling this a collapse can conceal continuities in farming, craft, language, and local settlement. Tiwanaku-associated ritual imagery and Wari-related techniques could outlast the institutions that once sponsored them. Communities did not vanish because a center lost its former influence. Later Andean societies inherited and reshaped practices from many predecessors, not merely from these two.

A useful comparison is what different sites let us see. Pikillacta's walls and planned compounds foreground administrative and construction questions. Tiwanaku's monumental stone center and surrounding landscape raise questions about public ritual, water, and settlement. Neither site supplies a complete roster of named rulers and offices like the archives available for some Eurasian empires. That difference is not a sign of less complex political life. It is a difference in surviving evidence, and it asks archaeologists to assemble institutional histories from material relationships rather than official biographies.

The perspective of ordinary producers matters here as much as in any empire. Someone spun fibers, dyed yarn, made elaborate woven cloth, prepared maize and potatoes, tended herds, and carried stones. Some workers may have labored under political obligation; others may have worked within households or communal institutions. A textile displayed in a museum can reveal astonishing skill while leaving its maker unnamed. If we admire the object only as imperial art, we miss the people and knowledge that made its precision possible. Economic history must include the processes behind the finished work.

Modern archaeological interpretation is also historically situated. Museums and early scholarship sometimes favored a story of one civilization leading inevitably to the Inca. More recent research has emphasized distinct regional trajectories, settlement systems, and local negotiations. The chronology of a site can change when new excavation or dating evidence becomes available. Political terms remain hypotheses to be tested. A good introduction to Wari and Tiwanaku should therefore avoid both a seamless imperial succession and the opposite claim that their influence was only artistic or religious.

Before the Inca, Andean societies had already developed far-reaching connections among highlands, valleys, coasts, fields, and ceremonial centers. Wari and Tiwanaku illustrate two powerful but different formations within that history. Their legacies were neither copies of one another nor simple rehearsals for an Inca future. They show how landscapes, labor, shared images, and political ambitions could be assembled in multiple ways. By attending to the differences, we can see a more complex past and ask more precise questions about what made Andean power possible.

## Kin, Fields, and Obligation: Social Worlds of the Andes before 1500

Between the great centers of power, households and communities maintained obligations that imperial histories can obscure.

Before a ruler could order the building of an Andean road, people had to decide how a household would obtain seed, graze animals, help relatives, and maintain claims to fields. Those decisions were not simply private matters. A person's relationships with kin, neighbors, ancestors, and local communities helped shape access to land, work, and ceremony. Mountain geography added another challenge: the resources of a high pasture, a valley field, and a coastal settlement differed greatly. We begin this part of the comparison with the institutions by which people made a life across those landscapes. They could support cooperation, define exclusion, and become objects of struggle when larger powers intervened.

The Andes are not one environment. In parts of what are now Peru and Bolivia, coast, western valleys, highlands, and high pastures lie within connected but difficult terrain. Different altitudes support different crops and forms of herding. Potatoes and other tubers were important in many highland settings; maize grew in suitable valleys, and camelid herds could supply fiber, meat, and transport. The details changed from region to region and century to century. Rather than imagine that every family farmed everywhere, we should ask how people secured access to products they could not easily produce in one place. Exchange, seasonal movement, alliances, and control of dispersed lands were possible answers.

One of the most important concepts in Andean historical study is the ayllu. The term often describes a group whose members claimed relationships through kinship, shared ancestors, places of origin, or land and labor obligations. Its meanings have varied across communities and periods. Ayllu was not necessarily a little village made up only of demonstrable biological relatives. A group's identity could be expressed through ritual responsibilities, residence, the use of fields, and obligations to people who were not close kin in a modern genealogical sense. Scholars continue to debate how far the institutional form can be projected into much earlier archaeological periods.

What would membership mean in practice? It could matter when a household needed help with planting, a funeral, a communal project, or access to a productive place. Relations did not have to be perfectly equal. Families differed in resources and influence, and membership could carry duties as well as benefits. Some Andean communities organized relationships in paired or complementary divisions, sometimes described as moieties. The arrangement could structure political and ceremonial roles, but it should not be imposed as a universal formula. We learn about these practices through colonial-era descriptions, archaeological layouts, and local histories, each of which captures different moments and different voices.

The evidence poses a difficulty. Spanish administrators, chroniclers, and Indigenous writers working in colonial contexts recorded much of the terminology historians use. Felipe Guamán Poma de Ayala, for example, wrote and drew about Andean society under Spanish colonial rule. Such records are indispensable and historically situated. They do not offer direct transcripts of every institution that existed a thousand years earlier. In a 2026 study, archaeologist Beth Scaffidi argues for approaches to ancient kinship that consider shared practices and places, not just biological ancestry inferred from skeletal or genetic material. Ancient DNA may reveal relationships, but it cannot alone identify who belonged to a socially defined ayllu.

Land access shows why the definition matters. A field might belong to a household's practical working world while its use was also embedded in collective relationships and ritual claims. The needs of a lineage, a sacred place, or a wider community might affect decisions. We should not picture an isolated farmer holding a modern deed, nor imagine that every Andean community lacked individual rights. The forms of tenure changed with political institutions and local ecology. A group able to coordinate a shared work party might be better placed to maintain terraces or canals, yet the same obligations could become burdens when powerful households or rulers claimed control of the resulting labor.

For decades, the model often called the vertical archipelago has helped scholars explain how Andean populations might gain access to products from distinct ecological zones through colonies or settlements at different altitudes. The historian and anthropologist John Murra developed the model partly from early colonial records. It remains influential because it asks a serious question: how did people link distant environments without assuming that all goods moved through markets like those of modern cities? But a model derived from particular documents cannot answer for all regions or centuries. Archaeologists test its predictions against settlement locations, plants, animal remains, and the movement of materials.

A study of Paracas society in southern Peru, between about 800 and 200 BCE, offers a useful challenge. Researchers examined settlements along the northern Nasca drainage, along with obsidian, marine materials, and camelid remains. They proposed a model they call economic directness: communities linked coast and highland resources through accessible settlements, caravan movement, and exchanges that did not always resemble the classic vertical-colony system. Their evidence does not reveal a modern money economy. It does show that reciprocity and dispersed colonies should not be treated as the explanation for every ancient Andean exchange. An effective theory needs to fit what archaeologists actually find in a particular place.

Work carried social meanings as well as economic ones. In later Andean settings, the word ayni refers to forms of reciprocal assistance in which helping someone carries expectations of support in return. The exact practices differed across time and community. Reciprocity was not the same as a society without hierarchy; who could ask for labor, and under what circumstances, mattered. Nor was every transfer of goods a balanced exchange between equals. Archaeological evidence of shared construction may suggest coordination without telling us whether the people who worked understood the project as mutual aid, religious duty, political requirement, or a combination.

The Inca Empire, which expanded rapidly in the fifteenth century, worked through these existing local worlds rather than governing an empty landscape. Inka officials used the mit'a, a system of required service, to draw labor for projects including roads, agriculture, and state needs. In some accounts that state obligation was connected to traditions of reciprocal provision and redistribution. The distinction matters: compulsory service to an empire was not the same institution as neighbors helping one another with a field. Both involved labor, but their authority, beneficiaries, and opportunities for refusal differed. A finished road can testify to extensive coordination while leaving unresolved what participation felt like to any particular builder.

Some imperial policies went farther. The term mitmaqkuna refers to communities relocated under Inka authority, often for state purposes. Moving people could transfer skills, reorganize production, or strengthen political control. A 2024 archaeological study of workers at Yanawilka investigates how people in such settings obtained and consumed food, reminding us that a category written in a state history still describes households with everyday lives. Required work, resettlement, and local kinship obligations could overlap, but they should not be collapsed into the single label Inca labor. Their effects depended on who moved, where, and under what conditions.

The people studied by archaeologists did not experience themselves as illustrations of a theory. A family might care about whether a terrace would yield food next season, whether a neighboring community would honor a relationship, or whether its ancestors' places remained accessible. Larger states could redirect work and revenue, yet local institutions might persist or adapt. Social history needs to account for that endurance without pretending that community life was always harmonious. Disagreements over fields, labor, rank, and ritual authority were among the matters institutions existed to manage. Ayllu membership could connect people, but also draw boundaries that excluded others.

Ancient Andean societies therefore offer no single answer to the question of how a community should organize itself. Ayllu, local reciprocity, variable forms of land access, exchanges between ecological zones, and imperial labor demands illuminate different scales of social life. The evidence comes from archaeology, later documents, and living traditions that cannot simply be combined into one timeless picture. We gain a clearer history by tracing where these institutions met and where they conflicted. The story of the Andes is not only about what an empire built; it is about the social relationships through which people fed, governed, remembered, and sometimes resisted it.

## Counting without a Page: Khipu and Knowledge in the Andes

A different form of record keeping brings those obligations into another medium: information preserved in knotted cords.

Imagine being responsible for a storehouse. Bags of maize arrive from one community; cloth is issued to another; a group of workers is assigned to repair a road. A record is needed, but it may not take the form of ink on paper. In the Andes, people developed systems of knotted cords that could preserve numerical and perhaps other kinds of information. These devices are usually called khipu, or quipu, from a Quechua term associated with knots. They offer a different route into the history of knowledge: what happens when a record can be tied, carried, checked, and interpreted, but cannot simply be read like a modern letter?

A khipu may have a main cord, with many hanging cords, and sometimes further branches. Its features can include the placement and type of knots, the color of thread, the direction of twist, and the way cords are attached. Some arrangements are plainly systematic. The Inca used many such devices to support government and accounting across the Andes. But the material form matters. A cord can be damaged, separated, or rehung. A surviving object may once have belonged to a larger set or been accompanied by a spoken explanation that no longer survives.

The best-understood Inca khipu records use a decimal place-value system. Knot forms and positions along a pendant cord can represent ones, tens, hundreds, and higher units. This is not merely decorative stringwork. It shows deliberate representation of quantity, with rules that permit different amounts to be recorded and compared. Modern researchers can often reconstruct numerical values, but a number without a label is an incomplete historical claim. Knowing that a cord records seventy-four does not necessarily tell us whether it counted sacks, households, labor days, or animals. Context and relationships among cords matter as much as arithmetic.

Inca administration did not operate in isolation from local communities. The imperial state required labor, provisions, transport, and information from territories incorporated through different means. Officials needed ways to organize obligations and compare reports. Khipu belonged in a world of roads, storage buildings, local authorities, and political coercion as well as skilled recordkeeping. A numerical device can serve people responsible for distributing food, but it can also make taxes and labor demands legible to rulers. Technical ingenuity and unequal power are not opposites.

Researchers Gary Urton and Carrie Brezine studied a group of twenty-one khipu associated with Puruchuco, an Inca administrative center on Peru's central coast. Their 2005 analysis argued that relationships among the records could reflect levels of aggregation, as information moved upward within an administrative system. That is a concrete way to test an idea about bureaucracy without having a Spanish-language translation of the cords. If the same quantities are combined or reorganized across several objects, the pattern may reveal the work records performed. Yet interpretation still depends on assumptions about what the groups and repeated values represent.

The work also raises a human question. Who knew how to tie and interpret these records? Colonial-era descriptions refer to specialists often known as khipukamayuq, people trained in making and reading khipu. Such testimony is important, but it was recorded after Spanish conquest under changed political conditions. We cannot assume every recorded job title or procedure worked identically in every Inca province before the conquest. We can, however, infer that specialized knowledge was needed to maintain consistent systems across distances. Records alone do not teach themselves to every passerby.

Khipu traditions were older than the Inca Empire. Researchers have identified cord-recording artifacts associated with the Wari or Huari Middle Horizon centuries before the great Inca expansion. Some early examples show patterns that differ from the later decimal system, including interpretations of groups of five. A study of Middle Horizon specimens used radiocarbon dating to clarify part of this history. The result does not prove that the Inca copied one entire system unchanged. It demonstrates a longer Andean record of experimenting with cords as durable carriers of structured information.

That history matters because the word Inca can hide generations of earlier Andean work. Techniques of farming, road building, textile production, and recordkeeping developed in multiple communities and political systems. The imperial government drew upon existing capacities while reorganizing them for its own purposes. When a khipu was made, its fiber had to be gathered or produced, spun, dyed or left undyed, assembled, and knotted. The cord is an administrative instrument and a product of material labor. Its history begins before a keeper makes the final knot.

Could khipu preserve stories rather than only numbers? This remains a major research question. Colonial writers reported uses that went beyond straightforward tallies, and some surviving devices have structures not readily reducible to decimal accounting. Researchers study color, knot direction, attachment, and sequence as possible bearers of categories or more complex information. No single accepted decipherment turns all known khipu into readable sentences. It would be as misleading to claim that every knot is a word as to insist that only numbers could ever have been remembered with cords.

The language of writing can itself mislead us. If writing is defined narrowly as marks that represent the sounds of words, a khipu's status may be debated. If the question is how a society stores and communicates structured knowledge across time and distance, the devices plainly belong in the conversation. Historians need not force them into the exact categories invented for alphabetic or logographic scripts. A cord record may work partly through physical arrangement and partly through training, speech, and context. This is not a lesser form of knowledge merely because its decoding remains difficult for modern researchers.

Conquest damaged the systems within which these records made sense. Spanish administrators sometimes tried to use khipu specialists' information while imposing new accounting practices and institutions. Other colonial policies and social disruptions undermined the transmission of knowledge. A surviving cord might later be collected as a curiosity, losing information about the storehouse, person, or community where it had functioned. Museum collections have preserved many objects, but acquisition can separate material records from the networks of interpretation that once gave them meaning. The history of collection is part of the history of what we can now read.

Living Andean communities have also maintained diverse forms of knotting and record-related practice, although continuity must be investigated rather than assumed to be identical everywhere. A contemporary practice cannot automatically supply the key to a particular Inca administrative bundle. At the same time, dismissing local knowledge in favor of an outside researcher's diagrams would be a serious methodological error. Archaeology, mathematical analysis, documentary history, and community-held knowledge can address different questions and sometimes correct one another.

Try again to picture the storehouse. A keeper may receive reports, compare quantities, and tie a record that someone else will interpret. The khipu could help maintain trust between offices, or help enforce an unwanted obligation. The arithmetic system is one part of its significance. The other part is that people created institutions in which information traveled without needing to resemble a European page. The fact that modern scholars can decipher some numerical structures but not every category is a limitation of present evidence, not proof that the original users had only a fragmentary system.

Khipu change how we describe Inca history. Roads, terraces, and stone buildings show the scale of material organization; knotted cords show a way of managing and communicating information within it. Their fibers carried decisions and counts even when no alphabetic document was made. By studying what the cords demonstrably encode, and being cautious about what remains undeciphered, we can recognize a sophisticated tradition of recordkeeping without inventing a translation. The knots have survived. Our responsibility is to explain what can be read from them, what remains unknown, and why both facts matter.
