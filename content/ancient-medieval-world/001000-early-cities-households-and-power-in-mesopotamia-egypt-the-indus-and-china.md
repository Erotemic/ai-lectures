# Early cities: households and power in Mesopotamia, Egypt, the Indus, and China

Early cities did not all organize power in the same way. Following households, water, records, and monuments across several regions will show both recurring problems and the different evidence available for their solutions.

## What Makes a City? Households, Fields, and the First Urban Worlds

Imagine a settlement large enough that you cannot recognize everyone you pass. Some neighbors grow food. Others spend most of their time making pottery, weaving cloth, recording deliveries, preparing ceremonies, building walls, or directing labor. People who have never met must agree, or be made to agree, about streets, water, storage, and obligations. A disagreement between two households may be settled by somebody who belongs to neither. The grain a family harvests may support a worker who has never planted a field. What would it take to make such a place endure?

The answer is not simply agriculture. For thousands of years, people grew crops without producing the kinds of large urban settlements we call cities. Nor is it writing: some societies coordinated extensive construction without the scripts we can currently read, while other people developed written administration in already established settlements. It is tempting to arrange these discoveries as a ladder—farming, then cities, then kings, then writing, then civilization. The excavated record is more interesting. Different communities assembled these capabilities in different orders and combinations. And the word civilization, when used to put some people above others, obscures more than it explains.

In southern Mesopotamia, in what is now Iraq, the great alluvial plain of the Tigris and Euphrates supported settlements with deep histories of cultivation and exchange. By the later fourth millennium before the common era, Uruk had grown into a center of extraordinary scale. Its monumental precincts, specialized production, imported materials, and clay records reveal a place whose needs could not be met by a single household. The Metropolitan Museum of Art describes Uruk's expansion as one of the decisive settings in which early writing took shape. But archaeologists do not possess the constitution of Uruk. A cluster of large buildings cannot, by itself, tell us exactly who commanded whom or how freely people chose their work.

One reason the southern plain mattered was the management of water. Rainfall alone was often insufficient for reliable agriculture in parts of lowland Mesopotamia. Irrigation channels helped make fields productive, but channels also demanded maintenance, allocation, and sometimes conflict resolution. Water could carry life into a field or destroy a canal bank. The question of who could open a gate, repair a breach, or command work on an embankment was both practical and political. Yet we should resist the older idea that irrigation automatically created kings. Cooperative villages could maintain waterworks, and governments could gain power through warfare, ritual, or claims over food as well as through canals.

An urban population also needed arrangements that crossed the boundary between farm and city. Sheep, barley, fish, timber, reeds, stone, copper, and other materials moved through landscapes where not every resource was available nearby. Some producers served institutions that stored and redistributed goods. Others exchanged through households and merchants. Archaeologists have found clay sealings and numerical tokens used to identify, count, or protect deliveries, but a seal does not necessarily document a free market purchase. It may register a ration, an allocation, a debt, or a transfer owed to an institution. What survived was often what administrators needed to keep, not what everybody experienced.

The earliest proto-cuneiform tablets from Uruk are therefore a remarkable but partial window. They largely concern accounting and classification, rather than a continuous story of city life. Later cuneiform could record literature, prayers, mathematical problems, legal disputes, and letters. The passage from shaped signs and numerical conventions to writing capable of expressing language took time. It was not a single moment of invention in which someone suddenly wrote the first history book. And the existence of an archive should not convince us that the archive's authors represent all the people who built the city.

Egypt offers another path. Along the Nile, river travel linked settlements across a long and narrow ecological corridor. By approximately 3100 before the common era, political unification associated in later traditions with the earliest dynastic rulers had created a royal state extending over Upper and Lower Egypt, though its consolidation was not a single unambiguous event. The Nile's annual inundation shaped agricultural rhythms. Royal administrators could count fields, organize transport, and command building projects across great distances. Egyptian writing developed early, but its forms, uses, and relationship to kingship differed from the clay-tablet traditions of Mesopotamia. Similar challenges did not produce identical states.

Farther east, agricultural settlements in the Indus region had their own long histories before the major cities of the third millennium. Communities in the broader Harappan world eventually built streets, organized craft production, managed water, and maintained forms of shared measurement over a vast area. At places such as Dholavira, elaborate reservoirs and differentiated quarters show planning adapted to local geography. The brief inscriptions on Harappan seals remain undeciphered. We can describe urban workmanship with considerable precision while knowing remarkably little about named rulers, political offices, or the spoken language of those inscriptions. This is an excellent test of whether we can recognize complexity without inventing a royal court to fill the silence.

The same warning applies beyond the great river systems most often pictured on classroom maps. On the Pacific side of South America, communities in the Supe Valley created large monumental centers, including Caral, beginning around the third millennium before the common era. The platforms and sunken circular courts required extensive knowledge and coordinated work. UNESCO identifies an entire regional system of settlements rather than a single isolated monument. And along the Yellow River and other parts of East Asia, several different farming and craft traditions contributed to later urban and dynastic formations. There was no single institution called the first civilization from which every other society descended.

How can we identify a city without defining it by the presence of an emperor? Archaeologists examine the extent and density of settlements, the diversity of work, the relationship between central places and surrounding farms, systems of storage and circulation, and patterns of building over time. They also look for differences among households and graves, for defenses, and for signs of institutions that persisted beyond the lives of particular people. No single artifact settles the question. A huge monument could have been raised over generations by people who met periodically. A town of modest size could support complex administration. The category depends on the evidence and the comparison being made.

In the archaeological record, labor is easier to notice in its finished form than in the lives of the workers. Walls still stand; the meals prepared for builders have vanished unless discarded bones, hearths, or plant remains survive. Great public buildings suggest organized effort, but not whether that effort was paid, exchanged, demanded as a tax, offered in devotion, or imposed under threat. In some places multiple arrangements probably coexisted. Treating monuments as proof of universal consent romanticizes power. Treating every coordinated project as slave labor does the opposite. Both erase the people whose actual choices and constraints we are trying to recover.

Cities also created vulnerabilities. Dense settlement could encourage the transmission of infectious disease, especially when water, waste, and animals shared crowded spaces. Dependence on concentrated storage made harvest shortfalls consequential. A political center that collected resources might help distribute them during hard times, or its demands might deepen hardship. Flood, migration, war, and changes in waterways sometimes caused settlements to contract while people reorganized life elsewhere. A city could lose its monumental center without its inhabitants, knowledge, or culture disappearing. Archaeological decline is not equivalent to the disappearance of a people.

From the perspective of someone working a small plot, the growth of a city could offer markets, craft goods, ritual gatherings, protection, and new neighbors. It could also mean taxes, land claims, labor demands, or obligations to institutions whose decisions were hard to contest. These are possibilities whose balance differed among societies; surviving evidence rarely lets us reconstruct an average person's view. But asking about that viewpoint prevents us from confusing the achievements of a ruling institution with the entire life of the community.

A further complication concerns naming. We speak of Sumer, Egypt, the Indus civilization, and early China as though each were a single actor. In practice, such labels collect multiple cities, periods, languages, villages, and political arrangements. Names are useful for locating evidence. They become misleading when we attribute to every household the policies of a king or assume that an artistic style has one ethnic meaning. A map is a guide to historical questions, not a portrait of a society's inner life.

The new urban worlds changed human possibilities. They concentrated specialists, enabled durable institutions, and produced records that make some ancient voices newly audible. They also concentrated inequality and changed the ways people could command one another. Their emergence was neither a universally shared achievement nor a fall from an imagined equal past. It was a set of experiments in how to live together at scale, conducted in many places, through many generations, under circumstances that nobody had designed in advance. The question is not which place deserves a prize for coming first. It is how these societies made coordination possible, and what that coordination cost.

## Kingdoms of Clay: Mesopotamia from Sumer to Babylon

From the general problem of urban life, we can turn to one unusually abundant body of evidence: the written records of Mesopotamia.

The clay tablet is small enough to hold in two hands. Its marks may record barley owed to a storehouse, a payment in wool, or a contract between people whose names survive although their faces do not. A monument announcing a king's victories can fill a courtyard. A tablet recording an obligation may fit in a pocket. We learn about early Mesopotamia by reading both, and by remembering that they were created for different audiences.

By the third millennium before the common era, southern Mesopotamia was a landscape of competing city-states: Ur, Uruk, Lagash, Kish, and others. Each possessed its own institutions and relationships with nearby fields, villages, and canals. A city's gods were understood as protectors of its community, and its temples could command land, labor, and resources. Rulers appear in inscriptions as builders, warriors, judges, and servants of divine powers. But these claims were part of government, not neutral reports of how government worked. To know what a ruler did, historians compare royal inscriptions with settlement archaeology, administrative documents, and accounts produced by rivals.

Consider what happened when communities disputed a boundary. The famous conflict between Lagash and Umma concerned fertile land and the right to control it. Lagash's ruler Eannatum celebrated his military success in the monument called the Stele of the Vultures. Its imagery and writing represent victory as something supported by a god. That is valuable evidence for political ideology and for a documented conflict. It does not give us a trustworthy inventory of every casualty or a fair account of Umma's claims. This distinction between an ancient assertion and an independently established event becomes one of the most useful habits a historian can learn.

Kings gained power through more than fighting. Someone had to allocate irrigation water, maintain channels, resolve claims to land, and transport supplies through river landscapes. Temples were institutions in which ritual and economic activity met. Priests and other officials could supervise goods and specialist workers. Households retained important economic roles, and trade was not wholly monopolized by palaces or temples. Merchants traveled to obtain stone, metal, and timber not available in the southern alluvium. A family could own property and arrange marriage agreements; an institution could demand work or receive an offering. Mesopotamian society was not one giant government warehouse, even when government archives dominate what survives.

The administrative record is astonishing for its ordinary detail. Under the Third Dynasty of Ur, roughly at the end of the third millennium, surviving tablets document deliveries, labor assignments, animals, fields, and the movement of goods on a large scale. Some people appear as entries in lists rather than as speaking individuals. This material enables historians to reconstruct administrative practice and sometimes the careers of officials. But not every person appears, and being counted by an institution is different from controlling it. The historian can calculate how many rations a record assigns without knowing whether the recipients judged the arrangement fair.

Sargon of Akkad, who ruled in the twenty-fourth and twenty-third centuries before the common era, is remembered as a ruler who extended military domination over multiple cities and regions. Akkadian became a major language of government alongside Sumerian. Royal memories of Sargon were elaborated long after his lifetime, so accounts of his birth or miraculous rise should not be treated like a contemporary diary. The importance of his empire lies in the ambition to connect several formerly competing political centers under one authority. But empires depend on local administrators, soldiers, tribute, negotiation, and continued access to food. Conquest is a beginning, not a permanent solution to the problem of rule.

The rise and fragmentation of political powers also remind us that continuity does not require a permanent dynasty. The Akkadian empire lost its unity. The Third Dynasty of Ur later rebuilt a large territorial state, only to fall in its turn. Cities survived political transitions, often maintaining local cults, languages, and ways of making a living. When scholars label these episodes as collapses, they normally mean the breakdown of particular ruling institutions. Potters did not necessarily stop producing vessels on the day a king lost his throne. Archives may become quieter because the office that created them disappeared or moved.

By the early second millennium, Babylon was one of several competing cities. Hammurabi, who ruled in the eighteenth century before the common era, built an extensive kingdom through alliances, campaigns, and administration. His name is especially associated with a tall inscribed stone monument usually called the Code of Hammurabi. The upper image shows the king before a deity associated with justice. Below are numerous legal pronouncements addressing injuries, debts, property, work, marriage, and family relationships. It is one of the great survivals of ancient law. But the word code can mislead if it suggests a comprehensive statute book applied uniformly in every court.

Some of the pronouncements distinguish between people of different social standing. Compensation for an injury could depend on whether the injured person was free or enslaved, and on the legal categories in which people were placed. Rules about marriage and inheritance describe obligations and possible claims, but they do not show that marriages all worked the same way. The stele proclaimed an image of royal justice. To study actual practice, historians also examine contracts and cases from surviving archives. Norms written for public display are evidence of what officials wanted to state; court records, where available, may show what parties argued and what decisions followed.

Women appear in these records as daughters, wives, mothers, property holders, workers, priestesses, and participants in disputes. Their legal rights and practical freedom varied by place, status, and period. A document showing a woman conducting a transaction establishes that such a transaction was possible for that person; it does not prove that everyone enjoyed the same options. At the same time, treating ancient women only as people to whom laws were applied ignores the economic and religious roles they performed. This is a case where detailed records can improve a story that once followed kings almost exclusively.

The famous temple institutions were also centers of learning. Cuneiform required training, and scribes practiced through lists, copied literature, worked mathematical problems, and learned specialized vocabularies. The languages of Mesopotamia were not interchangeable. Sumerian remained a language of scholarship and ritual after its ordinary spoken use had diminished. Akkadian, written in cuneiform signs adapted from earlier practice, flourished in administrative and literary settings. Texts copied by generations of students allow later historians to trace how stories changed. They also remind us that even in a society with writing, most speech left no durable mark.

Rivers and roads connected the cities to distant places. Traders and envoys traveled through the Persian Gulf toward Dilmun and Magan and exchanged goods with regions identified in Mesopotamian texts as Meluhha, often associated with the Indus world. Other networks extended toward Anatolia and the Iranian plateau. Such names do not automatically identify modern political borders. Imported stones or copper can document movement without establishing the language of a trader or the exact route of each shipment. Long-distance exchange could exist alongside very local economies, and diplomatic relationships were subject to interruptions.

Later tradition remembers individual kings as if the fate of a civilization rested in one pair of hands. The sources themselves encourage that impression: rulers commissioned many of the most conspicuous monuments. Yet the infrastructure of Mesopotamian life rested on farmers repairing canals, boat crews loading grain, artisans fashioning vessels, scribes keeping accounts, and families negotiating obligations. Some did this work with security and resources; others under restrictions, debt, or coercion. Our evidence is better at describing institutional categories than recovering the whole range of lived experience.

There is a final irony in the tablets. Clay is an ordinary, fragile-looking material, yet when fired or buried it can preserve an administrative record for millennia. A king may have intended his monument to speak forever; a clerk may have expected a tablet to be kept only until an account was settled. Historians can read both today. One celebrates authority; the other reveals how authority depended on counting, transporting, borrowing, and remembering. Early Mesopotamian history becomes more human when we listen to their disagreement.

## Beyond the Pyramid: The Nile, the State, and the People Who Built Egypt

Along the Nile, a different landscape and a different set of surviving records reveal another way to organize large projects.

If you stood on the edge of the Giza Plateau while the great pyramids were under construction, the buildings would not have been the only remarkable sight. Boats would be bringing goods through waterways connected to the Nile. Workers would need bread, water, housing, tools, and care. Stone-cutting teams, haulers, supervisors, craftsmen, and people preparing food would depend on one another. What survives most visibly today is the finished monument. Archaeology asks us to imagine the entire system required to produce it, then to test that picture against the material traces actually found.

Egypt's landscape offered a distinctive opportunity and an enduring uncertainty. Much of the country was desert, while the Nile created a long corridor of fertile land. The river's flooding replenished fields, but its height varied. Too little water could threaten harvests; too much could damage settlements and fields. Communities learned to work with its seasonal rhythm. The Nile also connected distant settlements by boat, supporting movement of people, grain, stone, and official communications. Geography made large-scale government possible, but it did not dictate exactly which dynasty would rule or what its subjects would believe.

The formation of a unified Egyptian monarchy around the end of the fourth millennium before the common era is known through artifacts, royal names, early inscriptions, and later historical traditions. The Narmer Palette is often associated with the symbolic union of Upper and Lower Egypt. It shows a ruler in scenes of power and conquest, not a documentary film of a single day's unification. The process of state formation took time, and political control was never identical in every village. The image tells us what royal authority sought to proclaim, while archaeological distributions and settlement histories help reveal how far that authority reached.

During the Old Kingdom, particularly the fourth dynasty in the mid-third millennium, rulers commissioned the monumental pyramids of Giza. The pyramid complexes were funerary and religious institutions as well as feats of engineering. Their construction called upon quarrying, transport, planning, skilled craft, logistics, and immense quantities of food. The king occupied a uniquely exalted religious and political position, with officials administering resources across the kingdom. But a monument's size alone does not reveal how workers were recruited or how their conditions differed. That question needs archaeological evidence closer to where people lived and worked.

For decades, excavators have studied the settlement at Heit el-Ghurab, near the pyramids. Ancient Egypt Research Associates documents buildings associated with workers, administrators, production, and provisions. Remains of bakeries, animal bones, pottery, and residential areas help reconstruct the labor system in a way a royal inscription cannot. These findings undermine the popular image of the pyramids as the product of an undifferentiated mass of enslaved laborers. They do not prove that every participant worked voluntarily under equal conditions. Egypt knew forms of compulsory obligation, and labor arrangements varied across time and task. Archaeology replaces an easy myth with a more complex question about organization, status, and responsibility.

Consider the food. Supplying thousands of people could require grain stores, bakeries, livestock, fuel, transport, and administrators deciding what went where. A laborer who carved stone depended on people farming along the river, slaughtering animals, tending hearths, shaping tools, and operating boats. Many of those essential workers did not receive a royal tomb or a painted portrait. Their activity is recovered through refuse and infrastructure. This is one of archaeology's most important corrections to written history: things thrown away may preserve the lives excluded from monumental memory.

Kingship was never simply stable. At the end of the Old Kingdom, central power fragmented and local rulers competed. Historians refer to the First Intermediate Period, but the term must not make us imagine that all cultural life ceased. Provincial elites became more prominent, and communities continued to use the river, build, exchange, and worship. Scholars debate how much low Nile flooding or environmental stress contributed to political change; the ecological explanation cannot be read directly from a royal inscription or extended uniformly to every district. Political fragmentation and the difficulties of subsistence interacted, but were not identical events.

The Middle Kingdom emerged after rulers based in Thebes consolidated control. Its political and artistic history is not merely a temporary interval between famous pyramids and famous tombs. Administrators and royal patrons developed projects in the Faiyum, produced literature, extended Egyptian influence into Nubia, and represented kingship in new ways. Texts such as the Story of Sinuhe explore fear, loyalty, displacement, and belonging within a courtly framework. They are literary works, not automatically transcriptions of an actual person's private experience. Yet they provide evidence for how educated authors imagined power and home.

Relations with Nubia demand attention on their own terms. Communities south of Egypt had their own languages, trade networks, leadership, and political histories. Egypt sought access to gold, resources, and strategic routes, and at several periods its rulers built forts or extended military authority into Nubian territory. But Nubia was not simply an appendix to Egyptian history. The kingdom centered on Kerma developed distinctive political and cultural institutions, and relationships shifted among commerce, diplomacy, and conflict. An Egyptian picture of captured enemies expresses an Egyptian state's ambitions; it is not a neutral biography of the people depicted.

The New Kingdom, beginning in the mid-second millennium, saw renewed consolidation and foreign campaigns. The royal court at Thebes, temples such as Karnak, and monumental tombs in the Valley of the Kings became important institutions and symbolic landscapes. Hatshepsut ruled as king and commissioned public works, including the great complex at Deir el-Bahri. Thutmose III pursued military expansion into the Levant. Later, Ramesses II presented his confrontation with Hittite power at Kadesh as a great victory, while other evidence supports a more complicated assessment of the battle and subsequent diplomacy. Royal imagery made claims about a ruler's cosmic and political success; it should not be treated as an impartial war report.

The New Kingdom also brought major forms of religious change and controversy. Amenhotep IV, who took the name Akhenaten, promoted the Aten in a transformation of royal worship centered on a new city at Amarna. The extent of these policies across households is difficult to infer from official art alone. Following his reign, traditional cults reasserted their positions. We should be careful with familiar stories about ancient religious revolution. A change in the court's monumental program may be historically consequential without immediately changing what every farmer or craft worker practiced.

Outside the royal centers, temples and estates were employers, landholders, and participants in redistribution. The village of Deir el-Medina, occupied by artisans and their families who worked on royal tombs, has yielded unusually rich documentation from later periods of the New Kingdom. Its records show labor arrangements, disputes, deliveries, and family matters. Because this community is exceptionally documented, it cannot simply be treated as representative of all Egyptians. Its value is that it makes the working life of a particular group visible beyond the grand images in the tombs they built.

Egypt's history was also shaped by exchanges with lands east and south of the Nile Valley. Timber, minerals, luxury materials, people, and ideas moved through routes reaching the Levant, Red Sea, and Nubia. Expeditions could be diplomatic, commercial, or coercive, and the distinction was not always clean. When an inscription lists tribute from a neighboring land, it may present commercial exchange through the language of royal superiority. Archaeology of imported goods can confirm contact, but not the precise balance of force and negotiation in every encounter.

It is tempting to summarize ancient Egypt as thousands of years of changeless belief and royal power. Yet dynasties rose and fell, capitals moved, local elites negotiated authority, different gods became prominent, foreign powers fought and bargained, and households confronted shifting demands. Historians use labels such as Old, Middle, and New Kingdom to organize these processes. The boundaries between them do not tell us when every local custom changed. And our sources are uneven: an elite tomb may preserve a carved autobiography, while the person who made its bricks appears only as a statistical shadow in the material record.

A pyramid remains a monumental statement of power, engineering, and religious imagination. It is also the end point of a long chain of ordinary work. Once we include the baker, quarry worker, boat crew, village family, local administrator, and people living beyond Egyptian control, the story becomes something larger than kings in stone. It becomes a history of how an ancient society organized survival, claimed legitimacy, and repeatedly remade itself along a river that was generous but never wholly predictable.

## The Cities That Do Not Name Their Kings: Life in the Indus World

Farther east, in the Indus world, the evidence changes again. Impressive urban works survive, while the names and offices behind them remain much harder to recover.

At Dholavira, in what is now Gujarat, a city's designers confronted a problem that was both simple and demanding: water was scarce, and rainfall was seasonal. They worked with channels, walls, and reservoirs to capture and manage it. In the Indus city of Mohenjo-daro, in present-day Pakistan, excavators found carefully planned streets, brick architecture, wells, and drainage. These places preserve decisions about how people lived together. Yet if we ask for the name of the official who authorized a reservoir, or the title of the person who settled a dispute between neighbors, we run into a silence that is fundamental to their history.

The urban Indus or Harappan civilization developed out of earlier communities in the region of the Indus and adjacent river systems. Its mature urban phase is generally placed around 2600 to 1900 before the common era, though sites have different local sequences and some settlements continued much longer in altered forms. Its geographical reach extended across large parts of present-day Pakistan and northwestern India. The term Harappan comes from a major archaeological site, not from a name we can confidently identify as the civilization's own. A modern archaeological label should not be mistaken for a language, ethnic identity, or political title shared by everyone who lived there.

The scale of urban coordination is visible in bricks and streets. Standardized proportions appeared across many settlements. Skilled workers made beads, seals, pottery, shell objects, metal goods, and textiles. Public and domestic spaces were planned in different ways at different sites, with water management especially prominent. Trade connected settlements within the Indus region and reached beyond it. Archaeologists have identified materials and objects indicating contact with the Gulf and Mesopotamia. But similarities in weights, seals, or brick dimensions do not by themselves reveal a single emperor issuing orders. Standards may have spread through shared practices, professional networks, governing bodies, or combinations we cannot yet separate.

Mohenjo-daro's elevated areas and lower town show urban planning, while the building commonly called the Great Bath suggests a setting in which water and communal or ritual activity may have been important. Calling it a bath is an archaeological description, not direct testimony about the ceremonies conducted there. The absence of a clearly identified royal palace or an Egyptian-style pyramid in a surviving excavation does not prove that hierarchy was absent. Dholavira contains differentiated spaces and fortifications; archaeological interpretations include signs of social ranking. The question is what kinds of authority existed and how they operated, not whether every society must leave behind the same form of royal monument.

Small inscribed seals are among the Indus world's most recognizable artifacts. Many carry animal images and short sequences of signs. Scholars agree that the signs form a structured system, but there is no accepted decipherment establishing the underlying language or the signs' complete communicative functions. Some researchers interpret the sequence as a script; others have proposed alternatives, and arguments continue over how to test competing hypotheses. Claims that a particular seal definitively names a king, a god, or a specific modern community should be treated skeptically unless supported by demonstrable decipherment. An attractive story is not an epigraphic result.

This is why the Indus world poses such a valuable challenge to the way history is taught. When an Egyptian ruler left an inscription, scholars could eventually read the name and compare its claim with archaeology. In the Indus case, we often know the arrangement of buildings much better than the names of their inhabitants. Written evidence, even if it survives physically, has not become readable testimony. The streets and artifacts still speak through their distribution, manufacture, and wear. They answer questions about craft, exchange, and planned construction while leaving other questions open.

Water systems show the strength of that material record. At Dholavira, reservoirs and channels were built in a landscape dependent on seasonal streams. At Mohenjo-daro, wells and drains indicate repeated attention to water and waste within densely built neighborhoods. Maintenance required labor across time, not only a founding decision. Who cleaned a drain? Who repaired a wall? Who could insist that a neighbor keep a passage clear? These questions are necessary to explain how the city functioned, even when we cannot assign answers to a named institution. A system's technical design is visible; its political enforcement is harder to reconstruct.

The city depended on people beyond its streets. Farmers grew crops that varied with regional conditions; herders raised animals; coastal and inland communities supplied raw materials and food. Craftspeople acquired agate and other stones, worked metal, and produced goods that traveled. Some of the material record reveals specialization, but the categories by which people identified their own occupations are largely lost to us. A bead workshop proves skilled production, not whether the worker was independent, attached to a household, or obligated to a political authority. We should recognize the missing evidence rather than fill it with the social structure of a different civilization.

The Harappan world's connections with Mesopotamia give us a second, indirect kind of testimony. Mesopotamian records refer to places including Meluhha, commonly associated with the Indus trading sphere, and archaeology finds objects and materials suggestive of movement across the Arabian Sea and the Gulf. The correspondence is important, but a Mesopotamian outsider's name for a trading partner is not the same as an indigenous account of that society. Nor can a foreign reference explain exactly which communities participated or who profited. Exchange networks produce evidence of contact more readily than evidence of equal bargaining power.

What happened when the large Indus cities changed? Older stories sometimes describe a sudden violent end or the destruction of a civilization. Archaeology instead reveals varied changes across a wide region: reduced prominence of some large urban centers, shifts toward smaller settlements in some areas, altered material traditions, and continuing regional life. The phrase de-urbanization is often more useful than disappearance. People moved, adapted, and reorganized; they were not necessarily eliminated. Chronology also matters. Transformations occurred over generations rather than on one historically recoverable day.

Environment was part of this process, but not a magic explanation. The summer monsoon and winter rainfall affect agriculture and river behavior in different parts of South Asia. Studies of cave deposits reconstruct changing precipitation patterns around the period of urban transformation. Other work on ancient channels has challenged accounts that posit a single great Himalayan-fed river vanishing during the cities' heyday. A climate proxy measures conditions at its own site with a model and uncertainties; it does not directly record the motives for a family's relocation hundreds of kilometers away. Historians have to link environmental observations to archaeological change rather than assume that matching dates establish a single cause.

There are also difficulties in the history of discovery itself. Excavation began under colonial conditions, and subsequent scholarship has developed across national borders with different institutions, priorities, and political pressures. Archaeological sites now lie in multiple countries. Modern arguments about national origins can turn undeciphered signs or disputed river identifications into declarations of present-day identity. But a third-millennium settlement is not a straightforward answer to a twenty-first-century political question. It deserves explanation on the basis of its own material history, not enlistment in a simplified modern ancestry claim.

We can therefore say a great deal without claiming to know everything. People in the Indus sphere designed complex settlements, managed water, exchanged goods, developed specialized crafts, and shared conventions across a large area. They had families, conflicts, skills, and institutions even if we cannot reconstruct them in the terms of a readable legal archive. They adapted to regional environmental changes and did not cease to have a history when some cities contracted. The lack of decipherment limits our knowledge of names and narratives; it does not make the people unknowable or their achievements less real.

A good history of the Indus world ends neither with a list of mysteries nor with a confident invented king. It ends with a discipline of listening to what the evidence can actually say. The reservoirs are still there. The bricks still bear the marks of construction. A seal is still inscribed. We know enough to describe a durable and complex urban society. We do not yet know enough to give that society an authoritative voice of its own. Keeping those two truths together is not a weakness in the history. It is how the history remains open to discovery.

## Bronze and Bone: Early Chinese States and the Problem of Dynastic Memory

Our final comparison takes us to early China, where royal questions preserved on bone give writing a different role in the surviving record.

Take a turtle shell or an animal shoulder bone. Prepare its surface, carve signs into it, and apply heat so that cracks appear. Someone trained to read the pattern announces an answer to a question put before the ancestors. Centuries later the bone lies buried. When excavators recover and identify its inscription, questions asked at a royal court become readable again: about harvests, journeys, ritual offerings, illness, or war. The oracle bones of the late Shang period are extraordinary because they bring the actions and anxieties of particular rulers within reach. They are also extraordinarily selective: the questions of a royal divination workshop are not the voice of an entire population.

The history of early China begins long before those inscriptions. Across the basins of the Yellow and Yangtze Rivers and beyond, communities cultivated different crops, worked stone and jade, made pottery, and formed increasingly complex settlement networks. Millet was important in many northern farming traditions and rice in many southern ones. The distribution was not a hard frontier, and people did not develop along a single cultural road toward one capital. At sites such as Liangzhu in the lower Yangtze region, elaborate waterworks, jade artifacts, and monumental construction reveal organized communities that existed before the securely documented Shang monarchy. Archaeological cultures are labels assigned to patterns of artifacts, not the names of uniform peoples.

The site of Erlitou, in today's Henan province, reveals substantial buildings, specialized craft production, and early bronze work during the second millennium before the common era. Later Chinese historical traditions speak of a Xia dynasty before the Shang. Some scholars connect Erlitou with the historical background of these traditions. Yet no contemporaneous inscription from Erlitou securely identifies it as the capital of a ruler named in the later Xia accounts. That is not a trivial dispute over a label. It illustrates the difference between excavated political complexity and the identification of an ancient site with the stories preserved centuries afterward.

By the late second millennium, the Shang ruled a significant political center in the Yellow River region. Its final royal capital at Anyang has provided inscribed oracle bones, tombs, workshops, and evidence of extensive bronze production. Royal power connected warfare, elite households, rituals, and the movement of resources. Kings communicated with ancestors through divination and performed ceremonies considered essential to political and cosmic order. A divination inscription records a question that a ruling institution considered important. Whether an anticipated event occurred exactly as predicted is a separate question, and some bones contain later notations about outcomes while others do not.

Bronze vessels make another part of the state visible. A large ritual vessel required copper, tin, fuel, specialized foundry skill, molds, transport, and sponsors with access to resources. In early Chinese bronzeworking, artisans developed a sophisticated piece-mold casting process, shaping clay sections around a model and reassembling them for casting. The Metropolitan Museum's accounts of Shang and Zhou bronzes show how ritual vessels served elite ceremonies and later became repositories of inscriptions. Their survival reflects craft mastery, but also the distribution of wealth and the work of many unnamed people beyond the foundry's most prestigious patrons.

One remarkable case is the tomb of Fu Hao, who appears in oracle inscriptions as a woman involved in the royal household and military campaigns under King Wu Ding. Finds from her richly furnished tomb and records from the same royal setting show that an elite woman could occupy a powerful role, including participation in warfare and ritual life. But Fu Hao's position should not be used to infer equal opportunities for every woman. A royal tomb is evidence about a specific social position, just as a king's own burial cannot tell us how common farmers lived. The achievement is in identifying a person through independent kinds of evidence while acknowledging the narrowness of the sample.

The Shang were not the only makers of bronze objects in the region. Excavations at Sanxingdui in Sichuan reveal a strikingly different visual language, including large bronze figures and heads, produced by people living beyond the familiar royal center of the north. These finds caution against equating the Shang's sphere of power with all ancient societies in the territory of today's China. Political and artistic traditions existed in parallel, influenced one another, and often left very different records. A history that follows only one dynastic sequence may fail to see them.

Around the middle of the eleventh century before the common era, the Zhou overcame Shang political power. Zhou elites then articulated a political explanation that later became enormously influential: the right to rule depended on the ruler's moral conduct, and Heaven could withdraw its authorization from a failing house. Historians call this the Mandate of Heaven. It was a theory of legitimate succession as well as a justification for conquest. The distinction matters. That a ruling family presented its victory as morally necessary tells us something about how it sought to secure authority; it does not prove that its account of every preceding event was correct.

Western Zhou rulers maintained relationships with regional lineages whose leaders governed territories, led armed forces, and performed ceremonies under a wider royal order. Bronze inscriptions are especially valuable evidence for grants, commemorations, kinship, and political obligations. But their purpose was often to celebrate status, preserve memory, or establish a claim for descendants. They do not give us a fully balanced picture of all conflict. Later histories and philosophical texts reworked Zhou memories again in light of their own political concerns. To write this history, scholars compare when a text was composed, the age of its reported events, and what archaeology independently confirms.

The Zhou system lasted in changing forms for many centuries, but regional rulers eventually acted with growing autonomy. By 771 before the common era, the fall of the western royal center and the movement of the court eastward marked a major political transition. Later periods would produce competing states and influential philosophical schools; those belong to a further history, not to an assumption that the Zhou already knew its own future. For people living through the transition, power was made through alliances, lineage obligations, land, warfare, and the survival of particular communities.

The economy beneath these royal stories was complex. Farmers cultivated and stored food; craft workers made ceramics, textiles, weapons, and instruments; soldiers fought and built defenses; families carried out ancestral obligations on different scales. In the material record, elite bronze vessels and elaborate tombs survive disproportionately. Excavated dwellings, tools, and burials can restore some balance, but the archaeology of poorer households is more difficult to interpret. A king's anxiety about rainfall, recorded in a bone inscription, suggests how essential farming was to the state while telling us very little about a particular farmer's experience of the same weather.

It is also easy to mistake the ancestry of a writing tradition for unbroken political identity. Some Shang characters are recognizable as ancestors of later Chinese signs, but linguistic and graphic change continued for many centuries. The institutions that used writing also changed. Dynasties came and went; religious practice diversified; regions maintained distinctive traditions. The continuity of a script does not mean that everyone shared one language or that people regarded themselves as subjects of one timeless nation. Modern terms must not flatten the changing boundaries of ancient political life.

At the center of this history are three different archives. There are buildings, graves, and objects that preserve what people made. There are inscribed bones and bronzes recording limited events and claims near their own time. And there are later transmitted texts, rich in political interpretation but composed at a distance from many episodes they describe. Each archive can answer some questions better than others. Used together, they make early Chinese history far more detailed than any could alone, while leaving important disagreements visible.

The oracle bone is a good symbol for this work. It preserves a question from a particular day: Will it rain? Will an expedition succeed? Will an ancestor respond? The question survived because an institution considered it worth recording. Our responsibility is to read it precisely, resist the urge to invent answers it cannot supply, and remember the many other questions never carved into bone. That is how a history of royal states remains a history of people, not merely a succession of names.

### Kingdoms of Clay: Mesopotamia from Sumer to Babylon

**Track:** Before the Modern World. **Background:** None. **Focus:** City-states, empires, temples, families, law, and documentary evidence, about 3000–1600 BCE.

#### Companion: political claims and archival limits

The Stele of the Vultures and Hammurabi's monument are royal claims; neither can stand alone as a reconstruction of social practice. Administrative tablets reveal which activities institutions registered, not everyone who labored or all norms enforced. Royal chronology, ruler names and the uses of cuneiform are supported by regional scholarship; individual motives not preserved in contemporary evidence are not invented here.

### Beyond the Pyramid: The Nile, the State, and the People Who Built Egypt

**Track:** Before the Modern World. **Background:** None. **Focus:** Environmental rhythms, royal states, construction labor, regional politics, and Egypt's changing kingdoms, about 3100–1070 BCE.

#### Companion: source perspective

Royal art, funerary evidence, excavated settlements, and modern chronologies support different inferences. The pyramid-worker settlement demonstrates complex logistical organization; it should not be used to assert one universal labor status. References to Nubian societies are included to distinguish southern political histories from Egyptian state self-representation. Cause-of-collapse hypotheses are presented as debated.

### The Cities That Do Not Name Their Kings: Life in the Indus World

**Track:** Before the Modern World. **Background:** None. **Focus:** Harappan cities, craft and water systems, undeciphered inscriptions, and the reorganization of urban life, about 3300–1300 BCE.

#### Companion: undeciphered does not mean absent

Claims about planning and craft are anchored in excavated sites and conservation records. Climate records are environmental proxies rather than direct evidence of particular historical decisions; competing river reconstructions are noted explicitly.

### Bronze and Bone: Early Chinese States and the Problem of Dynastic Memory

**Track:** Before the Modern World. **Background:** None. **Focus:** Regional cultures, Erlitou, Shang and Zhou, ritual bronzes, oracle bones, and transmitted political narratives, about 3000–770 BCE.

#### Companion: stratified evidence

Erlitou's identification with Xia remains debated. Later transmitted accounts of early dynastic succession are distinguished from inscriptions and artifacts of the relevant periods.
