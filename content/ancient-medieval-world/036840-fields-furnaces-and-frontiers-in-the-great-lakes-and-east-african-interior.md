# Fields, furnaces, and frontiers in the Great Lakes and East African interior

Fields, ironworking, pottery, and settlement changed on different schedules in the East African interior. Some cases reach deep into the first millennium BCE; others continue beyond 1500. The comparisons that follow concern evidence for work and interaction, rather than one uniform Iron Age frontier.

## Iron behind the Ports: Kasigau, Tsavo, and the Work of Trade

When a ship reached a trading town on the East African coast, the valuable things it received had histories that began far from the harbor. An ivory tusk did not grow in a warehouse. An iron spearhead was not made by the sea itself. Food that sustained carriers and craftspeople came from fields, herds, and foraging places. The region around Mount Kasigau in southeastern Kenya brings those inland histories into view. Between the Taita Hills and the Tsavo plains, archaeologists have found sites of iron smelting, homes, rock shelters, fields, and gathering places. Here trade was not simply something outsiders brought to Africa. It depended on African production and decisions about landscapes and labor.

Kasigau rises sharply from drier surrounding country. Its slopes, seasonal streams, and neighboring plains offer different possibilities for farming, grazing, hunting, and obtaining wood or mineral resources. Communities could inhabit a ridge, work a patch of cultivated land, and encounter neighbors in the plains below. None of these settings was isolated from the others. Research by Chapurukha Kusimba, Sibel Kusimba, David Wright, and collaborators has challenged an older habit of treating the coast as the active trading world and its hinterland as a nearly empty supplier of anonymous commodities.

In the villages of Rukanga and Kirongwe, archaeologists documented substantial ironworking remains. These include furnaces, fragments of ceramic tubes used to direct air into a furnace, accumulations of slag, and finished and unfinished metal implements. The ceramic tubes are called tuyeres. Slag is the material separated from metal-bearing ore during smelting; it is not simply burnt soil. These objects let investigators distinguish metal production from a place where iron tools were merely used. At some locations, evidence of both smelting and smithing occurs, meaning that people were obtaining usable iron and also shaping or repairing it.

The chronological evidence matters as much as the metal. The excavation reports include radiocarbon determinations that reach back roughly eleven hundred and fifty radiocarbon years before present, with phases of particularly intensive smelting within the later first and early second millennium of the common era. Radiocarbon years and calendar years are not interchangeable without calibration, and individual dates are tied to particular excavated contexts. The results nevertheless make sustained ironworking in the Kasigau area part of the history of East Africa before the fifteenth century. We do not need to assume that the furnaces were supervised by a distant coastal ruler.

Imagine the sequence of work, without assigning it to a fictional named worker. People had to find and prepare ore, collect fuel, construct or maintain a furnace, keep its air supply going, and then work the resulting metal. This was skilled and material-intensive labor. Wood for charcoal and access to suitable ores mattered. The finished iron might become a hoe, a blade, an arrowhead, or a spear point. Each form connects the furnace to different kinds of work, from clearing and tending fields to hunting and defense. An excavated tool may suggest an intended function; without its full context we cannot assign a particular act to it.

Kusimba and colleagues argue that some of Kasigau's weapons were connected to elephant hunting and the movement of ivory toward coastal markets. That interpretation is grounded partly in the equipment found and the known importance of ivory in wider exchange networks. It is not an inventory recording every hunting expedition, and it does not imply that all iron production was intended for export. Agriculture, household needs, conflict, and regional exchange could all create demands for metal. The possibility of specialized production makes more sense when placed among these overlapping uses, not when reduced to a single foreign market.

The plains show a different body of evidence. Surveys have located rock shelters and open-air sites, with stone tools, grinding hollows, and other traces of activities. A hollow ground into a rock may have been used to process grain or other plant foods, but form alone does not establish the plant or the date of each use. Several sites have multiple periods of occupation. Some shelters were later reinforced for defense, but archaeological reports date many of those fortifications to much more recent centuries. We should not project a shelter modified in the seventeenth century back onto a ninth-century ironworker's daily life.

At Kasigau, terraces also reshape the hillside. Research records describe extensive ridged and terraced fields that used local topography and seasonal water. Interviews with local residents and elders discuss landholding by families, agricultural choices, and the need to keep neighboring terraces maintained. Such testimony is valuable historical evidence about remembered practices and the physical landscape; it does not directly date the construction of every terrace. In the archaeological literature, the chronology of the old ironworking is firmer than a precise beginning date for every terrace or irrigation feature. Holding that distinction protects both kinds of evidence from being forced into a single story.

The relationship between craftspeople and cultivators is worth emphasizing. A smelter needs food and may rely on farmers or herders. A cultivator may need iron tools for preparing soil. A professional hunter may trade for metal, while metalworkers may acquire animal products, honey, or grain. These are possible relationships supported by the diversity of sites and later regional evidence, but their exact terms are rarely recoverable. An object can demonstrate work without revealing whether payment was made in goods, favors, labor, or obligations between relatives.

A further complication is the moral history of the things moving through these networks. Ivory and other animal products could become valuable exports because demand existed in distant markets. But elephant hunting had costs to animals, people, and local ecologies, and export trade could involve unequal power. Later in the region's history, violence and slave raiding influenced settlement and the use of defensive shelters. Those later events matter, but they should not be used to populate an earlier century with institutions for which no direct evidence has been found. Good history separates connected processes rather than pretending they all occurred at once.

The wider coast also depended on routes that did not always run directly from one inland producer to one harbor. A hunter, farmer, or ironworker could interact with intermediate communities, exchange goods in places that leave few archaeological traces, or supply local needs before any object reached a ship. Even where a distribution pattern connects inland iron to maritime commerce, it will not usually preserve each transaction. This is why Kasigau is more than an illustration of the Swahili coast's reach. It gives the inland communities their own economy, technology, and history, whether or not every object they made crossed the ocean.

The remains around Mount Kasigau ask us to describe African production before attaching it to a trade map. Furnaces required knowledge. Fields required care. Gathering sites required relationships among different people and places. Some of the material eventually moved into much wider networks, but the labor and decisions that made such movement possible were local. A history that begins at the dock sees an ivory tusk only as cargo. A history that follows the iron, the hillside, and the workers asks where the cargo came from, who transformed the landscape, and what else their communities were making for themselves.

## Building Fields from Rivers: Engaruka and the Engineering of Water

Moving inland to Engaruka changes the main evidence from iron production to engineered fields. Its long history also carries us beyond the survey’s usual 1500 boundary.

An old field can look like land that people merely cleared and planted. At Engaruka in northern Tanzania, some of the fields themselves were built. Farmers constructed low stone barriers and manipulated water so that sediment carried by streams collected behind those barriers. Layer after layer accumulated, creating deep cultivated soils in places where the modern environment might make such fields difficult to imagine. The remains of this system stretch across a large part of the valley below the Rift escarpment. They pose a question about East African history that is as much about work and practical knowledge as about settlement: how did people make a productive landscape, and how should we explain its eventual abandonment?

Engaruka belongs to the later part of the historical span we are studying. Archaeological radiocarbon evidence places the beginning of the system around the late fourteenth or fifteenth century of the common era. Its occupation and modifications continued for centuries after 1500, and the chronology of its abandonment varies somewhat among studies, generally reaching the eighteenth century or later. We should not compress this history into an event that ended in the Middle Ages. The site's importance for a curriculum approaching 1500 is that its foundations were already being laid as inland African communities developed increasingly elaborate ways of using water and growing food.

The setting matters. Engaruka lies below highlands that receive more rain than the surrounding lowland. Seasonal streams can carry water and sediment down steep slopes, and a perennial river has helped sustain agriculture there. A channel that looks dry today may have behaved differently in a wetter century. Earlier observers sometimes assumed that cultivation in this semiarid country must always have depended on the same amount of artificial irrigation and that abandonment proved the inhabitants had exhausted their environment. These assumptions turn present-day conditions into a timeless past, when precisely the history of water availability needs to be studied.

Across the landscape, archaeologists have recorded house terraces, field boundaries, channels, and many stone constructions. The old fields and irrigation works cover an area of roughly two thousand hectares, though not every part was cultivated in the same way or at the same moment. The visible walls are only one kind of evidence. Digging through a field reveals how material accumulated and whether a wall was raised in stages. Analysis of soil chemistry and its microscopic structures can show episodes of wetting, drying, and prolonged inundation. These methods make the field itself a sequence of historical events.

Carol Lang and Daryl Stump published a detailed geoarchaeological investigation in 2017. They found that many low stone walls acted as check dams, slowing sediment-rich water so that soil built up behind them. Some deposits exceed two meters in depth. Rather than being hauled grain by grain or basket by basket from a distant source, much of this material was carried by water. Its accumulation required people to position barriers, raise them when necessary, and manage flow as conditions changed. That is an engineering achievement built by repeated attention, not a single enormous construction event.

The farmers did not always treat water the same way. Soil micromorphology, the study of tiny features in a prepared thin slice of soil, reveals different regimes at particular fields. Some layers show alternating wet and dry conditions. Others contain features consistent with prolonged flooding or paddylike soils. This does not tell us that every field produced rice, and it would be unsafe to infer a crop species only from the way a plot was watered. The layers do show that the inhabitants could vary how water was delivered and retained, sometimes within the history of a single cultivated plot.

There was also a relationship between constructing fields and making them fertile. Flowing water transports sediment. If it is trapped where plants can grow, it can deepen a cultivated surface. At the same time, upstream erosion may be part of the process that supplies the material. A system may therefore benefit one area while altering another. Archaeology can expose the effects without immediately establishing whether they were intended or harmful. The Engaruka investigation found no corresponding evidence of severe salt accumulation in the analyzed soils. The researchers suggested that repeated sediment renewal might have helped avoid some problems known in long-irrigated fields, while leaving that causal explanation open for further testing.

A field system cannot operate without social arrangements. Someone must decide where a channel runs, who maintains a wall, and how water is shared when demand is greater than supply. Households might cooperate or dispute access. But archaeological sediment does not preserve a meeting minute or an irrigation code. We can confidently discuss recurring labor and technical coordination because the structures required it. We cannot infer the precise form of ownership, leadership, or punishment used at Engaruka without additional evidence. This is a distinction between what the landscape demonstrates and the institutions that remain partly invisible.

The chronology of construction may also relate to climate fluctuations. Research by Westerberg, Holmgren, and colleagues compared archaeological dates with environmental records from a nearby crater lake and argued that Engaruka's irrigation system responded to variable rainfall and persisted through relatively dry phases. Lang and Stump showed why this interpretation needs detailed sediment-level scrutiny: some fields were made when substantial water flowed, and the date of each episode of construction is not yet as precise as we would wish. The broader lesson is that an infrastructure system can endure change without requiring every intervention to have been a response to a single drought.

Trade networks may have mattered as well. Engaruka lay within a wider region of movement between highlands, plains, and caravan routes, and some scholarship connects its development with changing opportunities to supply food and water. This is a historical argument rather than the discovery of a market ledger listing a farmer's customers. The fields supported the local community regardless of whether particular harvests fed travelers. We should avoid treating farming as important only when it served a passing merchant or a distant port. Producing food for households was already a substantial economic and political undertaking.

Why was the landscape eventually abandoned? Explanations have included declining water supply, environmental change, trade shifts, and social movements. Earlier writing sometimes presented the abandonment as the inevitable result of residents mismanaging their land. The detailed geoarchaeology undermines that easy conclusion. It shows periods when water was abundant enough to build the fields, and it cannot by itself identify the causes of later hydrological change. Deforestation, climate variability, and shifts in river courses would leave different kinds of evidence; deciding between them requires more than observing an empty field. Disuse alone is not proof of failure or ecological irresponsibility.

There is no need to imagine Engaruka as either a lost miracle or a warning about a society that destroyed itself. Its visible remains document generations of practical problem-solving. The builders organized earth and water, understood slopes, and adjusted their fields as materials accumulated. Their successors lived through changing circumstances across the boundary of 1500. To study Engaruka is to recognize that agriculture has an architectural history: an old wall can be a device for making soil, a channel can be a record of cooperation, and the sediment beneath a crop can preserve centuries of decisions. We honor that knowledge best by dating each phase carefully and refusing to confuse the end of a settlement with an explanation of why it ended.

## What a Pot Cannot Say: Urewe across the Great Lakes

To compare another part of the interior, we must step much farther back in time. Urewe pottery raises questions about early communities around the Great Lakes, rather than continuing Engaruka’s later sequence.

A pot survives where a person's name does not. Its rim may be broken, its base missing, and its contents long gone. Yet the clay can preserve the pressure of fingers and the marks of a tool. Around Africa's Great Lakes, archaeologists find vessels with an especially recognizable combination of shapes and incised decoration. They call the related pottery Urewe, after a site in western Kenya. For a long time, these objects seemed to offer a tidy answer to a difficult question: who first brought farming and iron to these landscapes? The more closely researchers study the pots, the less tidy that answer becomes.

The Great Lakes are not a single ecological zone. Lake Victoria has fishing shores, islands, wetlands, and the slopes of surrounding hills. Farther west, the highlands of present-day Rwanda and Burundi rise between other lakes and river systems. A community could find quite different opportunities even a modest journey away: pasture, forest products, cultivable land, clay suitable for pots, and places to obtain ore or charcoal. When a type of pottery appears across this region, it does not follow that all its users lived in the same way. The geography alone warns against a map with one color and one kind of household.

A diagnostic Urewe vessel may have an indented, or dimpled, base, a carefully shaped rim, and arrangements of incised lines or impressed marks. Archaeologists compare these features in excavated fragments, often together with the vessel's fabric, thickness, and methods of construction. Not every fragment displays every feature. A small sherd with a decorated band may permit a confident comparison, while an undecorated body fragment may tell us little. When researchers classify a collection, they are assembling a pattern from incomplete objects, not reading a manufacturer's label with a date printed on it.

The chronology stretches across many centuries, with early occurrences in parts of the western lake region during the first millennium before the common era and later versions continuing into the first millennium of the common era. Different sites are not necessarily contemporary. Dates depend on the material sampled and the security of its association with the pottery. A charcoal sample from a hearth can help date the deposit in which sherds were found, but it does not date the moment when every vessel in that deposit was made. Broad labels such as Early Urewe and Transitional Urewe are working archaeological classifications rather than a clock that ran identically around every lake.

At Kakapel Rockshelter in western Kenya, the details become tangible. Excavators found a small Urewe-bearing area with rims, incised and impressed decoration, and a characteristic dimpled fragment. In the same long archaeological sequence, researchers documented food remains, changing occupation practices, and eventually directly dated crops. A Urewe pot there belongs to a history of actual meals and work, but it cannot by itself tell us the language of the person who cooked with it. Nor does a crop found at Kakapel prove that the people who shaped every Urewe pot elsewhere grew that same crop.

In 2026, a team led by Musa Mwitondi published a preliminary investigation of the Mara region southeast of Lake Victoria. At Balima, Mkirira, and Nyabekwabi, their work brought together stone tools, pottery, animal remains, beads, ochre, and other traces. The survey also recorded Urewe and other Iron Age pottery traditions in the wider region. One sherd from Nyabekwabi resembled manufacturing practices known at some Kansyore sites, but the authors specifically declined to assign it confidently to that tradition. Their restraint matters: the next excavation could change the classification, and the sampled sites still require firmer chronological work.

Why was Urewe once made into a larger historical story? The spread of Bantu languages into much of central, eastern, and southern Africa is a real and important subject of historical research. Linguists compare related words and grammatical features to reconstruct earlier connections; archaeologists identify changing pottery, settlements, farming, and metallurgy. Since early Urewe often appears in settings associated with iron or food production, it was tempting to make the three lines of evidence stand for one migrating population. But a person may learn a pottery style from neighbors, acquire a vessel through exchange, adopt a crop, or change language without carrying all of the other practices along.

Ceri Ashley's study of first- and second-millennium ceramics examined pots not only as ethnic labels or chronological markers but as things people used. Size, wear, form, and cooking context can raise questions about what was prepared, who used the vessel, and whether some communities specialized in fishing or other lakeshore work. Ashley argued for continuities from Urewe into later ceramic traditions, alongside changing social relationships. This is an interpretation built from comparative evidence, not an engraved record naming the owners of each cooking pot. It nevertheless shows why a vessel's practical life can be more revealing than a simple cultural name.

Consider an imaginary collection assembled in a museum drawer. One Urewe sherd comes from a household hearth; another lies beside iron-smelting debris; a third was recovered from a small lakeside deposit containing fishing equipment. Similarity in decoration may justify placing them within a broad ceramic tradition. It does not force the hearth to belong to an ironworker, or the fishing settlement to be founded by newcomers. Before making a claim about livelihood, we would inspect the animals, seeds, furnaces, tools, spatial distribution, and dates actually found at each place. The collection is hypothetical; the archaeological distinction is real.

There is a related danger in the phrase "arrival of the Iron Age." Iron, farming, pottery, and stone technology did not always arrive together or replace earlier practices overnight. Some communities used iron tools while continuing to work stone. Some acquired domestic plants and animals while retaining fishing or gathering. Some pots of one tradition occur beside objects that an older classification scheme would put in a different period. These combinations do not indicate that the excavation is necessarily confused. They can be evidence of long contact between neighbors with different skills, preferences, and resources.

The word Urewe is still useful. Archaeologists need names for recurring material patterns, and comparing pottery across sites helps build regional chronology. But the object of history is not the label. It is the people who dug clay, formed vessels, fetched water, kindled fires, cooked meals, passed on skills, and exchanged goods across an uneven landscape. Pottery can make those actions visible in fragments. It cannot identify a single ancestry, language, or political system for everyone who left such fragments behind. The strongest history begins with that difference between what the pot tells us and what we still have to ask.

## What the Slag Remembers: Early Iron Furnaces in Rwanda and Burundi

In Rwanda and Burundi, furnace remains add evidence for technical work, although their relationship to pottery distributions still needs careful argument.

A furnace disappears differently from a house. Its clay walls may collapse, its fuel burn away, and the iron made inside it leave the site in someone's hands. Yet the process also creates stubborn waste: lumps and masses of slag, the stony material produced as ore, fuel, and furnace lining react at high heat. Archaeologists studying early ironworking in Rwanda and Burundi can sometimes reconstruct a technical operation from those discarded remains. They are not reading a smith's instruction manual. They are solving an old physical problem with traces that the heat itself helped preserve.

Iron in the ground is usually not a ready-made metal blade. Much of it is chemically bound in ore. A smelter wants to separate enough iron from those compounds to obtain a workable, porous mass often called a bloom. Charcoal supplies heat and helps create the reducing conditions needed for this transformation. Air must reach the fire, sometimes through clay pipes called tuyeres. Temperature, the chemistry of the ore, the furnace wall, and the movement of air all matter. Too little heat or badly controlled reactions can leave much of the iron in the waste. A bloom is then repeatedly worked to expel trapped slag and consolidate metal.

In parts of the African Great Lakes region, early smelting remains include so-called slag-pit furnaces. A pit beneath the working chamber collected molten or semi-molten waste while the furnace operated. Researchers have recovered pieces of clay structure, tuyeres, charcoal, and the slags themselves. These fragments can reveal that high-temperature smelting occurred at a particular location. They do not, by themselves, give us the name of a workshop owner, prove how many people attended the fire, or establish that all communities who used related pottery possessed exactly the same metallurgical knowledge.

Paul Craddock, Ian Freestone, Andrew Middleton, and Marie-Claude Van Grunderbeek studied early smelting debris from Rwanda and Burundi in research published in *Historical Metallurgy*. By examining the chemical and mineral makeup of slag and surviving furnace clay, they could ask what reactions had taken place. Their analysis identified minerals including fayalite, hercynite, and wustite in the waste. Those unfamiliar names point to a familiar principle: heated materials do not merely melt and separate. They react, and the products can retain a chemical history of what entered the furnace.

One of their findings complicates the assumption that a successful furnace required some extraordinarily fireproof clay. The preserved furnace and tuyere clays were not particularly refractory, meaning they were not exceptionally resistant to high temperatures. The slag composition suggested either that clay was deliberately added as a flux, a material helping unwanted components combine and separate, or that significant clay entered the slag from the furnace structure itself. Those alternatives are not the same. In one, the operator chooses a material for a specific effect. In the other, the furnace lining contributes as the process runs. The surviving slag cannot always tell which occurred.

There were also differences among slag specimens, but the researchers argued that modest changes in composition could account for important-looking variations in mineral makeup. This is a warning against turning each mineralogical difference into a different "people" or an invented revolution in technique. Laboratory analysis can narrow how a furnace worked, and can sometimes reveal the limits of a proposed reconstruction. It rarely turns a fragment of waste into a complete account of workshop organization, ritual authority, commercial demand, or the social status of the smelter.

Dating these early operations requires a different chain of reasoning. Charcoal from a securely excavated smelting context can be measured by radiocarbon techniques, but the carbon date may still carry uncertainty about calibration and association. Did the charcoal belong to the smelting episode? Could old wood have been burned? Was the context disturbed? Archaeologists have reported ironworking in East Africa during the first millennium before the common era, but proposed very early dates and grand theories about the first invention of iron have long attracted scrutiny. Establishing that a furnace operated at one place is not the same task as proving that iron smelting was invented there before anywhere else.

Urewe pottery is often discussed alongside this early technology. In Rwanda and Burundi, the histories of ceramics, settlement, agriculture, and furnaces overlap in important ways. But the things we find can travel through different channels. Someone might make a pot without operating a furnace; an iron blade might pass from a smelter to a farming household; an apprentice might learn the craft from a neighbor whose language differed. The connections between potters, smelters, and cultivators are historical questions requiring evidence, not consequences automatically supplied by the label "Early Iron Age."

What might it have taken to operate such a furnace? Ore had to be obtained and prepared; fuel gathered and burned to charcoal; clay formed into a structure that could stand long enough to perform; air supplied; the bloom removed and worked. These are descriptions of the technical tasks, not scenes witnessed by archaeologists at a particular first-millennium workshop. Each task also had a landscape cost and a possible social organization. Did the fuel come from nearby woodland? Was the work seasonal? Was the iron used close to home or exchanged? We can investigate those matters with charcoal analysis, settlement archaeology, tool distributions, and comparative ethnography. Slag alone does not answer them all.

The distinction between smelting and forging is worth keeping. Smelting transforms ore into metal. Forging reshapes an existing piece of iron by heating and hammering it. A site with iron objects is therefore not automatically a smelting site. A site with smithing debris need not have produced its iron from ore. These stages may have occurred in different places and involved different workers. Archaeological maps of "iron production" become more informative when each excavated operation is identified separately.

Early furnaces in Rwanda and Burundi demonstrate technical knowledge that is part of the region's own archaeological history. Their evidence is more substantial than an old claim that iron technology must have arrived from some distant empire, but it also does not license a new myth in which the first furnace can be assigned a single heroic inventor. What survives is a chain of material decisions, reconstructed by excavators and analysts who can test some possibilities and leave others open. The waste of a furnace can reveal chemistry, craftsmanship, and chronology. Understanding the people around that furnace requires asking the wider historical questions as carefully as the metallurgical ones.

## Fuel and Iron in Buhaya: Technology, Forests, and Change beside Lake Victoria

On the western side of Lake Victoria, the history of fuel and furnaces supplies another regional test of those arguments.

On the western shore of Lake Victoria lies Buhaya, in what is now northwestern Tanzania. For archaeologists, the history of this landscape can be read partly in the remains of iron furnaces, charcoal, and altered vegetation. For people who worked there, iron was not only an object. It was a process that required fuel, air, skill, and a reliable supply of materials. The story of Buhaya shows why a technological achievement cannot be separated from the forest that fed its fires, or from the successive communities that changed the way those fires were made.

Iron was valuable because a forged implement could be made strong, edged, repaired, and reshaped. But ore did not simply turn into an axe when placed on a fire. Smelting demanded a controlled furnace. Charcoal had to burn hot enough, with the right supply of air, for iron compounds in the ore to be chemically reduced. The bloom that came out still had to be consolidated and worked. When archaeologists find a furnace or a slag deposit, they are finding the residue of organized tasks that began long before the firing and continued after it ended.

Peter Schmidt's research in Buhaya placed ironworking within a long and uneven chronology. In a 1997 study, he described evidence for production along the western lake shore beginning roughly twenty-five hundred years ago, with an especially marked expansion of smelting and settlement in the first half of the first millennium of the common era. Sites spread through parts of the landscape toward Lake Ikimba. The dates concern particular archaeological contexts, not a guarantee of uninterrupted occupation or the same workshop practices at every place. Buhaya is a region, and its history includes change over centuries.

Schmidt and metallurgist Donald Avery also conducted influential investigations of Haya iron-smelting techniques, combining archaeological inquiry, observation, and technological reconstruction. In 1978 they reported a sophisticated process in which incoming air was preheated, helping the smelting furnace reach conditions capable of producing high-carbon iron or steel. Their work challenged the idea that advanced metallurgical knowledge must be measured by resemblance to familiar European installations. At the same time, this is a published reconstruction and argument: it is not proof that every excavated furnace of every early period had the exact same configuration or achieved the same product.

Why would preheating the air matter? Imagine blowing cold air into a very hot fire. Part of the fire's heat must warm that air before it helps sustain the next reaction. If incoming air is already hot, more energy can remain available for the smelting process. In any real furnace, performance also depends on air flow, fuel, ore, structure, and heat loss. Preheating is a physical principle, not magic, and neither the principle nor a modern demonstration automatically fixes the design of a furnace that operated fifteen hundred years earlier. To connect a reconstructed process to the archaeological past, researchers must examine the surviving furnace architecture and production debris.

The landscape supplied much of what those furnaces required. Charcoal could be made from trees, but charcoal production consumed substantial quantities of wood. In his analysis of landscape change, Schmidt used charcoal identified from furnace contexts alongside environmental evidence to investigate how fuel use altered local forests. For early sites near Kemondo Bay, charcoal indicated that smelters exploited a range of woody species associated with wetter forest environments. As the first millennium progressed, later fuel remains indicated increasing reliance on secondary regrowth and less accessible environments. A chemical or botanical sample from a furnace can therefore tell a story about the forest outside it.

This change should not be reduced to a simple tale in which ancient workers carelessly cut down every tree. People also cleared land for cultivation, constructed homes, harvested other forest products, and responded to climatic variation. Pollen and sediment records help reconstruct the broader picture, but separating the contribution of each process is difficult. Schmidt interpreted the archaeological and environmental sequence as evidence that intensive production and settlement transformed woodland resources in the first half of the first millennium. Environmental change was both an effect of human activity and a condition to which later workers had to adapt.

The sequence did not move straight toward larger output and permanent industrial occupation. Schmidt argued that much of the region experienced a decline or abandonment of the earlier production system during the latter part of the first millennium. Settlement and iron production subsequently reappeared in different forms, including activity in the thirteenth and fourteenth centuries. Later furnaces are associated in his account with more efficient use of fuel. These are regional archaeological interpretations that researchers can revise with new excavations and dating. They are not a documented census of every family that moved away or returned.

There is also a social question behind the engineering. Who could obtain ore, clay, wood, food, and the labor needed for repeated firings? Who owned a completed implement, and who repaired one? Did some workers specialize, or did production follow seasonal agricultural rhythms? Ethnographic accounts of more recent ironworkers can suggest questions and show that technical knowledge may be transmitted within families, apprenticeships, and ritual settings. They cannot substitute for evidence about two-thousand-year-old people. Modern Haya communities are not living fossils of a single ancient practice.

The history of iron is sometimes presented as if one inventor found a perfect recipe and everyone afterward copied it. Buhaya suggests a different kind of history. A technology can develop through experiments, adaptations, changes in fuel supply, shifting settlements, and new social demands. Skill is not exhausted by the furnace's hottest moment. It includes recognizing ore, choosing clay, managing charcoal, adjusting air, reading the fire, and knowing when to stop. The archaeological record catches only fragments of this chain, but those fragments are sufficient to establish long-term local expertise and alteration.

When we place Buhaya beside the Urewe pottery sites and the wider Great Lakes landscape, we have to resist two opposite simplifications. It is wrong to regard early East African metallurgists as mere passive recipients of a technology. It is equally wrong to claim that a single pot style or a modern ethnic name identifies every person who contributed to centuries of ironworking. Buhaya's furnaces give us a history of knowledge, energy, and environmental change. The iron may have left the furnace. The waste and charcoal help explain the work that made it possible.

## Neighbors by the Lake: Kansyore, Urewe, and the Problems of a Farming Frontier

Bringing lakeshore communities back into view complicates any simple line separating fishers from farmers or one pottery tradition from another.

Imagine two archaeologists opening separate boxes of finds from the same lakeshore. One box contains stone flakes and pottery associated with a long history of fishing and gathering. The other holds pottery of a tradition often linked to early farming and ironworking. It would be easy to arrange the boxes into a story of "before" and "after": hunters first, farmers next. Yet the traces around Lake Victoria suggest that people with different ways of working and getting food sometimes lived near one another, used overlapping technologies, and exchanged knowledge. The boxes are categories devised to study the past; they were never walls around the people who lived it.

Kansyore is the name archaeologists use for a pottery tradition found at sites around the lake, often discussed in connection with communities that depended substantially on fish and other wild resources. Urewe refers to another broad pottery tradition frequently found in early Iron Age contexts. Those terms describe material patterns, not the name of two documented nations. A potsherd cannot tell us its maker's mother tongue. Nor can a stone tool prove that its user had never seen an iron implement. The distinction matters because a story about changing economies must account for the things people actually ate, worked, and exchanged.

Elizabeth Kyazike, in a 2022 study of hunter-gatherer and farmer interactions in Uganda, emphasized a problem for simple sequential models. At Kansyore Island, material associated with Kansyore and Early Iron Age Urewe traditions has been reported together in stratigraphic contexts, a finding she interprets as evidence for interaction and coexistence. Archaeologists still have to assess how deposits formed and whether materials were moved after burial. But Kyazike's argument is not merely that people occupied one shoreline centuries apart. She asks us to take possible contemporaneous contact seriously rather than assigning each category to an isolated stage of development.

A similar challenge comes from Wadh Lang'o and Usenge in western Kenya. Paul Lane, Ceri Ashley, and their colleagues brought animal remains, dating evidence, ceramics, and other archaeological traces together to revisit the transition to farming. Their 2007 study questioned a model in which incoming iron-producing farmers simply displaced an older population of foragers. Instead they proposed regional mixtures and long interactions over many centuries. A community might keep cattle and still hunt; make a familiar kind of pot while obtaining new tools; fish from the lake while cultivating plants elsewhere. The exact combination must be established site by site.

Consider what the lake provided before and after the introduction of crops or livestock. Fish could be caught in different seasons and at different shorelines. Wetlands held useful plants. Nearby slopes offered soils and grazing opportunities that were not identical to conditions on the water's edge. A family engaged in cultivation did not necessarily abandon fishing, just as a fishing household could acquire grain from neighbors without becoming farmers. The lake connected these possibilities. Exchange did not have to be a formal market transaction; it could occur through kinship, visits, shared work, or other social arrangements that the surviving artifacts do not directly record.

The material record is particularly good at showing some kinds of contact. A distinctive vessel shape at a new location may signal exchange, copying, movement of a potter, or common training. Domestic animal bones in a deposit show that the animals were present or consumed; they do not prove that every household owned herds. A charred crop seed can indicate access to that plant; to establish cultivation nearby, archaeologists need additional context. Stone artifacts may persist for reasons of economy, familiarity, or practical performance rather than evidence of ignorance. Each object answers one question and leaves others unanswered.

The 2026 survey and excavation report from Tanzania's Mara region adds a view from the southeastern lake basin. Researchers recorded stone-working practices, animal remains, ostrich-eggshell beads, pottery, and signs of activity in rock shelters. They also noted Urewe and other Iron Age pottery in their regional survey. At Nyabekwabi, an isolated potsherd had surface and fabric characteristics resembling some Kansyore examples, but the authors said more investigation was necessary. This is not a confirmed case of direct contact between two named households. It is evidence that the landscape held material histories more varied than a single frontier line on a map would convey.

Chronology remains the sharpest tool for testing a contact story. Two pottery traditions found within a hundred kilometers of one another are not necessarily evidence of neighboring people if the sites were occupied centuries apart. A mixed deposit may reflect true coexistence, but it can also result from later digging, soil movement, or repeated occupation of the same place. Researchers therefore examine layers, dates, refitting pieces, and relationships among animal remains, pots, hearths, and tools. This careful work can support a more interesting account than merely assigning each cultural label a fixed century.

The term "transition to farming" also conceals multiple developments. Crops with different origins appeared at different times. Cattle could be kept, slaughtered, exchanged, or obtained as products. Iron was a technology with several stages of production and circulation. Some people may have specialized in fishing, some in cultivation, some in combining activities. These arrangements could change without the replacement of everyone who lived in a district. There is no reason to assume that adopting a new tool always required accepting the political authority, language, or household organization of its maker.

Not every argument for mixture has the same strength. A researcher who documents two kinds of pottery in secure associated deposits can make a stronger local case than one who merely notes them in the same modern district. A faunal study that identifies domestic cattle changes what we can infer about animal use; it does not prove how cattle were valued or who controlled them. The caution should work both ways. We should not use an incomplete record to declare a clean population replacement, but neither should we claim we know exactly how peaceful, unequal, or intimate the contacts were. Exchange, competition, and intermarriage are questions, not outcomes automatically visible in a sherd.

From a distance the spread of farming may look like a broad arrow pointing eastward. At lakeshore scale, history becomes a set of places where people met, cooked, fished, cultivated, worked tools, and dealt with change. Some traditions continued; others shifted; new practices could be incorporated in selective combinations. Kansyore and Urewe are useful names for studying traces of this world, provided they do not become substitute characters in the story. The people were neighbors in a changing landscape, not simply representatives of successive archaeological boxes.
