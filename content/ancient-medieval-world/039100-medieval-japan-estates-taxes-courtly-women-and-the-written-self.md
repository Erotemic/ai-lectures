# Medieval Japan: estates, taxes, courtly women, and the written self

A field and a courtly room preserve different relationships within medieval Japan. We will move from the overlapping claims on land and labor to the written worlds of Heian women, returning to an earlier period as we change the evidence and the people in view.

## A Field under Several Names: Estates, Taxes, and Work in Medieval Japan

Suppose a field of rice grows behind a village in medieval Japan. Who owns it? The person who plants it might claim a customary right to farm; a local manager might be entitled to collect part of its yield; a temple might have been promised revenue from the land; and a courtier in a distant capital might hold another recognized interest in the same estate. The question has more than one answer because property was made of relationships, duties, exemptions, and enforceable claims. To understand medieval Japan beyond the court and its warriors, we need to follow those claims down to the field.

One powerful word in this history is shōen, usually translated as estate or manor. Estates developed through several different processes rather than through one founding decree. Some early arrangements involved newly cleared farmland. Later, rights over existing lands could be attached to influential patrons, temples, or shrines. Some estates had recognized immunities from particular provincial taxes or from certain kinds of official entry. But exemption was never a magical guarantee of freedom from demands. Authorities disagreed about what an estate owed, who could inspect it, and which rights local managers possessed. Written settlements were a way to argue about those boundaries.

The institutions that preserved documents shape the history we can tell. Kyoto University's collection of records from Kyō-ō Gokokuji, commonly called Tōji, includes thousands of documents spanning from the Heian period into early modern centuries. The archive contains records about land, payments, temple administration, and ritual affairs. The University of Tokyo's historiographical collections include estate maps and copies. Historians can examine an estate as a geographical place, an object of measurement, a source of tax income, and a subject of repeated disputes. We should also remember that the survival of temple papers makes temple interests easier to see than the words of many ordinary farm families.

Consider the map. A drawn border around paddies may seem to show a straightforward parcel of land. But an estate map is an argument made for a purpose. It may emphasize boundaries, waterways, shrines, and features useful in a dispute. The University of Zurich's research teaching materials explain how medieval estate maps developed visual conventions different from the squared survey maps of earlier periods. Their bird's-eye views were not neutral aerial photographs. To read one, ask when it was produced, who commissioned or copied it, and which kind of right its lines were intended to support.

A temple's annual account presents another sort of evidence. It may enumerate grain, other dues, delayed payments, arrears, transport expenses, and obligations associated with services or land use. Such a record can reveal the administrative effort required to turn agricultural work into distant institutional income. A monastery in Kyoto depended on rice grown elsewhere, stored and measured by people outside its walls. Transport itself cost labor and materials. If crops failed, officials could argue about the amount owed and whose loss it was. The numbers record a claim and perhaps a receipt; they are not transparent measures of what every household actually produced or ate.

The social world of an estate included people of unequal standing. Cultivators and local organizers negotiated access to fields and irrigation, while estate stewards or warrior families might seek greater authority. Some labor was performed within households; some relied on hired workers, dependents, or people under coercive obligations. The terms translated as farmer or peasant can conceal important distinctions in status and economic capacity. The archive names those who owned or managed claims far more often than it names those who weeded paddies, repaired embankments, or cooked for harvest crews. A history centered on land tax must make that imbalance visible.

There were also overlapping jurisdictions. A distant proprietor could assign local agents to enforce collection. Provincial officials might dispute an estate's claim to immunity. Military authority did not suddenly erase the court, and a warrior household did not necessarily control all rights in its district. In the centuries after the rise of the Kamakura government, some local managers gained leverage that older proprietors found difficult to resist. These were historically changing arrangements, not an eternal hierarchy of emperor, samurai, and peasants. Individual estates could follow different paths depending on their patrons, markets, and conflicts.

A study of the Ito estate in northern Kyushu, covering the twelfth through fourteenth centuries, illustrates how useful a sustained local history can be. Land administration linked distant courtiers and religious bodies with regional agents; local managers gained influence over time. The Mongol invasions of 1274 and 1281 added military demands and disruptions to an already evolving rural order. Yet it would be wrong to reduce every change in estate life to the invasions. Historians test how war interacted with ongoing struggles over appointments, taxation, and access to land. A local sequence complicates the familiar story in which one battle instantly produces a new social system.

Ordinary documents can also show how people understood obligations. A petition might allege that a steward collected too much, or that another village crossed an estate boundary. A receipt may mark grain successfully delivered. A register might distinguish fields left idle from fields expected to yield. None of these genres guarantees that the author tells the full truth. Petitions are meant to persuade, receipts to settle accounts, and maps to support claims. Their partiality is precisely why historians compare different records. When an argument is documented repeatedly from opposing sides, we may learn more than if an official calmly summarized a situation for us.

The question of landholding also changed over time. Research on documents from Tōji's Kuze estate near Kyoto shows that sales and transfers could coexist with large numbers of land-poor people in the late medieval countryside. A market in land rights did not mean that every household held an equal share or could sell property freely. Nor did village organization always require the direct presence of a distant lord. Rural communities could coordinate labor, bargain collectively, and develop their own relationships with authority. We have to ask whose interests were represented when a document says the village agreed to something.

The massive survival of documentary collections is itself a historical event. Records were assembled, recopied, dispersed, and reorganized across centuries. The University of Tokyo notes that many Tōdaiji documents are drafts or undated internal papers, and that later rearrangement has obscured original filing contexts. A document labeled with an estate name today may not sit among the papers it accompanied when written. The historian's work includes reconstructing the archive's own history. The digital image of a manuscript can make its letters accessible, but it does not solve problems of provenance and interpretation by itself.

This history helps explain why medieval Japan's political authority can look so complicated. Revenue rights, jurisdictional privileges, household arrangements, and military power were distributed rather than locked together in one neat ownership title. A temple could be economically strong in a region where a warrior held immediate coercive power. Farmers could possess recognized claims while still facing heavy burdens. A ruler's new proclamation mattered only insofar as people carried it into local practice, resisted it, or found ways to negotiate its terms. Institutions were made real in fields and storehouses.

Return to the rice field and the question of ownership. A map can show where it lies; an account may show what it owes; a petition may show who objected; a legal settlement may record who won a particular dispute. To learn what cultivation felt like, what risks families bore, and how much power their village exercised, we need to put those documents together and notice the silences between them. The field belonged to a landscape of work that made temples, warriors, and courts possible. Medieval Japanese history looks different once we begin there.

## A Room behind the Screen: Women, Writing, and Power at the Heian Court

To compare the written world of courtly women, return to Heian settings earlier than some of the estate evidence just discussed.

A court lady hears a remark from the other side of a screen. Its meaning depends on who spoke, who was allowed to hear, and how quickly she can answer with an appropriate poem. A letter might carry a few lines of verse on carefully chosen paper. A small error of tone could embarrass an entire household. This world of words is one of the settings in which some of the most enduring literature in Japanese history took shape. Its authors include women whose work tells us about court life, desire, competition, and political uncertainty. Their writing is evidence, but it is also art, and that difference matters.

The Heian period is conventionally dated from 794 to 1185, beginning with the establishment of the court's capital at Heian-kyo, the city later called Kyoto. For many of its centuries, political institutions retained forms derived from earlier state systems. Yet power changed within the court and beyond it. Fujiwara families gained influence through offices, marriage alliances, and relationships with emperors. Provincial governors and estate holders shaped life outside the capital. Against this background, the cultural life of a narrow aristocratic circle flourished, producing texts that later readers would treat as masterpieces.

Written Japanese used a complex relationship between Chinese characters and syllabic forms known as kana. Chinese writing and learned literary culture remained important, particularly for men pursuing official careers, but the emergence of highly developed vernacular prose and poetry was not simply the disappearance of Chinese influence. Language choices reflected education, genre, rank, and audience. Women at court became major authors in kana-based forms, although it would be wrong to say only women used kana or that every woman at court wrote books. A literary opportunity was distributed through privilege as well as talent.

Murasaki Shikibu, active around the turn of the eleventh century, is associated with The Tale of Genji. The work follows a courtly world of love, status, disappointment, aging, memory, and changing generations. Its fictional characters negotiate relationships through visits, messages, marriages, and the consequences of rank. The book is not an official record of imperial institutions, and Genji is not the novelist's diary disguised under another name. Yet the narrative's precision about the social meaning of communication offers a kind of historical insight that a roster of ministers cannot. It shows how an author could make social structures legible through imagined people.

In a literary scene, not receiving a response can be as meaningful as receiving one. A poem with the wrong seasonal reference, a messenger who arrives at the wrong time, or the color of a sleeve can shift an encounter. Such details were artistic conventions, and the conventions must be studied rather than treated as direct observation of every aristocratic household. Still, they arose from a culture in which verse, calligraphy, gifts, and personal reputation mattered to courtly relationships. Literature was one of the tools through which a governing elite recognized its own members.

Murasaki also left a diary, conventionally called The Diary of Lady Murasaki. It includes observations connected with service to Empress Shoshi, also known as Akiko, a daughter of the powerful Fujiwara no Michinaga. The courtly setting was political. Supporting an imperial consort through ceremony, education, household presentation, and the birth of children could help consolidate influence at the center of government. A diary passage may illuminate the texture of such service, but it is still a selected literary record. The author chose what to include, how to characterize people, and what voice to use.

Sei Shonagon, a lady in the service of another imperial consort, Empress Teishi or Sadako, is associated with The Pillow Book. Lists, observations, witty descriptions, and sharp judgments give this work a different motion from the long narrative of Genji. A category such as things that are pleasing or things that are embarrassing invites the reader into a world of finely ranked sensations. But this is not a transparent camera watching court life. Its arrangement, textual transmission, and cultivated persona shape what we hear. A brilliant insult can tell us something about status and competition without being an impartial verdict on the person insulted.

It is tempting to imagine these two authors as representatives of a single literary salon. In fact, the networks around their patrons were politically distinct, and the documents have complicated histories of composition and preservation. Later generations often made Murasaki and Sei into paired cultural icons, creating their own stories of rivalry. The historian should begin with what texts actually say, which dates can be established, and how the courts of Shoshi and Teishi were related to struggles among powerful families. Literary fame has its own history, separate from an author's everyday circumstances.

A third work, the Kagero Diary, is associated with a woman known chiefly as the mother of Fujiwara no Michitsuna. Her name is not securely recovered, and that absence is telling. The diary addresses marriage, frustration, religious concerns, and the vulnerability of a woman whose life was tied to a powerful man's intermittent presence. It is often read as intimate testimony, but its form is crafted and retrospective. Its author is not simply listing facts in chronological order; she is making sense of experience. What it reveals can be emotionally direct without being a household ledger.

These texts also tell us something about the politics of documentation. Male officials left diaries, court histories, and records of ceremony; women created forms that turned relationships and perception into literature. The distinction was not absolute. But it mattered who could seek office, who controlled property, who moved between residences, and who depended on kinship alliances. A woman in service to a consort could exercise influence through culture, information, and household connections while lacking the official power of a high-ranking male minister. That combination is more historically useful than a simple story of either female liberation or total silence.

What about the women outside this elite? Farmers, domestic workers, servants, and people who supported aristocratic estates rarely speak in these famous texts on equal terms. When a story depicts a servant, it does so through an author located within a privileged courtly world. Manuscripts also survived because later readers, patrons, scribes, and institutions valued them. The surviving literary archive can enlarge our picture of women's authorship while narrowing our view of the population. We cannot use Genji's households as a random sample of Japanese society around the year 1000.

The material history of books adds another complication. We do not possess a first manuscript of The Tale of Genji written in Murasaki's own hand. Copying introduced changes, and commentators and artists later reshaped how readers imagined the characters. Illustrated scrolls, calligraphic selections, printed editions, and modern translations each belong to a stage in the work's life. A famous line encountered today may have passed through centuries of editing before it reached us. That is not a reason to abandon the text as history. It is a reason to study how literature remains present by changing.

We can now put this literary world beside the estates and tax documents that help reconstruct medieval Japanese government. A property record may tell us who claimed rights to a field and which institutions received revenues. A court diary may show what an aristocrat considered worth recording. A work of fiction can expose how hierarchy shaped intimacy, even though its characters never lived. No single kind of writing is a substitute for the others. Their differences allow us to see the gap between administrative order and the fears, ambitions, and expectations of people living within it.

The enduring achievement of Heian women writers was not that they accidentally documented a vanished court. They made powerful artistic forms from a world in which words were bound to social action. Their works survived far beyond the political arrangements that first made them possible. When we read them historically, we learn both about the narrow spaces of elite life and the ability of authors within those spaces to examine the limits imposed upon them. A room behind a screen could be constrained by rank and family, and still become a place from which literature reached across a thousand years.
