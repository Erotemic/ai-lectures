# Metalwork and political authority in the Nigerian region

Metal objects can preserve skill and authority without explaining an entire political system. Igbo-Ukwu, Ife, and Benin are distinct historical places. Comparing their archaeology will show both what survives and what can be lost when famous objects are detached from the settings in which they were made and used.

## The Metalwork beneath the Courtyard: Igbo-Ukwu and the Limits of an Excavation

In 1938, Isaiah Anozie was digging a cistern in his compound at Igbo-Ukwu, in what is now southeastern Nigeria. He encountered metal objects and beads in the ground. They did not come with a written account of who had made them or why they were gathered there. The finds drew the attention of colonial officials and, eventually, archaeologists. Some were carried away; others remained within local collections or were later excavated. The event is a reminder that the history of a discovery includes local people long before the publication of a scholarly excavation report.

The metalwork from Igbo-Ukwu is technically elaborate. It includes vessels, ornaments, chains, and objects interpreted as ritual or ceremonial equipment. Some copper-alloy pieces were made using forms of lost-wax casting, a process in which a shaped model is replaced by molten metal. Other parts were shaped by different metalworking techniques. The finished objects required control over materials, heat, form, and successive stages of production. They were not made by someone simply pouring metal into a convenient hole. Skilled craftspeople developed and applied a demanding set of procedures.

It helps to understand the process without romanticizing it. A lost-wax object begins with a desired form, often worked in wax and surrounded by a refractory material. When heated, the wax melts away; metal can enter the resulting space. The details vary, and some of Igbo-Ukwu's forms would have required specialized planning to produce fine surfaces and intricate structures. We do not yet possess a full workshop archive naming individual metalworkers or telling us how apprentices learned. The objects demonstrate skill. They do not automatically tell us whether production was organized by a king, a lineage, an association, or another institution.

The discoveries led to excavations directed by Thurstan Shaw in 1959 and 1960 and again in 1964. Researchers commonly distinguish locations called Igbo Isaiah, Igbo Richard, and Igbo Jonah. Finds associated with them include metal objects, beads, pottery, and other materials, and they have been interpreted in relation to storage, burial, and ritual activities. Some finds came from archaeological contexts recorded in excavation; others had been recovered in earlier digging and collecting. Those distinctions matter because an object without a secure find context cannot answer the same questions as one documented in place.

The site's chronology is a continuing lesson in revision. Igbo-Ukwu has long been described as a ninth-century achievement, partly because of early radiocarbon results. Other researchers questioned the dating and pointed to conflicting evidence. Later analyses have refined the picture. A 2022 synthesis of renewed work reported high-precision dates suggesting the final use of the Igbo Isaiah context in the eleventh or twelfth century, while other recently investigated deposits extend across approximately the tenth to thirteenth centuries. That does not settle the dates of every object, every burial, or the span of each production practice. A date from one sample is not a birthday certificate for an entire artistic tradition.

The dating problem is not a reason to retreat to a vague story that everything happened somewhere in the distant past. It is a chance to ask better questions. What material was dated? Was it found with the object in question? Could an object have been treasured for years before being buried? Could a bead have been made elsewhere decades before reaching the compound? Chronology becomes stronger when these questions are explicit. A classroom label giving one century may be convenient, but a historian needs to distinguish manufacture, use, deposition, excavation, and modern laboratory measurement.

Beads are especially revealing. The excavated assemblages included large quantities of glass and carnelian beads. Chemical and isotopic research has identified varied raw materials and possible production connections, including glass associated with regions far beyond West Africa and beads made within African exchange networks. A bead's chemistry can illuminate the sources of its components, but it does not trace a single merchant's complete journey. Raw glass, finished beads, colorants, and tools can circulate separately. The result is evidence of long-distance connection without a surviving map of all the intermediaries who made that connection possible.

The metals raise a similar issue. Studies of composition and manufacturing technique can challenge older assumptions that sophisticated production must have been imported from elsewhere. Regional resources and locally developed skills deserve investigation on their own terms. Yet a result showing that particular metal came from a region does not identify every craftsman's place of training. Nor does an imported bead prove a direct voyage by an Igbo-Ukwu resident to the eastern Mediterranean. Historical networks are built through multiple exchanges, and the objects often preserve their results better than their pathways.

For a long time, writing about West African metalworking was shaped by comparisons with later and better-known centers such as Ilé-Ifẹ̀ and Benin. Those histories are important, but the Igbo-Ukwu objects are not preliminary attempts at another city's art. They have their own forms, technologies, and contexts. To arrange African artistic traditions as a ladder from crude to advanced is to impose a false developmental sequence. Technologies can be specialized for different purposes. A workshop capable of casting intricate ceremonial vessels need not make the same objects as a workshop producing figurative heads.

The local social setting remains difficult to reconstruct. Were certain objects regalia for a highly ranked person? Were they gathered for a shrine? Did particular households control access to rare materials? Some interpretations connect the finds with ritual authority and leadership, but there is not enough evidence to draw the complete social order of the region. The absence of a known palace does not disprove political hierarchy; an impressive burial does not prove an empire. We should resist allowing a few brilliant objects to stand in for the lives of farmers, potters, carriers, and people whose belongings did not survive.

The excavation's modern history also deserves attention. Colonial officials took part in collecting and moving objects, often before systematic documentation. More recent scholarship includes local accounts of how Anozie's discoveries were understood and handled by people living at Igbo-Ukwu. Those memories do not replace archaeological dating, but they answer questions the old institutional records may scarcely have asked: who encountered the finds, who possessed them, and how later authorities represented their recovery. The people of Igbo-Ukwu are part of the history of the archaeological project, not merely the descendants of its ancient subject.

A museum object presents another problem of scale. A beautifully photographed vessel can make a viewer imagine a whole city of equally ornate objects. In reality, scholars know only what has been excavated, collected, and preserved. Some pieces were removed before detailed contexts could be recorded. Many neighboring settlements have not been investigated with comparable intensity. The exceptional survival of an elaborate cache can exaggerate the apparent difference between one locality and another. A serious regional history requires ordinary pottery, settlement archaeology, and ecological evidence as well as the most spectacular metalwork.

What does Igbo-Ukwu teach us about the wider medieval world? Exchange routes and remarkable craft traditions developed across Africa independently of the courtly chronicles that dominate many global histories. Their existence does not depend on an Arab traveler having written them down, or a later European visitor having noticed them. Scientific analysis and excavation can establish parts of a story where no comparable narrative text survives. At the same time, those methods have limits: they cannot name every maker, establish every ritual meaning, or recover every transfer of ownership.

The objects beneath Isaiah Anozie's courtyard became famous for their workmanship. Their historical value lies also in the questions they refuse to answer too easily. Who organized specialized labor? Why were precious things deposited together? How did African production and distant exchange intersect? Why did early scholars assign one date, and why have later studies revised it? Those are not obstacles to history. They are the structure of a better history: one that gives local discovery its due, respects the skill evident in the objects, and treats uncertainty as a reason for more precise investigation rather than for a convenient legend.

## Faces from a City: Pavements, Craft, and Authority at Ilé-Ifẹ̀

West of Igbo-Ukwu, Ife offers a different context for exceptional metalwork and for arguments about political authority.

In the late 1930s, workers digging in a compound at Ife, in what is now southwestern Nigeria, encountered metal sculptures buried beneath the ground. Among them were heads cast with careful attention to eyes, lips, hair, and the surfaces of the face. Some bore elaborate headdresses. They did not come with labels telling us the names of those portrayed or the occasions on which they were used. But they belonged to a long urban history, and their discovery posed a question that goes beyond art: what kind of city sustained the makers, patrons, and audiences of these objects?

Ilé-Ifẹ̀ holds a central place in Yoruba religious, political, and historical traditions. Accounts of origins, divine actions, and royal ancestry make it a place of profound significance to communities today. Those traditions are historical evidence of how people understand authority, relationships, and beginnings, even when their sacred episodes cannot be dated like an excavated hearth. Archaeology asks complementary questions: where did people live, what did they make, how were spaces organized, and how did those arrangements change? The two kinds of evidence need not be forced into one timeline for either to matter.

Archaeological and art-historical syntheses describe settlement at Ife before the city's best-known period of sculptural production. An important transformation involved the paving of courtyards and passages with ceramic materials around the beginning of the second millennium of the Common Era. The Metropolitan Museum's historical essays call attention to this paving as a visible feature of urban growth. It took pottery production, fuel, transport, planning, and work to cover space in that way. Those materials also had a social role. A paved compound or pathway could distinguish a place, organize movement, and become part of a city's expectations about public and private life.

Between roughly the twelfth and fifteenth centuries, artists at Ife produced terracotta figures, stone works, and extraordinary metal sculpture. The word bronze is often used loosely for the metal heads, but individual objects may be made of brass or other copper alloys. The celebrated head in the British Museum is a brass casting, probably dating to the fourteenth or early fifteenth century. Its headdress suggests status, and it is commonly associated with royal imagery, but the museum notes that its original identification and use remain uncertain. A highly accomplished face is not by itself a portrait of a ruler whose biography we can reconstruct.

How does such a head get made? In lost-wax casting, a model is formed in wax, invested in a mold, and replaced by molten metal. Finishing the cast requires knowledge of heat, materials, and the properties of alloys. The process also depends on fuel, mining or metal supply, transport, and people whose skills may never appear in a ruler's story. Terracotta required other skills of shaping, firing, and finishing. Traditions of work were transmitted through families and workshops over time. We cannot identify the precise makers of most surviving pieces, or assume that later gender divisions of craft labor describe every workshop in medieval Ife.

The sculptures were found through different histories of recovery. The famous group uncovered at Wunmonije Compound in 1938 was discovered during construction, not through a carefully recorded excavation that followed a modern research plan from its first moment. Some other objects have strong contexts; others entered collections under circumstances that limit what can be said about their original positions. A head in a museum can be described precisely by material, dimensions, and surface treatment. Reconstructing where it once stood, the rituals around it, or who had access to it requires evidence that may have been disturbed or lost.

The city was not simply an artistic workshop serving a palace. Research on Yoruba urbanism considers Ife as a network of communities, craft areas, courtyards, markets, and sacred locations. Akinwumi Ogundiran's work emphasizes the interwoven social domains through which early Yoruba urban life was organized. An urban center can be politically powerful without all its residents living in one dense, uniform block. Neighborhoods could have different functions and forms of authority. Even a city famous for its kings needed households, food supplies, potters, and people who moved goods along regional routes.

Trade mattered, but not every bead or metal object reveals its full journey. Ife was connected to exchange systems spanning forest, savanna, river, and coast. Glass beads are particularly useful to archaeologists because their production, chemistry, and distribution can be studied. They also reveal local manufacture and technical expertise that cannot be reduced to imported luxury goods. A market's social importance includes traders who dealt in foods and everyday needs, not just distant prestige materials. The evidence for exchange routes is often stronger than the evidence for the individual people who negotiated prices along them.

Newer studies add crops to the picture. Archaeobotanical research has reported evidence of wheat and cotton from medieval Ilé-Ifẹ̀, prompting questions about foodways, textile production, and connectivity. Finding plant remains in a city does not tell us how every resident dressed or what a typical meal contained. A few identifiable traces can nevertheless change an account focused too heavily on sculpture. Cotton can suggest processing, weaving, trade, or the movement of raw materials; the relevant interpretation depends on context. Urban history has to bring cultivation and craft into the same frame as religious and political institutions.

The history of how outsiders received Ife art also matters. When these works became known in Europe, some observers refused to credit African artists with their production. They proposed outside origins, even fantastic lost civilizations. Such claims were not deductions forced on scholars by the objects. They expressed assumptions about African history that could be challenged by archaeology and local historical knowledge. The sculptures emerged from a Nigerian cultural and technical world, with their own artistic conventions. Recognizing their achievement does not require comparing them with Greek or Roman sculpture as a standard of value.

There is a more subtle danger in treating the sculptures as timeless symbols of an entire people. Yoruba languages, communities, and institutions have histories; their relationships changed over centuries. Ife's significance to later kingdoms and to contemporary Yoruba life is real, but it does not mean that every custom has remained identical since the twelfth century. Likewise, the object labeled a king's head in a display case may have had meanings we no longer understand. The uncertain parts of the story are not blank checks for invention. They are invitations to ask better questions and to listen to specialist and community interpretations.

The buried heads survived; many workshops, houses, and ordinary objects did not. The contrast can make elite and ceremonial life seem to be the whole city. Yet a bronze head depends on a landscape of labor, skills, transport, and exchange that exceeds what the artifact directly shows. Paved courtyards depended on people making and setting ceramic material. Food came from fields and markets. Sacred and political traditions gave social meaning to spaces that could also contain children at play, neighbors trading, and artisans learning their work. Those ordinary lives are harder to recover, but they belong to the same history.

Ilé-Ifẹ̀ is therefore more than the answer to a question about where a famous sculpture was made. It is a history of urban growth, craft knowledge, social imagination, and changing authority in West Africa. The faces are striking because they bring individuals close to us while withholding their names. We can honor that closeness without pretending to know their thoughts. What we can document is no less important: a city that made sophisticated art and sustained the social worlds in which such art had a place.

## Before the Brass Plaques: The Edo Kingdom and Benin City

The later history of Benin requires another change of place and evidence. The famous objects are part of a kingdom’s history, not a substitute for it.

The name Benin can confuse a modern map. The historical Kingdom of Benin was centered on Benin City, in what is now Edo State in Nigeria, not in the present-day country called Benin to the west. Its rulers carried the title oba, and its people developed political and artistic traditions that long preceded European voyages to the coast. The city stood within a landscape of forests, farms, paths, and communities connected through trade. If we look only at the celebrated metal plaques collected in European museums, we see the kingdom through objects often made after 1500. To understand its earlier history, we have to ask what other evidence survives.

Edo oral traditions recount an earlier line of rulers known as the Ogiso and a transition to an oba dynasty associated with Oranmiyan and connections to Ife. These traditions preserve important understandings of legitimacy and origins. They do not function as a dated list of contemporary inscriptions. Modern scholarship compares them with archaeology and the changing record of court art. National Museums Scotland describes the current dynasty as ruling from at least the thirteenth century, while also identifying the foundation traditions as oral history. Keeping those categories distinct allows us to take historical memory seriously without claiming that every event can be dated to a precise year.

Benin's urban history was shaped by its environment. The forest supplied wood, fibers, oil, and products used in households and exchange. Nearby lands produced food crops. Roads connected the capital with communities whose cooperation, taxes, and labor mattered to rulers. Political authority could be expressed in a palace, but it also depended on resources moving toward that palace and on the relationships among titled officers, households, and towns. A city in a forest is no less organized than a city on a dry river plain. Its archaeological traces differ because materials and environmental conditions differ.

Earthworks and channels associated with Benin City belong to a long history of organizing space. People built embankments and ditches that could mark boundaries and affect movement, security, and access. The scale of some systems reflects coordinated labor across generations. But an earthwork is not automatically a defensive wall in the modern military sense; its meaning can change from one section to another. Exact dates and functions require archaeological study, and a line visible in later maps cannot be confidently assigned to a single early oba without evidence. The landscape was built and rebuilt, not completed by one royal command.

Court craft was central to political life. Artists worked in materials including wood, ivory, and metals. Some specialized activities were organized through guilds whose relationships with the palace were part of the kingdom's institutional structure. Objects could honor rulers and ancestors, display status, or serve in ceremonial practices. The better-known brass plaques and many celebrated portrait heads are difficult to use as straightforward illustrations of the world before 1500 because large portions of the surviving corpus date from later centuries. They illuminate the enduring development of court traditions, not a camera-like view of the kingdom's earliest rulers.

Trade mattered before direct contact with Portugal in the late fifteenth century. Cloth, pepper, ivory, salt, and other goods moved through regional networks. The arrival of Portuguese merchants altered the range of imports and the objects that court specialists could acquire, including metals and cloth. But exchange did not begin with Europeans. To describe the kingdom as if it became commercial only when outsiders appeared would erase connections already supported by the geography of West African towns. The better historical question is how new maritime partners entered established political and economic relationships and how Edo rulers sought to manage those connections.

The royal court was an institution with rituals, offices, and interests, not simply the residence of one person. Genealogical traditions linked rulers across generations, while ceremonies helped make authority visible to participants. Palace artisans could create objects that communicated a particular political order. Yet ceremonial images leave out conflict, negotiation, and the everyday labor that supplied the court. We need to distinguish the intended meaning of an artwork from the full range of social relations around it. An altar or ivory carving might express royal continuity even during a period when power was contested outside the palace.

Families outside the court also had histories. Agriculture, markets, fishing, gathering forest products, and craft production supported urban life. Women participated in economies and households whose tasks rarely appear in royal imagery. If we tell the history only through named obas, the kingdom will seem to have operated without food producers, local officials, children, or people who transported goods. Archaeology and oral traditions can restore some of that world, although neither yields a complete household census. The point is to treat the capital as a place inhabited by a society, not merely the stage for a succession of kings.

Political expansion during the fifteenth century changed relationships with neighboring regions. Accounts of Benin's authority differ by period and by source. A later tradition of conquest might record an important political claim while compressing many smaller agreements and struggles into one episode. Boundaries cannot be drawn as sharply as those of modern states. Authority could be exercised through allies, military action, tribute, markets, and local offices, with a different reach in each context. An expanding kingdom is best understood as a set of relationships constantly maintained rather than an unchanging colored shape on a historical map.

There is also a modern history inside every surviving court object. British troops looted the royal palace in 1897, and artworks were removed into European and other collections. This displacement affects which objects researchers can compare, which communities can visit or study them, and how museum labels present their significance. A catalogue entry may record the material and an approximate century while saying little about the ritual setting from which an object was taken. That loss of context is a limitation of historical evidence, as well as a matter with continuing cultural and political consequences. The objects remain part of Edo history, not only of museum history.

The strongest accounts therefore combine several lines of evidence. Oral histories preserve names, relationships, and understandings of legitimacy; archaeology reveals changes in built space and material production; early external documents offer another perspective as contact increased. Each source has its own chronology. A later European description may document a custom observed at that time, but it cannot by itself certify that the same custom existed in exactly the same form in the thirteenth century. The same caution applies to later court art. History becomes more credible when sources are allowed to disagree or to speak at different scales.

Before 1500, the Edo kingdom was already a developing political and urban society. Its rulers claimed authority, its artisans made prestige objects, its people organized food and trade, and its capital occupied a carefully shaped landscape. The arrival of European traders is one event in that story, not the event that gave it a beginning. Benin's earlier centuries remind us why African history requires local chronology and evidence, and why the most photographed object in a museum is not always the best starting point for understanding a society. The city existed before the surviving displays through which many people now first encounter it.
