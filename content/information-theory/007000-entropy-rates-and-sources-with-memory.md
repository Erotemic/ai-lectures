# Entropy Rates and Sources with Memory

**Track:** Information Theory: From Questions to Communication  
**Lecture:** 007000  
**Preparation:** Entropy, conditional probability, the chain rule, and finite-state Markov chains.  
**Companion:** Stationary entropy rates, Markov calculations, and predictive coding.

Suppose a sensor sends a zero or a one every second. Inspect its long record and you find approximately half zeros and half ones. Would you conclude that the data contain one bit of uncertainty per second, the maximum possible for a binary measurement? If the readings were independent fair coin tosses, yes. But imagine the sensor nearly always repeats its previous reading. It stays at zero for a while, then switches to one, stays at one for a while, then switches back. Over a long period it may still produce equal numbers of zeros and ones. Yet someone who knows the previous reading can predict the next with high accuracy.

A distribution over individual symbols does not describe a sequence. The entropy of one reading and the entropy *rate* of the whole process can be very different. The distinction connects information theory to dynamical systems, time series, stochastic processes, predictive compression, and the mathematics of language models.

We can calculate the difference exactly in a simple case. Begin with a fair random bit, zero or one. At each subsequent time step, keep the previous bit with probability nine tenths and flip it with probability one tenth. Each choice to switch or stay is independent of the earlier choices. Because the initial bit is fair and the switching rule is symmetric, every reading remains fair. If we look at any one time in isolation, its entropy is exactly one bit.

But if we know the previous reading, then the next reading has probability nine tenths of agreeing and one tenth of disagreeing. The uncertainty about the next reading is the entropy of that switching choice, about point four six nine bits. We can describe a long record by sending its first bit and then describing whether each successive step switched. Most steps are "no switch," so the switch sequence can be encoded more efficiently than the original raw bits.

Let's do the block calculation. For a sequence of length n, the first reading requires one bit of entropy. Every transition afterward contributes the binary entropy of one tenth, because, conditional on the preceding value, the next step is a fresh switch or stay decision. The chain rule of entropy adds these conditional uncertainties. The exact block entropy is one plus n minus one times the binary entropy of one tenth.

For ten readings, that is about five point two two bits of entropy, rather than ten. The per-reading average is about point five two two. As n grows, the one-time initial bit contributes less and less per reading. The average converges to point four six nine bits per reading. We call that limit the entropy rate.

This calculation gives an operational compression idea immediately. Encode the first bit literally. Encode the subsequent switching indicators using a lossless block code designed for an independent Bernoulli source with one-tenth probability of a switch. If we use large blocks, the expected rate approaches the entropy of the switching process, plus a vanishing overhead for the first bit. This code does not require the decoder to guess the original readings from scratch: once it knows the first bit and the switch sequence, it reconstructs every later reading exactly.

The process is a two-state Markov chain. A Markov chain is defined by the rule that, given its current state, the next state does not depend further on the earlier history. That definition is about conditional independence, not about observations becoming independent. Our sensor has long stretches of repeated bits, so consecutive readings are strongly dependent even though the *transitions* can be generated by independent coin flips.

We now want to understand the general entropy-rate definition. For an arbitrary sequence of random variables, the entropy of the first n observations can be broken into a sum: uncertainty about the first, then uncertainty about the second given the first, then uncertainty about the third given both previous observations, and so on. Every new observation contributes only the uncertainty left after the past is known.

Divide the joint entropy of the n observations by n. If the result approaches a limit, that limit is the average information produced per symbol by a long block. For a stationary finite-alphabet process, this limit exists. Stationarity means that shifting the time indices does not change the joint distribution of any finite window. It is stronger than saying that the individual marginal distributions happen to be equal.

There is a second way to calculate the rate for a stationary process. Consider the uncertainty of the next symbol when we know longer and longer stretches of its past. Conditioning on more information cannot increase entropy. By stationarity we can align all those conditional entropies at a common observation time. The sequence of conditional uncertainties decreases toward a limit. The joint entropy per symbol is the average of those conditional uncertainties, and an average of a convergent sequence approaches the same limit. Thus the block-entropy definition and the limiting one-step prediction definition agree.

For a first-order stationary Markov chain, conditioning on the entire past is no better than conditioning on the immediately preceding state. The entropy rate therefore becomes the average, under the stationary distribution, of the uncertainty in one transition. For our symmetric sensor, both states have the same switch probability, and we recover the binary entropy of one tenth.

Not every two-state chain is symmetric. Suppose that from zero the probability of moving to one is one tenth, while from one the probability of moving to zero is two tenths. The source will spend more time in zero, because escapes from one are more frequent. In equilibrium, the fraction of time at zero is two thirds and the fraction at one is one third. Conditional on zero, the next-step entropy is the binary entropy of one tenth. Conditional on one, it is the binary entropy of two tenths. The entropy rate is the weighted average of those two uncertainties, about point five five three bits per symbol.

That calculation is a compact demonstration that entropy rate is not simply the entropy of the stationary distribution. In the asymmetric chain, the stationary one-symbol entropy is the binary entropy of one third, about point nine one eight bits. That quantity says how hard it is to identify a state seen at an arbitrary time when no preceding readings are available. The entropy rate, about point five five three, says how much *new* uncertainty each additional state contributes when the observer has the past.

We can push the distinction to an extreme. Start with a fair bit, then alternate deterministically forever: zero, one, zero, one, or the reverse. Every individual time has a fair marginal distribution, so its entropy is one bit. But after the first reading, all subsequent readings are certain. A block of any length has only one bit of entropy, the bit that selects the phase. Its entropy rate is zero. The process has uncertainty about which of two trajectories is realized, but no continuing production of new uncertainty.

There is a further subtlety. The marginal distributions can remain the same even when the process is not stationary; shifting time might change joint relationships. And even stationarity alone does not mean every long observed record looks like one representative pattern from one simple typical set. Stronger hypotheses such as ergodicity are needed for the most familiar almost-sure asymptotic equipartition results for dependent sources. The stationary finite-alphabet entropy-rate limit is easier to establish than the full typical-sequence theorem for general dependent processes.

That distinction helps us understand what a lossless code should learn. A simple symbol-by-symbol code based only on the marginal distribution will spend roughly one bit per reading on our symmetric switching sensor. A predictor that has access to the previous reading can assign probability nine tenths to a repeat and one tenth to a switch. An ideal arithmetic coder, driven by that conditional prediction, can approach the entropy rate instead. It is the conditional probability model that reveals the redundancy.

What if the predictor is wrong? Suppose it insists that each next bit is fair even after observing a long run of identical readings. Its expected log loss is one bit per step. The true conditional entropy is about point four six nine. The difference, about point five three one bits per step, is the extra expected coding cost of the wrong prediction. The same gap can be expressed as an average conditional Kullback-Leibler divergence between the true predictive distribution and the model's forecast.

This is a direct bridge to machine learning. Training a probabilistic sequence model by minimizing negative log likelihood means asking it to assign high probability to the observations that actually occur, given their contexts. If the model matches the true conditional distribution, its expected log loss reaches the conditional entropy. If the model misses predictable dependencies, it pays an extra divergence cost. That does not mean low log loss automatically confers understanding or usefulness; it means the model has learned to predict a chosen data source under a chosen statistical criterion.

Our one-step Markov chain is simple because the previous observed bit is the full relevant state. Many real sensors, documents, and biological sequences do not have that property. In a hidden Markov model, for example, an unseen state evolves with a Markov rule while observations are generated noisily from that hidden state. The observed process generally is not first-order Markov. Knowing only the previous observation may leave predictive information unused. Computing its entropy rate requires reasoning about a distribution over hidden states conditioned on the observed history, rather than blindly substituting a single transition matrix into the first-order formula.

There is a more general lesson about models and compression. An independent model can be perfectly correct about the fraction of zeros and ones while being very wrong about what happens next. A first-order Markov model can correctly describe local transitions while missing longer-range dependencies. A model that predicts well under one distribution may perform poorly when the process changes. The entropy rate is a property of the actual joint distribution; the realized bit rate of a compressor also depends on the quality and cost of the model used to encode it.

Return to the first sensor. Equal numbers of zeros and ones did not tell us whether the record was a stream of fresh coin flips or a series of long constant stretches. We needed the dependence across time. The chain rule let us quantify that dependence, stationarity gave us a long-run limit, and the Markov property made the calculation concrete. Information in a sequence is not the sum of isolated surprises unless the observations are independent. It is the accumulation of what remains unpredictable after everything already observed has been taken into account.

## Mathematical companion (not spoken)

For any finite-alphabet process,
\[
H(X_1^n)=\sum_{i=1}^n H(X_i\mid X_1^{i-1}).
\]
For a stationary finite-alphabet process, define \(a_n=H(X_n\mid X_1^{n-1})\). Stationarity and conditioning imply \(a_{n+1}\le a_n\), so \(a_n\downarrow h\ge0\), and Cesaro averaging gives
\[
\overline H=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac1nH(X_1^n)=\lim_{n\to\infty}H(X_n\mid X_1^{n-1})=h.
\]
For a stationary first-order Markov chain with transition matrix \(P\) and stationary distribution \(\pi\),
\[
\overline H=H(X_2\mid X_1)=-\sum_i\pi_i\sum_jP_{ij}\log_2P_{ij}.
\]
For a symmetric two-state chain with switch probability \(a\) and stationary fair state,
\[
H(X_1^n)=1+(n-1)h_2(a),\qquad \overline H=h_2(a).
\]
At \(a=0.1\), \(\overline H\approx0.468996\) bits per reading, while \(H(X_i)=1\) for each time.

For \(P(0\to1)=a=0.1\), \(P(1\to0)=b=0.2\), the stationary distribution is \(\pi_0=b/(a+b)=2/3\) and \(\pi_1=a/(a+b)=1/3\). Hence
\[
\overline H=\tfrac23h_2(0.1)+\tfrac13h_2(0.2)\approx0.553307,
\quad H(X_i)=h_2(1/3)\approx0.918296.
\]
For a conditional predictive model \(q(x\mid h)\) and true conditional law \(p(x\mid h)\),
\[
\mathbb E_{x\sim p(\cdot\mid h)}[-\log_2q(x\mid h)]
=H(p(\cdot\mid h))+D_2(p(\cdot\mid h)\Vert q(\cdot\mid h)).
\]
Averaging over histories gives the expected per-step log loss. In the idealized predictive-coding model it also gives the expected per-step code length up to implementation and finite-block overhead.

### Exercises and solutions

1. **First block.** For a stationary symmetric chain with switch probability 0.1, compute \(H(X_1,X_2,X_3)\). **Solution:** \(1+2h_2(0.1)\approx1.938\) bits.
2. **The alternating process.** Why is the entropy rate zero even though every bit is marginally fair? **Solution:** one initial random phase determines the entire future, so \(H(X_1^n)=1\) for all \(n\).
3. **Asymmetric stationary weights.** If \(P(0\to1)=0.1\) and \(P(1\to0)=0.2\), what is the stationary fraction of zeros? **Solution:** two thirds.
4. **Predictor mismatch.** Why is a fair-coin predictor costly for the symmetric 0.1-switch chain? **Solution:** it spends one bit per transition, versus entropy rate \(h_2(0.1)\approx0.469\), for an excess about 0.531 bits.
