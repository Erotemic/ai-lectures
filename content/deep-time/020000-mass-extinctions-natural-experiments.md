# Mass Extinctions as Natural Experiments

Open a rock core from the seafloor and move upward through its layers. At one depth, tiny shells of plankton are abundant and diverse. In a thin interval they become scarce; higher in the core, different forms dominate. Something changed in the oceans when those sediments accumulated. The evidence is a sequence of physical specimens, measured depths, chemical compositions and ages. The challenge is to reconstruct what happened to living populations across the planet, and what caused the change.

Paleontologists describe a mass extinction when an exceptionally large loss of species occurs across many groups and habitats during a geologically short interval. The fossil record includes a conventional set of five major Phanerozoic mass extinctions, but their causes, severity, durations and biological effects differ. The familiar list is a useful index rather than one type of event repeated five times.

These crises can function as natural experiments because different organisms, environments and regions were exposed to major disturbances. Yet there was no controlled experimenter to hold other variables fixed. Climate, sea level, volcanism, ocean circulation and ecosystems can change together. A convincing reconstruction therefore requires more than finding an extinction and an environmental anomaly close to the same date.

## How can a fossil record show extinction?

Start with the observable. A fossil is assigned to a taxon and located within a geological section. Geologists establish the sequence of strata, correlate sections in different regions, and estimate ages using radiometric measurements or other time markers. If a group disappears from many well-sampled sections and fails to reappear in younger rocks, that supports the inference that its lineage ended.

But a species can become rare, migrate out of a sampled region, change diagnostic traits or fail to fossilize. The final observed occurrence is generally a minimum bound on its true time of disappearance. Statistical methods attempt to estimate how much earlier or later an actual extinction may have occurred given the sampling pattern. The severity of a biological crisis depends on which taxonomic level is counted, how fossil taxa are identified, and how much rock of each interval survives.

A reduction in the number of named fossil genera is not automatically the same as a reduction in the number of living species. Some groups preserve better than others; taxonomic categories can be split or combined differently; sea-level changes can alter how many sedimentary environments are exposed. Global databases and sampling-standardization methods help, but they do not recreate every extinct population. Even the widely quoted percentage of species lost in an extinction event carries assumptions and uncertainties.

To infer a cause, we ask for a chain of evidence: a candidate forcing, its timing and geographic reach, a physical or chemical pathway from forcing to environmental stress, and a biological pattern consistent with those stresses. Ideally independent observations constrain each link. A coincidence in time is a starting point for investigation, not a causal verdict.

## A case with an impact layer

At the boundary between Cretaceous and Paleogene rocks, approximately sixty-six million years ago, many groups suffered abrupt losses. Non-avian dinosaurs disappeared, along with ammonites and many species of marine plankton. Birds, which are living dinosaurs, survived, as did some mammals, reptiles and other groups. Saying 'dinosaurs went extinct' without the avian qualification obscures the actual evolutionary history.

In 1980 Luis Alvarez, Walter Alvarez, Frank Asaro and Helen Michel reported a striking enrichment of iridium in thin boundary layers from several distant marine sites. Iridium is relatively scarce near Earth's surface and more abundant in many meteorites. The team proposed an asteroid impact as a source of the material and as a mechanism capable of disrupting the biosphere. What they measured was a chemical anomaly in dated sediment. The impact was then a hypothesis with predicted consequences.

Further observations provided an increasingly specific case: shocked minerals, impact spherules, a huge buried crater centered near Chicxulub on the Yucatán Peninsula, and sedimentary deposits produced by violent disturbance. Drilling into the crater connected its geology to the global boundary layer. A 2026 synthesis of the international crater-drilling expedition reports the iridium anomaly within the impact structure and multiple lines of evidence for the formation and aftermath of the crater.

This is a strong historical causal inference. The timing and global impact markers make the Chicxulub collision a central explanation for the end-Cretaceous crisis. The mechanisms still deserve separation. Material expelled into the atmosphere could reduce incoming sunlight, interrupt photosynthesis, and alter temperatures and food chains. Sulfur-bearing rocks at the impact site may have contributed sulfate aerosols; soot and dust also mattered. Estimates of the duration and importance of each mechanism depend on atmospheric modeling, source material, particle size and environmental feedbacks.

At the same time, the Deccan Traps in India were undergoing major volcanism around the same boundary. Their gases and eruptions affected climate and biogeochemical cycles. Researchers debate the magnitude and timing of the resulting stresses relative to the impact, using high-precision dates, soil horizons, isotopes and climate models. The available evidence strongly favors the impact as the decisive immediate trigger of the sharp boundary crisis, while the contribution of volcanic stresses to the broader background remains an active question. A multiple-cause history should be evaluated against the timing rather than assumed automatically.

## A very different catastrophe

Move much farther back, to the end of the Permian Period around two hundred and fifty-two million years ago. The marine fossil record shows enormous losses among invertebrate groups, with severe changes in terrestrial environments as well. This crisis is commonly judged the most severe of the five major Phanerozoic extinctions. Its biological recovery took place over a long and uneven interval, with ecological communities reorganizing rather than immediately returning to their previous form.

The leading driver is extensive magmatism in the Siberian Traps, a vast igneous province in what is now northern Asia. Eruptions and intrusions released gases and interacted with sedimentary rocks capable of supplying additional carbon and other compounds. Proposed pathways include intense greenhouse warming, disturbances to the carbon cycle, reduced ocean oxygen, ocean acidification and ecological feedbacks. These effects are not one instantaneous blow. Their intensity and timing could vary across many episodes and environments.

An important observational clue comes from chemical changes preserved in marine sediments. Carbon-isotope shifts can indicate large changes in carbon cycling, although several processes can create similar signals. Temperature proxies constrain ocean warming while depending on assumptions about seawater chemistry and preservation. Mercury anomalies may reflect volcanic inputs but also depend on transport, organic matter and deposition. In June 2026 researchers reported coupled mercury-isotope patterns across several Permian–Triassic sections that they interpreted as evidence for distinct eruptive and environmental crisis intervals. It is an informative new result, yet the geochemical pattern still requires a process model to connect it with volcanism and biological losses.

A 2026 review of marine recovery after the Permian–Triassic crisis describes a prolonged transition to new ecosystems. Recovery is not simply the reversal of extinction. Primary producers, herbivores, predators and habitat engineers may reassemble at different rates. The environments themselves have changed, and surviving species interact under conditions their extinct predecessors never experienced. Measuring recovery therefore requires deciding whether we mean species richness, trophic complexity, biomass, geographic extent or the reappearance of particular ecological functions.

## Why the other crises cannot be skipped

The end-Ordovician extinction, about four hundred and forty-four million years ago, is closely connected in leading reconstructions to glaciation, changing sea level and subsequent environmental stress, including oxygen changes in marine settings. Many animal groups then lived in the oceans, so marine fossil records dominate our view. The Late Devonian losses were extended and complex; changes in marine oxygenation, nutrient cycling, plants on land, climate and volcanism have all been implicated, with evidence differing across particular pulses. Near the end of the Triassic, around two hundred and one million years ago, massive magmatism associated with the Central Atlantic Magmatic Province is a major candidate forcing.

Those abbreviated cases illustrate a methodological trap. An asteroid impact can cause extinction, and large volcanic provinces can be associated with extinction, but neither is a universal explanation for every crisis. It is necessary to ask what happened in a particular interval. If a proposed trigger precedes the biological losses by too long or if the supposed environmental pathway fails in the relevant habitat, the hypothesis needs revision. Different extinction episodes can have different physical causes and different responses to similar stresses.

The climate implications also need precision. A temperature change that eliminates one community may be survivable for another. Warming can alter ocean stratification and circulation, which can affect oxygen supply, nutrient transport and the habitats available to animals. Ocean acidification can disproportionately affect some organisms that build mineralized shells. Food-web collapse can affect predators far removed from the initial physical disturbance. These are testable mechanistic ideas when combined with fossils, physiological evidence, geochemical proxies and models.

## Can we measure how selective an extinction was?

Suppose two fossil groups inhabit the same area before a boundary. Group A consists of surface-dwelling plankton dependent on sunlight-driven food production. Group B lives deeper in sediments and uses more varied food sources. If the first group collapses more sharply, that may support a scenario involving diminished surface productivity. But we also need to ask whether their shells fossilize equally well, whether they were sampled in the same environments, and whether their classifications remained comparable over time.

Paleontologists reconstruct selectivity using survival rates across many groups with different traits. Those comparisons can discriminate between candidate stresses, though correlations between traits complicate attribution. Large body size, habitat range and food source may be correlated rather than independent causes. Evolutionary history also matters: related lineages share many traits, so treating every species as an independent experimental unit can exaggerate confidence.

Another important distinction is the scale of dating. Sedimentary layers can preserve events that appear simultaneous when compressed into a geological time bin, but may have unfolded over thousands of years or longer. Radiometric dates have measurement uncertainty and geological uncertainties, including which mineral actually records the event. A causal order inferred from two overlapping dates may be much weaker than a dramatic chart suggests.

## Extinction and the limits of inference

We can now assign different levels of confidence without reducing history to a single percentage. We have very strong evidence for the end-Cretaceous impact and for a broad crisis at its boundary. The specific sequence from impact to atmospheric and ecological effects is supported but still refined by modeling and new deposits. We have strong evidence that Siberian Traps magmatism played a central role in the end-Permian crisis, while detailed feedbacks, local timing and recovery dynamics remain less certain. Other crises have their own histories and mixed constraints.

The fossil record also reminds us that survival is contingent. The forms that dominate after an extinction are the descendants of particular surviving populations, shaped by geography, biology and chance. Some future lineages flourish because competitors vanish, ecological roles become available, or conditions favor traits they already possessed. None of those outcomes was guaranteed.

Mass extinctions are natural experiments only in a qualified sense: they reveal how ecosystems responded to stresses that we can partly reconstruct. The sediments are observations. Their ages are calibrated. The forcing mechanisms are inferred from several physical traces. The long-term ecological consequences can sometimes be followed through millions of years of deposits. We have enough evidence to reject many simple stories, while accepting that we cannot replay the ancient oceans with every variable held fixed.

## Companion: A causal chain for a mass-extinction hypothesis

| Case | Observables | Strong inference | Active uncertainty |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| End-Cretaceous ~66 Ma | Global iridium horizon, shocked minerals, Chicxulub crater, fossil turnover | Impact central to abrupt K–Pg crisis | Relative roles and durations of dust, soot, sulfate, pre-existing volcanic climate stress |
| End-Permian ~252 Ma | Massive Siberian magmatism, isotopic excursions, temperatures, fossil losses | Volcanic forcing closely tied to severe biotic crisis | Carbon release pathways, spatial synchrony, marine feedbacks and recovery |
| End-Triassic ~201 Ma | Fossil turnover, CAMP magmatism, geochemical change | Strong volcanic-climate link | Precise phasing and ecological selectivity |
| Ordovician and Devonian crises | Fossil losses, sea-level/climate proxies, marine geochemistry | Major environmental stress and multiple crisis intervals | Specific causal contributions, duration and local versus global patterns |

**Extinction-rate caution:** A sample loss fraction \(f_{\mathrm{obs}}=(N_{\mathrm{before}}-N_{\mathrm{after}})/N_{\mathrm{before}}\) is a descriptive statistic for a sampled assemblage, not an unbiased estimate of the global extinction probability. Taxon definitions, rock availability, duration and the detection process alter the measurement.

**Causal test:** Compare (1) source forcing; (2) age relationship; (3) physical stress mechanism; (4) organismal exposure; (5) predicted biological selectivity. Failure at any link weakens the proposed chain.

### Questions for study (five-second pause each)

1. What did the Alvarez team directly measure in 1980? **Answer:** Iridium enrichment in boundary sediments, later interpreted in an impact hypothesis.
2. Why is an extinction's last observed fossil different from its exact extinction time? **Answer:** Fossil preservation and sampling are incomplete.
3. Why is the end-Permian crisis best studied separately from the end-Cretaceous crisis? **Answer:** They have distinct forcing histories, geochemical signals, ecological responses and timescales.
4. What additional evidence would help connect volcanic gases to a particular fossil loss? **Answer:** Independently dated emissions and environmental proxies, a plausible stress mechanism, and trait or habitat patterns consistent with that stress.
