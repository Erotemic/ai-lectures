# How the Moon Was Made: Samples, Impacts, and Competing Origins

On a clear night, the Moon looks like an object whose origin should be straightforward. It is large, nearby, and familiar. We know where it is to extraordinary precision, have watched it move for centuries, and have brought pieces of its surface into laboratories. Yet none of these observations is a recording of the event that created it. To infer how it formed, scientists have to make a history out of fragments: the chemistry of stones, the geometry of an orbit, the distribution of cratered highlands, and a few physical rules about colliding planets.

Consider the difference between two questions. What is the Moon made of? And what happened, over the course of hours or years, that produced it? The first has direct laboratory answers, although our samples are limited. The second must be reconstructed by finding processes that reproduce many independent features at once. A sufficiently imaginative collision simulation can produce something that looks like a moon. That does not make the simulated collision an observation. What makes an origin hypothesis useful is its obligation to explain measurements that were not built into its story.

## What Apollo put on the table

Before lunar samples were returned, scientists seriously considered several origins. Earth might have captured a body that formed elsewhere. Earth and Moon might have condensed together from nearby material. Or a fast-spinning Earth might have shed material into orbit. Each story needed a way of producing the present orbit and the Moon's particular bulk composition. The Apollo missions changed the comparison by replacing distant guesses about mineral composition with laboratory analyses of actual rocks.

Apollo brought back hundreds of kilograms of material from several landing sites. The sites are not a random sample of the whole Moon. Impact gardening, later volcanism, and the history of where spacecraft could land all matter. Even so, the rocks establish several things with force. The Moon experienced extensive melting early in its history. Much of the highlands is rich in anorthosite, a rock associated with the separation of buoyant minerals from a deep magma ocean. Lunar material is relatively depleted in elements that readily volatilize under intense heating. And the isotopic compositions of lunar and terrestrial rocks are strikingly similar for several elements.

These are not four independent photographs of one collision. They are constraints. Extensive melting points toward high energy or a different process capable of melting much of a young body. Volatile depletion points toward high-temperature processing, but can also have contributions from the source material and subsequent history. Similar isotopes point toward shared reservoirs or extensive mixing, but do not by themselves specify whether the mixing happened before or during the Moon-forming event. A good model must confront all the constraints together.

One particularly informative comparison involves oxygen isotopes. Oxygen atoms can have different numbers of neutrons. Their relative abundances vary among meteorite groups, encoding differences among reservoirs in the early Solar System. Lunar and terrestrial rocks occupy remarkably similar positions in these comparisons. If a giant impactor arrived from a compositionally distinct orbit and contributed most of the Moon, why does the Moon resemble Earth so closely? That puzzle has guided decades of dynamical simulations and laboratory work.

The result should not be phrased as 'Apollo proved a particular impact.' Apollo ruled against several simple older histories and strongly supported a violent, hot origin. Exactly how the violent event happened is still a modeling question. Even the composition of the impactor is not preserved as a labeled specimen marked with its name.

## Building a moon in a numerical experiment

A commonly taught hypothesis begins with a roughly Mars-sized body, conventionally called Theia, striking the growing Earth. The collision throws hot material into orbit. Some debris falls back; some escapes; some gathers into the Moon. The final configuration must possess the right angular momentum and leave Earth and Moon with plausible compositions.

Imagine doing this with only two conservation rules. First, the total angular momentum of an isolated Earth-impact system is approximately conserved. If a collision sends mass outward into orbit, it must redistribute rotational and orbital motion. Second, energy changes form. Kinetic energy can become heat, shock waves, vaporization, melting, and gravitational binding energy. Conserving total energy does not mean the collision remains cold or mechanically tidy.

These rules exclude many cinematic impact pictures. Material has to be placed into suitable orbits, not just thrown upward. A large splash of rock may look impressive in a simulation but escape entirely or return to Earth. A collision that builds the right-sized Moon may also leave Earth spinning too fast, or make the Moon disproportionately from material unlike Earth's mantle. Adding constraints can make the once-attractive single collision much harder to reproduce.

Simulations represent gravity and hot rock with approximations. Rock may be modeled as a fluid with an equation of state; particles or cells exchange mass, energy and momentum; cooling and condensation may be followed with further models. Results can depend on resolution, assumed material properties, collision angle, and the initial bodies. A simulation can test whether a history is physically feasible under those assumptions. It cannot recover which exact trajectory actually occurred without independent evidence to identify that trajectory.

Different giant-impact versions try to solve different parts of this problem. Some hypothesize high-energy collisions that mix target and impactor material. Others explore unusually rapid initial rotation, complicated sequences of impacts, or impact remnants that evolve dynamically before making a Moon. The existence of several viable numerical pathways is itself a clue: the surviving constraints may identify a family of histories more confidently than one uniquely reconstructed event.

## An orbit that has changed since formation

Today's orbit is another constraint, but it is not a preserved copy of the orbit on the first day. The Moon gradually moves outward as tides transfer angular momentum from Earth's rotation to lunar orbital motion. Laser ranging to reflectors left on the Moon measures the present recession directly. Extrapolating the current rate backward for billions of years would be misleading because ocean geometry, tidal dissipation and Earth's rotational state changed. The old orbit must be reconstructed from a history of tidal interactions, not simply a constant-speed rewind.

This complicates impact simulations in a productive way. A simulation that ends with an orbit unlike today's may still be plausible if long-term evolution could connect them. But that evolution is another model with parameters and independent evidence to satisfy. The collision and subsequent tidal history must work together. This is also why scientists ask about angular momentum instead of only the mass of orbiting debris. Two simulations that make equally massive moons can leave systems with very different spin and orbital energy budgets.

The Moon therefore supplies measurements at different timescales: immediate present-day motion, rock chemistry locked in long ago, and cratered terrain altered during intervening ages. Agreement among them is more impressive than a single attractive image of a collision, precisely because the clocks and physical processes are different.

## Reading the lunar clocks

Now turn to a different measurement. A lunar mineral contains radioactive parent atoms and their daughter products. Provided we understand the decay rate and the conditions under which the isotopic system closed to exchange, its ratios can tell us when that mineral or rock system last recorded a particular event. But 'the age of a lunar sample' is not necessarily 'the time the Moon first assembled.' A mineral may crystallize from a magma ocean long after an impact. A later collision may reset its clock. A breccia may combine fragments of different ages.

The oldest samples are especially valuable because they limit how late a massive early melting event could have happened. Yet turning a mineral crystallization time into an impact time requires a cooling and differentiation model. You must decide how long a magma ocean persisted, how minerals separated, and whether later heating disturbed the isotopic system. This is why apparently precise dates from different isotope systems can be compatible with more than one chronology of lunar formation.

A 2025 study of rubidium and strontium in lunar ferroan anorthosites illustrates the distinction. The authors measured and modeled an initial strontium isotope ratio and obtained a conditional Moon-formation estimate near 4.50 billion years ago for several impact scenarios. They explicitly noted that their age calculation does not determine a unique formation time in the canonical impact model. The precise measured ratio deserves one level of confidence; the inferred collision date, conditional on how Earth and the impactor evolved, deserves another.

Do not mistake a new estimate for an automatic replacement of older chronologies. A paper that shifts a preferred impact date may have improved a model, sampled new material, or chosen different conditions for isotope closure. The question is not whether one number looks newer. The question is which observations changed and whether the new assumptions explain other data more successfully.

## What the alternatives really claim

The modern multiple-impact hypothesis is an instructive live alternative within the broad family of collisional origins. Instead of requiring one exceptionally successful giant impact, a succession of impacts could place material into orbit and gradually build a moon. A 2025 study published in a 2026 journal issue used hydrodynamic simulations to examine impact chains and showed a potential pathway that relaxes some compositional constraints. This is a physical possibility demonstrated within a model, not geological proof that a specific chain occurred. It faces the difficult task of preserving or producing a plausible final lunar orbit and geochemical record across many events.

Older capture and co-accretion ideas have a different standing. As simple complete explanations, they struggle with the combination of high-temperature lunar history, volatile depletion, Earth–Moon isotopic similarity, and dynamics. Variants might be proposed, but changing a weak hypothesis until it can accommodate every observation is not the same as producing a successful independent prediction. The comparison should ask what a proposal would have led us to expect before seeing the samples.

Even among single-impact pictures, the word 'Theia' can give an illusion of historical specificity. We do not possess a recovered Theia crust, an impact scar with a date stamp, or a video of the event. The name denotes a hypothesized body in a class of formation models. The strong reconstruction is that the Moon's early evolution involved an energetic origin and extensive melting, plausibly associated with major impacts during terrestrial accretion. The exact number, geometry, and timing of impacts are not equally secure.

There are other clues worth pursuing. High-precision analyses of refractory elements can test whether lunar and terrestrial mantle material share a source. Improved ages from regions beyond Apollo landing sites can map when the crust solidified. Lunar seismology and gravity measurements constrain internal structure. Samples from the far side or from well-chosen deep excavations might help identify components that were poorly represented in the existing collection. None is a magic key, but each can eliminate histories that predict the wrong composition, sequence, or interior.

## What a conclusion can legitimately say

Picture a scientist holding a pale lunar anorthosite. The composition is measurable. The crystalline history is partly recoverable. A date comes from isotopic ratios, calculations and a physical account of when the mineral retained them. To move from that single stone to a planet-sized collision, the investigator needs observations from other stones, telescopes, and planetary dynamics. Each additional bridge should be stated rather than hidden.

There is a valuable hierarchy of confidence here. We directly measure the chemistry and isotope ratios of particular specimens. Multiple lines of evidence strongly support extensive early melting and a high-energy history. Giant-impact formation is the leading explanatory family, supported by those constraints and by physics simulations. But the exact Theia, the number of major impacts, the amount of post-impact mixing and the detailed timing of solidification remain actively researched.

The Moon is close enough to visit and old enough that its beginnings remain a reconstruction. That is not a failure of science. The useful accomplishment is learning which origin stories survive demanding tests, and identifying the measurements that might distinguish the survivors. In the story of the Moon, a handful of small rocks carry information about an event that reshaped an entire planet. The rocks do not tell us everything. They tell us much more than the naked Moon ever could.

### Questions for study

1. Why does a precise lunar mineral age not automatically date the Moon-forming impact? **Answer:** Crystallization, cooling, closure and later resetting can occur at different times from the impact.
2. What makes isotope similarity a constraint on impact models? **Answer:** Impact pathways predicting mostly compositionally distinct lunar material conflict with the observed Earth–Moon similarities unless additional mixing or source similarity is explained.
3. What does a collision simulation demonstrate? **Answer:** Feasibility under its assumptions, not a uniquely observed historical event.
4. How is a multiple-impact scenario different from old capture theories? **Answer:** It stays in the collisional high-energy family while changing the proposed number and sequence of encounters.
