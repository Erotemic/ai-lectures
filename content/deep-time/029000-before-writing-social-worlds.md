# Before Writing: Networks, Rituals, and Political Organization

Imagine arriving at a gathering place where great stone pillars stand in a carefully arranged enclosure. Animals have been carved into their faces. People traveled, brought food, and worked together to erect the stones. We can see the result of those actions in the surviving architecture. We cannot overhear their arguments about how the structure should be built or their explanations for why it was important. Even if a carved creature looks like a symbol to us, we do not automatically know what it meant to those who made it.

Written documents can preserve names, rules, prices and disputes, although writers have biases of their own. In the much longer history before writing, researchers rely on material traces of cooperation and conflict. A settlement plan can reveal how buildings related spatially. A burial can preserve selected objects placed with a dead person. A stone brought from hundreds of kilometers away demonstrates movement of material through some chain of contacts. None provides direct access to a society's political constitution.

The temptation is to organize human societies into stages: small bands, then farming villages, then chiefs, then states. Such a ladder would assign political forms from population size or monumental architecture. The archaeological record instead shows multiple ways to coordinate work and build meaning. Large-scale constructions existed among foraging communities. Dense settlements could contain varied household arrangements. Hierarchy can develop in some settings and change or diminish in others. Social history requires local, testable models rather than predetermined stages.

## How do we recognize cooperation?

At the most basic level, an excavated building indicates that materials were gathered and placed in a planned arrangement. Repeated buildings with similar forms may imply shared construction practices. A huge mound demands substantial labor, but estimating the number of workers requires knowing tools, earth volumes, transport distances and working time. If ten people could have built it over several seasons, that tells a different social story than if a thousand people were required in one month. Archaeologists model both possibilities rather than equating size with kingship.

At Göbekli Tepe in southeastern Anatolia, monumental enclosures with carved T-shaped stone pillars date broadly to the tenth and ninth millennia BCE, before the region was dominated by the fully developed farming systems that followed. UNESCO describes the builders as hunter-gatherer groups associated with Pre-Pottery Neolithic communities. The structures clearly required planned material work, and their imagery suggests shared conventions. Their specific use, including proposed ceremonial or mortuary roles, remains interpreted rather than directly observed. It would be a mistake to conclude that a temple always comes after agriculture, or that every enclosure was a temple of the same kind.

The phrase 'ritual center' is useful only if it leads to a test. If a site was used mainly for episodic gatherings, we might expect distinctive deposits from feasts, changing use patterns and signs that visitors arrived from multiple places. If it was part of a more continuously inhabited settlement, we would expect everyday domestic waste and recurring household activities. These alternatives can overlap. An ancient place can hold meetings, meals, exchange and ceremonies without its residents separating those activities into our modern categories.

At the other end of the world, the earthworks of Poverty Point in what is now Louisiana were built and used around thirty-seven hundred to thirty-one hundred years before present. Broad semi-elliptical ridges and mounds surround a large plaza. The builders lived in a hunter-fisher-gatherer economy, and materials such as stone reached the locality from far beyond the lower Mississippi valley. This combination shows that large-scale earthmoving and far-ranging exchange do not require a cereal-farming state. The detailed organization of labor, authority and attendance remains debated. The monument demonstrates the capacity for coordination; it does not by itself identify a king or tax system.

## What an imported stone tells us

A small stone tool may reveal a regional network more clearly than a large building. Suppose chemical analysis shows that obsidian at a village originated from a volcanic source two hundred kilometers away. That ties a deposit to a source, subject to the reliability of the chemical fingerprint. It does not tell us whether one traveler carried it the entire distance, whether several groups exchanged it in stages, or whether it arrived with a migrating household. All these pathways can produce the same final artifact.

To move further, archaeologists examine the number of imported objects, the degree of manufacturing at the destination, their distribution among houses and graves, and changes through time. Raw material found in many households may indicate broad access; concentration in one area could reflect specialized production, elite control, or simply the location of an excavation. A source map records geological origin. A trade-route map adds hypotheses about human interactions. The difference should be drawn explicitly.

Material similarity has the same difficulty. Pottery vessels made in a similar style could reflect migration, shared fashion, apprenticeship or exchange of finished goods. Ancient DNA may reveal population movement around the same period, but genetic similarity does not equal a political alliance, ethnicity or language. We can often show that practices spread across a region. Identifying the social institution responsible for that spread needs additional lines of evidence.

## Burials: evidence of choice and a trap for interpretation

Burials are often among the richest archaeological contexts because people deliberately placed bodies and objects together. A decorated burial might suggest that the dead person held a special role, that relatives invested labor, or that a community wanted to express a particular relationship. Yet grave goods are selected by living people. A wealthy burial does not necessarily measure the wealth of the deceased during life. Absence of ornaments does not establish that a person had no authority or affection from others.

Think of a cemetery with a small number of elaborate graves. One model interprets them as evidence of inherited rank. Another interprets them as occasional ritual performances, perhaps limited to a death category or special event. To compare the models, researchers ask whether rich burials cluster in particular families, whether differences persist across generations, and whether household architecture, diet and resource access show corresponding inequalities. A single impressive object is rarely sufficient to distinguish these explanations.

Health and labor can also leave skeletal traces. Evidence of repetitive physical strain might indicate certain activities, but similar stresses can arise from many tasks. Injuries may reflect conflict, accidents or other causes. Isotope ratios in teeth and bone can constrain diet and mobility. They cannot tell us how food was negotiated or whether unequal portions were considered unjust by those who ate them. Inferring institutions requires respecting the gap between a biological trace and a social rule.

## A household is not automatically a family tree

At Çatalhöyük in Anatolia, people lived in a substantial Neolithic settlement with burials beneath house floors. For years the physical arrangement raised questions about who shared houses and who was buried there. A 2025 study integrated 131 ancient genomes with archaeology and bioarchaeology. It found that earlier burials within the same buildings often involved close biological relatives, while later individuals sharing a building could be genetically unrelated. Across the sampled periods, within-building connections were more often maternal than paternal, and burial gifts for female children were more frequent than for male children.

These are observations and statistical conclusions about the sampled skeletons and contexts. The study strongly challenges the assumption that every house was simply the burial place of one biologically continuous nuclear family. It supports models involving changing household membership and a persistent importance of female-linked kin relationships. It does not, by itself, prove a society had women rulers, determine marriage rules, or reveal what residents called their kin. A matrilineal pattern in burials and a matriarchal political hierarchy are different claims.

The example also demonstrates the limitations of negative evidence. A genome can identify ancestry relationships only among people whose remains survived and yielded usable DNA. Those buried elsewhere, those whose bodies received different treatments, and those excluded from excavation are missing from the sample. A statistically striking pattern must be interpreted as a pattern of the sampled community and its funerary practices, not a complete record of every living person.

## Comparing political explanations

Suppose an archaeologist finds a settlement with large shared storage buildings. Hypothesis one: a chief controlled harvests and redistributed food. Hypothesis two: households jointly managed a reserve against seasonal failures. Hypothesis three: the buildings served occasional gatherings, each with separate contributions. The structures alone may fit all three accounts. A stronger test examines access routes, standardized containers, seals, signs of household inequality and patterns of consumption. An exclusive locked store with concentrated luxury goods would have different implications from several accessible stores spread across a community. Even those patterns might admit more than one political explanation.

The same caution applies to warfare. Defensive walls may reflect fear of attack, but walls can also constrain animals, mark spaces or protect against floodwaters. Projectile points in bodies and patterns of repeated violent injuries are more specific evidence for conflict, yet they rarely identify the political justifications or long-term institutions involved. Archaeologists need a chain of inference that connects material construction, plausible function and comparative alternatives.

One alternative is that substantial cooperation can exist without centralized command. Work may be organized through seasonal gatherings, kin networks, reciprocal obligations and shared ritual commitments. Another is that hierarchy can be episodic: a leader directs one building project but holds little authority over daily food distribution. Some societies may have deliberately limited durable power even while creating impressive structures. Archaeology can sometimes test these models by looking for recurring privilege, unequal access and centralized facilities. It should not assume the presence or absence of hierarchy from monument size alone.

Not every durable social institution creates large monuments. A network of people who recognize obligations to exchange food in bad years might live in small settlements, leave few grave goods and still sustain sophisticated arrangements. The very evidence that survives can make display-oriented or stone-building traditions appear more complex than equally organized societies whose institutions relied on perishable materials, spoken commitments or seasonal mobility. Archaeologists therefore compare preservation conditions before making claims about the relative sophistication of different groups.

## Histories without manuscripts

The absence of an ancient written archive is partly a limitation of preservation and partly a historical fact about the forms of communication people used. Oral histories, performance, objects and landscape practices can preserve knowledge in ways that written sources do not. Living descendant communities may maintain relationships to archaeological places and knowledge about traditions. Such testimony deserves accurate attribution and, where claims concern deep chronology or specific ancient events, careful comparison with the types of evidence capable of addressing them. A community's history cannot be dismissed merely because it has not been rendered as an excavated text.

Research also has an ethical dimension. Burials are human remains, and sampling ancient DNA often destroys part of a specimen. Descendant communities, national laws, museum practices and researchers may have different interests in whether and how that work should proceed. Respectful collaboration can change the questions asked, the interpretation of sites and the practical ability to do research. Scientific confidence depends on method and evidence; access to that evidence depends in part on responsible relationships with people connected to it.

Our strongest claims in this domain concern tangible organization: humans built monumental places, moved resources across long distances, buried people according to patterned practices and sometimes changed household relationships across generations. Political titles, moral values and everyday motives are much less directly recoverable. The best reconstructions specify which social arrangements could plausibly produce the traces and which new discoveries might distinguish those arrangements.

The stone pillars, imported obsidian and house-floor burials therefore tell a genuine history of thought and cooperation even though they are unable to reproduce a transcript of the societies that made them. We can recover skilled labor, repeated interactions and material signs of social difference. We should not fill the spaces between those observations with invented kings, universal progress stages or confident statements about the beliefs of people who left no such record. History before writing is still history, provided we are precise about how it is known.

## Companion: models for material social evidence

| Material record | Robust observation | Plausible interpretation | Competing interpretation |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Göbekli Tepe pillars | Coordinated stone construction and imagery | Shared ceremonies or communal gatherings | Multifunctional settlement structures |
| Poverty Point earthworks | Large planned terrain modifications | Coordinated gathering and labor organization | Multiple episodes of smaller-scale work |
| Imported obsidian | Stone with distant geological source | Exchange or travel connections | Several intermediary movements, not one direct route |
| High-investment burial | Unequal funerary expenditure | Elevated social rank | Ritual age/status category or family choice |
| Çatalhöyük house-floor genomes | Sampled kin relationships and changing burial patterns | Flexible households, female-linked kin emphasis | Specific political or marriage system remains underdetermined |

A rough labor estimate is \(L=V/(r h)\), with \(V\) the moved earth volume, \(r\) the average earth moved per worker-hour and \(h\) the hours each worker contributes. This computes only a **labor-equivalent headcount** under assumptions about rates and time. It says nothing by itself about coercion, wages, leadership or whether the work happened all at once.

### Questions for study

1. Why does a monument not directly prove the existence of a chief? **Answer:** Work can be coordinated by different institutions and across different periods; hierarchy requires additional evidence.
2. What does distant obsidian establish? **Answer:** The material originated at a distant geological source; the number and structure of exchange steps remain unknown.
3. Why does a burial tell us more about a funerary decision than a person's lifetime status? **Answer:** Living people choose burial treatment, which can express values other than wealth or political power.
4. What does the Çatalhöyük genomic study support without proving? **Answer:** It supports changing household burial kinship and emphasis on maternal-linked relationships, but cannot establish a specific political constitution.
