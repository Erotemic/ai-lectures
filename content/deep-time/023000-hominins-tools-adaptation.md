# Hominins, Tools, and Adaptation

At a dry field site near Lake Turkana in Kenya, someone bends over a stone with a sharp broken edge. The stone does not carry a label announcing its maker. Perhaps an early human relative struck it repeatedly, detaching flakes. Perhaps it broke through natural rockfall. The archaeologist studies the direction of fractures, the shape of the scars, and the location of other objects nearby. Recovering a human action from stone requires reconstructing a sequence of forces. Recovering the identity of the actor is harder still.

The human family tree is often drawn as a procession: stooping ape-like ancestor, increasingly upright hominins, and modern people walking out at the end. This picture fails in two ways. Evolution produces branches rather than a compulsory march toward one body shape; and different abilities can evolve at different times. Walking on two legs, reducing stone into flakes, surviving in open habitats, cooking food and producing art do not define one synchronized transformation. They leave separate kinds of evidence.

We can begin with bodies, move to tools, and finally ask what combinations of these observations support behavior. Along the way we must keep species names and technological names distinct. A fossil species is a classification of organisms. A stone-tool industry is a classification of material practices. Two industries might be made by the same lineage, and the same industry may be made by more than one lineage.

## Bones preserve constraints on movement

Our closest living relatives are not our ancestors in their present form. Humans and chimpanzees share an earlier common ancestor; each lineage has undergone its own history of change. To infer ancient locomotion, researchers compare hip joints, thighbones, knee angles, spinal curvature, feet, hand bones and the position of the hole through which the spinal cord enters the skull. They also compare living animal mechanics and fossil footprints.

Consider the knee. In a body whose mass routinely travels over one supporting leg, the hip and knee must manage sideways forces as the other leg swings. The angle of the thighbone and the geometry of the pelvis provide information about how forces could be supported. But a bone shape is compatible with a range of movements; we cannot observe a vanished animal's gait directly from a single joint. Researchers build biomechanical models, examine associated bones and test which interpretations agree with living reference species.

Australopithecine fossils show mixtures of traits associated with habitual bipedal movement and traits useful for climbing. These combinations are scientifically interesting precisely because they do not align with a single ladder of progress. A hand capable of grasping branches need not exclude effective movement on the ground. Different species occupied environments containing woodland, grassland mosaics and varied resources over long periods. Locomotor anatomy alone does not tell us the proportion of each day spent in one habitat.

Footprints can provide a different constraint. If multiple prints form a coherent trackway in an undisturbed ancient surface, we can study stride length, foot contact and direction of travel. An anatomical model connects these traces to gait and body size. Yet even a good trackway does not always tell us which named species made it. Two close relatives can have similar feet, and a single individual can walk differently depending on speed, surface and load.

## The oldest candidate technology is older than our genus

A 2015 study described stone artifacts at Lomekwi 3 in western Kenya, dated to about 3.3 million years ago. The assemblage included large cores, flakes and objects interpreted as anvils or pounding implements. The authors argued that repeated fracture patterns and spatial context revealed intentional use and manipulation of stones. These are among the earliest widely discussed archaeological artifacts, and they predate the earliest known fossils of the genus Homo.

If that attribution holds, the inference is that some earlier hominin had stone-working capabilities. It does not identify that hominin as Homo. The name Lomekwian describes the technological evidence at the site. No label on the stones tells us which lineage struck them, or whether several lineages practiced similar actions. Nor do those early artifacts imply that tool use first arose precisely at 3.3 million years ago. Wooden implements and many other objects would be far less likely to survive.

The simplest way to understand flaking is mechanical. When a hammerstone strikes a suitable rock at an angle, the energy can initiate a fracture that propagates through the material, detaching a flake with a sharp edge. A deliberate knapper selects the angle, force and position of impact, sometimes preparing a surface for the next blow. On a flake, archaeologists inspect the platform where force entered, the bulb of percussion, and patterns of scars from earlier detachments. They can compare the ancient objects with experimentally knapped stones and with stones broken by geological processes.

One flake by itself may be ambiguous. A collection of cores and flakes that fit together, exhibit systematic fracture directions and lie in an undisturbed archaeological context supports intentional production much more strongly. Refitting a detached flake onto its parent core is especially valuable: it reconstructs one stage in the reduction sequence. It does not reveal everything about the maker's mind, but it supplies physical evidence that particular pieces were once connected.

## Tools are about actions, not species labels

A 2023 study reported Oldowan artifacts at Nyayanga, near Lake Victoria, in deposits roughly 2.9 million years old. The excavators also found animal bones with marks interpreted as butchery and hominin teeth assigned to Paranthropus in the wider site assemblage. A tool assemblage and a tooth in associated deposits demonstrate a landscape occupied by hominins and technological activity. They do not establish that the owner of a particular tooth produced a particular flake.

Oldowan technology generally involves exploiting cores, sharp flakes and pounding stones in diverse ways. Its discovery in a setting with Paranthropus fossils challenges any simple assumption that a single Homo species exclusively used such techniques. But there are several interpretations. Different hominins may have visited the area at different times, one group may have left fossils and another the tools, or more than one group may have used the same toolkit. Distinguishing these cases requires finer stratigraphic and behavioral evidence than a shared broad date range.

Marks on bones present a second inference problem. A sharp stone can slice flesh and leave characteristic striations. Carnivore teeth can scrape or puncture bone. Trampling underfoot can create marks that resemble some tool damage. Specialists examine the microscopic cross sections of marks, their orientation, distribution over anatomical surfaces, and associated bone breakage. Even when human-related butchery is the best explanation, the mark does not tell us whether the animal was hunted, scavenged after death, or accessed after another predator had eaten.

Some interpretations about early human diets have changed because researchers looked beyond the dramatic fossil. Grinding wear, starch residues, plant-processing implements, tooth microwear and chemical isotopes can constrain food use, each with its own preservation limits. A hippo bone with cuts demonstrates access to animal tissue. It does not establish that animal meat supplied most calories, that a particular social group organized a hunt, or that a single dietary innovation produced a bigger brain.

## Anatomy and technology evolve at different speeds

The emergence of Homo is also a classification problem. A fragmented jaw dated near 2.8 million years ago has been discussed as early evidence of the genus. Later fossil assemblages identified with Homo habilis, Homo erectus and other taxa show different combinations of tooth size, braincase shape, body proportions and limb anatomy. These features do not change in lockstep. Some scholars disagree on whether particular fossils belong to an existing species or should be assigned a different name.

Homo erectus is associated with long-lasting populations across Africa and Eurasia and with body proportions that, in several specimens, support efficient terrestrial walking. But it is not a single snapshot of one uniform body. Fossils identified with it span wide geographic and temporal ranges. Researchers have to decide whether variation reflects sex, age, regional difference, evolutionary change, or several species that happen to resemble one another.

One reason early Homo dispersal is important is that objects and bodies outside Africa provide a constraint on the timing of movement. The Dmanisi fossils from Georgia, around 1.8 million years old, demonstrate early Homo outside Africa and display notable anatomical variation among individuals from one site. A theory that assumes every difference in skull shape marks a distinct species must contend with the variation found among these associated fossils. Conversely, variation at one site does not prove that all other fossil species are invalid. It tests how flexible a classification rule should be.

Many later stone traditions include large bifacial implements often called handaxes. These require particular sequences of fracture and shaping. It can be tempting to treat their appearance as a direct measurement of intelligence. Yet technical choices also depend on raw material, task, learning, access to resources and whether tools were carried or made for immediate use. A site with few handaxes may reflect different activities or local stone availability, not a less capable population.

The word 'culture' in archaeology usually refers to patterns of learned practice transmitted among individuals. Stone artifacts offer direct evidence of patterned manufacture and use. Demonstrating how knowledge was taught, however, is harder. Archaeologists can compare variation among sites, experimental learning, and differences between simple and highly staged tool production. But we cannot retrieve a classroom lesson or spoken instruction from a flake. Technologies may spread by observation, repeated independent discovery, social learning or contact between groups.

## The 2026 Denisovan cave evidence expands the picture

Some parts of human evolution were reconstructed initially from genetics before many diagnostic bones were available. Denisovans are a striking example. In September 2026, research on Bianfu Cave in southwest China used ancient proteins to assign several fossil remains to Denisovans. A companion study described human remains and archaeological layers, including fossils dated around 167 to 134 thousand years ago and a longer sequence of occupation and artifacts. The evidence expands Denisovan anatomy beyond a few widely separated remains and illustrates adaptation in a very different environment from the cold Siberian cave that first gave the group its name.

The studies do not establish that every artifact in the entire cave sequence was made by Denisovans. Nor can proteins recover the full social organization of the people who lived there. Site formation, overlap in occupation periods and attribution to a taxon still require care. But combining proteomics, morphology, chronology, animal remains and tools allows a richer reconstruction than any one line of evidence.

This is also a reminder that 'modern people' were not always alone. Neanderthals, Denisovans and other hominin populations occupied overlapping periods. The human past is a shifting collection of lineages, with local adaptations and interactions. No one fossil sequence is a progress chart whose destination was inevitable.

## What does adaptation mean in evidence?

In evolutionary biology adaptation can mean a trait shaped over generations by selection, while in everyday language it can mean an organism adjusting its behavior. The two senses should be distinguished. A shaped stone demonstrates a behavior. A skeletal difference may reflect evolved anatomy, growth history or environmental effects. To show that a trait was favored by selection rather than drift or developmental constraints, researchers need more than the observation that it seems useful today.

Archaeologists sometimes propose that climate instability favored flexibility in diet and technology. This is a testable idea when supported by dated environmental records, changing anatomy and technological responses. It is also vulnerable to stories made after the fact. If every apparent innovation is declared a response to the climate, without predictions that could fail, the explanation has little power. We need a model of what kind of change should occur, when, and in which environments, plus cases where similar climate change did not produce the predicted behavior.

A tool may be useful for cutting and appear repeatedly. That alone does not prove it was the first solution discovered by its makers. A species may survive a climate shift without inventing new stone forms. Fossil bones, tools and climatic proxies each have their own age uncertainties, and an apparent correlation may disappear when their chronologies are improved. Deep-time causal arguments must acknowledge this uncertainty in matching events.

We can make one prediction sharper. If a technological style requires a sequence of choices rather than a single accidental fracture, assemblages should contain reproducible core shapes, ordered scar patterns or refittable sequences consistent with planned reduction. Those are physical features that other researchers can examine. By contrast, a claim that a toolmaker 'felt the first spark of human creativity' cannot be independently tested from the artifact.

## The branching record

Return to the Lomekwi stone. Its fractured surface can preserve the geometry of an action from 3.3 million years ago. That is an extraordinary archive, but its precision is local: how a rock broke, where it lay, and which physical processes explain those fractures. Linking that action to a species, a motive or a broad evolutionary revolution requires added evidence and assumptions.

We have strong reasons to think that bipedal abilities and tool use evolved across multiple phases of hominin history, that early technological behavior preceded the currently recognized genus Homo, and that several lineages lived concurrently. We have much less precise knowledge of who made some of the oldest artifacts, how they taught one another, and what fraction of their behavior failed to fossilize. The next lecture changes the archive from stone and bone to molecular sequences, where a different kind of survival bias opens new windows while closing others.

## Companion: manufacture, species and function are different claims

| Evidence | What can be directly inspected | Supported reconstruction | What remains open |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Lomekwi 3 cores/flakes | Fracture scars and excavation context | Intentional stone manipulation at about 3.3 Ma | Exact maker species and earlier perishable technology |
| Nyayanga tools and cut-marked bones | Oldowan-style artifacts, bone marks, associated fossils | Tool production and animal-resource processing near 2.9 Ma | Whether Paranthropus, Homo or another lineage made each artifact |
| Joint geometry and trackways | Bones and sediment impressions | Constraints on gait and body mechanics | Complete behavior, species assignment from isolated traces |
| Dmanisi assemblage | Multiple early Homo skulls in dated site | Wide variation among related early Homo individuals | Taxonomic boundaries elsewhere |
| Bianfu Cave proteins and artifacts | Identified fossil peptides, bones and tool-bearing layers | Denisovan presence and broad subsistence/technology patterns | Assignment of every cultural layer and precise population history |

**Reduction-sequence model:** Let a core pass through states \(C_0\to C_1\to\cdots\to C_n\), where each transition removes material by an impact. A refitted flake and associated scar can constrain a transition. Multiple plausible sequences may lead to similar final shapes; observing the final core does not recover the maker's exact intention.

**Inference check:** Co-occurrence means \(P(\text{maker}=A\mid \text{tools and fossil of A nearby})\) is a question requiring competing likelihoods. It does not set this probability to one, especially when multiple hominins were present or strata were time-averaged.

### Questions for study

1. Why do Lomekwi tools challenge a Homo-only origin of stone technology? **Answer:** They are dated earlier than the earliest generally recognized Homo fossils, so other hominins are plausible makers.
2. Do Paranthropus teeth at Nyayanga identify the Oldowan toolmakers? **Answer:** No; spatial association is evidence but does not uniquely identify the makers.
3. What does refitting flakes to a core establish? **Answer:** A physically linked sequence of stone removal and evidence about knapping operations.
4. Why is a complex tool not a direct measurement of intelligence? **Answer:** Tool forms depend on available material, tasks, transmission and technological choices, and do not uniquely encode cognitive ability.
