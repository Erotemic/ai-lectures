# The Universe We Can Measure: Cosmology, Reconstruction, and the Limits of Our Knowledge

Imagine holding a spectrograph up to a faint galaxy. The instrument separates the galaxy's light into colors, and across those colors it finds a recognizable sequence of dark lines. Laboratory experiments have already taught us where hydrogen, oxygen, and other elements absorb light. In this galaxy, the pattern is displaced toward longer wavelengths. We can read how far the pattern has shifted. That is a measurement. The explanation that the galaxy's light has been stretched as the universe expands is an inference. Turning the shift into a distance, a date, or a history of cosmic expansion requires still more assumptions.

Cosmology begins with this chain of reasoning, repeated at enormous scales. We measure light, angles, brightnesses, spectra, and correlations. We combine them with atomic physics, gravity, models of how objects evolve, and statistical assumptions. From this material we infer a universe roughly thirteen point eight billion years old, whose ordinary matter accounts for only a small fraction of its inferred energy density. Much of this picture fits several independent sets of observations remarkably well. Important parts remain unresolved, including the physical identity of dark matter, the nature of dark energy, and what, if anything, preceded the earliest hot state our equations can describe reliably.

The central question here is therefore more precise than asking whether the Big Bang is true. Which parts of cosmic history have left signals we can measure? Which conclusions depend on a model? Which alternatives have already failed tests? Where does the evidence stop, leaving multiple plausible explanations or none that we can presently assess?

We will construct an evidence ledger as we travel backward. Whenever we make a claim, we will ask four things: what entered the detector; what physical interpretation connects that signal to the past; what else could produce a similar signal; and what observation could change our mind. Confidence must follow the strength and independence of the evidence. It cannot be assigned just because a scientific diagram looks complete.

## What astronomy actually observes

In everyday history, a document may record the actions of someone who witnessed an event. Astronomy has a stranger archive. Almost all of the information we receive from distant galaxies arrives as electromagnetic radiation. A telescope collects photons; a radio antenna detects electromagnetic waves; a spectrograph records their distribution across frequency. We can also detect cosmic rays, neutrinos, and gravitational waves, but those messengers reach us from particular sources under particular conditions. None gives us a camera positioned outside the universe, observing its full history from beginning to end.

Even a photograph of the sky is a construction. A detector responds to incoming radiation, electronics process the readings, astronomers calibrate them against known references, and software subtracts instrumental patterns or foreground emission. A false color can display a wavelength our eyes cannot perceive. The underlying measurements can be excellent while the familiar image remains a processed representation rather than the scene a human observer would see from a nearby spaceship.

Now consider distance and time. Light has a finite speed. If a signal traveled for a billion years before reaching us, it tells us about a source as it was when the signal left. Yet the distance to that source today need not equal one billion light-years. Space may have expanded during the journey. Astronomers distinguish lookback time, the light-travel interval, from present-day distance in an expanding cosmological model. We never directly observe a simultaneous three-dimensional snapshot of all distant galaxies. We observe a past light cone: nearby objects at comparatively recent times and more distant objects at progressively earlier times.

This creates a difficulty for claims about evolution. A distant galaxy appears at an early cosmic epoch. But is it different from a nearby galaxy because galaxies evolved over time, because the distant sample selects unusually bright objects, or because our instruments favor different kinds of objects at different wavelengths? Researchers model these selection effects, compare populations, and seek independent evidence. Looking back is powerful, but the archive has biases.

## A first strong inference: the universe expands

The redshift of a galaxy is the fractional change between an observed spectral wavelength and the corresponding laboratory wavelength. If a known line would appear at five hundred units in the laboratory and appears at seven hundred fifty units in the galaxy's spectrum, the redshift is one half. We do not need a theory of the whole universe to identify that ratio, although identifying the line and calibrating the instrument require ordinary physical knowledge.

For nearby galaxies, redshift increases approximately with distance. Early twentieth-century astronomers including Vesto Slipher measured galaxy spectral shifts, while Henrietta Leavitt's work on variable stars and subsequent distance calibrations helped establish astronomical distances. Georges Lemaître connected the expanding solutions of general relativity with observed galaxy motions before Edwin Hubble's influential nineteen twenty-nine paper. The historical story involved more people and uncertainties than a single discovery attributed to a single telescope.

A graph of recession speed against distance is often taught as the entire proof of expansion. We have stronger tests. The expanding-universe picture predicts that a distant event taking a fixed amount of time in its own rest frame will appear stretched by a factor of one plus redshift. In a study of more than fifteen hundred Type Ia supernovae, researchers found this time stretching close to the predicted dependence. That observation challenges simple explanations in which light merely loses energy along the way without corresponding time dilation.

Why not describe the whole result as a Doppler effect? A Doppler shift is a useful approximation for nearby galaxies. For cosmological distances, the standard description tracks how the scale factor of the universe changes while light travels. A galaxy can remain approximately at rest in its local expanding coordinate system while the wavelength of a traveling photon grows. The model is general relativity applied to a universe that is sufficiently homogeneous and isotropic on large scales. It does not say galaxies, solar systems, or atoms are continuously pulled apart; gravitationally and electromagnetically bound structures behave differently.

Expansion is supported by mutually reinforcing observations: redshift-distance relations, time dilation, large-scale clustering, and a cooling background of radiation. But a measured redshift by itself does not give a precise cosmic age. It is one observable in a longer inference chain.

## The oldest light we see

Everywhere we turn a suitable microwave receiver, after accounting for local foregrounds, it finds a nearly uniform bath of radiation. The cosmic microwave background, or CMB, has an almost perfect thermal spectrum. The COBE satellite's spectrometer measured a temperature near two point seven two five kelvin. The shape is extremely close to the blackbody spectrum expected of radiation once in thermal equilibrium. This spectrum is among cosmology's strongest direct observations.

Why do cosmologists connect it to an early hot phase? Imagine a universe much denser and hotter than ours. Free electrons scatter light, keeping radiation and matter closely coupled. As the universe expands, it cools. Eventually protons and electrons can form neutral hydrogen efficiently enough that photons travel far more freely. Those photons keep traveling while their wavelengths stretch, so the radiation becomes colder. A present-day microwave background with a precise thermal shape is a natural consequence of that history.

The commonly quoted time of this transition is about three hundred eighty thousand years after the beginning of the standard hot Big Bang timeline. The time is inferred using the model's expansion history and atomic physics. Nobody has detected a timestamp printed on the photons. The observed photon spectrum is a measurement; the claim that those photons last scattered at a particular redshift and cosmic age is a physically grounded reconstruction.

The background is not perfectly uniform. Across the sky its temperature changes by only tiny amounts, roughly one part in a hundred thousand after removing the large dipole pattern associated with our motion. The satellite missions COBE, WMAP, and Planck measured these variations increasingly well. What looks like a random texture contains a structured pattern in its angular correlations. Regions of the early plasma compressed and rarefied under gravity and radiation pressure, leaving a sequence of acoustic peaks.

That sequence is more revealing than a single average temperature. The characteristic angular size of the patterns depends on how far sound could travel in the young plasma and on the geometry through which we see it. The relative heights depend on the amounts of ordinary matter, gravitating matter that does not couple strongly to light, and other physical ingredients. The large-scale correlations depend on the primordial fluctuations from which the structures developed. Polarization provides another partially independent set of signals. A cosmological model must account for this whole network of relationships, rather than matching only the color of the sky.

The public maps can make the result feel like a photograph of the first moment of creation. It is closer to an archaeological surface: a measured radiation field whose variations encode the state of matter at a later stage. Light could not have traveled freely to us from the earlier opaque plasma. Earlier epochs are reconstructed indirectly, through what they left in this surface and in other surviving records.

## The six-parameter model and what it does

The most widely used baseline is called Lambda cold dark matter, often abbreviated Lambda C D M. Lambda represents a constant dark-energy density in the model; cold dark matter represents a gravitating component that moves comparatively slowly when structures begin forming and interacts weakly with light. Six fitted parameters specify the densities and other features needed to calculate the statistical pattern of microwave fluctuations under a set of physical assumptions.

The six numbers are impressive because they fit many distinct features of the data with a compact description. But the phrase six-parameter model can conceal how much is built into the framework. We also assume general relativity on cosmological scales, a particular large-scale symmetry, a mostly simple primordial fluctuation spectrum, ordinary atomic and nuclear physics, and a specified set of particle species. The model's six adjustable numbers operate within that larger scientific structure.

Under the Planck baseline fit, the cosmic age is about thirteen point eight billion years. A commonly quoted numerical result is thirteen point seven nine seven billion years with a formal uncertainty of about twenty-three million years for that particular data combination and model. The tiny quoted uncertainty describes how tightly that model's parameters are constrained by the assumed analysis. It does not mean that every conceivable alternative cosmological history has been excluded to within twenty-three million years.

The same analysis gives a present-day energy budget of roughly five percent ordinary matter, twenty-seven percent dark matter, and sixty-eight percent a component described as dark energy. These percentages are derived model parameters. They do not mean that instruments have collected separate jars containing these three substances. Ordinary atoms have a well-established microscopic description. The other two labels summarize phenomena that we infer gravitationally and cosmologically, without an agreed microscopic identity.

Does that make the model arbitrary? No. A flexible model can be fitted to almost anything if it has enough degrees of freedom. The striking feature here is how many observations the baseline can explain together: microwave temperature and polarization patterns, much of large-scale galaxy clustering, a characteristic distance scale in the galaxy distribution, and the abundances of light elements. The cross-checks are why the model commands serious confidence. We should retain that achievement even as we identify its gaps.

## Nuclear evidence from an epoch before the microwave background

To go earlier than the visible microwave surface, we need a different archive. Some of the lightest atomic nuclei were produced when the universe was only minutes old in the standard reconstruction. As the plasma cooled, nuclear reactions could assemble deuterium, helium, and small amounts of lithium. This process is called Big Bang nucleosynthesis.

A nuclear calculation predicts different abundances for different densities of ordinary matter. More baryons change how readily deuterium is consumed in subsequent reactions; the leftover abundance becomes a sensitive indicator of the baryon density. Astronomers observe deuterium by looking at absorption in relatively pristine gas along the sight lines to distant quasars. Identifying suitable clouds and accounting for chemical evolution takes care. The abundance is a measured property of surviving gas, and calling it primordial adds an inference about the gas's history.

A recent synthesis gives an inferred baryon-density parameter from deuterium of about zero point zero two two four, in close agreement with the value inferred from microwave fluctuations. Those two estimates draw on very different physics and epochs. Their agreement is stronger evidence for a hot early universe than merely repeating one observation in different words.

There is also a problem. Simple standard calculations overpredict the abundance of lithium compared with what is inferred from some ancient, metal-poor stars. Stellar depletion, observational complications, nuclear reaction uncertainties, or additional physics may play a role; the explanation has not been settled. The disagreement does not erase the deuterium and helium successes. It tells us that the detailed reconstruction remains a research problem even in a period where the broad hot-universe picture is well supported.

We have now reached a critical distinction. A radiation field observed today gives information about conditions hundreds of thousands of years into the standard timeline. Nuclear abundances let us infer conditions minutes after its starting point. These are two independent, imperfect archives. Neither is direct footage of those minutes.

## Dark matter: evidence for gravity, uncertainty about substance

Consider how galaxies rotate. At large radii, stars and gas often move faster than expected if gravity came only from observed stars and gas. On larger scales, gravity bends light around clusters of galaxies, allowing astronomers to infer the distribution of mass from its lensing effects. The growth of structure and the acoustic peaks in the microwave background provide further constraints on how gravity has operated through cosmic history.

A particularly instructive case is a collision of galaxy clusters called the Bullet Cluster. X-ray observations locate hot gas, which contains much of the ordinary matter. Gravitational lensing locates much of the inferred gravitating mass in different places. The simple proposal that all relevant gravity follows the observed hot gas struggles to explain that separation. A component that passes through the collision with little interaction fits it naturally.

Dark matter as a gravitational component therefore has support from multiple methods that are not identical to one another. Yet we have not identified a specific dark-matter particle. Direct searches and other experiments have placed constraints on many candidates. Modified-gravity ideas attempt to explain some astronomical phenomena without adding the same unseen matter, and particular models succeed in selected regimes. They must also face clusters, gravitational lensing, cosmological structure, and the microwave background together. A rotation curve alone cannot settle the choice.

Our confidence should be different for the observations, the claim that the standard gravitational model needs additional gravitating mass, and the claim that this mass is a particular new particle. Those are three different propositions.

## Dark energy and the uncomfortable expansion rate

The universe's expansion appears to have accelerated during its more recent history. Much of the evidence comes from comparing the brightness and redshift of distant Type Ia supernovae with predictions for different expansion histories. Baryon acoustic oscillations, or BAO, offer another tool: early pressure waves left a preferred separation scale in the later distribution of matter. Surveying millions of galaxies and quasars lets researchers infer how that scale appears at different redshifts.

Here too the word measurement requires care. A survey measures angles and redshifts. It extracts a clustering scale through statistical analysis. Interpreting that scale as an absolute distance requires a calibrated physical ruler, usually provided by the physics of the early universe. Some BAO ratios are more directly data-constrained than the inferred absolute distances. The connection between the early ruler and late expansion is one reason these observations test the standard cosmological history.

The simplest fit to the acceleration uses a cosmological constant, Lambda, whose energy density remains constant as space expands. Calling it dark energy does not identify what it is. In general relativity, the same observed expansion can be described using an effective energy density and pressure, but a fitted equation of state is not automatically evidence for a new physical fluid.

The Hubble tension is a particularly concrete disagreement. One route uses the microwave background and a Lambda cold dark matter model to infer today's expansion rate, about sixty-seven kilometers per second per megaparsec. Another builds a distance ladder using geometric calibrations, pulsating stars, and supernovae, and some prominent analyses obtain roughly seventy-three in the same units. The reported discrepancy is far larger than the quoted statistical uncertainties in those particular analyses. But different methods have different systematic uncertainties, and the microwave value is conditional on the assumed cosmology.

The interesting response is neither to dismiss the disagreement nor to declare immediately that the laws of physics have failed. We should ask whether a shared calibration, a population difference, a foreground, or a wrong assumption in the cosmological model could produce it. Independent distance indicators, alternative calibrations, and new observations make the tension more informative, whether or not it survives.

Data from the Dark Energy Spectroscopic Instrument, called DESI, have also increased interest in models where the dark-energy equation of state changes with time. The DESI measurements of baryon acoustic oscillations are real observations. When combined with selected microwave and supernova datasets, some fits improve if dark energy is allowed to evolve. The size and interpretation of that preference depend on which datasets, parameterizations, and statistical comparisons are used. BAO alone does not establish a discovery of evolving dark energy. A better fit is an invitation to test a hypothesis, rather than an identification of a physical mechanism.

## How far backward can the model safely travel?

We have excellent evidence for a period when the observable universe was hotter, denser, and more nearly uniform than today. We can calculate how temperatures and particle interactions would behave as we extrapolate into earlier phases. Nuclear physics provides a useful guide for the first seconds and minutes. The further back we go, the more the reconstruction depends on physical regimes that laboratories and astronomical observations constrain less directly.

One famous proposal is cosmic inflation: a period of extremely rapid accelerated expansion early in cosmic history. Inflation offers mechanisms for generating large-scale smoothness and a particular pattern of primordial fluctuations. Several of its predicted general features agree with the microwave observations. But there is no unique, experimentally identified field or potential responsible for inflation, and many inflationary models can make similar predictions. Some proposed primordial gravitational-wave signals have not been detected, placing limits on classes of models rather than eliminating every version of inflation.

There are alternatives worth studying seriously. In bouncing or cyclic cosmologies, a contracting phase may precede the expanding hot phase. Certain ekpyrotic models use a period of slow contraction to address problems inflation was designed to solve. These proposals confront demanding questions about perturbations, stability, entropy, and the physics of a bounce. They offer research programs with testable consequences in favorable cases, although no single alternative has reproduced the full observational success of the standard framework and become comparably established.

Other historical alternatives have fared worse. The classical steady-state picture maintained roughly constant large-scale conditions while the universe expanded. It struggles with the microwave background and the observed evolution of distant galaxies and radio sources. Simple tired-light explanations of redshift lack the observed time dilation of distant events. These ideas remain valuable for understanding why particular observations are decisive. Listing them beside viable speculative proposals without explaining their failures would give a misleading impression of the evidence.

Can we talk about the beginning itself? When cosmologists extend classical general-relativistic solutions backward, they may encounter a singularity where their description ceases to be physically usable. A mathematical singularity in an extrapolated model does not constitute an observation that time, space, and everything else were created from nothing at a measured instant. Quantum gravity remains incomplete, and we do not know whether the relevant history began, bounced, or involved a description for which our familiar time variable is inadequate. Claims about what happened before the hot phase must be kept distinct from measurements of the later universe.

## An evidence ledger for cosmology

At this point, we can grade statements without pretending to possess perfectly calibrated numerical probabilities for competing worldviews. A high-confidence conclusion can be robust across multiple observations and reasonable model variations. A model-dependent conclusion can be tightly constrained within a specified model yet uncertain under broader alternatives. A speculative proposal can be mathematically interesting without having distinctive observational support.

Here is how that distinction works in practice. The existence of a near-thermal cosmic microwave background is measured with high confidence. The inference that the observable universe passed through a hot, dense, coupled plasma is also strongly supported, because it explains the spectrum and detailed fluctuation pattern while agreeing with light-element evidence. The precise cosmic age of thirteen point eight billion years is a high-precision inference within a cosmological framework; the framework must be named when quoting its small error bars.

The need for additional gravitating mass under conventional cosmology has broad support, but the identity of a dark-matter particle is unknown. Late-time acceleration has substantial observational support, but its fundamental cause and the possibility of time evolution remain less settled. The presence of a particular inflationary field, a bounce, or a unique beginning is not established by current observation. In most cases, our confidence in the signal is higher than our confidence in the explanation of the signal.

How might one test an alternative responsibly? Begin by specifying what the proposal predicts that differs from a baseline model. It might predict an altered distribution of primordial fluctuations, a distinctive gravitational-wave spectrum, an altered relation between distances and redshifts, or a different pattern of structure growth. Then ask what measurements could discriminate those predictions, including instrumental effects. A theory capable of fitting every result after freely adjusting hidden details may explain little, even if its mathematical story is attractive.

There is a further caution about uncertainty. Two experiments that use different instruments may still depend on the same calibration or the same physical assumption. Agreement is strongest when the paths to the conclusion really do involve different potential failure modes. Conversely, a discrepancy can arise from a systematic effect that quoted error bars do not fully capture. Evidence assessment involves models of instruments, astronomy, and statistics, as well as the physics being tested.

## What can we say we know?

We can say with considerable confidence that the observable universe has expanded from an earlier hot and dense state, that the microwave background preserves measurable information about its early plasma, and that primordial nuclear reactions account for much of the light-element pattern. We have observational evidence of the later growth of stars, galaxies, and clusters across billions of years. These are achievements of reconstruction supported by many separate measurements.

We can also say that important parts of our description remain provisional. The early fluctuations have no uniquely established microscopic origin. Dark matter is well evidenced through gravity without an identified constituent. Dark energy is an effective description whose physical origin is unsettled. A disagreement over the present expansion rate may indicate neglected systematic errors or a missing piece of physics. And our standard model does not answer why there is a universe, whether time had a first moment, or what might lie beyond the observable region.

A useful way to understand deep history is to treat every epoch as a reconstruction problem. A line in a spectrum, a microwave correlation, a deuterium absorption feature, or a rock's radioactive isotope ratio is a surviving trace. From each trace we build a chain of explanation. The chain can be exceptionally strong without being complete. At its best, science tells us which links have been tested, which assumptions they depend on, and where the present evidence cannot yet take us.

### Small mathematical constructions

1. **Redshift**: \(1+z=\lambda_{\mathrm{obs}}/\lambda_{\mathrm{emit}}\). For cosmological expansion, \(1+z=a(t_0)/a(t_{\mathrm{emit}})\) in the Robertson–Walker description. The first relation describes the observational ratio; the second introduces the cosmological model.
2. **Lookback versus distance**: \(t_L(z)=\int_0^z\frac{dz'}{(1+z')H(z')}\); a comoving radial distance in a flat FLRW universe is \(\chi(z)=c\int_0^z\frac{dz'}{H(z')}\). One measured redshift maps into different distances and times for different \(H(z)\).
3. **Baseline expansion model** (negligible radiation only at late times): \(H(z)^2/H_0^2=\Omega_m(1+z)^3+\Omega_k(1+z)^2+\Omega_\Lambda\); to extrapolate to early hot epochs include \(\Omega_r(1+z)^4\). This is conditional on GR, component equations of state, and the FLRW ansatz.
4. **Cosmological time dilation**: \(\Delta t_{\mathrm{obs}}\simeq(1+z)\Delta t_{\mathrm{emit}}\). A 2024 DES analysis reports exponent \(b=1.003\pm0.005\,(\mathrm{stat})\pm0.010\,(\mathrm{sys})\) when fitting the power \((1+z)^b\) to a large supernova sample.
5. **Evidence is conditional**: \(P(\theta\mid D,M)\propto P(D\mid\theta,M)P(\theta\mid M)\). Narrow posterior uncertainty in parameters \(\theta\) given model \(M\) does not include uncertainty over alternative choices of \(M\) unless such alternatives are explicitly compared.

### Competing interpretations and discriminating tests

- **Hot Big Bang versus classical steady state:** CMB blackbody spectrum, light nuclei, and cosmic source evolution sharply discriminate; the historical steady-state theory does not fit their combination.
- **Expansion versus simple tired light:** Spectroscopic redshift alone is insufficient; supernova light-curve time dilation provides a successful discriminator for expansion.
- **Dark-matter component versus modified gravity:** Compare galaxy rotations, cluster collisions and weak lensing, CMB peak structure, and the growth of structure under the same model; a single galaxy is inadequate.
- **Cosmological constant versus evolving dark energy:** Distinguish the BAO measurement from the CMB-calibrated standard ruler and SN calibrations; test new data and model selection, not only maximum-likelihood improvement.
- **Inflation versus bouncing or ekpyrotic proposals:** Calculate distinctive scalar, tensor, and non-Gaussian signals under explicit stable models; do not equate model-building interest with observational confirmation.

### Study questions with answers

1. **Question:** A detector reports a CMB temperature of approximately 2.725 K. Have we therefore directly measured the age 380,000 years? **Answer:** No. The spectrum is measured. The emission-era age is inferred from atomic processes and a modeled expansion history.
2. **Question:** Why is agreement between deuterium-derived and CMB-derived baryon densities valuable? **Answer:** Their main observables and physical dependencies differ; agreement constrains common cosmological assumptions more strongly than repeated use of a single dataset.
3. **Question:** Why can a twenty-three-million-year error bar on a 13.8-billion-year age be misleading when quoted without qualifications? **Answer:** It is a formal parameter uncertainty under a specific model and datasets, not an uncertainty budget across all candidate cosmologies.
4. **Question:** Does a five-sigma disagreement between two inferred Hubble values prove new physics? **Answer:** No. It indicates that the quoted statistical models and uncertainties do not comfortably reconcile the results; unmodeled systematic errors and model failure must both be investigated.
5. **Question:** What would strengthen a bouncing cosmology? **Answer:** A precise, distinct prediction verified in an independent observation, with a physical mechanism stable under perturbations and consistent with CMB, BBN, and large-scale structure constraints.
6. **Question:** What is the distinction between detecting gravitational effects attributed to dark matter and discovering a dark-matter particle? **Answer:** Gravitational inference constrains an effective mass distribution under a theory of gravity; identifying a particle requires additional evidence about its microscopic properties.
