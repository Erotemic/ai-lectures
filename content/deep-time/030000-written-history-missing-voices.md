# Written History and the Problem of Missing Voices

A small clay tablet survives in a museum collection. Someone pushed a stylus into soft clay, making a cluster of marks: a commodity, a quantity, perhaps a person or institution responsible for receiving it. Thousands of years later, the tablet can tell us that an administrator recorded a transaction. But the people who carried the goods, prepared the food, and argued about the transaction might never have written a word. Their lives were equally real. The archive remembers the part of the event somebody had reason to record.

It is tempting to imagine that the invention of writing turns a dim prehistoric world into a transparent historical one. Suddenly we have names, dates, proclamations, contracts, letters and complaints. Some of the past becomes astonishingly articulate. Yet writing also introduces a new kind of filter. A document is a material object made by someone, for an audience, at a particular moment, under incentives that may be recoverable only in part. An inscription need not be true because it can be read. An archive need not represent the people who lived nearby simply because it contains many documents.

The fundamental problem for written history is to move from what the source **says** to what the source is **evidence for**. This is the same discipline that turns isotope measurements into geological history and spectral lines into astrophysical inference. But human sources bring intention, memory, rhetoric, translation and unequal access to writing into the causal chain. The historian must reconstruct not just an event but the making and survival of its account.

## When accounting begins to speak

Some of the earliest surviving Mesopotamian tablets from the late fourth millennium BCE record commodities and quantities. Writing developed within urban and institutional settings, and early cuneiform is closely associated with administration. A record of beer or grain allocations may preserve a number more securely than it preserves the experience of a recipient. It can show that a category existed in an accounting system without establishing that the category described an entire person's social identity.

To see the distinction, imagine finding a tablet that lists ten vessels of grain sent to a workshop. One straightforward interpretation is that a clerk registered a distribution. The physical tablet and its signs directly support the existence of a record; comparison with the numerical and sign system supports the reading of its content. That does not tell us whether the delivery was on time, whether someone stole part of it, whether its workers were pleased, or whether the grain was ever delivered at all. A receipt is evidence that somebody asserted or registered a transaction. A matching set of inventories and excavated food-storage facilities would make the reconstruction stronger.

A major change in the written record came as writing systems expanded beyond numerical and administrative purposes to preserve law, correspondence, storytelling, learning and commemoration. These genres bring their own conventions. A royal inscription might be designed to persuade subjects and posterity that a ruler was victorious. An exercise tablet tells us about scribal education even if the sentences were copied rather than composed by the student. A contract may record how parties wanted an obligation expressed under law, not every informal agreement they made. The strength of written evidence grows when we recognize the genre rather than treating every sentence as a witness statement.

A government proclamation and a private letter might describe the same event differently. Instead of averaging them, a historian asks who had access to which information, who risked punishment for speaking, what each writer hoped the recipient would do, and how each document reached the archive. Disagreement can reveal a contested event, conflicting interests, or vocabulary that does not translate cleanly. Sometimes the disagreement is the most informative part of the record.

## The accident of what survives

Clay can survive for millennia; paper, bark, wood and textiles are generally more vulnerable to moisture, insects, fire and human reuse, although exceptional conditions preserve them. Two societies may leave radically different numbers of written documents even if both used elaborate systems of recording information. What looks like an intellectual imbalance can be a preservation imbalance.

At the Library of Congress, a modest collection of cuneiform tablets includes accounting records, school exercises and commemorative inscriptions. Some school tablets were meant to be erased and reused. That simple observation has large implications. Objects produced every day can be archaeologically rare because they were designed to disappear. Objects meant to commemorate rulers, institutions or gifts may be deliberately durable. Our archive can therefore overrepresent what was intended to last.

An archive has several selection stages. Somebody had to make the record. Somebody had to retain it. The material had to survive. Archaeologists, collectors or archivists had to find it and recognize it. And a reader must be able to access and understand it. At every stage the surviving set can become less representative of the community that originally produced information. The missing records have no labels announcing how many there were.

Consider a palace archive discovered after a fire. The blaze may have destroyed the building while hardening some of its clay tablets, preserving records the inhabitants intended to use only briefly. A disaster can increase the survival of one category of evidence while erasing people and activities that took place outside the administrative rooms. We can learn a great deal about royal logistics without obtaining a census of ordinary thought.

Documents also move. A tablet purchased decades ago without an archaeological context can still be read, but its provenance is thinner: the building, layer and association with other objects may be lost. A surviving sentence and a secure original context are separate kinds of information. Provenance affects what we can infer about the writer's institution, the intended audience and the sequence of events.

## When signs become language again

Sometimes a civilization leaves writing we can see but cannot yet read. Decipherment then becomes an experiment in historical inference. The signs must be grouped, compared, ordered and related to a language or writing system. A convincing reading should work on texts not used to devise it. One attractive translation of a single monument is insufficient.

Ancient Maya writing offers a striking history of this process. For much of modern scholarship, researchers could identify calendrical patterns while major parts of the texts remained inaccessible. In the twentieth century, Tatiana Proskouriakoff recognized that sequences of dates and signs on monuments corresponded to events within individual lifetimes, such as accessions and deaths. These were histories of people and political events, not just astronomical cycles. Yuriy Knorozov's work demonstrated the importance of syllabic values in the script, alongside signs representing words. Scholars could then test readings by comparing glyphs across inscriptions and with Mayan languages.

This changed the kind of past recoverable from the same stone. The monuments had always contained their inscriptions; the new evidence was also linguistic and comparative. Once a reading predicts names and grammatical patterns elsewhere, it gains support. A proposed decipherment that works only by assigning each sign whatever meaning a story requires does not meet that standard.

Even a deciphered monument remains an authored account. A royal text may name victories, ancestors and rituals to legitimate power. Reading it restores the writer's perspective, including its self-interest. Archaeology can then investigate whether dates align with construction phases, whether attacks left material traces and whether other monuments offer conflicting accounts. Reading expands the available questions. It does not eliminate the need to test the answers.

The problem is especially clear for scripts that remain undeciphered. Researchers may classify sign frequencies, positions and recurring sequences without establishing a stable translation. A resemblance to a familiar symbol is not enough. When several proposed readings explain the same few inscriptions equally well, we should say the writing is presently unreadable and identify what kind of bilingual inscription, longer corpus or linguistic pattern would change that.

## A damaged book, newly accessible

Access to an archive can change without new artifacts being dug from the ground. Herculaneum, near the Bay of Naples, was buried by the eruption of Vesuvius in 79 CE. Carbonized papyrus scrolls survived but many could not be unrolled without damage. Their physical presence was known; much of their text was inaccessible.

By 2026, high-resolution X-ray tomography, computational segmentation and ink-detection techniques had recovered substantial writing from some scrolls. Researchers announced approximately twenty columns of text from a scroll catalogued as PHerc. 1667, including a likely ethical or philosophical work. The process requires several independent checks: that an apparent line belongs to the papyrus surface, that the image feature is ink rather than a crack or imaging artifact, and that a proposed Greek letter sequence makes linguistic sense. New computation can make a historical witness accessible, but interpretation must still be checked by papyrologists.

This is a useful correction to the phrase 'lost knowledge.' Sometimes evidence was destroyed. Sometimes it is buried. Sometimes it sits in a cabinet but cannot yet be read. The three situations suggest different research programs. Excavation helps with the second; imaging may help with the third. Neither guarantees recovery of what has vanished entirely.

## Written history has people outside the archive

Who could write, commission a text, or afford its preservation? In many places and periods, administrative and elite documents are abundant relative to first-person statements by agricultural laborers, enslaved people, children and other people with limited institutional power. Their absence from a collection does not prove their absence from public life or intellectual life.

A census or legal record can give an enslaved person a number or a category selected by an administrator. A transaction may document the violence of ownership while withholding the person's own name, relationships or testimony. It is possible to use such records rigorously, but only by naming the perspective that produced them. We should not let the bureaucratic category stand in for the person. Household archaeology, cemetery evidence, material culture and surviving testimony can complicate a record written by someone with authority over another.

Nor is written documentation the only form of historical transmission. Oral traditions can preserve routes, genealogies, law, landscapes and remembered events. They can change over time, as written accounts do. Their reliability is best evaluated in terms of the claim and transmission practices involved, rather than granted or denied by a universal rule. A community may preserve knowledge through narratives, seasonal performance, ritual and material marks that outsiders have not learned to interpret.

The Smithsonian's National Museum of the American Indian describes winter counts among Northern Great Plains peoples: pictographic records, often on bison hides, associated with year-by-year histories. A surviving image may rely on a keeper and a living narrative to explain the event it denotes. Transcribing only the pictograph can erase information the form of record was designed to carry through speech. Respectful historical work may require collaboration with knowledge holders, attention to permissions and recognition that some knowledge is not meant for unrestricted publication.

There is an important distinction between an oral history interview and a centuries-old oral tradition. An interview can preserve someone's direct recollection of events they experienced. An inherited tradition may pass narratives across generations with different rules for continuity, teaching and transformation. Neither should be forced into the conventions of a state archive. Both can offer historical evidence, and their claims can sometimes be compared with archaeological, environmental or other testimony without treating the participants as mere sources of data.

## Cross-examining a document

Suppose a royal inscription asserts that a city was completely destroyed. Excavation reveals a burn layer and a phase of abandoned buildings. These facts may corroborate violence and destruction, but they do not prove that every resident was killed or that the ruler commanded all the events described. Another excavation might reveal people rebuilding nearby. The phrase 'completely destroyed' can be rhetorical even if a military campaign was real.

Try a different example: two merchants exchange letters about an undelivered shipment. Their accounts agree that a shipment was expected, but each blames the other. If a cargo seal appears in a dated warehouse deposit, that is another line of evidence. We can confidently recover the dispute and perhaps the existence of goods; we may never know who was acting in good faith. This is a sensible historical endpoint, not a failure of scholarship.

A useful source question has three parts. First, what did the artifact directly preserve: marks, material, date and context? Second, what reading of those marks is justified by linguistic and archaeological comparisons? Third, what event, institution or attitude might have generated a source of that kind? Each answer introduces assumptions. A source with excellent provenance and a secure translation can still leave the third question open.

Historical writing can give us the words of someone who lived thousands of years ago. That is a remarkable expansion of our evidence. But the words reached us through a series of choices about recording, storing, protecting, discovering and translating. If we keep that chain visible, a tablet can testify both to the transaction it records and to a society's institutions of memory. We also retain space for the people whose words were never inscribed.

| Early Mesopotamian institutions tracked allocations | Proto-cuneiform administrative tablets and numerical signs | Sign readings, archaeological context and comparison with other tablets | Strong evidence for recording; no guarantee each registered delivery occurred |

| A royal inscription records an event accurately | Wording of inscription | Corroboration with other records and material consequences | Secure reading can coexist with propagandistic claims |

**Model for archival selection.** Let a historical activity be an event \(H\). A record is observed only through successive filters of creation, preservation, recovery and interpretation. Symbolically, \(P(\text{record found}\mid H)\) depends on all these processes. This is a **conceptual model**, not a validated numerical probability. It explains why 'not mentioned in the archive' generally cannot be equated with 'did not occur'.

### Questions for study

1. A tablet lists grain deliveries. What is directly established? **Answer:** That somebody recorded the stated deliveries; independent records or material evidence are needed to confirm the deliveries occurred as described.
2. What made Maya decipherment convincing? **Answer:** Readings of dates, names and syllabic signs generalized across texts and matched linguistic and historical patterns, rather than fitting one monument ad hoc.
3. Why does digital unwrapping require expertise beyond image processing? **Answer:** Surface geometry, distinguishing ink from artifacts, and testing letter readings all affect the reconstructed text.
4. How might an archive systematically miss a group of people? **Answer:** Differential access to writing, deliberate retention, material decay, collecting practices and institutional cataloguing can all favor some voices.
