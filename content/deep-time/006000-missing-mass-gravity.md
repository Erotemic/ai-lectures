# Missing Mass: Dark Matter or Different Gravity?

Imagine you are watching a galaxy from far away. You cannot place a scale beneath it. You cannot measure its mass by lifting it, or even see most of its stars separately. What you can do is split its light into wavelengths, identify familiar atomic spectral lines, and measure how their wavelengths change from one side of the galaxy to the other. Those differences reveal motion. And motion, combined with a theory of gravity, gives us a way to weigh something that we cannot touch.

This is where the missing-mass problem began. There are several versions of it, and they should not be compressed into the sentence that scientists found invisible matter. Some observations establish motions; some map the bending of light; some measure patterns in the ancient microwave sky. Taken together, they strongly support an additional gravitating component in the current cosmological framework. They do not directly show us the microscopic identity of that component. Nor have all alternatives to the standard gravitational description been exhausted.

To understand why the problem persists, follow the actual chain of inference, beginning with a single orbit.

## A star at the edge

If a small object travels in a nearly circular orbit around a central mass, the gravitational pull supplies the centripetal acceleration. The required acceleration is the orbital speed squared, divided by the radius. For a spherical distribution, the Newtonian pull is the gravitational constant times the mass enclosed within that radius, divided by the radius squared. Equating the two gives a useful result: orbital speed squared equals the gravitational constant times enclosed mass, divided by radius.

Suppose almost all the galaxy's mass lies inside the star's orbit. As the orbit grows wider, the enclosed mass hardly increases. The predicted speed then declines as one over the square root of the radius. This is roughly what happens in the outer solar system, where the Sun contains nearly all the gravitating mass.

But a galaxy is not a solar system. A thin disk is not a sphere, and gas, dust, stars, and a bulge all contribute to its gravitational field. Astronomers model those distributions rather than blindly using the point-mass formula. They observe emission from hydrogen gas, including the twenty-one-centimeter radio line, and optical spectral lines in regions of star formation. After correcting for viewing angle, beam smearing, random gas motion, and other effects, they obtain rotation curves: orbital speed as a function of distance from the galactic center.

In many spiral galaxies those speeds remain approximately level well beyond the brightest stellar regions. If the mass traced by light and ordinary gas were the whole gravitating distribution under Newtonian gravity, the predicted curves would often decline more. A roughly flat outer speed implies that the mass enclosed by wider and wider radii continues to increase. A large, extended halo supplies an economical explanation. The halo's material would emit little or no detectable light, and its gravitational pull would keep the outer gas moving rapidly.

Be precise about this result. The observed quantity is the motion of emitting gas. Turning its speed into an inferred mass requires gravity and a dynamical model. The halo interpretation is powerful because it works across diverse systems and connects to other evidence, but no telescope photographs a cold dark matter particle when it measures a rotation curve.

## A remarkable relation inside those curves

The rotation-curve evidence contains a challenge for any easy story about invisible halos. In 2016, Stacy McGaugh, Federico Lelli, and James Schombert compared thousands of measurements from 153 disk galaxies. At each measured radius they estimated two accelerations: the actual centripetal acceleration inferred from rotation, and the Newtonian acceleration predicted by the visible stars and gas alone. Across their sample, these followed a tight relation.

In regions where the baryonic acceleration is high, the two values are nearly equal. In regions where it is very small, the observed acceleration is typically larger. The transition is associated with a characteristic acceleration of order one times ten to the minus ten meters per second squared. The narrowness of the observed relation is a real empirical constraint. One must explain why the dynamics track the distribution of ordinary matter so closely even when the discrepancy is greatest.

Mordehai Milgrom proposed modified Newtonian dynamics, usually called MOND, in the early 1980s. A simple phenomenological version says that at accelerations far below a scale called a naught, the effective acceleration behaves roughly like the square root of a naught times the Newtonian acceleration. If the baryonic mass is concentrated within a radius, the Newtonian acceleration declines as one over radius squared. Taking its square root produces a decline as one over radius. The orbital speed squared is radius times that acceleration. The radius cancels, producing an approximately flat rotation speed without an independently specified dark halo.

That is a concrete success of the low-acceleration idea. It also predicts a relation between the asymptotic rotation speed and the amount of baryonic mass. The question for a fair test is not whether MOND can be made to fit one striking curve, but whether it can describe the whole range of galaxies and be extended consistently to clusters, gravitational lensing, the microwave background, and cosmic structure.

The standard dark-matter framework must answer a different version of the same challenge. Its halos grow through gravitational instability and interact with gas that can cool, form stars, explode, and rearrange the central mass. Those processes can correlate baryonic distributions with the overall acceleration. Whether a particular simulation reproduces the observed scatter with the correct selection and measurement uncertainties is a quantitative question, not a fact settled by saying that galaxies contain halos.

## Weighing a galaxy cluster with bent light

Rotation curves involve an assumption about how matter moves. Gravitational lensing gives a different type of measurement. Mass-energy bends spacetime, and light from a background galaxy passing near a foreground cluster is distorted. With enough background images, astronomers infer a projected gravitational lensing field, again using a gravitational theory, usually general relativity.

Consider the Bullet Cluster, two galaxy clusters that passed through one another. Individual galaxies are so far apart that many pass with little direct collision. The hot plasma between them does interact, slowing and compressing, and it shines in X-rays. The stellar galaxies and the hot gas become separated in the sky. Now compare the X-ray map to the lensing reconstruction. The dominant lensing mass concentrations lie nearer the galaxies than the bulk of the hot gas. The hot gas is ordinarily the largest directly inventoried baryonic mass component of a rich cluster, so its displacement creates a stringent test.

Within general relativity, a substantial component that moves through the collision without behaving like the gas explains the separation naturally. The original lensing analysis reported a strong positional offset. This is among the most persuasive arguments for an unseen, relatively collisionless gravitating component.

Yet the popular image needs careful reading. The blue lensing map is a reconstruction from distorted background light; it is not direct illumination of dark matter. Its interpretation depends on the lensing equations and mass-model uncertainties. Modified-gravity proposals that aim to explain it must supply a relativistic lensing theory and, in many implementations, some additional unseen mass anyway. An alternative that works beautifully for isolated disk rotation curves has not therefore solved the collision problem.

The observational layers should remain separate: where X-ray photons came from; where galaxies are located; how background light is distorted; what gravitational mass distribution best explains those distortions. This is a stronger scientific argument than treating a processed composite photograph as a photograph of a substance.

## The older universe supplies a second experiment

The microwave sky constrains the gravitational inventory in a way that neither galaxy rotation nor cluster collisions can. Before neutral atoms became common, photons and charged ordinary matter formed an interacting plasma. Gravity compressed overdense regions while radiation pressure resisted. The result was an acoustic oscillation whose characteristic peaks remain in the angular correlations of the microwave background.

Ordinary matter and a cold component that does not participate in the photon plasma affect those peaks differently. Baryons add inertia to the oscillating plasma. Nonbaryonic matter contributes gravitational wells and changes the timing and growth of perturbations without sharing the same photon pressure. In the standard model, the relative peak heights, peak positions, damping tail, and lensing signal constrain the two components separately. Light-element abundances from primordial nucleosynthesis provide an independent check on the ordinary-matter density.

A popular number says that about eighty-five percent of matter is dark. That number is a ratio inferred in a particular cosmological model; it does not mean eighty-five percent of everything in the universe is matter. Dark energy enters the cosmic budget separately. The remarkable feature is that models of the early microwave plasma, galaxy clusters, and later large-scale clustering all point toward a missing gravitating component despite involving different instruments and physics.

Could gravity itself be different? Certainly gravity can be tested beyond the situations where it has already passed tests. A relativistic modified-gravity theory may add fields or alter how perturbations behave. But those theories must recover gravitational lensing, the observed sound-wave pattern, galaxy formation histories, and high-precision local tests. A simple modification of Newton's law at low acceleration is not by itself a complete cosmological alternative. Nor does successful cosmological fitting prove a particular dark-matter particle exists.

## Looking for the particle, not just its pull

If an extra component is physical matter, what could it be? It must survive for cosmological times, gravitate, and interact weakly enough with light to evade ordinary astronomical detection. Candidate families include weakly interacting massive particles, axions and axion-like fields, and other dark-sector possibilities. They are distinct hypotheses with different predicted masses, production histories, and laboratory signatures.

Direct-detection experiments often place extraordinarily sensitive detectors underground, shield them from ordinary radiation, and look for rare energy deposits. The LUX-ZEPLIN detector uses liquid xenon; an interaction can produce light and freed electrons. Nuclear recoils, electronic recoils, radioactive contamination, and neutrino interactions must be distinguished statistically. In August 2026 LZ reported evidence of coherent scattering of solar neutrinos off nuclei, alongside no significant light-dark-matter excess in the relevant search. This neutrino result is a demonstration that the detector can resolve a known but extremely rare process.

In September 2026 the collaboration also reported one high-energy nuclear-recoil-like event that was difficult to account for with its modeled background. When the analysis considered the range of searched hypotheses, the global significance was about two point six sigma. That is an anomaly worth following, and far below a particle-discovery claim. One candidate event cannot identify the cosmological dark component. Nor would a null result from one target and coupling model disprove the existence of all dark matter.

There are other approaches: missing energy at colliders, axion conversion in strong magnetic fields, gamma rays or cosmic rays from hypothetical annihilation, and laboratory tests of light new forces. Each probes some region of a vast space of possibilities. The absence of a confirmed laboratory discovery keeps particle identity in the open column of our evidence ledger.

## New maps, old caution

A very recent example shows how new instruments enlarge the evidence without instantly settling an interpretation. On November twelfth, 2026, the Euclid mission released its first major foundation data set, including a catalog of more than fifteen thousand strong-lensing candidates. These are possible or confirmed configurations in which foreground mass distorts the light of more distant sources. They increase the range of systems on which mass models can be tested.

But that release is a foundation catalog, not the final weak-lensing cosmology analysis. The mission plans its shear measurements and related higher-level products for later delivery. The difference between collecting candidate lenses and publishing a fully calibrated cosmological inference is exactly the distinction we need throughout deep history.

Here is the balance of the evidence. The mismatch between the gravitating field and the known baryonic inventory is supported by several independent classes of observation. Cold dark matter, treated as a gravitating component that interacts weakly with radiation, is an effective explanation across cosmological scales. Galaxy-scale acceleration regularities remain important tests of how dark matter and baryons interact during galaxy formation, and have given modified-dynamics theories genuine predictive successes. Those theories face hard requirements from relativistic lensing and the early universe. The identity of any dark particle, its interactions, and the final explanation of every galaxy-scale pattern remain unresolved.

A successful future discovery might be a particle recoil repeated with the expected statistics and corroborated elsewhere. It might instead be a gravitational theory that accounts for all these data with new, independently testable predictions. Until then, we know rather more about the gravitational effect than the microscopic cause. That distinction is a productive uncertainty, because it tells experimentalists precisely what a new observation must decide.

### Equations and reasoning checks

1. Circular orbit under spherical Newtonian gravity: \(v^2/r=GM(<r)/r^2\), hence \(v^2=GM(<r)/r\). For a thin disk use the disk potential instead of assuming spherical symmetry.
2. Outer flat speed \(v\simeq v_f\) corresponds under spherical Newtonian gravity to \(M(<r)\propto r\). This scaling cannot continue unchanged to infinite radius in a finite-mass halo.
3. Rotation-derived acceleration \(g_{obs}=v^2/r\); baryonic acceleration \(g_{bar}\) comes from a gravitational potential estimated from measured stars and gas. Empirical low-acceleration limit approximately \(g_{obs}\sim\sqrt{a_0g_{bar}}\), with \(a_0\) of order \(10^{-10}\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}\).
4. For approximately isolated baryonic mass \(M_b\), MOND's low-acceleration limit implies \(v_f^4\simeq G M_b a_0\); uncertainties and external-field effects complicate real galaxies.
5. Gravitational weak lensing measures a projected distortion field, not a local three-dimensional mass density directly; the inversion and cosmological distance factors depend on modeling.
6. A particle search excludes a range of masses and couplings under its chosen interaction model. It does not constrain every possible dark-sector theory equally.

### Study questions and answers

1. **What is directly measured in a rotation curve?** Spectral shifts and sky positions, converted to rotation velocities after geometric and instrumental corrections.
2. **Why does a flat rotation curve motivate additional enclosed mass?** With Newtonian gravity and a roughly spherical outer mass distribution, \(M(<r)=rv^2/G\) keeps increasing with radius if \(v\) stays flat.
3. **Why is the baryonic radial acceleration relation important to both sides?** It is an empirical regularity that modified dynamics can parameterize and dark-matter galaxy-formation models must reproduce.
4. **What makes a colliding cluster more stringent than a single rotation curve?** The X-ray plasma and lensing centers separate, allowing comparison of independently located baryons and gravitational field.
5. **What did LZ establish in 2026?** Evidence for solar-neutrino coherent scattering and a separate intriguing high-energy event, without a confirmed dark-matter detection.
6. **What would identify a microscopic candidate?** A reproducible signal with predicted coupling, mass or spectrum, assessed against backgrounds and independently corroborated.
