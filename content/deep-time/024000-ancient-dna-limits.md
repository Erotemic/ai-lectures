# What Ancient DNA Can and Cannot Tell Us

A bone fragment from a cave can look completely anonymous. Its surface may have no feature that identifies it as human rather than another mammal. If some of its molecules survived, however, a laboratory may detect sequences that place its former owner among lineages unknown from anatomy alone. This transformation changed the study of human history. But the laboratory has recovered chemical traces from one organism, not its language, its name, or a record of everyone who lived beside it.

Ancient DNA feels more authoritative than a stone tool because it contains letters resembling those used to represent genomes today. That impression can be misleading. The extracted molecules are fragmented, chemically damaged, mixed with DNA from microbes and later people, and sampled from a remarkably selective subset of ancient environments. Converting them into an ancestry history involves laboratory controls, alignments, statistical models and choices about which comparison populations are included.

The appropriate question is therefore not simply, 'What does the DNA say?' It is: Which molecules were recovered, why do we believe they are ancient, how were they compared, and which demographic histories can produce the observed pattern? We can follow that chain from a tooth to a claim about contact between populations.

## A damaged molecule is both a problem and evidence

After death, cellular repair stops. DNA breaks into short fragments and undergoes chemical changes. One well-known process converts some cytosine bases into uracil, leading sequencing procedures to read certain positions as thymine if the damage is not corrected. The ends of old fragments often show a characteristic pattern of these substitutions. This damage can corrupt an ancestral-sequence inference. It can also help distinguish a genuinely ancient molecule from a much cleaner recent contaminant.

Imagine processing a tooth in a laboratory. The exterior may carry DNA from excavators, museum handlers, conservation material and microbes. A researcher might clean its surface, sample inner material, extract short molecular fragments and build a sequencing library. The laboratory includes negative controls: extraction and library-processing procedures carried out without archaeological sample material. If human sequences appear abundantly in a blank, there is a problem. Independent replication, fragment-length distributions, terminal damage patterns and consistency across regions of the genome all contribute to authentication.

It would be wrong to call a result authentic merely because the sample was found in an old sediment layer. Equally, a failure to recover DNA does not mean the ancient organism lacked a lineage or was absent from that region. Preservation is strongly affected by temperature, moisture, microorganisms, soil chemistry and time. Cold caves have yielded data that warm humid environments often destroy. The geography of available genomes is therefore a geography of molecular preservation and excavation, too.

Researchers typically sequence many tiny pieces and align them to reference genomes. They observe which bases are read at each position and with what confidence. When only one fragment covers a position, that is not equivalent to knowing the true diploid genotype of the ancient person. Different pipelines handle low coverage through genotype likelihoods, pseudo-haploid sampling or restricted analyses. The resulting ancestry inferences must account for uncertainty rather than treating each missing or damaged base as a trustworthy identity card.

Sampling itself has a cost. Grinding a fragment of bone or tooth consumes part of an archaeological object. That object may hold cultural and familial significance to descendant communities. Decisions about destructive sampling, ownership, permitted research questions, data access and repatriation are therefore ethical and political decisions as well as technical ones. Scientific curiosity alone does not authorize unlimited sampling.

## How a previously unknown lineage appeared

The discovery of Denisovans illustrates the opportunity. Molecular studies of remains from Denisova Cave in Siberia identified a deeply divergent human-related lineage that was not first recognized from a complete, obviously distinctive skeleton. Ancient DNA revealed relationships among Denisovans, Neanderthals and modern humans, while later comparisons showed genetic contributions from archaic populations to some people living today.

The original genomic result did not reveal what Denisovans called themselves or which artifact style belonged exclusively to them. Indeed, later discoveries revealed that the population was geographically widespread. In 2024, proteomic and archaeological research at Baishiya Karst Cave on the Tibetan Plateau connected identifiable hominin remains and an associated animal assemblage to Denisovans. In September 2026, ancient proteins identified additional Denisovan remains from Bianfu Cave in southwest China, expanding the observable anatomy and geographic distribution of this lineage.

Proteins are another molecular archive. Some persist where intact DNA is scarce. Their amino-acid sequences can be compared with reference sequences to identify taxonomic relationships, sometimes distinguishing closely related hominins. But protein sequences usually supply far fewer variable positions than a high-quality whole genome. A secure protein-based identification can answer a question about who was present while leaving many questions about the person's ancestry, kinship and individual biology unresolved.

A species designation is not a genetic barcode with an infallible threshold. Ancient populations exchanged genes, lineages split and rejoined, and the definitions of taxa are contested. Genome sequences can reveal that a fossil belongs closer to one sampled lineage than another under a model. They do not automatically make every population boundary equivalent to a biological species boundary.

## Ancestry is an inference about shared variation

Suppose we sequence two ancient individuals and one person living today. We can count genetic positions where their samples share a particular allele. Some similarities reflect shared ancestry deep in the past. Others reflect more recent gene flow. Some may be accidental, caused by mutation at the same site, sequencing error or reference bias. Researchers therefore compare patterns across very many positions and across multiple populations, rather than relying on a few memorable variants.

A useful starting picture is a branching tree. If populations split and remain isolated, groups farther apart on the tree tend to share fewer newly derived variants. But human history violates permanent isolation. Populations meet and have descendants. The right model may be a graph, with branches representing population divergence and extra connections representing admixture. Both the branches and connections represent statistical summaries of population histories, not actual maps of individuals walking one by one.

One common statistical strategy compares four groups and asks whether the pattern of allele sharing is symmetrical under a proposed tree. Under the simplest no-admixture relationships, certain counts of shared derived alleles should balance on average. A consistent excess on one side can reject that simple tree. It suggests some additional relationship, often gene flow, but details depend on other assumptions and comparisons. A significant statistic may identify a model mismatch without uniquely identifying when, where and how many people migrated.

An estimate that a modern population carries a certain percentage of Neanderthal-related ancestry is a genome-wide historical estimate, generally conditional on reference genomes and demographic models. It is not a percentage of that population's 'humanity', intelligence or culture. All living people are equally members of Homo sapiens. Biological ancestry is not equivalent to an ethnic identity, linguistic community or cultural tradition, which can change rapidly through adoption, contact, conquest and individual choice.

## The timing of an encounter can be estimated from fragments

When two previously separated populations have children, each offspring receives pieces of chromosomes from both ancestries. Recombination in later generations cuts those inherited segments into shorter pieces. On average, long identifiable ancestry tracts suggest more recent admixture than very short ones, assuming a recombination model and suitable reference populations. This is a clock, but it does not work like a radiometric clock in a crystal. It uses an expected distribution of segment lengths and an assumed generation time.

A study published in December 2024, appearing in a 2025 journal volume, analyzed genomes associated with approximately forty-five-thousand-year-old modern humans at Ranis, Germany, alongside the Zlatý kůň genome from present-day Czechia. The researchers inferred that the Neanderthal ancestry shared with sequenced non-Africans was introduced through admixture around forty-five to forty-nine thousand years ago. This is an estimate based on identifiable ancestry segments, direct archaeological chronology, recombination assumptions and the sampled lineages.

That result does not mean humans left Africa once, exactly forty-seven thousand years ago. Some modern-human dispersals outside Africa were earlier, and the genetic descendants of those movements may not be represented among living populations or the ancient individuals sampled so far. Nor does the inference exclude additional localized contacts with Neanderthals before or after the broadly shared event. A high-confidence segment-sharing inference can coexist with an incomplete history of migration.

A genome recovered from one person also does not represent everyone living in that person's region. The 2025 Ranis and Zlatý kůň work found that those individuals belonged to an early-diverging population with no detectable contribution to later sampled populations. This is a warning against drawing a modern population map backward and assigning every archaeological culture to the genetic ancestor of a present-day nation.

## What a negative result can mean

In 2026 researchers produced genetic data from twenty-seven late Neanderthals from sites in Belgium and France. The study found relationships among several regional individuals and did not detect recent gene flow from modern humans into the sampled Neanderthals, despite temporal overlap in the broader region. This is a result about the sampled genomes at a particular level of detection. It does not prove that no person in that region ever had a child with a member of another lineage. A smaller, unsampled event, or ancestry that left no detectable contribution, could escape the analysis.

Negative evidence is often subtle. If a study fails to find a lineage in a burial ground, possibilities include genuine absence, insufficient preservation, sampling limited to one social subgroup, or a period outside the migration of interest. The more specimens, places and dates investigators can compare, the more informative an absence becomes. But prehistoric population sizes are not reliably counted by the number of bodies excavated. Burial customs and preservation create major selection effects.

Sometimes a statistical model adds a 'ghost population', an unsampled ancestor whose contribution helps explain genetic sharing patterns. This can be a useful shorthand for missing ancestry. It is not evidence that researchers have excavated a mysterious separate species. Several different histories involving structure within known populations can produce similar statistical signals. A graph that fits the observed alleles must be tested against alternative graphs, dated genomes and archaeology before it becomes a historical narrative.

The apparent precision of a genome-wide plot also deserves care. A principal-component chart compresses millions of markers into two or three axes selected to explain variation in the chosen sample. Move the reference populations and the axes can move. Cluster labels are tools for analysis. They are not natural ethnic categories and cannot recover ancient self-identification.

## Integrating the molecular and archaeological archives

What can ancient DNA tell us well? It can establish genetic relatedness among sequenced individuals, identify some archaic ancestry, reveal connections between ancient populations, and sometimes date episodes of mixture within defensible ranges. It can overturn the assumption that artifacts in two places must have been made by unrelated populations, or expose population changes hidden behind stable material styles. Where dated genomes form a temporal series, we can track ancestry shifts rather than inferring the whole past from people alive today.

What cannot it tell us by itself? It cannot recover speech, beliefs, social roles, political organization, or the complete set of migrations. Even genetic relatedness between skeletons does not tell us whether they lived together, spoke the same language or considered themselves part of the same community. Archaeological objects, isotopes, settlement patterns and ancient environmental records supply different constraints. Oral histories and knowledge maintained by living communities can inform research questions and the interpretation of places, without being reduced to genetic categories.

Consider two possibilities. In one, a new pottery style spreads through trade while the people using it remain largely the descendants of their region's earlier inhabitants. In another, many migrants arrive and bring the style with them. Pottery alone may not distinguish these processes. Genome data from carefully dated people before and after the change can help. But a measured change in genetic ancestry still does not tell us whether the pots were traded, copied locally, or learned from migrants. Material and genetic changes have related but different histories.

The strongest account is built by aligning independent, dated archives while keeping the uncertainties visible. Molecular degradation and contamination govern which sequences are trustworthy. Population-genetic assumptions govern the movement from sequences to ancestry models. Archaeology and context govern the movement from ancestry models to a historical episode. Ethics governs whether and how samples may be taken and interpretations shared.

A tooth can preserve a real connection between families separated by tens of thousands of years. That is a major scientific achievement. It becomes more informative, rather than less, when we refuse to make it answer questions it cannot answer. Next we will combine these molecular constraints with sea levels, archaeological dates and terrain to reconstruct how human populations reached different parts of the world.

## Companion: a chain of molecular and demographic inference

| Stage | Data | Supported statement | Failure or ambiguity |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Extraction | Short fragments from dated bone, tooth or sediment | Molecules recovered from an archaeological sample | Contamination, mixing, destruction |
| Authentication | Lengths, terminal damage, blanks, independent controls | Ancient origin supported for certain fragments | Different preservation and treatment effects |
| Sequence inference | Read bases, quality, coverage, alignments | Genotype likelihood or variant observation | Mapping bias, sequencing errors, deamination |
| Allele sharing | Frequencies and patterns among reference populations | Support for relationships, admixture or demographic structure | Alternative population graphs may fit |
| Segment lengths | Distribution of introgressed tracts | Approximate generations since mixture | Recombination rate, selection, multiple pulses |
| Historical interpretation | Genomes plus dates, artifacts, isotopes, context | Population interactions within constrained settings | Language, identity, individual migration not observed |

**Simple model:** After \(g\) generations, the length of an introgressed ancestry tract measured in Morgans is often approximated with exponential scale \(1/g\) under a single-pulse, neutral, randomly recombining model. This is a useful intuition, not an unconditional dating formula. Selection, chromosome position, multiple mixture episodes and detection thresholds affect the distribution.

**Four-population logic:** An allele-sharing statistic testing patterns conventionally labeled ABBA and BABA asks whether the two patterns occur at equal rates under a specified tree. A difference can reject that tree but does not identify a unique causal history without additional samples and assumptions.

### Questions for study

1. Why can terminal DNA damage help authentication? **Answer:** Ancient fragments often show reproducible end-specific chemical changes that recent contaminants are less likely to share.
2. Does the Ranis ancestry estimate date the first departure of modern humans from Africa? **Answer:** No. It dates a particular shared admixture history under a population model.
3. What is a ghost population? **Answer:** An unsampled ancestry component proposed to explain genetic patterns; it need not be a newly identified species.
4. Why should a genome-based ancestry estimate not be treated as ethnicity? **Answer:** Biological ancestry and cultural or political identity are different attributes and operate on different historical processes.
