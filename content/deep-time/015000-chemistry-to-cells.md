# From Chemistry to Cells: What Origin-of-Life Research Has Achieved

In the previous lecture we met microbial communities preserved in ancient rocks. By the time they left those traces, evolution had already solved many difficult problems: gathering resources, maintaining boundaries, reproducing, and passing functional information from one generation to the next. How did such a system begin?

One temptation is to start with a complete modern cell and ask for a miniature version of it. That approach creates a problem almost immediately. A modern cell uses proteins to copy genetic information, genetic information to specify proteins, membranes to keep its components together, and metabolism to power all these operations. Each activity seems to depend on the others. We should not assume that the first evolving systems already possessed every component of modern biology.

A better approach is to ask what smaller achievements the physical world can produce and which of them can become coupled. Can raw materials form complex organic molecules? Can some of those molecules form chains that retain information? Can small compartments assemble and reproduce? Can a chemical network use an energy gradient to sustain further reactions? And, most importantly, can a population of such systems transmit differences so that useful variations increase over generations?

Researchers have demonstrated important parts of this sequence. Nobody has demonstrated that one particular laboratory pathway is the path that actually occurred on early Earth. A laboratory experiment can establish feasibility under specified conditions. Geological reconstruction requires an additional chain of evidence connecting those conditions to the ancient planet.

## Simple ingredients, difficult context

The early Earth supplied water, carbon-bearing gases, nitrogen compounds, minerals, metals, sunlight, electrical discharges and abundant temperature gradients. Those ingredients allow a large range of chemistry. The familiar mid-twentieth-century Miller–Urey experiment demonstrated that organic compounds including amino acids could be produced from simple gases and electric discharges under one set of simulated atmospheric conditions.

That experiment did not create life, nor did it establish the composition of the entire early atmosphere. Its lesson is narrower and valuable: molecules useful to biology need not themselves come from organisms. Subsequent work has explored different gas mixtures, ultraviolet-driven reactions, minerals, meteoritic deliveries and reactions in water. We can make many components of biology by several plausible chemical routes. The difficult question is which combinations were available together, in what concentrations, for how long, and without being destroyed by competing reactions.

Consider a simple carbon-containing molecule dissolved in water. Even if it can react to form something more complex, it may be far too dilute. The desired reaction may be slower than hydrolysis, the process by which water helps break bonds. Salt concentrations that stabilize one component may destabilize another. Ultraviolet light can supply energy but also destroy products. To judge an origin scenario, we must study the entire cycle of supply, concentration, reaction, removal and replenishment.

Environmental diversity can help. An evaporating pool concentrates dissolved compounds. Rain or a flood dilutes them and transports products elsewhere. A porous rock offers surfaces and compartments. A hydrothermal vent supplies fresh chemical reactants and a steep difference in temperature or acidity. None of these places is a known birthplace; they are laboratories of possible reactions.

## The problem of making a chain

Amino acids can join into peptides. Nucleotides can join into nucleic acid polymers. But a bag of monomers cannot store and transmit an ordered sequence in the way a sufficiently long nucleic acid chain can. The formation of bonds matters because sequence opens new possibilities: molecules with differing catalytic and binding behavior, and eventually templates that guide production of similar molecules.

Water creates a familiar paradox. Life needs water, but some polymer-forming reactions must overcome the tendency of water to reverse the bond-forming process. Researchers therefore examine minerals and wet–dry cycles. In work published in 2024, experiments exposed nucleic acid monomers to repeated wetting and drying and reported formation of longer nucleic-acid chains using mass spectrometry and nanopore-based analysis. This shows that repeated physical cycling can help polymer formation under the experiment's chemical conditions.

It does not show that the products constituted an autonomous RNA organism. We must still ask about the sequence distribution, reaction selectivity, molecular stability, and whether newly produced strands can copy information with useful accuracy. It is also necessary to ask whether the starting monomers would have been available in the same purity and concentration on early Earth.

A 2025 study explored another possibility: RNA growth and template copying at clay–water interfaces undergoing oscillating conditions. Its authors constructed a theoretical and computational model in which those interfaces could support nonenzymatic polymerization and replication under specified environmental cycles. The result is an interesting prediction, and may suggest experiments. It is a simulation-based mechanism, not direct geological evidence that an ancient tide pool copied RNA, and not an experimental demonstration of the complete process.

These different approaches need not exclude one another. A mineral surface might concentrate or assemble molecules; later a membrane could collect them. Or chemical networks could become organized before information-rich polymers were prevalent. The order remains open.

## Can an RNA molecule do two jobs?

In modern cells, DNA is a durable information store, proteins perform much of the chemical work, and RNA carries messages and also performs essential structural and catalytic roles. Some RNA molecules, called ribozymes, catalyze chemical reactions. This makes RNA a candidate for an earlier system in which related molecules could both carry heritable information and facilitate their own or one another's production.

The RNA-world idea does not require us to imagine a complete modern cell containing only RNA. It proposes a historical stage in which RNA-like information and catalysis were more central, perhaps supported by small molecules, metals, short peptides, or mineral surfaces. The precise nature of that environment remains unresolved.

Laboratories have evolved catalytic RNA molecules and studied template-directed extension. Those experiments show that selection and molecular function can arise in RNA-based systems. But replication is hard. Strands must be assembled from ingredients, copied without excessive errors, separated again so that copying can repeat, and protected from destructive chemistry. An isolated copying reaction is a useful module; it is not a self-sustaining prebiotic ecosystem.

A 2026 review by Paul Higgs examined the experimental case for RNA-first scenarios. It argued that substantial chemical progress supports the general idea while emphasizing unresolved challenges, including replication, chirality, and integration with metabolism and compartments. A careful lecture can regard RNA-first as a serious research program without declaring it a solved history of Earth's origin.

One alternative emphasizes metabolic networks. Instead of beginning with hereditary polymers, chemical cycles driven by environmental energy could have generated useful compounds and repeated patterns. Iron–sulfur minerals and hydrogen-rich hydrothermal systems are proposed settings for reactions that resemble fragments of carbon fixation. This family of ideas faces a different version of the inheritance problem. If a chemical network can persist while every component freely diffuses away, what exactly is copied between generations? What permits adaptive change rather than mere chemical equilibrium?

Proponents of RNA-first and metabolism-first scenarios often study complementary problems. The historical outcome could have involved coevolution: rudimentary compartments, catalysts, energy coupling and templating processes emerging in overlapping stages.

## A container is more than a bag

A fatty molecule can have one part attracted to water and another repelled by it. Under suitable conditions, such molecules self-assemble into sheets that close into vesicles: little enclosed volumes with a membrane-like boundary. This can happen without enzymes or genes. A compartment keeps some products close to one another and allows chemical conditions inside to differ from those outside.

But a membrane is a tradeoff. If it leaks everything, it fails to preserve a distinct interior. If it seals too effectively, nutrients cannot enter and waste cannot leave. A proto-membrane must possess some useful permeability and must remain stable in the available temperature, salt and acidity conditions. Modern phospholipid membranes are sophisticated biological products; the earliest containers may have used simpler chemistry.

In 2025, researchers reported that the amino acid cysteine could react with short-chain thioesters to generate diacyl lipids that assemble into vesicles in water. This demonstrates one possible route from simple reactants to membrane-like structures. It does not establish that those reactants coexisted in the right abundances in a particular early-Earth location, or that the vesicles grew into the first cells.

A second 2025 experiment reported self-reproduction of fully abiotic polymer vesicles through photochemical reactions and physical feedback. It is an instructive result because some behavior associated with cells—growth, boundary formation and reproduction—can arise without the biochemical machinery of an organism. The experiment also warns against defining life through one visual resemblance. A reproducing vesicle may have no inherited sequence that undergoes open-ended evolution.

Now bring in heredity. In 2024 a research team built a synthetic protocell system in which a DNA replicator could undergo adaptive evolution inside lipid vesicles over experimental rounds. That is a remarkable engineering demonstration of coupling compartmentalization with genetic change. Yet it relied on sophisticated preexisting biological machinery, including a protein synthesis system and a phage-derived DNA polymerase. It should never be presented as the spontaneous origin of DNA replication from raw minerals. It shows that the modules can be integrated, while deliberately supplying some of the hardest components.

Taken together, these experiments demonstrate multiple attainable functions, but their boundary conditions differ. The results cannot simply be placed in chronological order to manufacture a verified narrative of ancient Earth. The assembly problem is to find a continuous pathway in which each intermediate stage can form and persist under compatible conditions.

## What energy must accomplish

A chemical system needs available free energy to maintain non-equilibrium structure. Picture warm fluid emerging from a hydrothermal vent and mixing with colder water. Differences in temperature, acidity and chemical composition can drive reactions. Or picture light initiating reactions at a surface while products are concentrated by evaporation. A gradient supplies a potential way to do chemical work; whether a particular reaction proceeds still depends on kinetics, catalysts and the rest of its environment.

Chemists describe reaction favorability with changes in Gibbs free energy under specified conditions. When a reaction has negative Gibbs free energy, it is thermodynamically favored in that setting; a favorable reaction can nevertheless be kinetically slow. A positive free-energy reaction may proceed when coupled to another reaction that releases more energy. Life relies on exactly this kind of coupling, but simpler chemical networks may have exploited it before enzymes existed.

The central challenge is persistence across cycles. Suppose a reaction produces an RNA strand once, but the next heating step destroys every strand. We have demonstrated synthesis, but not a path to cumulative evolution. Suppose a vesicle divides but its contents are randomly lost each time. We have demonstrated reproduction of a structure, but perhaps not heritable function. Suppose a compartment contains a catalyst that accelerates its own formation and copies are passed into daughter compartments. Now selection becomes possible. We would need to measure its fidelity, survival and variation rather than merely call it 'alive.'

We can construct a thought experiment with two kinds of protocells. Type A makes copies slightly faster, but is fragile; type B is slower but survives drying. After one cycle A may dominate. After a month of alternating wet and dry conditions B may dominate. Selection belongs to an environment and a reproductive process. It is not enough to identify a molecule that works well in isolation.

## Places, alternatives, and missing bridges

Surface-pool scenarios can exploit wet–dry cycles, sunlight and concentration. Their challenges include fluctuating water supply, damaging ultraviolet radiation and the stability of fragile polymers. Submarine hydrothermal settings offer continuing gradients and mineral pores. Their challenges include dilution, high salinity or temperature in some locations, and the need to couple products to an inheritable system. Neither category is one uniform environment; both contain large variations.

Lipid-first scenarios emphasize boundary formation and compositional selection. RNA-first scenarios emphasize information and catalysis. Metabolism-first scenarios emphasize sustained chemistry and energy. These are research programs with overlapping experiments and distinct missing links. Some hypotheses suggest that organic building blocks arrived from asteroids or comets. The presence of such compounds in space is supported by chemistry and meteorite samples, but importing ingredients is different from showing that organisms arrived already alive. Panspermia in its strong form moves the question of origins elsewhere and requires independent evidence of transport and survival.

What have we actually reconstructed about the event on Earth? Very little in detail. We do not know its date, location, sequence of innovations, or whether there were multiple attempts and extinct early lineages. Laboratory feasibility studies are valuable precisely because they narrow which physical mechanisms are plausible. If a scenario demands an impossible reaction under every tested reasonable condition, it loses credibility. If a complete self-maintaining hereditary cycle can be built from plausible ingredients, it gains credibility. Even that achievement would not reveal where our own ancestors lived without independent geological or biological evidence.

We should end with a distinction about confidence. We know that ordinary chemistry can generate many organic building blocks. We know that membranes can self-assemble and that catalytic and hereditary functions can be experimentally investigated, and sometimes combined using engineered support. We have plausible but incomplete candidate pathways toward evolving protocells. We do not yet possess a uniquely evidenced route from Earth's prebiotic environments to the ancestors of all modern organisms.

In the next lecture, the surviving geological record becomes much stronger. Once life was established, biological activity and the planet's chemistry began reshaping one another. We will follow the rise of oxygen through mineral and isotope archives, and ask why one of the most consequential biological products took so long to accumulate in the atmosphere.

**Thermodynamics:** \(\Delta G=\Delta G^\circ+RT\ln Q\) for reaction quotient \(Q\). Favorable \(\Delta G<0\) does not imply rapid kinetics. For coupled reactions, free-energy changes add, but the coupling mechanism must physically exist.

**Evolutionary bookkeeping:** A system with reproduction, variation and heritable differential survival can exhibit selection. Division without faithful transmission of relevant differences does not establish Darwinian evolution.
