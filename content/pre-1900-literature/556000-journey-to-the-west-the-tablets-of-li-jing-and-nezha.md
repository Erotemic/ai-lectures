# Journey to the West: The tablets of Li Jing and Nezha

## Victory at the cave mouth

The woman had carried Xuanzang to the surface because Sun Wukong was inside her belly and would not stop hurting her until she obeyed. Now the master stood beside Bajie and Sha Wujing, breathing open air. The demon opened her mouth and begged the monkey to leave. Wukong approached the throat carefully. She could still close her teeth and bite him as he emerged. He therefore made his iron staff into a sharp little brace, set it where the jaws could not shut, and sprang outward. In the same motion he reclaimed the staff and grew back to his fighting size.

The woman drew her two swords. She had been reduced to carrying her prisoner like a servant, yet humiliation had not robbed her of courage. Wukong attacked. Steel flashed in front of the dark opening, and the pair fought across the stony slope. Bajie watched with growing impatience. Why had Wukong come out of the demon's belly alive when he could have struck her dead from within? Sha Wujing replied that the important thing was that their master was safely out. The demon, however, still lived. They might help their elder brother finish the matter.

Bajie liked the sound of finishing a matter. He took up his rake; Sha swung his staff; they rushed to Wukong's side. All three attacked at once. The demon could not resist the pressure for long. She slipped away, and where they thought she was standing a second figure appeared brandishing the same two swords. Bajie struck it down triumphantly. But at his feet lay an embroidered shoe, not a dead enemy. It was the same trick the woman had used at Zhenhai Monastery. While the disciples fought the substitute, she had returned to the cave entrance.

Xuanzang was seated alone nearby. He heard a familiar voice and looked up to see the woman beside him. Before he could shout a warning, she seized him, gathered up the baggage, broke the white horse's bridle, and vanished with all three into the depths. When Wukong turned, he found only an empty place and half a length of rein lying on the ground.

The monkey picked it up. His anger was so sharp that for a moment he could not speak. He had brought Xuanzang from the cave by a trick of extraordinary delicacy, and his brothers had abandoned the very task that made the rescue worth undertaking. Tears came to his eyes. Bajie, still full of the pleasure of striking the false demon, tried to turn the disaster into another joke. The third attempt, he said, would surely succeed. Wukong gave him no answer. He placed the horse and baggage, now absent, above all quarrels, instructed the others to guard the opening, and dropped back underground.

## An empty palace and two names

He returned to the pavilion where the demon had entertained Xuanzang. Its doors were shut. Wukong forced them open, expecting once more to find the beautiful courtyard and a reluctant prisoner behind the lattice. Instead the residence stood stripped of its furnishings. Servants, dishes, and master had all disappeared. The buried country was large; she had taken advantage of its hidden passages and could be anywhere within it. Wukong stamped in fury and shouted Xuanzang's name, but no one answered.

Then he noticed a thread of incense smoke drifting from a rear chamber. The household had removed its tables and decorations, yet left one object undisturbed: an altar bearing a censer and two memorial tablets. Wukong read them carefully. One honored Li Jing, the Pagoda-Bearing Heavenly King, as a respected father. The other honored Nezha, Li Jing's third son, as an elder brother. Both names belonged to the heavenly armies Wukong had encountered in the days of his rebellion.

The discovery pleased him more than a captured guard could have done. He no longer needed to search every hidden valley inside the cave. If this demon truly belonged to Li Jing's household, then the household would have to answer for her. He tucked his staff behind his ear, took the tablets and censer, and returned to the opening laughing. Bajie and Sha rushed forward, expecting to see their master. They were astonished to find him displaying pieces of furniture.

"Is that Xuanzang in your hands?" Bajie demanded. "Then why is he so flat?"

Wukong set the tablets before them and explained his inference. The woman called Li Jing her father and Nezha her brother; therefore the Pagoda-Bearing King's family could supply the missing answers. He would go to Heaven and lodge a complaint. Bajie was not so easily reassured. A lawsuit against heavenly nobles was no small game, and a false charge might bring its own punishment. Wukong replied that the tablets were evidence. If an officer had failed to keep his family from preying upon travelers, someone should be made to ask what had happened.

He even composed a formal accusation as they listened: the Tang monk had been abducted, lives had been lost, a female demon claimed kinship with Li Jing and Nezha, and those bearing responsibility for her should be summoned before the Jade Emperor. It was a strange sight: a monkey who had once waged war against Heaven preparing a legal memorial for its ruler. Bajie could not fault the ingenuity. He urged Wukong to return quickly before another ceremony or imprisonment could endanger the master.

## A case before Heaven

Wukong crossed the clouds and passed through the Southern Gate. He was no longer an unknown enemy of the heavenly court. The guards recognized the Great Sage and made way. In the halls he met celestial officials who looked uneasily at the tablets under his arm. The Monkey King's lawsuits were not expected to be simple. He was ushered before the Jade Emperor, bowed, set out his evidence, and submitted his complaint. The emperor ordered Taibai Jinxing, the aged heavenly envoy who had negotiated with Wukong long ago, to summon Li Jing.

Li Jing received the imperial order at Cloud Tower Palace. He was already offended by the sight of Wukong. Centuries earlier he had commanded troops sent to subdue the rebel monkey and had failed. Now the same monkey stood at his door carrying an accusation against his family. The Heavenly King read the words and angrily denied the central claim. He had three sons: Jinzha, Muzha, and Nezha. His daughter was a little child named Zhenying. None was the adult monster who held the Tang monk.

Wukong did not apologize. The altar, he answered, was not an invention of his own. He had brought it from the demon's house. Li Jing was convinced the Monkey King had committed the offense of a malicious accusation and ordered guards to bind him. A sword was fetched. Taibai Jinxing warned that Wukong had arrived under an imperial command, but the Heavenly King's temper had gone beyond caution. Wukong remained extraordinarily calm. He had been tied up before, and the sword had not yet been made that could frighten him by being displayed.

The stroke was stopped by another blade. Nezha stood between the Heavenly King and the prisoner, holding his sword across his father's weapon. Li Jing's anger turned instantly to fear. On an ordinary day he carried a golden pagoda in his hand, a token and instrument by which the ancient hostility between father and son had been restrained. At this moment it had been set aside. He snatched it up again before asking Nezha why he had intervened.

Nezha's family history was no ordinary quarrel. The novel recalls his birth with signs upon his hands, his early violence in the waters, his conflict with his father, and his terrible return of flesh and bones to his parents. After death his spirit sought aid from the Buddha, who restored him with lotus root and leaf. His anger toward Li Jing had remained, until the Buddha placed the restraining pagoda in the father's keeping. Even while helping Wukong with a rescue, Nezha was bringing into the scene a history of damage, fear, and uneasy reconciliation.

But he had stopped the sword for another reason. His father had forgotten a promise.

## The daughter who was not born in the house

Some three hundred years before, Nezha explained, an animal spirit had stolen incense offerings and sacred lamps at the Buddha's seat. Li Jing and Nezha were sent to seize the offender. The spirit was a golden-nosed, white-furred rat. The captors could have killed her. Instead, at the Buddha's instruction, they spared her life. She was so grateful that she adopted Li Jing as her father and Nezha as her brother, honoring them thereafter with incense and memorial tablets.

The rat spirit had since acquired other names. She had once been called Half-Cut Guanyin, and now styled herself Lady Earth-Flow. The names did not make her the Heavenly King's blood daughter, but the relationship of obligation was real enough for her to keep the tablets and for Wukong to discover them. Li Jing stared at his son. The account was true. He had indeed forgotten the old act of mercy and the trouble to which it might lead.

He moved to release Wukong. The monkey would not let him. Having been tied up and threatened with execution, he now insisted on remaining bound while he was carried before the Jade Emperor. Let the emperor see how a summons for a family inquiry had been answered! Li Jing's confidence vanished. Nezha looked miserable. Taibai Jinxing, who had hoped for an orderly investigation, found himself negotiating with the injured plaintiff instead of the supposed defendant.

The old envoy appealed to Wukong's memory. Who had urged Heaven to recruit the Monkey King, instead of beginning with war? Who had obtained his early title, even if the stable-keeper's position had proved less flattering than promised? Who had sought to make peace when the heavenly armies could not manage him? Wukong conceded that the old man had worked on his behalf. He agreed to be untied if the family helped find Xuanzang without further delay.

The agreement nearly foundered on another point. Taibai Jinxing proposed to report that the original plaintiff had withdrawn rather than pursue a public accusation. Wukong protested at being made to look like a fugitive. But the envoy reminded him of the difference between time in Heaven and time on earth. The longer they disputed forms and charges, the longer Xuanzang remained in a demon's power. Wukong remembered his master's condition, accepted the practical settlement, and required Li Jing and Nezha to lead troops to the cave at once.

## The lower chamber

The heavenly soldiers assembled at the Southern Gate. Li Jing and Nezha descended with Wukong to Trap-Sky Mountain. Bajie and Sha were almost past hope when the sky filled with armed figures and celestial banners. The Heavenly King apologized for the delay. It was his household's old obligation, he acknowledged, that had allowed the rat spirit to claim protection. Wukong showed them the small entrance and warned them that the hidden world below was far larger than the opening suggested.

Nezha and Wukong led the search. The soldiers moved through the bright underground country, opening gates, checking chambers, and clearing paths. They found the first abandoned residence and then one empty building after another. To the weary men the cave seemed impossible to search; its scale made every confident order sound small. Yet the rat spirit had not left the place. She had moved into a cramped recess in a dark southeastern corner, believing that a grand army would look for a grand palace and overlook a low little room.

A soldier found an attendant at the doorway. The attendant shouted a warning, but too late. Wukong forced his way in, and Nezha followed with the troops. Xuanzang was there; so were the white horse and the luggage. For once nothing had been eaten or destroyed. The demon saw Nezha and abandoned her weapons. She begged the heavenly prince for mercy, recognizing the elder brother she had honored with incense for centuries.

Nezha did not pretend she was innocent. She had been spared once, and she had repaid that mercy by killing and abducting. This time she was bound under the Jade Emperor's authority and taken to answer in Heaven. Bajie and Sha wanted immediate punishment for the wrongs their master had suffered. Li Jing refused to let them kill a prisoner seized under imperial order. There would be an accounting beyond the mouth of the cave.

The pilgrims emerged into daylight. Xuanzang bowed to the Heavenly King and Nezha, then thanked his disciples. Bajie recovered the horse; Sha repacked the baggage; Wukong finally had no reason to descend into Bottomless Cave again. The rat spirit was marched away under guard. The road west lay open.

The pilgrimage had crossed a second hidden country and survived another assault on Xuanzang's freedom. But the lesson Wukong carried away was not that every demon could be defeated by a stronger staff. Some were protected by old favors, powerful names, and obligations Heaven itself had forgotten. When the next kingdom threatened them, he would remember how useful it could be to make a ruler look at what his own authority had permitted.
