# Journey to the West: The king who returned

## One pill from Heaven

By the time Xuanzang stopped reciting the tightening spell, Wukong was willing to promise almost anything. Bajie had made a sport of the rescue, turning the monkey's own trick into a demand that he revive a king who had been dead for three years. The body lay upon the floor of Baolin Temple, dressed in soaked royal robes, its features unchanged by the water that had hidden it.

Wukong offered one solution. He could travel to the underworld, find which judge held the king's soul, and bring the spirit back. He had once forced the officials of death to strike his own name from their registers. He knew the way.

Bajie objected at once. He had already heard Wukong promise a cure in the living world. The monk ought not to accept a different kind of miracle merely because it was easier for the monkey to arrange.

Xuanzang recited the first words of the spell again.

'In the living world!' Wukong cried. 'I will do it in the living world. Only stop the pain.'

There was a medicine, he explained, kept above the thirty-third heaven in the palace of Taishang Laojun. It was called a Nine-Revolution Soul-Restoring Pill. If one could obtain a single pill from the old master of the alchemical furnace, it might restore life to the drowned king.

Xuanzang told him to go quickly.

Before leaving, Wukong demanded that someone mourn beside the body. A king who had been dead for years deserved proper lamentation, and the arrangement would make the chamber look as though a real funeral were being held. He fixed his eyes on Bajie.

The pig knew what was coming. He had been wet, cold, and cheated of his promised treasure. Now he would be expected to cry all night for the person he had carried.

Wukong explained that there was a difference between making noise and mourning properly. A man could howl without tears; he could force tears without true sorrow; but only when voice, tears, and grief were joined could it be called a great lament. Bajie must demonstrate that last kind.

Bajie found a piece of paper, rolled it into a thin twist, and pushed it into his nose. He sneezed several times. His eyes watered. Then he began to wail with such dedication that Xuanzang himself grew sad at the sound.

Wukong approved. He warned Bajie not to stop while he was away. Sha Wujing lit incense by the body. The whole room began to resemble a mourning hall, except that the most demonstrative mourner had made himself cry with paper and seemed privately delighted by his success.

The Monkey King sprang into the sky.

He passed the gates of Heaven without calling on the Jade Emperor, flew beyond the familiar celestial palaces, and went directly to Laozi's furnace. Within the Tushita Palace attendants tended the fire and worked the plantain fans that helped sustain it. Their master was seated among the vessels when Wukong arrived.

Laozi's expression changed immediately.

'Watch the pills,' he told his attendants. 'The thief has come back.'

Wukong protested that he had reformed. He was an honorable disciple of Xuanzang, performing a work of compassion. A venerable immortal ought to greet him as a visitor, not accuse him before the servants.

Laozi recited the old account: the monkey's theft of immortality pills, his capture, forty-nine days in the furnace, and the destruction that followed his escape. Then he remembered something more recent. Had they not just met at Flat-Top Mountain, where Wukong had been very reluctant to return the five treasures belonging to Laozi?

Wukong said he had returned every single one. It was not his fault that Laozi's attendants had been ruling a cave and trying to eat his master. But he had not come to argue over those matters. He needed a medicine for a drowned king.

He gave the full account: the drought in Wuji, the Daoist stranger, the murder by the well, three years of impersonation, the ghost who appealed to Xuanzang, and the king's body pulled from the water. Then he made his request.

'A thousand soul-restoring pills will do.'

Laozi stared at him.

A thousand? Were these pills lumps of clay to be scooped up by the handful? The old master ordered Wukong to leave.

'Then a hundred,' Wukong offered.

No.

'Ten?'

Still no.

Wukong sighed and made as though to go away. He would find some other immortal to help, he said. Surely another palace might have a spare pill.

Laozi watched him turn toward the door and grew anxious. The monkey was no longer asking for medicine; that did not mean he would leave empty-handed. Once out of sight he might slip into a storeroom and take as many as he could carry.

The old master called him back.

He took a gourd from his medicine shelf and shook a single golden pill into his hand. One pill, he said. No more. It was a gift for the work of rescuing the king, not a supply for the Monkey King's private use.

Wukong accepted it, then tossed it into his mouth.

Laozi seized him by the head. He had warned the attendants against theft, and now the thief seemed to be swallowing the one pill he had been given to help a dying man. Wukong laughed and showed that he had hidden it in the pouch of his cheek. He was only testing whether the medicine was genuine, he said.

Laozi pushed him toward the door. The pill was real. The king could be revived. And the fewer minutes Wukong remained in the palace, the better.

## Breath for the drowned king

When Wukong descended to Baolin Temple, the sun was climbing into the morning sky. Bajie was still beside the body, weeping loudly enough to be heard outside the gate. The paper twist had done its work, and he now seemed determined to prove that he could mourn longer than anyone expected.

Wukong congratulated him. The crying could stop. He had brought the pill.

Bajie wiped his eyes and claimed that of course Wukong had succeeded. A monkey with a habit of stealing treasures could always obtain a little medicine if he was sufficiently motivated.

Sha Wujing brought water. Wukong took the golden pill from his cheek, placed it between the dead king's lips, and washed it down his throat. For a long while nothing happened. Xuanzang watched anxiously. The king had lain in water for three years, and whatever the Dragon King had done to preserve his appearance had not restored his life.

Then a sound came from within the body. There was movement in the stomach, the faint stirring of an interior that had been utterly still. Wukong looked at Xuanzang and complained that Laozi might have given him worthless medicine.

Xuanzang had studied enough to recognize the sign. The pill had begun to restore what had been lost, he said, but the breath of life was still absent. If someone could give the king one clean breath, the medicine might complete its work.

Bajie stepped forward. He was perfectly capable of breathing, he said, and very experienced at it.

Xuanzang stopped him. He remembered the pig's former life of violence and consumption, and in the novel's language he judged his breath impure. Wukong, who had once lived on fruit, peaches, and the food of mountains, was to give the breath instead.

The Monkey King bent over the body and breathed into the king's mouth.

For a moment there was silence. Then the body convulsed, the limbs moved, and the drowned monarch opened his eyes.

He sat upright. The world had changed from darkness to morning, and the person before him was not the murderer who had looked into the well but a strange monkey and a monk wearing a pilgrim's robe. He remembered appearing as a ghost to Xuanzang. He had not expected to find himself alive when the sun rose.

He fell to his knees and thanked Xuanzang. The monk tried to direct his gratitude toward Wukong and his companions, but Wukong would not allow him to diminish his master's part in the rescue. They were together in the work, he said. The king could thank them all.

The temple monks arrived with breakfast and were astonished to see the waterlogged royal figure sitting among the travelers. Wukong explained what had happened. He had gone to Heaven for a medicine; the king had returned to life; now the party needed to enter Wuji and expose the impostor.

There could be no obvious royal procession. The king's garments would be recognized long before he reached the throne. The monks provided plain robes and worn shoes, replacing the clothes he had worn in the well. In simple clothing he looked less like a monarch than a humble attendant who had joined the pilgrims on the road.

Wukong asked Bajie to divide the baggage into two loads so the king could carry one. Bajie quickly arranged matters so that his own was lighter. He was pleased to have gained a porter, though he worried that the benefit would last only until they reached the city.

The king accepted the heavier load without complaint. After three years dead and a morning in which he had been restored to life, he did not regard carrying luggage for a few miles as a hardship. He would have followed the monks to India if they had asked.

They told him he need go only as far as the royal palace.

The five set out: Xuanzang on his white horse, Wukong alert beside him, Sha Wujing watching the road, Bajie with his lightened burden, and the reborn king carrying the other half. The monks of Baolin Temple came to the gate in ceremonial robes to see them off. Wukong urged them not to follow. A large public procession would announce the plan before the party could reach the throne.

## The stranger in his own palace

Wuji's streets were lively. Merchants argued over goods, travelers crossed the market squares, and officials moved between the gates. There was no obvious sign of a kingdom ruled by a demon. The fields had recovered from the old drought. The markets had food. People went about their days while the man who had governed them for three years sat in another man's place.

The true king walked among them in ordinary clothing, carrying the belongings of foreign monks. He saw the towers of his palace above the roofs and felt tears rise. Those buildings had held the shape of his life, the rooms where he had ruled, spoken with his wife, and watched his son grow. Now they had been used by the creature who pushed him into the well.

Wukong touched his arm and told him not to weep where guards might see. The restoration must be revealed at the right moment, not before.

At the palace gate Xuanzang presented his travel documents. He came from the Great Tang, he said, and requested the necessary endorsement for passage through the kingdom on the way to India. The guards reported the arrival to the throne and were ordered to admit the company.

Xuanzang was anxious about court etiquette. He told his disciples to bow properly to the king, no matter what they knew privately. Wukong refused. If they bowed to the monster, he said, they would give the imposture the very honor they had come to take away. He told the others to follow his example. If he bowed, they might bow. If he stood, they should stand.

They entered the hall without the expected ceremony.

Ministers lined the steps. Guards waited in their positions. On the throne sat a figure indistinguishable from the man carrying luggage behind the pilgrims. The false king wore the same face and spoke in the same voice, but he carried himself with the assurance of someone whose authority had not been challenged for years.

He demanded to know why foreign monks refused to pay respect to the ruler of Wuji.

Wukong answered that he was a disciple of Xuanzang, an envoy of the great Tang Empire. He had no reason to prostrate himself before a smaller country's ruler simply because that ruler sat on a throne.

The impostor grew angry. He ordered his officials to seize the pilgrims. Wukong pointed his finger and spoke a spell. The ministers and guards froze where they stood, unable to advance, their mouths open in the middle of commands.

The false king sprang down from the throne, ready to attack. Before he reached Wukong, the prince came forward and pleaded for restraint.

The young man remembered the conversation at Baolin Temple and his mother's private tears. He also knew that if he openly accused his apparent father, the court might divide before the truth could be shown. He appealed instead to diplomacy. Xuanzang was an envoy of the Tang emperor. If Wuji killed an envoy, a powerful neighboring state might respond. Would it not be wiser to question these travelers before condemning them?

The false king accepted the argument. He asked where Xuanzang had come from, who had sent him, and why he traveled west.

Wukong answered at length, telling the familiar story of the pilgrimage. He described the Tang emperor's dream of the underworld, the great religious assembly, Guanyin's instruction, and the gathering of the disciples along the road. He named himself, Bajie, and Sha Wujing. Then, casually, he mentioned that the travelers had recently acquired a fourth attendant to carry their belongings.

The impostor's attention sharpened. Who was this extra follower? What was his name? Had he been properly admitted to the company? Could he be made to give an account of himself?

The true king trembled. His three years in the well had taught him nothing of how to stand before a court that had forgotten him. Wukong stepped in front of him.

The servant, he said, was deaf and mute, and had known misfortune from childhood. He would speak on the man's behalf.

Then Wukong began to recite a confession.

It sounded at first like the story of a poor wanderer. Soon it turned to the great drought five years before, the suffering of the people, the arrival of a Daoist from Zhongnan, the miraculous rain, and the friendship between ruler and holy man. The court listened. The false king's expression changed. Wukong described the garden, the glazed well, and the murder. He spoke of a false ruler who had worn another man's features and a man restored to life now standing in the court as a servant.

By the end there was little disguise left in the words.

The impostor seized a guard's sword and fled into the sky.

## Two masters in the same hall

Bajie was furious. Why had Wukong told the entire story before trapping the demon? Now they would have to chase him again. Sha Wujing agreed that the danger had not passed.

Wukong reassured them. The prince could now bring his true father forward, the queen could come to see her husband, and the officials could begin to learn how they had been deceived. Wukong would catch the false king himself. He told his brothers to protect Xuanzang and the royal family, then leaped after the fleeing cloud.

The demon fled northeast, carrying the stolen sword. Wukong closed the distance. The monster shouted that he had ruled the kingdom without troubling the monkey. Why should Sun Wukong interfere in the business of another country's throne?

Wukong answered with his staff. To take a kingdom by murder was not a private matter that became legitimate when strangers looked away.

They fought above the roofs, blade against iron. The demon held his own for a few exchanges, then saw that he could not defeat Wukong directly. He turned and plunged back toward the palace.

Wukong followed him into the hall and stopped.

There were now two Xuanzangs.

One stood exactly where the true monk had been. The other stood beside him, wearing the same robe, carrying the same face, and calling to Wukong in the same voice. Both appealed to the disciple not to strike. Both looked frightened of his staff. If the monkey guessed wrong, he could kill the master he had sworn to protect.

Wukong called to Bajie and Sha Wujing. Which was the true Xuanzang?

They could not tell. They had watched the fight in the sky, then looked down to find two identical masters. Neither brother had seen the instant of transformation.

Wukong called on the divine protectors who attended the pilgrimage. They knew the master they guarded; surely they could tell him which figure was false. Yet the demon heard the appeal and used his power to slip away to another part of the hall. When Wukong raised the staff, the protectors stopped him. He had nearly struck the true monk. The monster returned and mingled the two shapes again.

For once Bajie was the one with the simple answer.

'Brother,' he said, 'you call me a fool, but the answer is sitting on your head.'

Wukong glared at him.

'Have our master recite the tightening spell,' Bajie continued. 'Only he knows it. We can each hold one of these monks. The real one will speak the words. The false one will only pretend.'

Wukong hated the idea. The spell would give him a terrible headache. But the alternative was to strike at a face that might belong to Xuanzang.

He agreed.

The true monk recited the words. The golden band tightened. Wukong cried out and clutched his head, while one of the two figures merely mumbled nonsense in an attempt to imitate the sound. Bajie knew at once which one he held. He let go of the false monk and raised his rake.

The demon fled for the third time.

Now all three disciples pursued him. Wukong, still aching from the spell, drove him from above. Bajie and Sha Wujing blocked the paths below. The demon could no longer escape by putting another man's face between himself and the iron staff.

Wukong climbed into the clouds and prepared to strike downward with all his strength.

A voice called from the northeast.

'Sun Wukong! Stop your hand.'

## The lion and the bodhisattva

Manjushri Bodhisattva came upon a bright cloud, carrying an object that shone like a mirror. Wukong lowered his staff and bowed. Manjushri told him that he had come to reclaim the creature Wukong was about to kill.

The mirror showed the demon's true form: a great blue-haired lion, with fierce eyes and claws, no longer the courtly Daoist or the king whose face he had stolen. The prince had been made to call this lion Father. The ministers had addressed him as Majesty. He had sat on the throne for three years while the lawful ruler remained at the bottom of a well.

Wukong asked how Manjushri's lion had escaped and become such a monster.

The bodhisattva answered that it had not escaped. It had been sent.

Long before, he said, the true king of Wuji had been praised for generosity toward monks. Manjushri had visited the court disguised as an ordinary religious beggar, intending to bring the king toward spiritual attainment. The king, offended by the stranger's questioning, had ordered him bound and submerged in the royal river for three days and nights. Divine guardians had rescued Manjushri. In response, the lion had been sent to take the king's place and leave him under water for three years.

Wukong did not let the explanation pass without protest. Three days and three years were not the same, and the deception had reached far beyond the king. A family had been divided, a country governed under an assumed identity, and the monks had been asked to spend a long night pulling a corpse from a well. Could this truly be described as a harmless repayment?

Manjushri replied that under the false ruler the kingdom had enjoyed regular rains and peaceful conditions. The lion had not, he said, been harming the people.

Wukong asked about the queen. The lion had shared the palace with her while passing as her husband. Was that too of no consequence?

The bodhisattva explained that the lion had been castrated and could not have intercourse with her. Bajie, inclined to verify anything that seemed likely to embarrass a creature of great pretensions, made an inspection and delivered one of his coarse jokes.

Wukong was satisfied that the accusation he had just raised could not stand in the way he feared, but he did not praise the arrangement. The queen's years of estrangement remained. The prince's belief in his father had been manipulated. The true king's death was not undone by the claim that his country had prospered while he lay in the well.

Manjushri called the lion back to its proper form and took his seat upon it. Wukong held his staff at rest. This was another demon whose origin lay inside the structure of Heaven, and another test in which the pilgrims discovered only afterward who had authorized the danger.

The bodhisattva rose and departed with the lion for Mount Wutai.

Below, the restored king waited with his son and queen, and the ministers of Wuji faced a question they had never expected to answer: how could they recognize their lawful ruler after three years of obeying someone who looked exactly like him?

The false king was gone. The work of repairing a household and a kingdom would not be settled by a single spell.

As for Xuanzang and his three disciples, they still needed to leave the city and continue west. That departure belonged to the next chapter. For the moment, a man who had been murdered in his garden had returned, a son had learned to question appearances, and a monkey who mistrusted celestial explanations had once again been called to save the day without being given the whole story.
