# Journey to the West: The river that would not carry a feather

The Yellow Wind King was gone. His cave had been emptied, Xuanzang had been freed from the post where he had been bound, and Sun Wukong had recovered the use of his eyes. That was enough to start walking again. It was not enough to make the road easy.

Summer gave way to autumn as the three travelers went west: Xuanzang riding his white horse, Wukong ranging ahead or circling back whenever the country looked dangerous, and Zhu Bajie carrying the bundles and occasionally asking whether another human dwelling might appear before dark. Along the way they had learned to recognize one another's footsteps. The master's horse kept a steady rhythm. Bajie's rake clinked against the baggage. Wukong sometimes disappeared so completely that there was no sound at all, and then a voice came from a treetop telling them to hurry.

The country became flat. That ought to have made the journey easier. Instead the road ran straight toward an expanse of moving water.

Xuanzang stopped his horse. There was no opposite bank in sight, no bridge, no ferry, and no fisherman waiting to name a price. Dark waves rose as though something beneath the river kept stirring it. The surface was not a broad sheet but a restless succession of folds, ridges, and troughs. It looked less like a river than a field of mountains trying to roll away.

The monk asked Wukong how wide it was.

Wukong sprang up onto his cloud, shaded his eyes with his hand, and looked west. He could see farther than a person standing on the ground could imagine. When he came down, he said that the crossing was some eight hundred li. He could leap it himself in a moment. That was not the problem. The problem was transporting a man, a horse, a rake, a staff, and the luggage across water that would not support them.

Bajie looked about for a village or boat shed. There was neither. A stone tablet stood near the shore. On it was the river's name: Flowing-Sand River. Beneath the name was an explanation. Its waters were so treacherous that a goose feather could not stay afloat upon them; even a reed flower would sink to the bottom. The river was immeasurably deep, and the crossing stretched for hundreds of miles.

Xuanzang gazed at the white horse. His own body could be carried on its back for many days, but the horse could no more trot on that water than he could walk upon it.

Bajie said they would have to find a way around.

Wukong looked along the horizon. There was no visible end to the river, and he had never been a patient traveler in search of detours.

Before they could discuss the matter further, the water broke open.

A head appeared above the foam: red hair in a wild mass, eyes bright and round, a blue-black face, and a voice like a drum struck inside a cavern. A yellow cloak clung to broad shoulders. About the creature's neck hung nine human skulls. In his hands he carried a great staff, the sort of weapon a celestial guard might have used before a throne.

The creature did not ask whether the travelers were lost. He came straight for the monk.

Wukong seized Xuanzang and bounded up the bank, carrying him away from the first rush. Bajie dropped the luggage and brought up his nine-toothed rake. The water-creature swung his staff into its teeth with a crash.

For the first time the newly recruited pig and the strange man of the river met in earnest battle. Each fought like someone who remembered great strength and a great humiliation. Bajie's rake could tear through hard earth and shatter stone. The stranger's staff matched its reach. When one lunged, the other turned. When one struck downward, the other caught the blow and thrust toward the ribs. Neither gave ground.

Twenty exchanges passed without a clear advantage.

Wukong watched from the bank. He had guarded the master; he had allowed Bajie to begin the fight; he had even stood still long enough to see that his brother could hold his own. But watching two strong opponents fight without joining them was a trial of its own. He twitched the iron staff in his fingers. Then, telling Xuanzang to remain where he was, he charged.

The river creature saw the monkey, recognized a danger greater than the one already in front of him, and disappeared below the surface.

Bajie turned on Wukong in disbelief. Hadn't the monkey seen? A few more blows, another moment of pressure, and the stranger might have tired. Why had he rushed in and frightened the quarry away?

Wukong answered with a grin that he had not enjoyed a proper use of his staff in weeks. The fighting had looked too inviting.

That was a poor answer to Bajie and an even poorer one to Xuanzang, whose road was still blocked.

The monk suggested that the creature might know the way through the river. Perhaps, if they captured him without killing him, they could compel him to guide them across. Wukong agreed that this was sensible. Bajie suggested that Wukong go down into the water and do the capturing himself.

For a moment the great sage became remarkably modest.

In the clouds and upon the earth, he said, he could perform marvels. Under water it was another matter. He could enter by magic and protect himself with a spell, or become an aquatic animal; but the freedom of movement he enjoyed in the open air was diminished. Bajie, on the other hand, had commanded the heavenly river's water troops in his former life. Surely a marshal could dive into a river and fetch one troublesome fellow out.

Bajie replied that a river might hide an entire family of monsters. He did not care to be dragged down by seven generations of some stranger's relatives. Wukong promised that he need only provoke the creature into fighting and then feign defeat, drawing him up toward the shore. Wukong would wait there and bar the retreat. This time, he promised, he would be patient.

The arrangements sounded straightforward. Bajie took off his outer garment, secured his rake, parted the water, and plunged into the dark.

Below the surface lay the creature's dwelling. It did not resemble a palace fit for a king. It was a place claimed by an exile, safe from ordinary men because ordinary men could not survive the waters around it. Bajie approached, and the creature came to meet him. Their weapons struck. The sound traveled through the water like thunder.

They exchanged insults as well as blows. The stranger spoke of the staff bestowed on him in Heaven, of his former rank as the official who attended the emperor's curtain, and of the small offense that had destroyed his place among the immortals. At a celestial feast, he had dropped and broken a precious glass vessel. The Jade Emperor had sentenced him to death. An immortal had interceded, and the sentence had become exile instead. In this river he had lived by killing and eating those who came too near.

Bajie knew something of falling from a high office for a shameful offense. He had once commanded the heavenly waters himself, before his own misconduct sent him into a pig's body. The two former officers could have recognized one another's predicament. Instead they were too busy trying to knock each other senseless.

For two hours they fought without a victory. The river heaved around them. Then Bajie gave ground and headed toward the eastern bank. The stranger pursued him. Wukong, watching above, prepared to spring.

But the monkey was a little too eager. He came down with his staff raised while the stranger still had water at his back. With one twist the creature vanished beneath the waves. Bajie surfaced, spluttering and furious. He had been told to draw his opponent farther out, not to make him turn away at the very edge.

They spent the night on the bank. Wukong went to secure food while Xuanzang rested as best he could. The river continued to move in the darkness. Its roar was indifferent to the three figures who had stopped beside it.

At dawn the plan began again. Bajie entered the water and found the stranger awake and ready. Another long combat followed, neither weapon gaining the advantage. Bajie pretended to lose, drew the creature toward land, and tried to entice him onto firm ground. But by now the stranger understood the trap. He stopped where he could still dive, and shouted that if Bajie wished to fight, he should fight in the river.

Wukong tried a sudden descent from the sky, the strike of a hunting bird. He was quick. The river creature was quicker to retreat. A splash, a widening ring in the water, and he was gone.

The brothers returned with nothing.

Xuanzang's distress was no longer merely the anxious question of how to reach the opposite shore. He had watched two powerful disciples exhaust themselves without making the slightest progress. The weakest member of the company, who could not leap, transform, or dive, was the only one whose passage mattered. He could do little but wait and suffer the weight of being the reason for their struggle.

Wukong looked at the water and then to the south. He knew who had commissioned the pilgrimage, who had found the companions in their punishments, and who had intervened when strength failed. Guanyin had set this company on the road. He would go and ask her how to move it forward.

Bajie offered a respectful message of thanks. The bodhisattva had once called him away from his old life, and he had been too occupied on the road to return and express his gratitude. Wukong promised to deliver the greeting, sprang onto his cloud, and was gone.

At the purple bamboo grove, Guanyin received him. Wukong explained that the monk had reached a river so broad that a crossing seemed impossible. There was a ferocious water monster, an able fighter, who would not permit them to pass. Bajie had fought him repeatedly. Wukong himself had tried to seize him, but could not force him out of the water.

Guanyin heard him out. Then she asked a question that brought the whole complicated struggle into a different light.

Had they told the creature who they were?

Wukong explained that they had been too occupied with catching him. They had hoped to make him carry the master across. As for explaining the pilgrimage, perhaps Bajie had said something in the midst of their battle, perhaps he had not.

The bodhisattva knew the creature. He had once been the Curtain-Raising General in Heaven. He too had been offered a way of atonement, and he had promised to wait for the scripture pilgrim. He had already been given the religious name Sha Wujing, Wujing meaning awakened to purity. When the travelers encountered him, all they needed to do was identify the monk and the mission.

Wukong thought of two days of wrestling, ambush, and shouting. A few words at the beginning would have settled the matter.

Guanyin did not leave the party to solve the water-crossing alone. She called her attendant Hui'an, also known as Muzha, and entrusted to him a red gourd. He was to take it to the river, summon Wujing by name, and unite the nine skulls hanging around the creature's neck into a pattern around the gourd. Together they would make a vessel that could float on the water where even a feather sank.

Wukong and Hui'an returned to the shore. Bajie recognized the attendant and greeted him with sincere thanks. Hui'an answered politely, but the immediate business was more pressing. From a cloud over the water he called the name Guanyin had given the exiled general.

Wujing rose almost at once.

He was still wary of Wukong. He had not forgotten the iron staff and the eagerness with which its owner had tried to strike his head. But he recognized Hui'an, and he understood the message when the attendant named the travelers on the shore as the scripture party.

Wujing stared across the river. The pig, he said, had been fighting him for days. The monkey had attacked him each time he came near land. Neither had mentioned that they had come to escort the Tang monk. Why had they made themselves enemies when they had come to take him away from the river?

Hui'an told him that all three had been summoned for the same purpose. Wukong was not an enemy sent to punish him; Bajie was not another demon seeking his territory. Both were disciples, former rebels and offenders traveling beside a human master. If Wujing wished to redeem his old promise, the master was waiting.

The former river monster put away his weapon, adjusted his robe, and came ashore. Before Xuanzang he knelt and asked forgiveness for the attack. The monk looked at the red hair, the terrible face, the weapon, and the nine skulls, and then listened to the name Guanyin had given the man. Was he truly ready to accept the discipline and labor of the pilgrimage?

Wujing answered that this was the very service for which he had been waiting.

Xuanzang asked Wukong for the razor used in giving the disciples their monastic appearance. Wujing's hair was shorn. He bowed again before his new master and then paid the proper respects to the two disciples who had been trying to kill him a little earlier. The monk also called him Sha Heshang, or Sha the monk, a name by which the new pilgrim would often be known.

This did not erase his past. The skulls were still about his neck. He had described eating people; he had worn evidence of that violence as a necklace. Neither a haircut nor a new name made those deeds disappear. But he now had a place to begin a different life.

Hui'an called for the crossing. Wujing lifted the nine skulls from his neck and arranged them in a nine-part pattern. Guanyin's red gourd rested among them. The improbable assembly steadied upon the weak water like a boat on a sheltered lake.

Xuanzang was invited to step aboard.

He took his seat. Bajie supported one side, Wujing the other. Wukong followed with the dragon-horse, moving between cloud and mist, while Hui'an watched from above. There was no battle. The river that had thrown up ridges of foam quieted under their passage. The monk crossed without soaking his feet.

On the far bank the party stepped onto firm ground. Hui'an recovered the gourd. The skulls dispersed into nine gusts and vanished. Xuanzang bowed his gratitude toward Guanyin, and Wukong watched the attendant return to the southern sea.

For the first time there were four pilgrims on foot or horseback, not three: the monk, the monkey, the pig, and the former general of the river. The new disciple could shoulder loads, attend to the horse, and fight with a weapon already tested against Bajie's rake. More important, he had accepted the same road.

A great river had not yielded to force. It had yielded to recognition: the right name, the right purpose, and the choice of a former enemy to become a companion.

The next stretch of road opened before them. Wukong went ahead, Bajie took up his burdens, and Sha Wujing walked into a life in which he would no longer wait at the bottom of the water for his next victim.

## Non-spoken source and reading companion

**Scope:** Selected transmitted Chinese chapter 22, from the approach to Flowing-Sand River through the crossing and Sha Wujing's acceptance as the fourth pilgrim. This is an original English prose retelling, not a line-by-line translation. Dialogue is newly rendered, and brief sensory transitions serve narrative continuity.

**Primary witness:** [*Journey to the West*, chapter 22](https://zh.wikisource.org/wiki/%E8%A5%BF%E9%81%8A%E8%A8%98/%E7%AC%AC022%E5%9B%9E) (`cl-jtw-ch22`). The river is eight hundred li across; the inscription invokes the feather and reed flower; Wukong's premature attacks spoil Bajie's efforts to draw the opponent ashore. The monster is the former Curtain-Raising General, already named Wujing by Guanyin. Hui'an/Muzha brings the gourd used with the nine skulls to ferry the monk; the skulls dissolve after the crossing. Do not simplify the monster into a harmless misunderstood stranger: his earlier predation is stated in the chapter.
