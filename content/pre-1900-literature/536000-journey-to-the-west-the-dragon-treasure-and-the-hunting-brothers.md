# Journey to the West: The dragon treasure and the hunting brothers

## A message sent through water

The king of Jisai watched Sun Wukong and Zhu Bajie lift two bound fish spirits into the air. They had come from the Golden Light Monastery, where the monks were still recovering from years of false accusation and torture. They had confessed that the treasure stolen from the tower was now kept beneath the Emerald Waves Pool by a dragon king and his family. The king had seen many strange things since the pilgrims arrived. Now he saw two of them depart on clouds, carrying prisoners as if they were sacks of grain.

Xuanzang stayed in the royal city with Sha Wujing and the released monks. He did not claim the powers that had uncovered the crime. Wukong and Bajie had discovered the watchers in the tower; Wukong had protected them long enough to make them testify. Now the pair were going to recover the treasure itself. The monk asked only that the work be finished and the innocent people be allowed to live in peace.

Wukong and Bajie descended beside the pool at the Broken-Stone Mountain. The water looked calm. Beneath it, Wukong knew, stood the palace where the Bull Demon King had attended a feast not long before. Wukong had already entered that palace once in the shape of a crab, when he needed to steal the Bull King's mount and impersonate him. A palace may hide under water, but it does not easily hide from someone who has learned its doors.

He did not simply release the two fish prisoners. He transformed his staff into a blade, cut an ear from one and part of the other's lip, and sent them bleeding into the pool with a warning. Tell the Dragon King of All Saints, he said, that the Great Sage Equal to Heaven stood at his gate. Return the Buddhist treasure stolen from Jisai's tower, or the Great Sage would overturn the waters and destroy the household.

The creatures fled through the water, dragging the chains that had held them. Fish, turtles, and other inhabitants of the pool surrounded them, asking why they were wounded. They could hardly answer for pain. In the palace, the old dragon king was drinking with his son-in-law. The two messengers fell before them and repeated Wukong's demand.

The old dragon blanched at the name. He knew Wukong's reputation. His son-in-law was less impressed. He had fought warriors from many places, he said. He was not going to return a treasure because a monkey threatened him. He dressed himself for battle and took up a crescent-bladed spade.

## The son-in-law's many heads

The Nine-Headed Son-in-Law rose from the pool, parting the waters as he came. His armor gleamed and his weapon flashed. His face at first appeared human, but Wukong could see that the creature had more eyes and mouths than any ordinary warrior. He demanded to know who had come to disturb his father-in-law's house.

Wukong stepped forward with his staff. The son-in-law asked what business a traveling monk's disciple had with a king's missing treasure. Let the king of Jisai defend his own property, he said. Wukong was supposed to fetch scriptures in the west. Why fight somebody else's battle?

Wukong might once have agreed that a distant king's trouble was none of his concern. But he had walked through the ruined Golden Light Monastery. The king had imprisoned men who were innocent, and two groups of monks had already died under punishment for a crime committed by the dragon household. The thieves had stained a sacred tower and left others to suffer in their place. Those monks followed the same religious path as Wukong's master. He would not stand aside now because the thief happened to live in another realm.

The son-in-law did not accept that answer. He swung his crescent spade, and Wukong met it with the iron staff. The fight crossed the slope above the pool. Metal struck metal, cloud and water shook, and for more than thirty exchanges neither warrior could force the other to retreat. Bajie stood watching until he saw a chance to strike the son-in-law from behind.

But the creature had eyes facing every way. He caught Bajie's rake with one part of his weapon while resisting Wukong's staff with the other. For several more exchanges he endured the attack from both sides. Then he rolled upward and revealed his proper form: a giant birdlike monster with nine heads crowded around its body, powerful hooked claws, and wings spreading wide over the mountain.

Bajie stared. He had seen demons in every shape imaginable and still found this one difficult to believe. Wukong leapt after the creature into the air. The bird turned, brought one of its many heads around, and opened a mouth broad enough to seize Bajie by his bristles. Bajie struggled, but the creature dragged him down through the water to the palace. There he threw the pig disciple onto the floor and ordered the attendants to bind him.

The old dragon welcomed his victorious son-in-law. Servants hurried to prepare another feast. For a moment, the thieves had good reason to think that the affair had turned in their favor: one enemy was tied up, and the other remained outside in a territory where water gave him little advantage.

## The crab and the rake

Wukong did not rush through the front gate. He remembered the route he had used when pursuing the Bull Demon King. He turned himself into a crab and descended into the pool. He moved through familiar corridors, along the palace's side passage, and beneath the notice of servants preoccupied with their celebration.

Near the western corridor he found Bajie bound to a pillar. Wukong spoke softly. Bajie recognized his brother's voice before he saw the little crab. The prisoner's first complaint was that he had been seized unfairly; his second was that his rake had been taken. Wukong broke the cords with his claws and told him to wait outside by the great gate. Then he went searching for the weapon.

He found the rake inside the main hall, where its points still shone among the trophies. Invisible to the guards, Wukong took it down and carried it to Bajie. The pig disciple gripped the familiar handle and recovered his courage at once. He wanted to charge straight back into the palace.

Wukong listened to his plan. If Bajie forced the enemies outside, Wukong could meet them at the water's edge, where his staff was more useful. Bajie could navigate the pool better than his elder brother, having once commanded the heavenly river's armies. There was no reason not to let him try.

With a great shout Bajie burst into the banquet hall. He overturned tables and shattered ornaments. The palace attendants ran in every direction. The old dragon and his son-in-law rose from their feast to see the prisoner standing free with his rake, breaking furniture and demanding the return of Jisai's treasure.

The son-in-law took his spade again. The dragon king called his sons and grandsons to assist. Bajie fought through the crowd, but the number of opponents grew. He gave ground, struck once more to keep the enemy near, and retreated toward the surface. The dragon king pursued him with his household warriors.

As they came out of the water, Wukong stepped from his hiding place. One blow of his staff killed the old dragon. The water reddened. The son-in-law and the surviving members of the family drew back to the palace, taking the dead king's body with them.

Bajie stood on the bank, breathless and proud of the destruction he had made. But he did not want to go down into the palace again. Its inhabitants had learned what Wukong could do, evening was coming, and he preferred a meal and a night's rest. Wukong argued that they had to recover the treasure before returning to Jisai. Killing the dragon had not yet repaired a single floor of the tower.

## Old enemies share a meal

While the brothers argued, a procession passed through the sky. They saw hunters with bows, knives, hounds, and the game of the day's expedition: birds and animals carried by attendants. Leading them was Erlang Shen, the divine warrior who had once helped capture Wukong during his revolt against Heaven. Six sworn companions accompanied him.

Wukong recognized the company and wanted its help, but he was not eager to greet the commander who had defeated him in those distant days. He told Bajie to fly up and announce him. Bajie was delighted to act as herald. He called out that the Great Sage Equal to Heaven wished to pay his respects.

Erlang stopped and sent his companions to invite Wukong. The former rivals greeted one another as brothers rather than enemies. Erlang had heard that Wukong had escaped the long imprisonment under the mountain, accepted Buddhist discipline, and was traveling to obtain scriptures. He congratulated him. Wukong did not claim his task was finished. The scriptures lay far away, and he was still meeting trouble on the road.

He explained the theft at Jisai: the blood rain, the missing relic, the monks punished for years, the false witnesses found in the tower, the battle above the pool, and the dragon king's death. Erlang knew the old dragon's domain. He was surprised that the household had undertaken so reckless a crime, but the arrival of the Nine-Headed Son-in-Law explained the change.

Erlang proposed attacking before the family could recover from its defeat. Bajie pointed out that the light was failing. The six companions suggested a different arrangement. They had food and drink from their hunting expedition. They could share a meal beneath the stars and assault the pool at daybreak. Wukong said that as a disciple he followed the rules of vegetarian eating. The hunters provided fruit and suitable provisions, and the gathering settled into an outdoor feast.

This was not the same company that had chased Wukong in his old rebellion. They remembered the old fight, but they now spoke to him as a pilgrim. The shared food and laughter joined two parts of his life that had once seemed impossible to reconcile.

By morning Bajie was ready for another attack. He went down through the water and stormed the dragon palace again. The inhabitants had scarcely begun mourning their king. The dragon's son was dressed in funeral garments when Bajie struck and killed him. The son-in-law and a younger member of the dragon family came out to fight, while the terrified women and servants fled through the chambers.

Bajie gave ground deliberately and led his pursuers toward the surface. As the son-in-law emerged, Wukong, Erlang, and the six hunters joined the fight. The younger dragon fell beneath their weapons. The son-in-law changed into his nine-headed bird form and tried to escape through the air.

Erlang raised his bow and fired. The bird twisted sharply, intending to seize the divine warrior with one of its heads. But Erlang's hound sprang up and tore that head from the creature's body. Blood fell through the air. Wounded and furious, the Nine-Headed Son-in-Law beat his wings and fled toward the northern sea.

Bajie wanted to chase him. Wukong stopped him. Their purpose was to recover the relic and clear the monks, and they could not know how long the wounded creature might fly. The hunters remained troubled that an enemy so dangerous had escaped, but the travelers let it go. The son-in-law was not killed; he vanished into the distance alive, though badly wounded.

## The two boxes

Wukong had another method for recovering what the thieves concealed. He assumed the wounded son-in-law's appearance and hurried toward the palace, with Bajie following as though in pursuit. Princess All Saints saw what appeared to be her husband fleeing and demanded to know what had happened.

The disguised Wukong said the enemy was too strong. She must hide the treasure before Bajie broke in. Alarmed, she ran to the inner chamber and returned with two boxes: a golden container holding the Buddhist relic stolen from the tower, and a white jade box containing the nine-leaf spirit fungus stolen from Heaven. The fungus had preserved the relic's brilliance during its time in the depths.

Wukong took both boxes. Then, before the princess could reconsider, he changed back into his own form. She understood the deception and lunged to recover the treasures. Bajie struck her with his rake, and she fell dead.

An old dragon woman tried to slip away. Bajie caught her and raised his weapon. Wukong stopped him. The household had lost its king, children, and princess; the surviving dragon woman might be useful as a witness when they returned. He would not have Bajie kill her too. They bound her and brought her out of the pool.

Erlang and his six companions were waiting on the bank. Wukong showed them the boxes and thanked them for their assistance. Erlang answered with courtesy, giving the king's fortune and Wukong's effort their share of the credit. Wukong invited the hunters to Jisai to see the work completed, but Erlang had his own road to travel. The two groups parted in friendship.

## A light returns to the city

At the gates of Jisai the released monks came to meet the returning disciples. They recognized the boxes before they knew what was in them. News went ahead to the palace: Wukong and Bajie had recovered the tower's treasure and brought one of the dragon household back to testify.

The king came down from his throne to receive them. He ordered a feast at once, but Xuanzang asked that the relic first be restored. The celebration could wait until the place from which the treasure had been stolen was whole again. Wukong reported the battle, Bajie's capture, the rescue under water, Erlang's intervention, the death of the old dragon, the escape of the son-in-law, and the two boxes brought from the palace.

The king questioned the captured dragon woman. She said her husband and the Nine-Headed Son-in-Law had arranged the theft of the Buddhist relic. The princess had separately stolen the nine-leaf fungus from the Queen Mother's palace to preserve its light. She begged to live; her husband, children, son-in-law, and daughter were gone. Wukong agreed that she would live, but he imposed a severe sentence. She would be fastened through the shoulders with an iron chain and made to guard the tower for the rest of her life.

The king and the court went to Golden Light Monastery with Xuanzang and the disciples. Wukong climbed the thirteen floors. At the summit he placed the recovered relic back in its vessel. He fastened the old dragon woman to the central pillar. He summoned the city's earth and temple guardians and ordered that food be brought to her once every three days. Then he took the spirit fungus and swept it across every level of the tower, carrying the light of the treasure down through the building before returning the fungus to its container beside the relic.

The tower shone again. Radiance traveled from the summit across the city and out over the country. The people who had once accused the monks now stood beneath the light that proved the monks had spoken the truth. Years of wrongful imprisonment could not be undone by a bright tower, and the dead could not return. Yet the survivors were free, and the king could no longer pretend that the missing relic was a crime committed by his own temple.

Wukong suggested a new name. The old name, Golden Light Monastery, had belonged to an age of stolen brilliance and careless accusation. The new name would remember the dragon household whose theft had nearly destroyed it. The king ordered a sign made for the Dragon-Taming Monastery, the royal temple now rebuilt and vindicated.

The king offered gifts of gold and precious stones. He commissioned portraits to preserve the names and forms of Xuanzang and his disciples. The pilgrims accepted the travel document properly marked for their passage, but they refused the treasure. They were not collecting payment for a temple repaired or a king embarrassed. They had come to obtain scriptures, and that labor remained unfinished.

When the four pilgrims prepared to leave, the tower cast its restored light over the city streets. Somewhere far away, the wounded nine-headed bird was still in flight. Beneath the tower, the dragon woman faced a lifetime of captivity. Within the monastery, the released monks began again the work that accusation had interrupted. The travelers had resolved the theft, but they carried with them the difficult knowledge that victory and the repair of harm were not always the same thing. Then they went west.
