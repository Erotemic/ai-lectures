# Journey to the West: The fire that rain could not quench

## An uncle at the gate

The door of Fire Cloud Cave was guarded by little demons carrying knives and spears. They were playing with their weapons when two travelers arrived at the stone bridge: one was small, sharp-faced, and carried an iron staff; the other had a long snout, tremendous ears, and a rake with nine teeth.

The little guards abandoned their game and ran indoors.

Their master was in the rear court. He had already had Xuanzang stripped and bound, with his wrists and ankles drawn together, and had ordered fresh water brought to wash him before he was put in a steamer. The news of visitors interrupted these preparations.

Red Boy was not frightened. He knew the monkey would come. What surprised him was how quickly Wukong had found a cave hidden deep among the mountains. He ordered five little carts to be wheeled into position outside the gate. Their attendants arranged them according to the five elements: wood, fire, earth, metal, and water. Red Boy took a spear with a flaming point, bound a bright sash about his waist, and walked out barefoot to receive his guests.

Wukong greeted him with a broad smile.

'Nephew! What sort of welcome is this? Your father's old brother has come to call, and you receive him with weapons?'

Red Boy examined the monkey. He had no memory of this creature. He demanded to know who had given him permission to claim kinship.

Wukong began listing names that belonged to a much earlier age. Before he had disturbed the courts of Heaven, he said, he had traveled through the world with six other kings. The Bull Demon King, who called himself the Great Sage Who Pacifies Heaven, had been their eldest brother. There had been rulers of seas, mountains, winds, and strange beasts among them. The little monkey from Flower-Fruit Mountain had been the seventh, the Great Sage Equal to Heaven.

'Your father and I shared an oath before you were born. I am the youngest of your uncles. That makes you my nephew. Now bring out my master and we can speak properly.'

Red Boy's face darkened. He believed none of it. His father was a mighty demon king; this monkey was a nuisance demanding possession of a valuable prisoner. He drove his spear at Wukong's chest.

Wukong stepped aside and replied with his staff. They rose above the cave in a clatter of metal. Again and again the spear came at the monkey's throat or ribs; again and again the staff swung toward the child's head. For twenty exchanges neither gave way. But Bajie, watching from below, noticed that Red Boy had begun to defend more than attack. Wukong was pressing him.

Bajie worried that the monkey would win without help and claim all the credit. He leapt into the air and brought his rake down beside Wukong's staff.

Red Boy withdrew at once. Wukong shouted to Bajie to pursue him. They rushed toward the cave, expecting a victory, and found the child standing on the middle of the five carts.

Red Boy raised a fist and struck his own nose twice.

Bajie laughed. Had the boy been beaten so badly that he was trying to draw blood and make himself look injured?

Then Red Boy opened his mouth.

## The five carts

Flame poured from his lips, and thick smoke burst from his nostrils. The carts caught fire in sequence; their arrangement brought the power of the five elements into his spell. What rose above the ravine was not an ordinary blaze from timber or an alchemist's furnace. It was the Samadhi True Fire, a heat refined by years of practice and set loose through the child's own body.

The cave vanished inside a red wall. Flames lifted into the sky and struck outward across the rocks. Bajie imagined his own ample flesh roasting, with seasonings provided by the demons. He retreated across the bridge as quickly as his legs could carry him.

Wukong did not retreat immediately. He knew fire. He had survived the furnace of Laozi, endured the fierce heat of heavenly medicine, and escaped with his iron staff still in hand. He made the protective sign against flames and ran into the blaze, calling Red Boy to face him.

He could bear the heat. The smoke was another matter.

In Laozi's furnace the fumes had scoured Wukong's eyes and left him with the golden vision for which he was famous. That vision had never made the eyes comfortable in smoke. Red Boy's thick clouds closed around his face, stinging and blinding him, while the child slipped back to the cave entrance. Wukong could no longer see where to strike. He jumped clear of the fire and returned to the pines.

Bajie was there with Sha Wujing. The pig spoke as if he had meant to lead a tactical withdrawal all along. Wukong accused him of running away and abandoning his brother. Bajie pointed out that he was alive, dry, and unlikely to become the demon's next dish. Perhaps there was wisdom in knowing when a fight had turned against you.

Wukong asked whether the boy's spear work had been better than his own. Bajie admitted that it had not. Was Red Boy stronger in close combat? Not really. Then Wukong accused Bajie of spoiling a duel that was nearly won. The pig protested that he had only wished to help.

Sha Wujing listened to their argument and began to laugh.

Both older disciples turned upon him. Did he have some marvelous power he had been hiding? Had he secretly discovered how to overcome the True Fire?

Sha Wujing replied that the plan did not require a marvelous insight. They had been fighting fire as though it were a soldier. Water overcame fire in the ordinary ordering of the five elements. Why not ask a dragon king to send rain?

Wukong stopped arguing. It was a plan worth trying.

He flew east over the mountains and the ocean, descended into the palace of the East Sea Dragon King, and explained his need. Ao Guang received him politely. Wukong's visits to the dragon court had not always been gentle affairs; years earlier he had come away with his iron staff and the irritation of half the palace. Now he was asking for assistance in rescuing the monk whose journey had brought him back into lawful service.

Ao Guang was willing, but he could not summon the full weather of the world by himself. Rain required the coordinated powers of the four seas. He summoned his brothers, and they gathered their watery forces: fish as officers, crabs and shrimp as troops, creatures of shell and scale moving under the dragon banners. Soon an army of rain followed Wukong back toward Mount Hao.

Wukong told the dragon kings to remain hidden above the ravine. He would engage Red Boy again. When the boy summoned his fire, the dragons were to pour rain upon it until it went out.

The cave doors opened. Red Boy laughed at the monkey's return. Hadn't he been scorched enough? The fight began again. Wukong's staff beat against the child's spear. Red Boy sprang back to his five carts, punched his nose, and sent the great fire rolling up the mountain.

'Now!' cried Wukong.

Rain descended like overturned seas. It hammered the trees and filled the ravine. Water ran over the stones, washed the cliffs, and made the ground slippery beneath the fighters' feet.

But the fire did not go out.

The rain was a dragon king's own rain, able to extinguish common flames. Red Boy's power was not common flame. The torrents beat upon it and seemed to feed it. Smoke thickened. Fire flared higher. Wukong forced his way forward, trying to reach the child through the furnace of air, and Red Boy blew a blast of smoke directly into his eyes.

The monkey stumbled away. His face burned; his eyes ran with tears; he could scarcely see the edge of the ravine. He jumped into the cold stream to cool himself. Heat met cold so violently that his breath stopped. His spirit seemed to leave him. His body drifted into the current, helpless and curled.

The dragon kings drew back the rain. Above the ravine they saw their old acquaintance floating downstream, no longer moving. They shouted for the other disciples.

## A borrowed breath

Bajie and Sha Wujing rushed along the bank. Sha plunged into the stream, seized Wukong by his clothes, and dragged him to shore. The monkey's body was cold. His limbs were drawn together. Sha Wujing knelt over him in grief. The disciple who had survived an imperial furnace and the armies of Heaven seemed to have died in a mountain brook.

Bajie refused to declare him dead. He felt the chest and found a trace of warmth. Then he began to arrange Wukong's body, straightening his limbs and seating him with his legs crossed. He rubbed his own hands together until they were hot, pressed them over the monkey's face and breathing passages, and worked a method of massage he remembered from an earlier life.

For a long moment there was nothing. Then air moved through the blocked channels. Wukong gasped.

'Master!'

Sha Wujing caught him by the shoulders. Even in this condition, he said, Wukong's first word was for the person he was trying to save. Bajie took a more practical view: the monkey would not be breathing at all if the pig had not known how to restore him. Where was his thanks?

Wukong opened his eyes. He was weakened, and his body hurt. He admitted that Red Boy had nearly killed him. The dragon kings had done what he asked, but their rain had only made the situation worse. Their powers were not enough against this fire.

Sha Wujing reminded them of the promise made when their pilgrimage began. Guanyin had said that if they faced a danger beyond them, they could call upon greater aid. Wukong had fought Heaven's troops in the old days. If ordinary celestial warriors could not prevail over the monkey, why should they prevail where he had already failed? The enemy required someone with greater authority and a different kind of power.

'Guanyin,' Wukong said.

He tried to rise into the air but was still too weak to take his somersault cloud. Bajie offered to go. Wukong gave careful instructions: reach the South Sea, bow before the bodhisattva, keep your eyes down, explain the exact place and name of the demon, and ask for help. Do not boast, invent, or stop to quarrel along the way.

Bajie agreed and flew southward.

Red Boy, meanwhile, came out of Fire Cloud Cave to see what his enemies would try next. He saw a broad figure traveling south through the clouds. Southward lay Guanyin's domain. The child's quick mind supplied the answer at once: the pig was going to fetch the bodhisattva.

He knew the routes through these mountains better than any visitor. Taking a short path, he raced ahead of Bajie, changed into Guanyin's form, and sat upon a cliff as though waiting to receive travelers.

Bajie saw the figure and bowed in relief. He delivered his message. Wukong had been hurt, Xuanzang was captive, and the demon was Red Boy of Fire Cloud Cave.

The false Guanyin listened and offered a simple solution. Perhaps the monks had provoked the child. Let Bajie come to the cave. They could speak with its master together, apologize if necessary, and retrieve the prisoner peacefully.

Bajie wanted the matter settled. If a bow would recover Xuanzang, he said, he would bow.

He followed the figure to the very cave from which he had fled. Red Boy stepped inside ahead of him. The doors closed. Little demons sprang from behind the pillars, bound the pig, and shoved him into a magic leather bag. They tied the mouth of the bag and hung it from a beam.

Only then did Red Boy resume his own shape.

Bajie roared from inside the sack. He had come to rescue a monk and instead delivered himself for the steamer. Red Boy promised that after a few days of hanging in the bag, the pig would be a fine feast for his servants. Bajie cursed him, his ancestors, his deception, and the entire cave.

## The visitor disguised as a bundle

Back beneath the pines, Wukong felt a foul gust cross his face and sneezed. Something had gone wrong. Sha Wujing suggested that Bajie might merely have chosen the wrong road. Wukong knew his younger brother too well to be comforted.

He forced himself to the cave despite his aching limbs. When the guards saw him they raised their weapons, and Wukong changed into a decorated bundle lying by the roadside. The little demons picked it up, delighted that the monkey had fled and abandoned a parcel. They carried it inside as possible useful cloth.

There, while no one was looking, Wukong left a single hair in the bundle's shape and transformed his true body into a fly. He followed the sound of muffled abuse to a leather bag hanging in the hall.

Bajie was inside. His curses were so continuous that Wukong nearly laughed aloud. The pig, certain that no one friendly could hear him, still promised the demon that his great brother would come, undo the bag, and scatter the monsters with his staff.

Wukong rested on the bag's seam and listened. He could not free Bajie yet without exposing himself, and Xuanzang remained somewhere deeper in the cave. He must learn what Red Boy intended before making another move.

The child called for his six trusted officers. They entered with names that seemed designed to outrun one another: Cloud-in-Mist and Mist-in-Cloud, Swift-as-Fire and Quick-as-Wind, Upheaval-Here and Here-Upheaval. Red Boy ordered them to go at once to the Bull Demon King and invite him to a banquet. They were to announce that his son had captured the holy Tang monk. Father and son would share the flesh that promised long life.

The little officers hurried away.

A fly rose from the leather bag and drifted after them. Wukong had heard enough. His earlier claim of kinship had failed at the cave gate. If the son would not listen to an uncle, perhaps the son would listen to his father.

The monkey followed the six messengers into the mountain air, already preparing another face.
