# Journey to the West: The ginseng fruit tree

## The bridegroom in the branches

In the pines behind the house that no longer existed, the pilgrims found Zhu Bajie hanging in the cords of his disastrous wedding garment. They had spent the previous night in what appeared to be a wealthy widow's mansion, with three daughters eager for husbands. Bajie had accepted the invitation. The others had declined. At dawn the house had disappeared, and a message had identified its occupants as four holy beings come to test the travelers. Their reward for Bajie's eager cooperation was the sight of him trussed among the trees.

Wukong was delighted.

He called up to the suspended bridegroom, asking whether his new family was treating him well. Where were the three pretty daughters now? Had their husband risen too early for the wedding breakfast?

Bajie tried to hold his tongue. The bindings were painful, his body was cramped, and the sound of Wukong's laughter was harder to endure than either. When Sha Wujing came forward and began loosening the cords, Bajie bowed to him with sincere gratitude. He could hardly look at Xuanzang.

The monk demanded an assurance that he would not abandon the pilgrimage at the first offer of food and comfort. Bajie admitted his error. He would carry the loads, bear the hard road, and do his best to remain faithful. Wukong repeated one or two of the more embarrassing details of the marriage arrangements, and Bajie begged him to speak of something else.

They collected the bedding and continued west.

The land rose into beautiful mountains. Cloud shadows moved over streams and pines. There were orchards, stone steps, clear ravines, and plants thriving in a region that seemed untouched by the violence of the road behind them. Xuanzang looked at the heights and hoped that such a lovely country might mean they were drawing near the destination. Could this be the land of the sacred mountain where the Buddha taught?

Wukong told him it was much too soon. They had still traveled only a small fraction of the immense distance. Sha asked how far they must go. Bajie asked a more practical version of the same question: how many years of walking and carrying would that mean?

The monkey answered according to the traveler. A creature who could fly might cross the distance in days; Wukong himself could circle the route many times before sunset. But Xuanzang could not go faster than a human monk's body and the horse beneath him. The journey could not be measured solely by the power of the strongest member. Wukong added that a mind sincerely turned toward the teaching might discover the sacred mountain nearer than its legs could ever reach.

Xuanzang heard this but continued watching the path. Whatever wisdom the remark contained, he had still been commanded to reach the western land and carry back actual scriptures. A thought could not take their place.

Then the travelers saw a monastery-like building among the trees. Its roofs were set behind graceful walls, and cranes moved above the pines. Beside the entrance stood an inscription: a blessed region on Longevity Mountain, the dwelling of Five Villages Abbey. Over the gateway another declaration announced a house whose immortals shared the lifetime of Heaven itself.

Wukong laughed at the boast. He had visited Heaven when he had been far less inclined to obedience, and even there the officials were not so fond of proclaiming themselves equal to the universe. Bajie suggested that they inspect the place before deciding whether its residents were frauds. An establishment this fine might have an equally fine kitchen.

It was not a Buddhist monastery. It was the home of a Daoist immortal named Zhenyuanzi, an ancient master known as the Ancestor of Earthly Immortals. To understand what happened there, it helps to know something the travelers did not yet know.

Zhenyuanzi possessed a tree that bore a fruit like no other. It was called the ginseng fruit tree, and its fruit was also known as grass-returning cinnabar, a wonder of longevity. Once in three thousand years it flowered. After another three thousand it produced fruit. After still another three thousand the fruit ripened. Roughly ten thousand years passed between harvests. In a cycle the tree bore only thirty fruits.

They were the shape of tiny children, complete with limbs and faces. Smelling one bestowed centuries of life, and eating one was said to confer tens of thousands of years. A traveler familiar with ordinary fruit trees might have expected these wonders to hang as apples or peaches did. The ginseng fruits were more disturbing. They resembled living infants suspended from their stems.

The immortal himself was away. He had received an invitation from the primordial heavenly teacher to attend a great religious discourse. Of his many disciples he had taken most to the gathering. He left two attendants in charge, Qingfeng and Mingyue, names meaning Clear Wind and Bright Moon. The two were young only by the standards of immortals: one was more than thirteen hundred years old, the other around twelve hundred.

Before his departure Zhenyuanzi had given them an instruction. A friend of his would pass by: a Buddhist monk traveling from the eastern Tang lands to the west. The disciples were to treat him with honor and offer him two ginseng fruits.

The boys were puzzled. Why would their master honor a foreign Buddhist monk in a Daoist establishment? Zhenyuanzi explained that Xuanzang's story extended beyond this present lifetime. He had once been known as Golden Cicada, a disciple of the Buddha. At a religious assembly long ago he had served Zhenyuanzi tea. The immortal had not forgotten the courtesy. Their schools of religious teaching differed; the memory of a kindness did not need to.

Only two fruits were to be offered. Zhenyuanzi had counted his harvest carefully. Twenty-eight remained upon the tree after two had already been eaten at the opening of the garden. He warned the boys that the monk's disciples might be troublesome, and the sacred tree was not to become a general refreshment stall for everyone who happened to pass through.

## The fruit with a human face

The travelers entered the abbey and found the two young attendants ready to receive them. They bowed to Xuanzang and led him into a main hall. At the center was not an image of a Buddhist saint or the Daoist highest gods, but two great characters: Heaven and Earth.

Xuanzang asked why they worshipped only those names.

The boys answered with immense confidence. Their master, they said, was intimate with the greatest immortals; he counted some as friends, others as juniors. Why should he bow to lesser figures? Heaven and Earth were the proper objects of his respect.

Wukong laughed again. The boys were speaking about rank the way celestial clerks had spoken before the Great Sage first made trouble in Heaven. He accused them of boasting and suggested they knew less of the heavenly court than they claimed.

Xuanzang stopped the quarrel. The host was absent, and they had no cause to create a dispute with his household. Let everyone attend to a task. Wukong could look after the horse, Sha the bags, Bajie the food. They would ask permission to use the kitchen, eat a modest meal, and be on their way.

While the disciples occupied themselves, Qingfeng and Mingyue went into their master's private rooms. They found a golden implement used to strike the precious fruit from its stem, a platter, and a cloth to cushion the fruit when it fell. Carrying these objects, they went into the garden and carefully brought down two of the infant-shaped wonders.

They presented the platter to Xuanzang as a humble offering.

The monk recoiled.

On the cloth lay two objects with little heads, arms, and legs. To him they looked like infants not yet three days old. He asked, appalled, what sort of house would serve children as refreshments. Had the boys murdered infants in a place devoted to religious practice?

Qingfeng and Mingyue explained that they were fruit, grown upon a tree in the garden. No child had been harmed. These were a rare gift, especially chosen by their master for this distinguished visitor.

Xuanzang refused to believe it. A tree did not bear human beings, he said, and he would not eat what looked so terribly like a child. He asked them to take the platter away.

The attendants were irritated. Their master had ordered them to offer two priceless fruits, and the guest for whom they had been saved had called them monstrous. Yet the fruit was not meant to be left sitting for long, and the monks' refusal did not diminish its value. They returned to their room and ate one each.

The kitchen stood against the wall of the attendants' quarters. On the other side of that wall, Bajie was trying to prepare the meal.

He heard the talk about the ginseng fruits. He listened while the attendants grumbled over Xuanzang's refusal, and then heard the sound of two extraordinary delicacies being enjoyed by people who already had a thousand years to spare. The thought of such a fruit made him abandon interest in his cooking.

When Wukong returned from tending the horse, Bajie beckoned him into the kitchen. There was treasure in the abbey, he whispered. A fruit that gave long life. The master had been offered some, had refused, and the two little Daoists had gone off and eaten it themselves. Was there not a way for the monkey to bring his brothers a taste?

Wukong had stolen peaches from the Queen Mother's orchard and swallowed pills from Laozi's storehouses. Stealing a fruit was a familiar branch of his accomplishments. He asked where it grew and what tool was needed to take it down. Bajie described the golden striker he had heard the boys mention.

The monkey slipped invisibly into their room. The golden tool was hanging near the window. He took it and made his way past a garden of flowers, then a vegetable plot, and then through another gate.

Beyond it stood the ginseng fruit tree.

It was enormous. Its roots were as thick as a building's foundations, its trunk rose high into the air, and its leaves gathered in a deep, green crown. Among those leaves the strange fruits hung in small human shapes. Wukong, who had seen gods with three heads and demons with nine, still stared at the fruit with astonishment.

He sprang into the branches and struck one with the golden tool. It fell. He jumped after it and searched the ground.

There was nothing there.

No fruit, no broken skin, not even an impression in the soil. It was as though the precious object had struck the earth and passed straight through it.

Wukong was indignant. Somebody was stealing from the thief. He summoned the spirit of the garden and accused the minor deity of pocketing his prize.

The spirit protested. He had no right to handle even one fruit, let alone to take one from the Great Sage. The ginseng fruit had a peculiar nature. Five substances affected it in different ways. Contact with metal knocked it from the branch, but if it touched earth it vanished into the ground. The way to gather one was to strike it with the proper tool and catch it in cloth before it could fall. Other materials, too, could damage or transform it. Such fruit must be handled with its own laws, not like an ordinary peach.

Wukong saw the mistake. He climbed the tree again, held open the front of his robe, and struck down three fruits in succession, catching each safely in the cloth. Then he returned the golden tool to the attendants' room and went back to the kitchen.

Bajie brightened. Sha was called from his duties, and Wukong opened the fold of his robe. The three brothers looked down at three small fruits that appeared to be staring back at them.

Sha recognized what they were. He had never eaten one, but in his old service at the celestial court he had seen such objects presented as gifts at immortal banquets. He knew their rarity.

Wukong handed out one each.

The monkey ate his carefully. Sha also took time to understand the taste. Bajie, who had been aching with anticipation, swallowed his in a few impatient bites.

Then he looked from one brother to the other. What had it tasted like? Had there been a pit? Were there layers of sweetness? He had been so eager that the pleasure had passed before he could notice it. Surely, now that Wukong had shown how easy it was, they might fetch just one more fruit so he could eat properly.

Wukong refused. This was not a bowl of rice that could be refilled. A thousand generations of ordinary people might live and die between harvests. They had taken three, and three would be enough.

Bajie grumbled that the others had enjoyed their fruit more than he had.

The two attendants, returning for tea, heard him. They found the golden implement out of place. Fear replaced irritation. They hurried to the garden and counted what remained.

Four fruits were missing. One had fallen into the earth, three had been eaten, but Qingfeng and Mingyue could not know the details. They knew only that someone in the abbey had trespassed upon their master's most treasured possession.

They confronted Xuanzang.

The monk had not eaten a fruit and had not permitted his disciples to steal one. He demanded to know what the boys meant. Bajie claimed ignorance. Wukong laughed in his habitual way, which the boys took as evidence of guilt. When the monk asked for a truthful answer, the monkey admitted the theft: Bajie had wanted to taste the fruit; he had taken three; the three disciples had eaten them.

Why, the attendants asked, had four disappeared?

That question turned the dispute from confession into accusation. Bajie immediately wondered whether Wukong had kept a fourth to himself. Wukong knew that the first fruit had sunk into the earth, but the boys were already insulting the travelers as thieves and gluttons. The monk urged them to accept responsibility and apologize. The attendants' anger grew. They had been trusted with a sacred tree and had allowed four fruits to disappear while their master was absent.

Wukong could tolerate being called a thief somewhat better than being lectured about his thievery by two boys he considered pompous. But when the abuse continued, his patience broke.

He pulled a hair from his head and changed it into a convincing double of himself. The double stayed with Xuanzang and the others, apparently enduring the rebuke. The real Wukong slipped back to the garden.

There stood the tree, the one object that made the abbey richer than kings or gods.

He took out his iron staff, struck the trunk, and applied the strength with which he had once shaken Heaven. The great tree cracked, shifted, and fell. Earth opened around its roots. Leaves showered the garden. The remaining fruits disappeared as they met the soil. A living marvel that had required ages to grow lay broken across the paths.

Wukong returned, absorbed his hair-double, and said nothing.

When the two attendants went to look again, they found the ruined garden. They were too frightened even to begin shouting. Their master had trusted them with a tree whose fruit took thousands of years to ripen. Now not only the harvest but the tree itself seemed lost.

They did not challenge Wukong at once. Instead they made a plan. They would apologize to the travelers, pretend that the earlier count had been mistaken, serve their food, and lock them inside the abbey until Zhenyuanzi returned. Better to preserve the culprits for judgment than to try to fight the great sage themselves.

They went back wearing smiles. The fruit, they said, had not been missing after all. They offered apologies and a meal with an abundance of small dishes. Bajie took the change of tone as proof that he had been right all along: one should not accuse honest guests of stealing without checking one's garden.

As the bowls were raised, the doors slammed shut. Locks went on the hall and then on the outer gates. From outside came a fresh stream of accusations, no longer concerned with a few fruit but with the destruction of the whole tree.

Xuanzang put down his bowl.

He asked Wukong, quietly at first, whether he had done what the boys alleged. The monkey could no longer deny it. The monk was appalled. Taking food without permission had been bad; destroying the tree because the owners complained was something worse. The travelers had entered as guests, accepted shelter, and caused a disaster.

Wukong had no answer that would make the act right. He waited for the household to fall asleep and then said he could get them out.

Bajie feared that the monkey would escape as an insect and leave the others trapped. Xuanzang reminded Wukong of the tightening fillet around his head: an escape that abandoned the master would not be an escape from duty. Wukong assured them that he meant to bring everyone.

One lock after another yielded. In darkness they led out the horse and baggage and went west, hoping that the house of Five Villages would be left behind them.

## A sleeve larger than the sky

Zhenyuanzi returned to find two frightened attendants and a story that sounded almost unbelievable. The distinguished monk had come. His disciples had stolen fruit. The boys had quarreled with them. And the ginseng fruit tree, the ancient heart of the abbey, had been torn from the earth.

The immortal was angry. He did not collapse in helplessness, but he was determined to find the travelers and make them answer for what they had done. He traveled west on a cloud so swiftly that he passed them without seeing them. When he looked back, he discovered they had barely traveled a fraction of the distance he had covered.

He descended in the appearance of an ordinary Daoist traveler and spoke to Xuanzang beside the road. Had the monk passed Five Villages Abbey on Longevity Mountain?

Xuanzang answered with innocent politeness. Wukong, who knew immediately where the question led, interjected that they had taken another road.

The stranger named the ginseng fruit tree. The monkey's pretense fell away. Zhenyuanzi revealed himself and demanded that the tree be restored.

Wukong raised his staff.

The immortal stepped into the air and defended himself with no weapon beyond the whisk in his hand. They exchanged blows, but Zhenyuanzi had no need to match the monkey's violence. He opened one sleeve of his robe toward the wind. The sleeve expanded in an impossible way, and the entire company—monk, monkey, pig, Sha, horse, and baggage—vanished inside it.

Bajie tried to tear his way out with the rake. The cloth felt soft in his hand, yet no weapon could pierce it. Wukong understood that they had entered a technique called a universe in the sleeve. They were not in an ordinary pocket. They had fallen into a space ruled by someone else's power.

Back at the abbey the immortal shook them out like objects from a bag. His disciples tied Xuanzang to a pillar and bound the three companions nearby. The horse was given fodder; the luggage was stacked out of the way. Zhenyuanzi ordered a special whip brought out, a seven-star lash made from dragon hide.

The first person to be punished, he said, would be Xuanzang. He was the master of the party and must answer for his disciples.

Wukong objected immediately. The master had not stolen the fruit or destroyed the tree. He himself had done it. If someone must be beaten, beat him.

Zhenyuanzi accepted that. The whip fell thirty times, one stroke for each fruit in a complete harvest. Wukong turned his legs hard as iron. The blows rang out, but they did not hurt him.

Then the immortal ordered another beating for the master, whose discipline had failed. Again Wukong intervened. Xuanzang had known nothing of the theft until afterward, he said. If blame attached to a disciple's conduct, let the disciple bear it. Zhenyuanzi noticed that the arrogant monkey could at least show loyalty to his teacher. Another thirty strokes struck Wukong's iron legs.

By night the prisoners were still tied, and Xuanzang's shoulders ached from the ropes. He reproached the disciples for bringing him to this humiliation. Wukong reminded him that he had taken the punishment. The master answered that he had still spent the day tied to a pillar and had not been able to reach the road. Sha quietly pointed out that the rest of them had been tied there too.

When the abbey slept, Wukong shrank enough to slip his ropes, freed the others, collected the horse and baggage, and led them away. This time he wanted to give them a head start.

He ordered Bajie to pull up four willow saplings. The pig grumbled but brought them. Wukong arranged the trees where the prisoners had stood, sprinkled them with his own blood, and changed them into speaking likenesses of the four pilgrims. The ropes were fastened as before. The false prisoners could answer when called.

By dawn the real travelers were a considerable distance away. Xuanzang was tired and asked to rest. Wukong, who could endure many sleepless nights, allowed it.

At Five Villages Abbey, Zhenyuanzi ordered the whipping to begin again. The apparent prisoners answered when addressed. But as the blows fell, Wukong, miles away, felt a shiver. He understood: his transformed tree was being beaten. He ended the spell.

The disciples at the abbey found themselves whipping willow trunks.

Zhenyuanzi laughed despite his anger. The monkey, he admitted, was worthy of his reputation for escaping nets and tricks. But an escape was not the same as an answer. He set off in pursuit again.

This time the three disciples attacked together. Wukong's staff descended, Bajie's rake came in from another direction, and Sha's great staff swept toward the immortal. Zhenyuanzi turned his whisk and warded off the weapons without visible difficulty. Then he opened his sleeve.

All four pilgrims, the horse, and the baggage disappeared a second time.

Back at the abbey Zhenyuanzi changed his punishment. Xuanzang, Bajie, and Sha were wrapped in cloth and coated with lacquer, their faces left exposed. The boys fetched a great cooking vessel and set it above a fire. The immortal ordered the vessel filled with oil. Wukong would be thrown into it and fried for the destruction of the tree.

Wukong looked at the kettle with a curiosity that would have seemed monstrous in another prisoner. He had survived Laozi's furnace in Heaven. A pan of hot oil might merely warm him. But he understood that this immortal was no ordinary jailer and that a new trick could endanger his master.

He secretly changed a stone lion near the courtyard into a bound likeness of himself. His true body slipped out of sight. The disciples struggled to lift the false monkey; it was astonishingly heavy. At last enough hands heaved it into the boiling oil.

The stone smashed the bottom of the vessel. Oil poured out, the fire hissed, and the unfortunate attendants recoiled with burns from the splashes. Once the illusion had vanished, they saw the broken lion lying in the ruined kettle.

Zhenyuanzi's fury gave way to frustration. He could not easily hold or harm the monkey. But Xuanzang was still there. If the disciple could escape every punishment, perhaps the master would pay for him.

He ordered a fresh vessel prepared for the monk.

Wukong heard. He came down from his hiding place and begged the immortal not to touch Xuanzang. He would take the punishment himself. The man who had pulled down a tree rather than endure an insult now stood between his master and a burning pot.

The conflict had come to the point where a further contest of force could settle nothing. Wukong could break kettles, escape bonds, and survive beatings. Zhenyuanzi could capture the entire company whenever he chose. The only way out would have to alter the reason they were fighting.

Wukong offered to restore the tree.

Zhenyuanzi regarded him with skepticism. The fruit tree was not a field shrub one could push back into the earth. Its roots were older than kingdoms. But if Wukong could bring it to life again, the immortal said, he would release the pilgrims and count the quarrel settled.

Xuanzang looked at his disciple. Where could he possibly find a remedy for such a wound?

Wukong had a saying ready: cures for extraordinary ills might be sought across the sea, among immortals whose arts exceeded those known upon earth. He would visit the islands of the eastern ocean. He promised to return within three days.

The master gave the promise a sharp edge. If the monkey did not return, he would recite the spell that tightened the fillet about his head. Wukong accepted the condition. Before leaving he warned Zhenyuanzi to feed the monk properly and keep him in comfort. A disciple might travel across the world, but he could still worry whether his teacher would receive enough tea.

Then he rose into the air and was gone.

## Searching the islands for a remedy

The eastern ocean held islands known to immortals and almost unreachable to ordinary travelers. Wukong came first to Penglai, where bright mists hung around high pavilions and three venerable figures were playing a board game: the personifications of Prosperity, Eminence, and Long Life, often called Fu, Lu, and Shou.

They greeted the monkey with the affectionate suspicion that old acquaintances reserve for a friend who has caused too much trouble. Had he not renounced his disorderly life? Was he not escorting the Tang monk west? What had brought him away from the road?

Wukong told the truth. He had stolen three ginseng fruits, quarreled with the attendants, and uprooted the tree. Their master had captured the entire party. Wukong had agreed to find a cure, and the clock was running.

The three immortals were appalled. They knew Zhenyuanzi and the value of the fruit. They had no medicine capable of restoring a primordial tree once its roots had been destroyed. They might bestow long life and offer blessings, but what Wukong needed was not a few more years for a person; it was new life for a tree at the boundary of heaven and earth.

They offered to help in another way. If the master worried that Wukong would be late, they would visit Five Villages Abbey, greet Zhenyuanzi, and ask that the three-day limit be extended. The monkey thanked them. He could not afford to waste the day in leisure, so he took his leave and flew onward.

The three reached the abbey while the pilgrims waited.

Bajie recognized them and immediately began treating them as old acquaintances of his own. He pulled at Long Life's head and clapped a monk's cap upon it, as if dressing a festive figure for a procession. When scolded, he invented a joke about adding a cap to a blessing. He searched the folds of Prosperity's robe for edible gifts, invented another pun when corrected, and seized small implements to beat like a set of festive bells. Xuanzang repeatedly ordered him to behave.

The immortal visitors accepted the chaos with more patience than the household boys had shown. They explained to Zhenyuanzi that Wukong had asked for a remedy, that he was still seeking one, and that they hoped the master would grant time rather than punish Xuanzang for a delay. Zhenyuanzi honored his visitors and agreed to wait.

Meanwhile Wukong landed on the island of Fangzhang, a place of shining buildings and immortal gardens. He sought the Eastern Lord, Donghua Dijun, whose knowledge of pills and longevity was famous. A servant named Dongfang Shuo appeared, and he and the monkey exchanged jokes about stealing peaches and elixirs. Such jokes were easy for creatures whose lives had passed through celestial kitchens and banquet halls. This time Wukong was not laughing much.

He explained the ginseng tree's destruction. The Eastern Lord had a precious pill capable of aiding living beings. But the plant Wukong had uprooted was a primordial root of the world, not an animal with a wounded body. That pill would not repair it. The immortal sent him away without a cure.

Wukong crossed to Yingzhou, another of the legendary islands. There he found a gathering of nine elders who lived among the lovely things immortals enjoyed: rare fruit, clear drink, flowers, music, and conversation. They were pleased to see the Great Sage until they learned why he had come. Then they too reproached him for his temper and admitted that they had no remedy.

They invited him to remain and enjoy their hospitality. He accepted a small refreshment and refused to linger. Xuanzang was waiting under another man's authority. The longer Wukong stayed among the immortals, the more likely the master would be left suffering on the road.

The monkey had traveled across places that promised long life. None could restore the ruined tree. He had learned the limit of one kind of power: immortals could prolong life, protect it, and enjoy it in brilliant gardens, but not all of them could renew what had been destroyed at its root.

There remained one figure whose help had moved the pilgrimage forward whenever its companions proved unequal to a difficulty.

Wukong turned his cloud toward Guanyin's abode.

At the southern sea he came upon the guardian of the Purple Bamboo Grove, the black bear spirit whom he had once hunted on Black Wind Mountain. The bear now lived under the bodhisattva's discipline. He greeted the monkey by his religious name.

Wukong bristled. He had spared the bear, he said, and the bear should show the Great Sage proper respect. The guardian reminded him that one who sought the way should not endlessly rehearse another's old offenses. Guanyin was expecting him.

Wukong entered and bowed.

The bodhisattva asked where the pilgrim had reached. When she heard Longevity Mountain and Five Villages Abbey, she knew the Daoist immortal who lived there. Wukong admitted the whole affair: the refusal of the offered fruits, the theft instigated by Bajie's hunger, the insults of the attendants, his own decision to destroy the tree, and the two captures from which he had failed to free the party permanently.

Guanyin was displeased. Zhenyuanzi was no petty hedge priest. Even she accorded him great respect. The tree was one of the rarest living things in the world. Why had Wukong acted so destructively before discovering whose property he was handling?

He did not try to persuade her that the deed had been harmless. He asked for help. The others had been released upon his promise. He must honor it.

The bodhisattva asked why he had wasted precious time among the islands before coming to her. Then she told him what was in her porcelain vase.

It held sweet dew with the power to revive the roots of immortal plants. Long ago, she said, Laozi had tested her by heating a willow branch until it was dry and scorched. When she placed the branch in her vase, it became green again. If the dew could restore a burned branch, it could surely revive a tree that had been pushed down.

Wukong's relief was immediate. He had begun the journey seeking a medicine he could not name and had found the answer in the same place he had visited to recruit Sha Wujing. Guanyin took up her vase and willow spray and set out with him.

## Water that could touch the roots

At Five Villages Abbey the three immortals were still visiting. Zhenyuanzi and Xuanzang were receiving them when the news came that Guanyin had arrived with Wukong. The old master went out to welcome her. The visitors exchanged respectful greetings. No one mistook the meeting for an ordinary quarrel between monks and Daoists. Each knew that an act of real destruction had brought them together.

Wukong urged them toward the garden. There lay the ginseng tree, its roots exposed, branches broken, and leaves withered. Xuanzang saw for himself what his disciple had destroyed. Qingfeng and Mingyue stood near the wreck, scarcely daring to hope that the visitors could mend it.

Guanyin asked Wukong to open his hand. She dipped the willow branch into the vase and drew a sign of renewal upon his palm. Then she told him to place that hand beneath the roots.

He reached into the torn soil. Water began to gather there, clear and bright as a hidden spring. Guanyin gave a precise instruction: the water must not be handled with vessels that would interfere with the tree's response to the five elements. They must gather it in jade, not in ordinary metal or wood.

Zhenyuanzi had no large jade ladle. He did possess jade cups. Would those serve?

Guanyin said that they would. The attendants brought cups and little vessels. Wukong, Bajie, and Sha lifted the fallen tree together and settled its roots back into the earth. Others supported the trunk while the precious water was scooped up in cup after cup. Guanyin scattered it over the roots and branches with her willow spray, reciting the necessary words.

There was a pause in which the entire company watched and listened.

Then the tree began to answer. Fresh green moved along the branches. Leaves unfolded. The exposed roots gripped the earth. Where the trunk had been broken, living wood recovered its strength. The fruits appeared again among the leaves, as though the tree had never fallen.

The two attendants began to count.

There were twenty-three fruits.

That was one more than they had counted after the theft. They were bewildered. They had seen twenty-two on the branches when they accused the travelers of taking four; now the revived tree bore twenty-three.

Wukong seized upon the opportunity to clear up the remaining grievance. He had not secretly eaten a fourth fruit. The first one he struck had fallen to earth and vanished. The three brothers had eaten only three. When the tree was revived, the lost fruit had returned with it. Guanyin had understood the fruit's response to the elements, which was why she had required jade vessels for the water.

Bajie had no comment that would improve his position. His suspicion of his brother had been proved wrong, but he remained a man who had swallowed a miracle so quickly that he never knew its taste.

Zhenyuanzi's anger gave way to gratitude. The tree stood upright; the ancient root was living. The debt that had brought him after the travelers could now be settled in a better form than flogging or hot oil.

He ordered a feast. Ten ginseng fruits were struck down for the company and arranged with great care. Guanyin, the three visiting immortals, Xuanzang, Zhenyuanzi, the three disciples, and the abbey's own inhabitants were all included in the sharing. Xuanzang, who had fled from two infant-shaped fruits when they were first offered, finally understood what the objects were and accepted one. He had not been wrong to recoil from what he believed were murdered children; he had been mistaken about the nature of the food.

Bajie, at least, had a second opportunity to notice the flavor. Wukong could hardly resist observing that one need not swallow everything before looking at it.

The abbey filled with a different kind of conversation. The guests who had recently been prisoners sat at a table with the man who had captured them. The Daoist master and the Buddhist protector had worked together to repair the harm. A terrible quarrel had ended with a tree restored and fruits distributed rather than hoarded.

Zhenyuanzi went further. He proposed that he and Wukong become sworn brothers. That was not an erasure of the destruction, nor did it mean that all their differences had ceased to exist. It was a new relation formed after the monkey had accepted responsibility, sought a remedy, and brought the ancient tree back to life. There was drink, food, and the formal warmth of a bond between them.

For several days the company remained at the abbey as honored guests. They rested, ate, and recovered from the pursuit. Xuanzang's body was stronger after the rare fruit, but his mind was fixed upon the road. There were still scriptures to obtain and a country waiting for his return. Hospitality was welcome; endless delay was not.

At last he persuaded Zhenyuanzi to let them depart. Wukong took leave of his new sworn brother. The two attendants bowed to the travelers who had caused them so much fear. Bajie gathered the bundles. Sha attended to the horse. The gates of Five Villages Abbey opened, and the pilgrims stepped once more onto the western road.

The story of the tree had begun with an act of hospitality: two fruits offered to an old friend. It had passed through a refusal, a theft, an insult, and an act of disproportionate anger. It could not have been settled by finding someone strong enough to defeat Wukong or someone clever enough to evade Zhenyuanzi. Both had already proved impossible in their own ways. The tree had to live again.

Now it did. The four companions had gained a few days of peace, and the monkey had learned, at great expense to others, that even his extraordinary strength could not make a broken thing whole without help.

## Non-spoken source and reading companion

**Scope:** Selected Chinese chapters 24, 25, and 26, joined as one continuous ginseng-fruit-tree narrative. Chapter 24 opens by freeing Bajie from the cords of the four-sages test; this manuscript begins there and ends after the restoration, feast, and swearing of brotherhood, with the departure established in chapter 27's opening. This is original English narrative prose, not a line-by-line translation. The source's poems and dialogue are paraphrased or recast.

**Primary witness by scene:** [Chapter 24](https://zh.wikisource.org/wiki/%E8%A5%BF%E9%81%8A%E8%A8%98/%E7%AC%AC024%E5%9B%9E) (`cl-jtw-ch24`): Zhenyuanzi's instructions to Qingfeng and Mingyue, the old relationship with Golden Cicada, Xuanzang's refusal of infant-shaped fruit, the theft, and the fruit's five-element properties. [Chapter 25](https://zh.wikisource.org/wiki/%E8%A5%BF%E9%81%8A%E8%A8%98/%E7%AC%AC025%E5%9B%9E) (`cl-jtw-ch25`): quarrel, uprooting of the tree, escape, Zhenyuanzi's universe-in-the-sleeve captures, the willow-tree decoys, whipping, lacquered bindings, and stone-lion substitution in the oil vessel. [Chapter 26](https://zh.wikisource.org/wiki/%E8%A5%BF%E9%81%8A%E8%A8%98/%E7%AC%AC026%E5%9B%9E) (`cl-jtw-ch26`): the immortals on Penglai, Fangzhang, and Yingzhou; Guanyin's revival by sweet dew; jade vessels and the count of twenty-three restored fruits; the fruit banquet and sworn brotherhood.

**Continuity detail:** Zhenyuanzi's garden initially has twenty-eight fruits; he orders two served, which the attendants eat after Xuanzang refuses. Of the next four that disappear, Wukong loses one to earth and steals three to share with Bajie and Sha. The boys count twenty-two remaining, and after the tree is restored there are twenty-three. The apparent discrepancy is a deliberate part of the story, not a manuscript arithmetic error. The original chapter uses the interplay of metal, earth, and other elements to govern handling the fruit. It also explicitly describes the immortal trying to beat the pilgrims and have Wukong fried in oil; this retelling does not remove the cruelty from the conflict.

Yu, revised [*The Journey to the West*, vol. 1](https://press.uchicago.edu/ucp/books/book/chicago/J/bo12079590.html) as a scholarly bibliographic reference, not a copied English source.
