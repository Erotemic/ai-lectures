# Faust II: empire, Helen, land, and the final salvation

## Faust — Part Two, Story: A New Morning and the Emperor’s Paper

Part Two begins with a sleeping Faust in a pleasant landscape. Spirits move around him, and Ariel calls for rest that will restore his ability to act. The opening does not show a trial of what happened to Gretchen or an apology to her family. It shows the protagonist's consciousness being renewed. The omission can be troubling. It is also structurally important: Faust resumes his quest without the audience being told that its first great human catastrophe has been repaired.

At dawn, Faust turns toward the rising sun, only to find its direct radiance unbearable. He looks instead at a rainbow suspended in the spray of a waterfall. Light becomes visible through reflection and changing colour. The image suggests that human beings may approach the highest truth indirectly rather than possess it in an overwhelming flash. The scholar who once demanded the Earth Spirit's immediate presence begins this new part with a figure for mediated knowledge.

The scene shifts dramatically to an imperial court. The emperor is surrounded by officials who describe the state's difficulties: disorder, unpaid soldiers, strained revenues, and an administration unable to satisfy its demands. Mephistopheles enters the political theatre, disguised as a court fool. The problems are public rather than private. Faust's adventures will now involve the management of whole populations, and the devil will apply his appetite for fraud at a larger scale.

The court prepares a masquerade. Costumed groups bring before the audience familiar emblems of wealth, power, beauty, appetite, and labor. The spectacle is witty and difficult to reduce to one allegory. Amid it Mephistopheles helps stage a fantasy of abundant resources hidden in the soil, while Faust appears in a symbolic role. Political authority enjoys the appearance of limitless possibility. The theatre itself becomes a means of preparing the emperor to accept what sounds like a financial miracle.

In the following pleasure-garden scene the court discovers that paper money has been issued, bearing the emperor's authority and supposedly secured by buried treasure in imperial lands. The emperor's signature has been used with the assurance of advisers who know how to turn future or invisible riches into current purchasing power. Suddenly many debts appear payable and the court celebrates. The scene is not a modern textbook proof that all paper currency is fraud. It is a satire of a government that takes a promise for an achieved increase in wealth, and of advisers who use the ruler's appetite for a quick solution.

Faust and Mephistopheles are useful at court because they can create appearances that allow power to continue without confronting its material limits. That is different from a public policy capable of providing lasting goods. In Part One the wizard entered a young woman's bedroom and planted jewels. In Part Two, the agents of enchantment help a state distribute newly authorized paper claims. Both scenes ask how an alluring representation can be mistaken for a secure possession.

The emperor enjoys the performance enough to demand another: he wants to see the ancient lovers Paris and Helen of Troy. This demand will push Faust beyond social comedy and financial trickery toward the realm of classical images. The court can order a show, but neither Mephistopheles nor Faust has simple command over the sources of the beauty the emperor requests. The next scenes explore what happens when ancient myth must be made present before a modern audience.

## Faust — Part Two, Story: The Mothers and Helen’s Image

The emperor has requested a spectacle featuring Paris and Helen, figures from the Trojan War. Mephistopheles is quick with ordinary tricks, but this demand defeats his usual methods. He cannot simply pull the classical ideal of beauty from a pocket. He tells Faust that he must enter the domain of the Mothers, mysterious powers beyond familiar space and time. Their name suggests originating forms and creative possibilities, but Goethe never turns them into a clearly mapped religious order. The very strangeness of the encounter is the point.

Faust receives a key that will guide him. He must descend into a realm without normal directions and find the means of summoning images. Mephistopheles describes the journey with a mixture of solemnity and mischief. Faust does not know how to move in a place that has neither reassuring landmarks nor the sensory world he once longed to possess. Yet he goes. In Part One he insisted on immediate experience; here he has to pass through an abstraction so extreme that the ordinary word experience seems inadequate.

Back at court, expectation becomes impatience. Guests wander through illuminated halls, requesting magical favours, as if the powerful stranger's art must work like a service. Goethe lets this portion of the drama laugh at the consumers of wonders. The emperor is not asking what it means to encounter antiquity; he wants a famous spectacle on command. Meanwhile the real risk of conjuring has been delegated to Faust.

In the Hall of the Knights the summoned forms of Paris and Helen appear. The onlookers respond in different ways, projecting desire and judgment onto the figures. Some admire, some criticize, some compare what they see to their own expectations. Helen seems to surpass ordinary beauty, but the scene does not offer a fixed definition of what makes her beautiful. It offers a performance of people reacting to a compelling image.

Faust becomes more than an exhibitor. He grows jealous when Paris approaches Helen, as though the apparition belonged to him. He tries to intervene in the classical scene as if he could change it by his own desire. When he reaches toward the apparition with the key, the conjuring is broken by an explosive disturbance. Faust collapses. The show ends not with the emperor's secure possession of antiquity but with the magician injured by his attempt to seize it.

This collapse has a precedent. Faust once summoned the Earth Spirit and discovered that he was not its equal. Now he encounters a vision of classical beauty and assumes he has a right to possess what he can evoke. The magical instrument brings an image into view, but not the power to command its meaning. Mephistopheles' usual knowledge proves insufficient before the classical world; Faust's own impatience completes the failure.

The drama will continue through another long detour. Faust is carried back to his former academic surroundings, where an odd scientific creation is about to emerge. That path ultimately leads him toward Helen again, but not through the same conjuring act. In Part Two, progress rarely means moving in a straight line from desire to attainment. It means crossing forms of knowledge, poetic traditions, and scales of time that resist being reduced to a private wish.

## Faust — Part Two, Story: The Old Study and Homunculus

The opening of Act Two returns to a place the audience knows: Faust's old study. He lies unconscious, having collapsed after trying to possess Helen's apparition. Mephistopheles enters the Gothic room with a sense of recognition. The physical setting has endured while characters and fashions have altered. Goethe makes the place a museum of Faust's former ambitions. The desk and narrow walls still stand, but the man who once felt trapped by them now exists at the edge of myth.

An old student, now confidently intellectual and dismissive of established wisdom, confronts Mephistopheles. The exchange satirizes the university, intellectual arrogance, and the claim that each younger generation has finally understood what its elders could not. It is not a straightforward victory of youthful science over old superstition. The devil speaks through these arguments, exposing how pride and fashionable language can occupy positions on either side of a dispute.

Wagner, once Faust's assistant, has devoted himself to experiments. In a laboratory he produces a Homunculus, a tiny, luminous human-like intelligence enclosed in glass. Goethe gives the figure astonishing verbal confidence almost from birth. Homunculus understands something of the situation surrounding sleeping Faust and wants to find a fuller mode of existence. This is a comic scientific fantasy, not a plausible description of laboratory embryology or a claim that Goethe predicted a specific modern technology.

The contrast with Faust is deliberate. Faust has a human body and worldly experience but is dissatisfied with both. Homunculus has intellectual brilliance and an artificial enclosure but lacks ordinary physical formation. Neither is complete. Wagner is proud that he has made life through his art, though the creature immediately seems to exceed the creator's intentions. Like other imaginative works about making human beings, the scene asks whether the successful production of a form gives the maker authority over its future.

Homunculus diagnoses Faust's need to enter a classical imaginative world. A route is proposed toward the Greek landscape of the Classical Walpurgis Night. The usual northern witches' festival has a counterpart in a world populated by figures from Greek myth. Mephistopheles, so comfortable on the Brocken, is not entirely at home among these older forms. His powers, his comic habits, and even his preferred grotesques do not transfer without friction.

The group departs: Faust in pursuit of Helen, Homunculus in pursuit of embodied life, and Mephistopheles looking for a way to take part in a realm whose rules he cannot simply dictate. Their separate goals make this episode more comprehensible than a catalogue of monsters. The new character is not merely a diversion from the Helen plot. His longing to break out of glass will take the work toward the sea, natural generation, and the tension between an idea of life and the conditions that make life possible.

What has changed since the first study scene? In Part One, Faust abandoned the academic disciplines because knowledge had left him spiritually empty. In Act Two, academic science appears almost capable of creation, yet what it has made longs for a world beyond the laboratory. The difficulty has not been solved by making science more magical. Goethe has multiplied the forms of incompleteness, and sent them into a landscape where metamorphosis rather than possession may be the most important kind of becoming.

## Faust — Part Two, Story: Classical Walpurgis Night and the Sea

In the strange night of Greek myth the travelers separate. The landscape holds the plains of Pharsalus, memories of civil war, river deities, seers, sphinxes, and creatures who belong to incompatible myths and epochs. Goethe does not require the reader to believe all these figures once occupied one historical place. The dramatic space brings together a cultural inheritance: antiquity as an immense store of forms through which later Europe imagined nature, politics, beauty, violence, and fate.

Faust pursues signs of Helen. He consults figures whose knowledge belongs to other ages, including the centaur Chiron, remembered as the educator of heroes. Faust seeks not a museum object but the living woman whom the apparition had made irresistible. Mythic guides can direct him toward further initiation, but no single speech gives him possession of the past. In this part of the play antiquity must be approached through a journey involving landscape, story, and imagination.

Mephistopheles, meanwhile, negotiates the grotesque underworld of Greek forms. His habitual sarcasm makes him conspicuous rather than omnipotent. The distinction between northern and classical supernatural worlds is comic but also conceptual. The devil who could orchestrate the witches' dance in Part One now encounters a cultural order he neither created nor fully understands. He eventually takes on an appearance suited to that realm, but adaptation is not mastery.

Homunculus undertakes the most coherent journey of the act. Trapped in his glass vessel, he seeks a means of achieving natural existence. He consults the pre-Socratic philosophers Thales and Anaxagoras, who represent contrasting ways of imagining the world's formation, and then Proteus, a figure of changing shapes. His longing leads toward water and the long process of transformation. Goethe's mythological sequence combines scientific interests with poetic speculation. It is not a reliable ancient theory of evolution retold as fact, but it does stage a conviction that becoming is more fundamental than the sudden possession of a finished form.

At the sea, the scene becomes ceremonial. Marine beings celebrate the passage of a beautiful figure, Galatea. Homunculus approaches the goddess's shell or vessel in intense excitement. His glass enclosure is broken, and his light disperses into the waters. The ending is both a loss of the witty talking creature and a poetic suggestion of entry into the generative life of nature. It cannot be described simply as an ordinary human birth. Goethe leaves the transformation in the language of image and celebration.

The sea festival and Faust's search for Helen share a question but not an answer. Both concern the desire to cross the distance between an imagined form and living reality. Homunculus moves toward the material conditions of existence rather than insisting that mind alone should be enough. Faust's pursuit, in contrast, remains caught in a vision of an ideal woman whose reality is inseparable from poetry and legend.

Listeners sometimes experience the Classical Walpurgis Night as an interruption of the plot. It will feel less so if we identify the journeys within it: the search for Helen, the devil's displacement among foreign gods, and Homunculus's attempt to become. In Part One, the poet used a witches' gathering to distract Faust from Gretchen's suffering. In Part Two the mythic night is more than distraction. It is the medium through which the work asks what must change when a human desire crosses from one form of life, art, or history into another.

## Faust — Part Two, Story: Helen, Faust, and Euphorion

Act Three opens in Sparta after the Trojan War. Helen, accompanied by a chorus of captive Trojan women, approaches the household of Menelaus. Even in this scene her position is precarious. She has been treated as the cause of a war, yet she is also a woman returning to an uncertain reception from her husband. The atmosphere darkens when a formidable figure called Phorkyas warns of sacrifice and danger. The figure is Mephistopheles in a disguise suited to the Greek world. Instead of securing a peaceful restoration, Helen is directed toward a fortress ruled by a stranger.

The stranger is Faust. Goethe deliberately makes the scene cross historical and literary styles: ancient Sparta meets a castle that belongs to medieval romance. This is not a plausible chronological incident. It is the dramatic meeting of traditions the poet wishes to bring into conversation. Faust greets Helen with reverence and, through an exchange shaped by language and metre, begins a courtship. Their speech itself becomes part of the encounter between classical and later European art.

The scene changes toward Arcadia, a pastoral landscape where the union appears temporarily fulfilled. Helen and Faust have a son, Euphorion. He is beautiful, energetic, musical, and unable to remain within ordinary limits. The idyll is not simply a happy interval between catastrophes. Its son embodies the dangerous marriage of creativity with an impatience for unbounded height. He dances, challenges restraint, and increasingly imagines a glory associated with danger and battle.

Euphorion's behavior also contains an act of coercion. He seizes a young woman, who resists and turns into flame. Goethe makes it impossible to identify the young man's high energy entirely with innocence. Euphorion then climbs, attempts flight, and falls to his death. The chorus invokes Icarus. The scene has often been read in relation to the poet Byron, whose death in Greece was recent for Goethe when this material was developed. That connection is a valuable historical interpretation, not permission to flatten Euphorion into a one-to-one coded biography.

Helen cannot remain. At the child's death her appearance withdraws, and Faust is left with her garments or veil as a remnant. The dramatic union of two worlds has produced a moment of poetry and a catastrophe, not a permanent marriage. The very impossibility of holding Helen resembles Faust's earlier seizure of her apparition in the imperial court. Beauty has appeared, inspired, and vanished. Its passing is part of its meaning.

The chorus does not simply disappear as a single undifferentiated group. Its continuing voices and transformations help the ancient world recede into other aspects of nature. The act concludes with movement onward for Faust. His desire will turn away from the figure of perfect beauty toward action in a political and physical landscape. There he will attempt to reshape not an image but the land itself.

Act Three is sometimes described as the wedding of Classicism and Romanticism. That phrase is useful if we remember it is an interpretation of the play's artistic experiment, not a historical marriage. Greek tragedy, medieval courtly imagery, lyric pleasure, heroic youth, and loss occupy the same theatrical space. The result is not an achieved synthesis that lasts forever. It is a work of art about the intensity and instability of creation, staged through a family whose members cannot remain together.

## Faust — Part Two, Story: The Emperor’s Battle and the Coastal Grant

When the Helen episode has ended, Faust appears in a mountain landscape. The mood changes sharply. He has lost an ideal that seemed for a moment to become embodied, but he no longer seeks a private romance. His imagination turns toward the movement of the sea and the prospect of reclaiming land from it. Where the young scholar wanted to escape the world, the older Faust now wants to act upon its physical shape. It is a new ambition, yet it carries the same desire to exceed limits.

Mephistopheles returns and offers attractions that fail to interest Faust. The devil can produce fantasies of luxury and sensation, but Faust speaks of a practical project: controlling water, building barriers, making new territory where the sea now lies. Its scale will demand political authority and organized work. The devil sees how the idea might be linked to the emperor, whose troubles did not end with his court's magical finances.

The empire is in civil conflict. A rival claimant threatens the ruler, and the army needs help. Faust and Mephistopheles arrange a victory through devices that include supernatural spectacle and deception. Their intervention is not a conventional account of good military strategy. Goethe juxtaposes political force, technical improvisation, magical fraud, and soldiers' willingness to read strange events as signs of victory or defeat. The result is a battle won under questionable influences.

At the rival emperor's tent, the participants turn toward rewards and the distribution of advantages. New relationships of obligation are written into political victory. The emperor offers Faust rights over coastal territory, including land that must still be wrested from the sea. This is the opening Faust requires. A vision that began as the impulse of one man now has an imperial document behind it.

Goethe does not let the political settlement remain a simple reward for public service. Powerful clergy and princes demand gifts, privileges, and guarantees. The archbishop insists upon extensive compensation in the name of spiritual authority, even reaching toward the coastal grant. The emperor who seemed supreme while battle was being won must bargain with institutions that expect a share of what he can give. The scene is a satire of rulers, religious office, and opportunistic elites, not an argument that every member of a class behaves alike.

The end of Act Four therefore prepares a difficult moral test. Faust could use his enlarged power to improve conditions for others. The sea-control project has an intelligible public purpose. But he has obtained it through service to a ruler, manipulations supplied by Mephistopheles, and an allocation of land made without asking every person already living near it. Ambition now operates through administration, labor, and coercion, not merely secret magic.

At the outset of the whole play, Faust had felt dismayed at the gap between intellectual fame and the possible harm of his medical practices. Decades of imagined experience later, he stands poised to remake a landscape for the public good while relying again on means whose human costs he has not fully measured. The next act will make those costs visible through an elderly couple living in a house that does not belong to him.

## Faust — Part Two, Story: Philemon, Baucis, and the Fire

Act Five opens far from the splendor of the imperial court. A traveler returns to a small dwelling where a very old couple, Philemon and Baucis, once saved him from a storm at sea. Their generosity has survived in his memory. Now the landscape around them has changed. Faust's immense reclamation project has pushed the sea away and created ports and cultivated ground. From the traveler’s perspective, the transformation is astonishing; from the couple's perspective, it is also threatening. The old home and its chapel remain as a small enclave within a new system of control.

Philemon and Baucis belong to an ancient classical tradition about hospitality, but Goethe does not simply reenact that myth. He uses familiar names to give moral weight to a modest, settled life. Their house is valuable not because it commands trade routes or supplies the greatest possible yield, but because it is theirs, and because they have used it to shelter somebody in need. This makes it a pointed counterweight to Faust's immense calculation of human benefit.

From his palace, Faust hears their little bell. He cannot bear it. He has reached the position of a rich and powerful organizer, but the existence of one patch of land outside his possession disturbs him more than his achievements satisfy him. He wants the old people removed and offers an alternative estate. His wish is not presented simply as a plan to murder them. But his impatience makes their consent irrelevant, and he gives the task to Mephistopheles and his brutal helpers.

Mephistopheles has just boasted that his ships have multiplied through trade and seizure; he sees little separation among commerce, war, and piracy. This boast warns the audience how his efficient execution of a command may differ from the lofty ends Faust imagines. The devil himself invokes the biblical story of Naboth's vineyard, in which a ruler's desire for another person's property leads to injustice and death. Goethe puts that comparison into the mouth of the accomplice, not an impartial historian.

At night the small house burns. The old couple and the returned traveler perish in the violence surrounding their displacement. Faust is appalled when he learns what has happened and insists that he wanted an exchange, not a robbery. His protest matters because intention and execution are not identical. But it is not an acquittal. He used Mephistopheles, ignored the residents' attachment to their home, and treated resistance as something to be overcome by command.

The burning home links Part Five back to Gretchen's chamber in Part One. In each case Faust encounters a dwelling that represents a life outside his control. In each case he briefly perceives or could perceive the value of that life, then enlists the devil to secure an outcome he wants. The difference is scale. The earlier injury unfolded through gifts, sexual secrecy, and social shame. This one unfolds through property claims, labor, and organized violence. A man who can imagine public improvements still cannot recognize that his good intention does not give him absolute jurisdiction over the lives of others.

Faust's project has not become worthless because of this crime. The dikes and new land may sustain many people. Goethe's drama is sharper than a simple condemnation of engineering or economic development. It shows the inability of a calculation about total benefit to answer the question of what may rightly be taken from particular people. The unresolved tension leads into Faust's final encounter with Care, old age, death, and the judgment Mephistopheles expects to claim.

## Faust — Part Two, Story: Care, the Grave, and Salvation

After the burning of Philemon and Baucis's home, four gray figures approach the palace. Their names are commonly rendered Want, Debt or Guilt, Need, and Care; English choices vary because the German names carry more than one shade of meaning. Only Care can enter. She confronts Faust with what his restless life has evaded. He has seized one experience after another, always expecting the next achievement to end a sense of lack. Care is not merely a supernatural villain. She speaks a knowledge of dependence, anxiety, and finitude that Faust has spent a lifetime denying.

Faust tries to reject her power. Care breathes upon him and blinds him. He announces that inner light remains, and summons workers to complete the great project. Without sight, he judges the world through an image of what he hopes to create. He hears digging and imagines the advancing earthworks that will make fertile land secure from the sea. In fact, Mephistopheles has ordered grave-digging spirits, the Lemures, to prepare Faust's burial place. The same sound supports opposed realities: the project the dying man imagines and the grave the audience knows is being made.

Faust pictures a people living freely on a land maintained by collective struggle against the water. The vision may be his most expansive public hope. It is not simply a desire for private wealth or bodily pleasure. Yet its language remains compromised by the command to conscript, compel, and direct labor. We have just witnessed the destruction of a family home that stood in the way. Goethe does not arrange the evidence so that the final aspiration can be separated neatly from the methods by which it was pursued.

In a famous moment Faust says that to such an instant he could bid it stay. The conditional phrasing leaves the promised outcome unsettled. The devil's contract concerned a moment so satisfying that Faust would wish it permanently fixed. At his death Faust speaks from an imagined future rather than from an actually completed social order. He falls, and Mephistopheles believes his claim is mature. Whether the wording of the bargain gives the devil a legal victory is a question the play raises, but the ending will not be decided by contract law alone.

Angels descend with roses. Their presence overturns Mephistopheles' expectations in a scene that mixes solemn spiritual action with comic embarrassment. The devil, who has been an able expert in temptation, finds himself distracted by the beauty of the angelic beings. As he loses his control of the occasion, the angels carry away Faust's immortal part. They appeal to love and to a life of continued striving. This is not a speech in which Faust persuades a judge that every injured party has been compensated.

The final scene moves into mountain gorges populated by holy figures, penitents, and a vision of divine femininity. Gretchen appears again among the redeemed, now praying or pleading for Faust. Her return shows that the play has not forgotten the tragedy with which Faust's story began. Yet the spiritual meeting does not turn her earthly life into a happy romance retrospectively. Her mother's death, her brother's killing, and the infant's death remain events that the text has required us to confront.

The concluding chorus speaks of the eternal feminine drawing us upward. There is no short paraphrase that converts the entire ending into an agreed doctrine. Christian imagery, poetic aspiration, grace, desire, and the claim that striving can be redeemed coexist in a theatrical conclusion. Goethe gives the devil a defeat, but he does not give the reader a ledger in which each human cost is canceled by Faust's achievements.

The work ends beyond the horizon of individual satisfaction. Its final movement is upward, but its entire history is still present beneath it. The challenge for a reader is to keep both in view: an ending that imagines salvation, and a tragedy that has shown how devastating an imperfect human being's quest can be for those whose lives intersect with his power.
