# Journey to the West: the dragon horse and the burning monastery

## Journey to the West - The Dragon at Eagle-Sorrow Stream

The road changed as winter came on. Xuanzang and Wukong moved among cliffs, narrow paths, and mountains that seemed to crowd together above them. Bare trees shook in the cold wind. The monk rode the white horse from the Tang court, and Wukong carried the baggage or scouted ahead, keeping the dangerous road clear.

One day Xuanzang heard the sound of rushing water, louder than the wind among the stones. He called to his disciple and asked what stream could make such a noise. Wukong recognized the region. This was the Coiled-Snake Mountain country, he said, and the water was likely Eagle-Sorrow Stream, a ravine famous for its depth and rushing current.

They came to the bank and paused to look. The water sprang out from the heights, flashing in the cold light. It ran deep between steep rocks. Xuanzang held his horse's reins and watched the current below.

Without warning something broke the water apart. A dragon rose from the ravine, sending a wave against the rocks, and lunged toward the monk. Wukong reacted first. He dropped the baggage, seized Xuanzang, and pulled him clear of the horse. The dragon missed the monk, but it caught the white horse, saddle and bridle together, and swallowed it in a single mouthful. Then it disappeared beneath the stream.

Wukong carried Xuanzang to a safer place and returned for the packs. The horse was gone. He searched the banks and then rose into the air, shading his eyes with his hand. His fiery gaze ranged over the surrounding country. No white horse fled across the distant hills. Wukong returned and announced what had happened: the dragon had eaten it.

Xuanzang could scarcely believe him. How could any creature have a mouth large enough to swallow a horse with its saddle? Perhaps the horse had bolted into a hollow or escaped among the rocks. Wukong reminded him that his eyesight could detect the smallest movement across great distances. He could see insects from high in the air, he said, and he had seen no sign of a horse. There was only one explanation.

The monk began to weep. Without a horse, how could he travel across the mountains and deserts still to come? This was not an idle complaint. He had already learned how hard it was to walk across a single hillside with a tired animal, and the journey stretched far beyond anything he had experienced. Wukong's patience broke. He told Xuanzang to sit where he was while his disciple went down and demanded the horse back.

But Xuanzang was afraid to be left alone. What if the dragon came out again and carried him away? Wukong could not bear the contradiction. His master wanted the horse restored, yet would not let the only person capable of confronting the dragon leave his side. The monkey shouted that if Xuanzang meant to sit beside the stream forever, nothing he could do would help.

At length Wukong went to the water. He drew out the iron staff, stood at the edge, and shouted insults into the ravine. The dragon came charging out. They fought above the stream, twisting across the spray and the cliffs. The dragon had claws and teeth and the power of the river, but the monkey's staff was too heavy, and his skill too great. After a prolonged struggle the dragon withdrew into deep water where a staff could not easily reach.

Wukong returned to his master without a horse. Xuanzang asked whether the creature had truly eaten it. His disciple could hardly contain his indignation. Of course it had; why else would the dragon have fought so bitterly? Xuanzang then reminded him of his boast that he could subdue dragons and tigers. If he could kill a tiger without effort, why had this dragon escaped?

Wukong turned back at once, determined that no one should think him incapable. He went to the stream and used a power that churned its depths, lifting mud and water until the quiet places of the ravine became violent. The dragon, unable to rest in the stirred-up water, sprang out again in anger. Wukong demanded the horse. The dragon admitted it had swallowed the animal. It could not simply cough it back up, however fiercely the monkey threatened it.

They fought again. The dragon could not match Wukong's staff, so it changed itself into a small water snake and vanished among the grasses and crevices. The monkey searched with the staff ready to strike, but the lesser form slipped through the ground and disappeared. Now strength had twice failed to solve the problem. Wukong could defeat the dragon in a fight but could not retrieve the dead horse, and he could not find the creature once it had hidden.

The monk waited on the bank, exhausted and afraid. The dragon had eaten the very animal that made the journey possible. Wukong had risked fighting it twice and still had nothing to show except fury. They did not yet know that the creature in the water had itself been placed there by Guanyin to serve the pilgrimage. Neither master nor disciple had thought to ask the one question that would have changed the whole encounter: whether the dragon was also waiting for someone who sought the scriptures.

## Journey to the West - Guanyin and the Dragon Horse

Unable to find the dragon that had swallowed Xuanzang's horse, Wukong summoned the local mountain and earth spirits. They came before him trembling. His first impulse was to punish them for failing to greet him since his release from the mountain. But they had an explanation. None had known exactly when the Great Sage would be free, and they had feared to appear uninvited.

Wukong spared them and asked a more urgent question. What was the dragon in Eagle-Sorrow Stream, and where did it hide? The spirits explained that the stream had once been free of such a creature. Guanyin had brought a young dragon there and ordered it to wait for a pilgrim. The dragon had been told not to make trouble, although it occasionally seized animals to feed itself. The spirits did not know why it had attacked the Tang monk's horse. If Wukong wanted to bring it under control, they advised calling the bodhisattva who had placed it there.

Wukong wanted to go at once. Xuanzang, cold and hungry, objected. How long would his disciple be gone? He was frightened of being left by the same water where the dragon had attacked. A voice came from above them. A golden-headed guardian, one of the divine protectors assigned to the pilgrimage, offered to carry the message to Guanyin himself. The local spirits could remain and watch the monk.

The guardian departed for the southern sea. He came to Guanyin's abode and explained the trouble: the dragon waiting in the ravine had eaten the pilgrim's horse, and Wukong could not bring it out. Guanyin was astonished. The creature was a son of the Dragon King of the Western Sea. He had damaged a precious pearl by setting fire to his father's palace, and his father had reported the offense to the heavenly court. The son had been condemned to death. Guanyin had pleaded for him and received permission to give him another chance by serving the scripture-seeker as a mount.

This was the answer to a puzzle begun long before Xuanzang had left Chang'an. Guanyin had passed the ravine on her eastward mission and arranged for the condemned dragon to wait there. The plan had not failed because the dragon refused to serve. It had failed because no one in the first battle had recognized anyone else.

Guanyin came to the ravine and called the dragon out. The young creature rose from the water in a human form and bowed. He explained that he had waited for the pilgrim, but that a fierce monkey had appeared, shouted at him, and attacked. Hungry, he admitted, he had swallowed the horse. Wukong protested that the dragon had never asked who he was. The dragon replied that he had indeed demanded an explanation, but the monkey had answered only that he must return the horse. No one had mentioned obtaining scriptures or the Tang monk.

Guanyin listened to both. Wukong's powers were unquestionable, but his instinct was to intimidate first and explain afterward. She told him that he would meet more beings on the road who had already agreed to join the pilgrimage. If he announced the mission before reaching for his staff, some might submit without a fight. The advice was gentle compared with the blows that he and the dragon had already exchanged.

Then Guanyin took the precious pearl from the dragon's throat and touched him with dew from her willow branch. She spoke the word of transformation. The great, dangerous body of the water dragon shrank and changed into the form of a strong white horse. It was not the same horse that had come from Chang'an; that animal had been consumed. This was a new companion wearing the form of a mount, fitted to travel distances that would destroy an ordinary beast.

Guanyin instructed the transformed dragon to carry Xuanzang faithfully. If he served the journey and atoned for his offense, he could obtain a reward beyond the life of an ordinary dragon. The young dragon submitted. Wukong took charge of him and prepared to return to his master.

But the monkey was unhappy. The trouble had been immense already, he told Guanyin. If every stream, mountain, and traveler concealed another such complication, how could anyone expect him to guard a fragile human monk all the way west? He declared that he would rather give up the task.

Guanyin reminded him of his former efforts to seek immortality and understanding. Was he, after enduring a five-hundred-year punishment and finally finding a path toward a better end, ready to abandon it over the loss of a horse? She promised that divine help would answer him in times of real danger. And if he were trapped by a peril beyond the other guardians' power, she herself would come.

As a further gift, she plucked three leaves from her willow branch and changed them into three fine life-saving hairs, which she set at the back of Wukong's head. In the most desperate circumstances he could use them to produce a way of escape. The monkey, whose arrogance had so often carried him into trouble, now received a small reserve of help whose value depended on knowing when to ask for it.

Guanyin departed. Wukong led the white horse back toward Xuanzang and began explaining what had happened. The monk had thought he had lost his means of travel. Instead he had acquired a companion whose own history of punishment and release resembled Wukong's. The pilgrimage was slowly gathering a company not of flawless saints, but of beings given a chance to change their lives through service.

## Journey to the West - Across the Ravine and Onward to the Next Temple

Xuanzang looked at the white horse that Wukong had brought back from the stream. It resembled the horse that had carried him out of the Tang capital, but his disciple explained that the lost animal could not be restored. The dragon that had swallowed it had now taken its place. Guanyin had arranged the substitution long before master and disciple arrived at the ravine.

The monk had scarcely accustomed himself to traveling with a monkey capable of flying and changing shape. Now he was to ride an animal that had only recently possessed claws, scales, and a dragon's jaws. Yet the new horse stood quietly. The dragon's purpose on the journey was different from Wukong's. He would carry Xuanzang across the long road, while the monkey guarded him and managed the baggage. There was room for both forms of service.

The stream itself still barred their way. Wukong could cross it in a bound, but Xuanzang could not ride through the torrent. After seeking the bank, they found a small raft coming downstream with an old boatman at its pole. Wukong called to him and asked him to ferry the pilgrims west. The man brought the raft close enough for them to load the horse, packs, and monk. Xuanzang climbed aboard carefully, aware of the moving water below him. Wukong helped with the baggage. The pole pushed off and the raft slipped across Eagle-Sorrow Stream.

The passage was swift. On the farther bank Xuanzang reached for a small payment, but the boatman declined it and pushed away. The monk kept thanking him as he disappeared into the current. Wukong was amused. Had his master not recognized the ferryman? He was the spirit of the stream, whose absence had nearly earned him a beating from the Great Sage. The boatman had been wise not to ask for money. Xuanzang could not tell whether his disciple was joking or reporting another part of the invisible world that surrounded them.

They traveled until the light began to fail. Ahead stood a building with the appearance of a village house but a roof adorned in the manner of a shrine. Xuanzang thought it might offer lodging. Wukong said it looked more like a temple. At the entrance they discovered a local shrine, and an old caretaker welcomed them. He wore prayer beads and offered tea. The monk paid respects to the image inside, then asked what the shrine was for.

The caretaker explained that it honored the community's earth spirit. Local people made offerings at the seasons of planting and harvest, asking for good crops, healthy livestock, and peace through the cycle of the year. The monk listened with interest. The customs were different from those of the Tang lands he had left. Every few days of travel now carried him into practices he did not know. The journey would be not only a passage across dangerous mountains but a passage through human and divine worlds with different ways of ordering their lives.

When Xuanzang explained that he was traveling west for the scriptures, the host was pleased and gave them food and shelter. He also told Wukong that a full set of horse gear could be provided in the morning. The dragon had changed into a horse, but he lacked a proper saddle and bridle. A mount meant to carry a traveler for many years needed more than four legs and a strong back.

At daybreak the caretaker appeared with the promised equipment: saddle, reins, fittings, and all that a well-appointed traveler might require. The gear suited the white horse as though made for him. Xuanzang thanked the man warmly, and Wukong helped put everything in place. As they prepared to leave, the caretaker offered a riding whip as well. With such gifts their little company looked more capable of continuing than it had before the attack at the stream.

Just as Xuanzang thanked the old man one last time, the caretaker vanished. When the travelers looked back, the shrine itself had disappeared: where a building had stood there was open ground. A voice called from the air. The visitor had been the spirit of the mountain and the local earth, sent by Guanyin from Mount Luojia with the saddle and bridle. The pilgrims must go west diligently, the voice told them, and not neglect their task.

Xuanzang immediately got down from the horse and bowed toward the vanished spirit. He bowed again, asking that his gratitude be conveyed to Guanyin. Wukong laughed until he could scarcely stand. The spirit was already far away, he told his master; there was no use kneeling forever before empty air. Xuanzang reproved him for refusing to bow to a divine helper. Wukong replied that he had never spent much time bowing, even in front of the Jade Emperor, and that the spirit ought to be glad the Great Sage had not demanded an explanation for failing to welcome him earlier. The monk called this boastful nonsense and told him to get moving.

The gifts had not been merely the work of a friendly stranger. Guanyin's arrangement had reached into the ordinary equipment of travel. The newly transformed dragon was ready for the road, and the monk who could not have walked across the continent now had a mount suited to it. Some helpers came in radiant glory; others appeared as patient householders, caretakers, or boatmen, arranging the necessities without which a journey could not proceed.

Days passed. Winter loosened its hold. After a long interval of mostly peaceful travel, trees began to show new growth, and the cold colors of the hills changed with the first movement of spring. Wukong bore the baggage and watched the road; Xuanzang rode the dragon horse. Their quarrels had not ended, but their work had found a rhythm. The monk had food to seek, a horse to care for, and vows to keep. The monkey had to find shelter, guard against danger, and resist the impulse to answer every obstacle with his staff.

Near sunset one evening they saw the roofs of a religious compound beyond the trees. Xuanzang pointed them out. They might find a place to rest there. Wukong agreed and urged the horse onward. The buildings rose before them, quiet in the late light, and the monk hoped that the shelter would offer the same kindness he had received at Famen Temple and the hunter's house.

But a temple could conceal dangers of its own. The next trouble would not begin with a tiger or a dragon announcing itself in the open. It would begin with an object of beauty, a monk who admired possessions, and the possibility that hospitality could turn into greed.

For now the travelers approached the gate together: Xuanzang, Sun the Pilgrim, and the white dragon horse. The famous journey had finally acquired its first companions, not through a peaceful recruitment but by the collision of old punishments, mistaken attacks, the generosity of strangers, and the difficult business of learning to travel in one another's company.

## Journey to the West — The Bell and the Elder Monk

The white dragon horse stopped before a monastery whose roofs rose one behind another among the trees. Xuanzang had been looking for a place to sleep, and here was one with gates, halls, bell towers, and a shrine to the very bodhisattva who had put him on the western road. The sign over the main hall read Guanyin Monastery. The monk felt the relief of reaching a house devoted to a power he had reason to thank.

The first monks who came out to receive them bowed courteously to the traveler from the Great Tang. They asked his country and his purpose. Xuanzang explained that the emperor had sent him toward the Western Heaven for scriptures, and that he hoped the monastery might shelter him for one night. They invited him inside for tea. Then one of them noticed what was leading his horse.

Wukong had been quiet for the length of that exchange. He was carrying the baggage and holding the reins, looking in every way like an alarmingly capable porter. The host stared at his face and asked what sort of creature the monk had brought with him.

Xuanzang lowered his voice. It would be better not to ask that question loudly, he advised. His disciple was quick to take offense. Whatever the face suggested, the fellow was useful.

In the great hall Xuanzang saw the image of Guanyin and immediately wanted to make his devotions. He bowed, spread himself upon the floor, and thanked her for the help he had already received on the road. The monastery monks began the proper accompaniment. One sounded the drum. Wukong found the great bell and joined in with enthusiasm.

By the time Xuanzang had finished bowing, the sound was still crashing through the buildings. The bell rang again, and again, at intervals chosen entirely by the monkey. At last someone protested that the ceremony was over. Why was he still beating the bell?

Because a monk, Wukong replied, ought to ring the bell for as long as he was a monk.

The disturbance brought more monks out of the cloisters. They had expected to find some rude traveler interfering with their worship. Instead they saw a hairy face, a pointed mouth, and a figure that looked ready to tear a stone door off its hinges. Several fell to the ground and called him Thunder Lord. Wukong seemed delighted by the mistake. A thunder god, he said, would have to count himself one of Wukong's descendants. He told them not to tremble. They had visitors from the Tang court to entertain.

Tea was served in the abbot's rooms. The horse was stabled. The baggage was carried in. Xuanzang tried to restore the calm of a proper visit, apologizing for any inconvenience and answering questions about the road. Then the hosts announced that their eldest master had come to greet the travelers.

Two boys supported him through the doorway. He was astonishingly old. His spine bent, his mouth had lost teeth, and his face was deeply lined. Yet his hat shone with stones, his clothing was embroidered, and even his shoes had the decoration of a rich man's collection. His staff was adorned with expensive fittings. The old monk was treated with a respect approaching awe by the community around him.

He greeted Xuanzang and asked how far the travelers had come. They spoke of mountains and borders already crossed. The elder shook his head: he had spent his life within the walls of his monastery and had not seen a fraction of the world his guest described. He called himself a man looking at the sky from the bottom of a well.

Xuanzang asked his age. Two hundred and seventy years, the elder replied.

Wukong, who had lived through five centuries under a mountain after already making himself immortal, muttered that this venerable monk would still be a very distant junior of his. Xuanzang gave him a warning glance. The elderly host, apparently taking it for nonsense, asked the disciple's own age. Wukong would not say. He had already said quite enough to make his master uncomfortable.

The servants brought tea on a pale jade tray in gold-trimmed cups. Xuanzang praised the vessels. The old monk made a show of modesty. Such pieces were of little consequence, he said, compared with the wonders of the Tang empire. Surely the travelers had brought some treasure from that great country for him to look at?

Xuanzang answered that travelers could not carry much. Even if China possessed treasures, the road was long, and a pilgrim needed very little.

That was almost the end of it. The guest would have taken his tea, offered thanks, and gone to sleep. But Wukong remembered the embroidered robe folded in their baggage. Guanyin had given it for Xuanzang to wear in special circumstances. The monkey thought it would settle this business of treasures at once. If the host wanted to see something splendid, why not show him the robe?

The monks laughed. A robe, a treasure? There were chests full of robes in the monastery. Their elder had been gathering them for centuries. If the visitors were impressed by textiles, the hosts could provide a display.

The elder ordered the storehouses opened. Twelve chests were carried out. Lines were strung across the courtyard and racks set in place. Robe after robe appeared, sewn with silk, embroidery, gold thread, elaborate patterns, and precious fabric. They filled the space like a market of costly ceremonial garments.

Wukong examined them with the frank amusement of someone who had once stolen peaches from Heaven. Very fine, he agreed. Now put them away and let us show ours.

Xuanzang caught his sleeve. He had noticed the direction of the conversation. It was no longer hospitality, but competition. He whispered that they were alone in a strange country; showing an object that could awaken greed might put them in danger. A person who saw such a thing could begin to desire it, and desire could become a plan. If a greedy man demanded it, refusal might cost more than the treasure was worth.

Wukong brushed off the warning. He could defend a robe, surely. Had he not defeated the armies of Heaven?

The monkey reached into the baggage. Xuanzang saw that argument was useless. The room was full of rich cloth and curious monks. The most precious robe in their possession was about to be unfolded, and his disciple had no intention of concealing it.

## Journey to the West — The Robe and the Murder Plot

Wukong opened the bundle. Before he had quite unfolded the robe, light glimmered from inside the wrapping. He lifted the fabric by its edges, shook it out, and a glow of red and many colors spread through the chamber.

The monks who had just been showing off hundreds of embroidered garments stopped talking. Their master's robes were costly, but this one seemed to carry its own radiance. Pearls and sacred decorations glittered across it. Threads that should have looked like ordinary silk appeared to be worked with light. A garment meant to be worn by a pilgrim had become the center of every eye in the room.

The old monk drew near and stared. In a moment the pride he had taken in his storehouses collapsed. Seven or eight hundred robes had not been enough to prepare him for one that outshone them. He fell to his knees before Xuanzang, and tears gathered in his eyes.

Xuanzang assumed that some misfortune had overcome the elder. What was wrong? How could he help?

The old man said that his failing eyesight was the misfortune. The day was ending; the robe had been spread out for only a few moments; he would never see its craftsmanship properly in this dim light. Xuanzang offered to have lamps brought. The elder declined. The robe was so brilliant that lamplight would only trouble his eyes further. He needed time, not illumination.

Could he, he asked, take it to his room for the night? He promised to study it carefully and return it untouched at daybreak.

Xuanzang turned toward Wukong with the desperate look of someone watching a prediction come true. The elder had begun by asking to see the robe. Now he asked to keep it. What request would follow next?

Wukong told his master not to be frightened. He could hand over the robe. If anything happened, the monkey would take responsibility.

The elder's attendants carried the wrapped treasure away. Beds were made up for the guests in the front sleeping hall, a vegetarian breakfast was promised for the morning, and the monastery settled down for what ought to have been an ordinary night. Xuanzang lay down unwillingly. Wukong seemed little troubled.

In the abbot's quarters, the elderly monk placed the robe before him and wept. His young attendants were bewildered. The thing he wanted was right there. If he wanted to examine it, why not open the wrapping? Why cry over a possession he had been allowed to hold?

He tried to explain. Looking was not owning. He was two hundred and seventy years old, and what had his long life given him? Hundreds of fine robes, yes. Respect from his community, yes. But not this robe. If he could wear it even for a day, he said, he might die satisfied that he had truly lived as a monk.

The attendants suggested an easy compromise: invite the pilgrims to remain another day, and he could wear the garment for that day. Keep them a week, and he could enjoy it for a week. Keep them longer, and there would be no hurry to part with it.

The old man shook his head. It would still have to go away. The robe belonged to Xuanzang. However long their guests remained, the morning of departure would eventually arrive. The elder wanted not a long loan, but possession without an ending.

One of his favorite younger monks, Guangzhi, found a solution to suit such a desire. The visitors were tired and sleeping. The monastery had many strong men. Why not bring knives, break into the sleeping hall, kill both travelers, bury their bodies secretly in the rear garden, and keep the white horse and baggage along with the robe? The object could then become a treasure handed down through the community.

The old monk's tears stopped. He praised the proposal.

Another junior monk, Guangmou, saw a difficulty. The pale-faced pilgrim might be easy to kill. His hairy disciple was another matter. Nobody quite knew what that creature could do. A failed attack with swords would bring ruin on everyone. Better not to fight him at all.

Guangmou suggested fire. Send every group of monks out with bundles of dry wood. Pile them around the travelers' hall, light the wood on all sides, and leave no open door through which the guests could escape. If people outside the monastery noticed, the monks could say that careless visitors had caused a fire and burned themselves to death. Sacrificing a single guest building would be a small price for the robe.

The assembled monks approved the safer-looking plan. Wood was gathered from different parts of the compound. Dry grass and brush were carried through the dark. By the end of the preparations, heaps of fuel surrounded the sleeping hall. There were many hands at work, but apparently very few voices willing to say no.

Inside, Xuanzang slept. Wukong did not sleep in the human fashion. He rested while keeping his senses alert, and before long he heard footsteps too numerous for a quiet monastery. He heard the scrape of heavy bundles being dragged across the courtyard. He listened to the movements continuing where no one should need to be moving at that hour.

He might have thrown open the door and confronted the monks at once. Instead he remembered the warning Xuanzang had given him and decided to learn exactly what was happening. He changed himself into a little bee and slipped through a gap beneath the roof.

Outside, monks were stacking the last armfuls of kindling. Fire was being brought to the wood. The old man's wish for one more robe had led his entire house to prepare the murder of two guests.

Wukong could see a simple answer. He could return to his natural size, raise the iron staff, and break the plotters' heads. But Xuanzang had already rebuked him for killing the six robbers on the road. If he killed the monastery monks too, the disagreement would begin all over again. So he considered an alternative.

He would not stop the fire. He would save his master and let the thieves discover what it meant to burn down their own house. The thought pleased him. It was not the same thing as mercy, but to Wukong it felt very much like restraint.

## Journey to the West — A Canopy Against Fire

The monks were ready to light the wood. Their guests were asleep. The windows of the sleeping hall had been surrounded, and fuel lay against the walls. Wukong, now restored from the shape of a bee, considered how best to keep the fire from reaching Xuanzang.

Water would have put it out. But Wukong was not interested in putting it out. He wanted the thieves' plan to be revealed by its own consequences, while the master who had entrusted himself to his protection remained unharmed.

He somersaulted into the sky and arrived at the Southern Gate of Heaven. There the heavenly guards recognized him at once. The Great Sage who had once thrown their court into confusion was back. Several began making fearful bows before he raised a hand and explained that he had not come to fight.

He needed to borrow an object from the heavenly king who guarded the western quarter, known in Chinese as Guangmu, the Wide-Eyed King. The king came forward to greet him and asked about the pilgrimage. It was known in Heaven that Guanyin had enlisted guardians to watch over Xuanzang and that Sun Wukong had become his disciple. Why had the monkey abandoned the road to come back among his former enemies?

Wukong explained that dangerous men were about to set fire to the place where his master slept. He needed the king's fireproof covering, and he needed it immediately.

The king was puzzled. If the problem was fire, why not ask for water? Would that not be a more natural way to save the innocent?

Wukong replied with absolute confidence. A good rain might save the monks' buildings, and that was precisely what he did not intend. He wanted Xuanzang and the horse safe, not the monastery. The thieves had chosen their own fate. He would protect his party and let everything else burn.

The king laughed without approving. The monkey had left the army of rebellion, but his temper was still the old one. He gave Wukong the covering, and Wukong promised to return it.

In another instant the monkey was back over the monastery. He spread the miraculous shelter across the roof of Xuanzang's sleeping hall, covering the monk, the white dragon horse, and their possessions. The fire could rage against that protected space without harming what lay beneath it.

Wukong then went to the roof of the elder's own room, where the stolen robe had been placed. He intended to watch both the treasure and the fire, confident that he could protect the room if necessary. The monks ignited their fuel. Red tongues climbed the walls, smoke rose through the courtyards, and the first beams began to crack.

The fire was doing well enough on its own. Wukong thought it could do better. He drew breath, worked his magic, and sent a great wind through the monastery.

The flames rushed after the wind. Sparks blew across roofs and through passageways. The blaze climbed from building to building, swallowing the halls in which the monks had prayed, studied, eaten, and stored their goods. The bells and drums that had sounded during Xuanzang's devotions were lost under the roar of burning timber. The monastery people ran in disorder, hauling chests, cooking vessels, and furniture from rooms that had already begun to smoke.

Xuanzang remained asleep in the protected hall. He did not know that anyone had tried to murder him. He did not know that Wukong was making the surrounding buildings burn more fiercely. That ignorance, for the moment, was part of his disciple's design.

Twenty miles away on Black Wind Mountain, a strange resident woke to a red light at the window. He first mistook it for dawn. Then he looked more closely and saw a fire large enough to illuminate the neighboring hills. It came from the direction of Guanyin Monastery.

The creature went to help. He rose into the air, reached the burning compound, and called for water. Buildings were already collapsing; the halls at the front were almost gone. Yet one sleeping hall remained untouched beneath an invisible protection. Farther back, one chamber still stood, and on its roof somebody was stirring the flames with wind.

The visitor understood that this was no ordinary accident. He entered the surviving chamber. The place seemed to hold a patch of radiance amid the smoke. On a table lay a bundle wrapped in cloth. He opened it and saw the embroidered robe.

He had come to fight the fire. Instead he took the treasure. No one stopped him. Everyone was busy with disaster, and the monkey who had promised to guard the robe was occupied with the destruction outside. The strange visitor gathered up the garment and returned to Black Wind Mountain.

Wukong had succeeded at the part of his plan he cared about most. Xuanzang was safe. The dragon horse was safe. The treasure had seemed safe too. But because Wukong was convinced he could control the whole situation, he had not noticed the new thief enter through the confusion he had helped create.

By the time the wind died down and the fire began to exhaust itself, nearly the entire monastery was in ruins. What the elder had hoped would be a secret crime against travelers had become a calamity visible from the mountains. And the robe for which he had risked everything was no longer in his room.

## Journey to the West — Ashes and the Missing Robe

Before dawn the great fire at Guanyin Monastery began to die. The monks who had planned to burn two guests now searched through their own ruined buildings for whatever could be carried away. Some dragged chests out of the ashes. Others tried to make shelter against walls with no roof. Cooking pots appeared among piles of blackened wood. People who had spent the night intending murder were now trying to salvage enough belongings to survive another night.

Wukong took the fireproof covering back to Heaven and returned it to the Wide-Eyed King. The king had doubted the monkey would be careful with a borrowed treasure; Wukong was offended that anyone should expect him to steal it. A thing properly borrowed, he declared, should be properly returned, so that it might be borrowed again. The king invited him to stay and talk. Wukong declined. He was the disciple of a pilgrim now, and could not sit around gossiping in the heavenly court.

He came back to Xuanzang's sleeping hall as a bee, changed into his ordinary shape, and woke the monk. Morning had arrived. It was time to travel.

Xuanzang dressed and opened the door. The view was incomprehensible. Where he expected galleries and covered corridors, he saw broken walls and heaps of smoking timber. The monastery seemed to have disappeared during the night while he slept.

Wukong said there had been a fire. He had protected their sleeping hall, and had not disturbed his master's rest. Xuanzang looked out at the wreckage and wanted to know why the same powers had not been used to save the other buildings.

Then the monkey explained the murder plot. The monks had tried to burn them in order to keep the robe. He had overheard everything, arranged for protection, and let the plan turn against its makers.

Xuanzang was horrified. Had they really meant to kill him? Then another suspicion came. Was Wukong telling the truth, or had he made a disaster out of some insult suffered at the monastery? The monkey protested that the monks themselves had started it. Yes, he admitted, he had added a little wind. They had wanted a fire, and he had given them one.

Xuanzang could scarcely believe the distinction mattered to him. When fire broke out, he said, the proper help was water. Not wind. Wukong answered that he had not begun the attack. Why should he reward murderers by rescuing their property?

The quarrel broke off when Xuanzang asked about the robe. Wukong had left it in the elder's room, where the fire had not reached. Surely it was unharmed. The monk warned that if this precious gift were lost or damaged, he would not hesitate to recite the spell that tightened the band around Wukong's head.

They crossed the courtyard and found monks who had expected to see two corpses. At the sight of the travelers they recoiled, thinking that the dead had come back for revenge. Wukong demanded the robe, not their lives. He sent them to look at the protected hall, whose doors and windows were unmarked by fire. The sight terrified them further. They began to believe that the guests were divine beings whom they had foolishly attacked.

The elder's room had survived, but the robe was gone. The old master was already frantic. His plot had failed; much of his monastery was ash; the treasure he had planned to own had disappeared from beneath his nose. When the monks rushed in to announce that the travelers were alive and demanding their property, he found no answer to give them.

He struck his head against a wall and died.

The remaining community broke into grief and confusion. Their elder was dead, their buildings destroyed, and they still had no robe to hand over. Wukong demanded a full account of the people living in the monastery. The names came to two hundred and thirty. He had the monks searched and the surviving trunks opened. He examined clothing and stores, insisting that the missing robe could not simply vanish.

Xuanzang watched the searches with rising distress. He had told Wukong not to display the robe. The monkey had ignored him, boasted that he could answer for any trouble, and now the treasure had disappeared in a compound filled with frightened people. The monk began to recite the tightening spell.

Wukong fell to the ground clutching his head. He begged Xuanzang to stop. He promised to find the robe. As soon as the pain ceased, he sprang up, snatched out his staff, and turned on the monks. Xuanzang stopped him again. Threats and beatings would not make a vanished object appear.

At last someone told Wukong about the neighboring mountain. To the southeast was Black Wind Mountain. A creature known as the Black King lived in a cave there, and the dead elder had often held discussions about religion and cultivation with him.

That was news worth hearing. Wukong asked the distance. Only about twenty miles. He considered the blaze that had lit the whole night sky. Anyone living that close could have seen it and come to investigate. The elder's room had not burned; the robe had gone missing during the confusion. Perhaps the mysterious neighbor had taken it.

Xuanzang objected that this was a guess. Wukong admitted no uncertainty. He had traced many criminals with less evidence than this, and the fire could have attracted a thief from far beyond twenty miles. He would fly there and see.

Before leaving, he ordered the monastery monks to bury the dead elder, feed Xuanzang properly, and care for the white horse. He made a demonstration of the iron staff against a burned wall. Bricks shattered, and several more lengths of masonry fell. The monks agreed, trembling, to give their surviving hall and all their attention to their guests.

Wukong rose into the air. Xuanzang remained among the ruins, afraid for the robe but unable to continue the pilgrimage without it. The monastery's greed had brought fire upon itself, Wukong's retaliation had magnified the destruction, and a third figure had seized the prize. The search was only beginning.
