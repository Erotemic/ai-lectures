# Journey to the West: The house that fed ten thousand monks

## A sign over a prosperous door

With Tianzhu behind them, the pilgrims traveled through country whose towns welcomed monks and whose mountains were no longer crowded with every kind of enemy. This did not mean that everyone behaved well or that the end of the road had come. It meant that a man could sometimes ask for a meal without expecting the host to reveal a second head after sunset.

Xuanzang still felt the weight of his mission. The emperor had entrusted him with travel papers long ago and had received no scriptures in return. The years that had passed were not an answer. He wanted to move west while they had the strength and the light. His disciples knew that anxiety. Bajie understood it less than he understood the appeal of a proper meal after another day on the road.

They came to Tongtai Prefecture and the district of Diling. In a street lined with shops, Xuanzang saw two elderly men talking beneath a veranda. Their conversation had turned to the rise and fall of great men and governments. They wondered which of the famous heroes of earlier times still mattered to anyone, and whether the words people had spoken about power would survive the lives of those who wielded it. Xuanzang bowed and asked the name of the place and where a traveler might obtain vegetarian food.

The men answered without much ceremony. They told him of Kou's house, a wealthy household farther along the street. Over its entrance was a public notice promising that no monk seeking food would be turned away. If Xuanzang wanted a meal, he should go there, they said, and let the elderly men return to their discussion.

The company followed the directions. People stared at the three disciples. Xuanzang kept asking them to lower their heads and avoid frightening the townspeople. Bajie attempted obedience until he smelled food from the houses. At last they reached a gate set beneath a carved frontage. The sign was exactly as the old men had described. It was not merely an invitation; it was a promise made to strangers whose faces the owner had not yet seen.

Bajie started through the entrance. Wukong caught him by the shoulder.

"An open sign does not mean an open pantry," he told him. "Someone will come out to greet us. You can wait long enough for that."

A servant appeared with a basket and a weighing scale. He took one look at the company, dropped what he was carrying, and ran indoors to say that four extraordinary monks had arrived. Moments later an elderly gentleman came out with a walking stick. He was not alarmed. He called for the visitors to enter and led them through a courtyard to rooms set aside for religious guests.

The owner introduced himself as Kou Hong, known by the courtesy name Dakuan. He was sixty-four years old. His home contained a Buddhist hall with lamps and incense, a place for reading scriptures, rooms for the visiting monks, and kitchens able to provide vegetarian meals. He showed them the room for worship before asking about their journey. Xuanzang bowed before the images, then turned to speak with the host.

"I have traveled from the Great Tang to ask the Buddha for scriptures," he explained. "We only hope for a meal before continuing."

Kou Hong put down his stick with evident happiness. He told them that when he was forty he had vowed to feed ten thousand monks. He had kept records of those he had served for twenty-four years. Recently he had counted them again. The total stood at nine thousand nine hundred ninety-six. Four more were needed.

The servants looked from one visitor to another. There were four of them. Kou Hong was delighted. The four travelers would fulfill the vow that had occupied so many years of his life. He asked their names and begged them not to hurry away. He would arrange a proper ceremony to mark its completion. The road to the Buddha's mountain, he added, was perhaps eight hundred li away. After so many years, could they not rest here a while?

## Kou Hong's family

The old gentleman's wife heard that guests had arrived. Servants warned her that only one of the visitors looked like an ordinary holy man; the others were terrifying to see. She had spent her life watching her husband receive monks and was unwilling to judge a visitor by his face. She dressed and went to greet them. Even so, Bajie's booming voice made her draw back. He introduced himself as one of the three disciples, and she recovered enough to welcome him.

Kou Hong's sons, Kou Liang and Kou Dong, came from their studies and bowed to the guests. They were young scholars, curious about a country far beyond the borders they knew. They asked Xuanzang how long he had traveled and whether the great regions of the world described in their books could truly be reached by a man walking along the roads. Xuanzang told them that he had crossed fourteen seasons of hardship and delay, and that his disciples had protected him through dangers he could not have passed alone.

Kou Hong ordered a feast. The food came in courses: rice, vegetables, fruit, noodles, steamed breads, and more than enough for guests who had gone without a hot meal on many earlier evenings. The old man was as pleased to watch their appetites as Bajie was to satisfy his own. He saw the four additions to his account book not as numbers alone but as human travelers who had crossed the world to reach his door.

Xuanzang thanked him and tried to leave after the meal. Kou Hong would not hear of it. He had waited twenty-four years to complete his promise; surely the guests who fulfilled it could spare him a few days to celebrate. Xuanzang saw that refusal would wound the man's sincere happiness. He consented to stay for a time.

The household became a place of ceremony. Local monks were invited. The family prepared lamps, incense, banners, recitations and elaborate offerings. The visitors heard the same instruments Xuanzang remembered from ceremonies in the Tang lands. The languages and details had changed through the long westward journey, but the rhythm of ritual was familiar. Kou Hong had not traveled with the pilgrims. His vow had been a pilgrimage of a different kind, one made by receiving people rather than going away.

For three days the completion service continued. The family gave food, the monks recited, and the neighbors came to congratulate the old gentleman. As the incense of the final offerings went out, Xuanzang told Kou Hong that the time had come to depart.

## The difference between welcome and delay

Kou Hong feared he had offended his guests. Perhaps the food had been inadequate, or the ceremonies poorly arranged. He asked Xuanzang to forgive any failure of hospitality and remain longer. Xuanzang assured him that the welcome had been beyond anything he had deserved. The problem was the promise he had made long before to the emperor in Chang'an. He had imagined the pilgrimage might take a few years. Fourteen had passed. He had not yet seen the scriptures, and he feared the journey home might take as long again. He could not keep resting merely because the rest was agreeable.

Bajie burst in with an argument that was simple and forceful. The house was safe, the master was generous, and the food was excellent. Why abandon everything good in order to beg another meal in an unknown place? They might stay a year and make the whole household happy. Xuanzang reproved him for speaking as though the mission were a journey from kitchen to kitchen.

"I may go by myself," he said, "if all my companions wish to settle here."

Wukong seized Bajie and gave him a thump for upsetting their master. Sha told him that this was one occasion on which silence would have made him appear wiser. Bajie rubbed his head and blamed everyone except the person who had spoken.

The old man's wife then made her own offer. Her husband's vow had been fulfilled; she had savings and wished to give the four guests another half-month of care under her own name. The sons, too, wanted a chance to provide food from their own means. Their generosity was real, but to Xuanzang it threatened an endless chain of obligations. If the father fed them, and the mother fed them, and each son took a turn, he might be a revered guest forever and never reach the Buddha at all.

He thanked each member of the family and refused. The refusal stung. After all they had done, they felt, why should the guests not allow them one more pleasure? The mother and sons withdrew, resentful despite their goodwill. Kou Hong did not reproach Xuanzang. He understood that even a pious vow, once fulfilled, could become a form of possession if it prevented another person from keeping his own.

He asked for one final evening to arrange a farewell.

## A procession out of town

Before dawn the house woke with preparations. Servants cooked, neighbors arrived, musicians tuned their instruments, and monks and Daoist priests assembled to accompany the travelers. Kou Hong had hired bearers and prepared flags and banners that filled the street with color. The pilgrims rose early, but there was no chance of leaving unnoticed.

A farewell feast waited in the great hall. Bajie saw the spread and advised Sha to eat generously, because no road could promise another such breakfast. Sha told him that only one full stomach could be carried at a time. Wukong added that if Bajie ate so much he could not walk, no amount of courtesy would make the journey continue. Bajie proved them both wrong to his satisfaction by eating quickly and stuffing more bread into his sleeves for later.

Kou Hong's family came forward to see the guests away. The old man did not disguise his grief. This house had fed one traveler after another for twenty-four years, and now four strangers were leaving it with his promise complete. He was happy, but he was also losing a purpose that had made each day distinct. Xuanzang bowed to him with unusual tenderness. There was no gift he could offer equal to the food and shelter the family had given, except the promise to speak of their generosity when he met the Buddha.

The party moved through the town amid music, waving banners and neighbors who had come out of their shops to see what procession could be so important. Beyond the walls they stopped at a roadside pavilion for one last parting. Kou Hong asked Xuanzang to visit again on the return journey. Xuanzang said that if he succeeded in obtaining the scriptures, he would come back and thank him.

After another stretch of road, the old gentleman finally turned toward home. He was crying as he went. Bajie watched the procession disappear and wondered whether such good food would ever come again. Wukong looked at the road ahead. Xuanzang moved forward with the travel papers at his breast.

They had left the household at last, and everyone believed its great act of generosity was complete. But the banners and music had told the town how rich Kou Hong was. Men who had watched the festivities were making plans of their own. By night, the kindness that had opened his door would attract a far more dangerous company.
