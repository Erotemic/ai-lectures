# Journey to the West: Blood on the road and Wukong's dismissal

## A pleasant season, and a road with thieves

The days grew warmer. The travelers had passed through Western Liang, where an enchanted river had brought Xuanzang and Bajie to the brink of giving birth, a queen had offered the monk a kingdom and a marriage, and a scorpion demon had carried him off in a storm. They had escaped those ordeals with Guanyin's help and the intervention of the great rooster-star. Now the road looked green and open. The weather invited rest rather than alarm.

The travelers crossed hills bright with new foliage and descended toward fields where farmers had worked the soil. Bajie began to complain that the horse was moving too slowly. He was hungry, and the sooner the white horse reached a village, the sooner they might eat. He waved his rake and tried to urge it on. The animal barely acknowledged him.

Wukong laughed. He knew horses better than Bajie supposed. Long ago, when Heaven had made him Keeper of the Heavenly Horses, he had learned how to manage an entire stable. He waved the iron staff and shouted, and the white horse sprang forward with such speed that Xuanzang could scarcely hold the saddle. The others had to hurry after him.

It was a small joke, but it delivered the master alone into danger. A band of more than thirty armed robbers emerged from the roadside and blocked his path. They wanted money and valuables. Xuanzang fell from the horse in terror and begged them to spare him. He told them that he was a pilgrim without rich possessions and that his disciples were coming behind.

The robbers did not believe that a fine horse and such a traveling party could be completely without treasure. They demanded his belongings, threatening violence. Xuanzang tried to explain that he was bound for the western scriptures. The men cared less about the purpose of the journey than about the cash they hoped the monks carried.

When Wukong arrived, he found the robbers ready to punish the master for failing to produce their ransom. He stepped forward with his small, mocking voice and offered them a needle, the only thing he said he owned. They jeered at him. He pulled the needle from his ear, enlarged it to a heavy iron staff, and planted it in the ground. The robbers tried to lift it. It would not move.

"You have tried my weapon," Wukong told them. "Now see what happens when I try yours."

They attacked him with swords and cudgels, but those weapons could hardly harm him. He laughed at the blows. Then he swung the staff in return. Two of the robbers fell with shattered heads. The others scattered and fled.

## The burial

Xuanzang was riding away when Bajie and Sha reached him. He asked them to tell Wukong not to kill the men. Bajie went forward and found his brother standing among the bodies. When he asked where the robbers had gone, Wukong said that most had run off while two leaders were sleeping nearby. Bajie bent to examine them and discovered what Wukong meant by sleep. There were holes in their skulls. They would not wake.

The monk returned, saw the blood, and was appalled. Wukong had certainly saved him from a robbery. But Xuanzang did not believe that rescue entitled his disciple to kill people who could have been frightened away or disarmed. He ordered that the bodies be buried and offered to recite prayers for them.

Bajie objected to digging the graves. The monkey had killed them, and it should be the monkey's task to bury them. Wukong, still angry, threatened Bajie with the staff until he obeyed. Bajie scratched at the rocky ground with his rake and finally used his snout to root out softer earth, making enough of a pit to cover the bodies. The narrator's comedy did not remove the fact that two men lay dead beneath the roadside mound.

Xuanzang had no incense or candles. He gathered dirt to stand in their place and spoke a funeral prayer. It began by describing the bandits and the circumstances of their deaths. Then the monk made a distinction which embarrassed his disciples. If the dead men brought a complaint before the judges of the underworld, he said, they should remember that the blow had been struck by a creature surnamed Sun, while the scripture-seeking monk's family name was Chen. They should not confuse the murderer with the man he served.

Bajie readily agreed that he and Sha had not struck the blows either. But Wukong found the division intolerable. For whom had he fought? Who would have died or lost his belongings had Wukong not intervened? The same master whom he had protected now wished to shift all the weight of the killing onto his shoulders.

The monkey spoke his own prayer to the dead, striking their grave with the staff. If they wanted to complain in Heaven or Hell, he said, they should try it. He had known the officials of both worlds for centuries. The gods recognized him, the kings of the underworld knew his face, and no petty accusation was likely to frighten the Great Sage Equal to Heaven.

Xuanzang had hoped his disciple might learn reverence for life. Instead Wukong used the occasion to proclaim that he feared no court, mortal or divine. They rode on in resentment. The dangers of the world had brought them into conflict often enough, but this dispute had not come from a demon. It came from their different answers to a question of what a protector may do.

## The old man's son

At dusk they approached a farmhouse. An old man emerged. The sight of three strange disciples alarmed him, but Xuanzang spoke gently and explained that his companions, however monstrous in appearance, were under vows of service. They wished only a meal and a place to sleep.

The man and his wife eventually admitted them. They fed the travelers and made space for them in an outbuilding. Conversation turned to the family. The old man was named Yang; he was seventy-four years old. He had only one son and a grandson. The son, he said with grief, had turned to crime. He left home with violent companions, robbed travelers, burned houses, and sometimes killed those who resisted. He had been away for days.

Wukong wanted to know why the old man continued to shelter such a son. Perhaps he could find the fellow and put an end to his offenses. The father was horrified by the idea. His son had behaved wickedly; that was true. But he was still his only son. If the old man died, who would bury him? He feared his son's crimes and feared losing him as well.

Bajie and Sha urged Wukong to keep out of the household's affairs. They were not magistrates, and the parents had not asked them to punish anyone. Xuanzang, meanwhile, remembered the two robbers lying in the mound. He began to wonder if the son might have been one of the men in the band. The thought gave him little comfort. The old couple had fed the pilgrims; what if their child and the attackers belonged to the same story?

Before dawn the feared son returned with other robbers. He found a white horse tethered outside and learned from his wife that pilgrims had been given shelter. The company recognized the opportunity. These were the travelers who had killed their leaders, and they were sleeping under the very roof of one of the gang. The robbers prepared food, sharpened weapons, and planned to attack before sunrise.

Old Yang heard them. He could not defeat his son and the men with him, but he could warn the strangers. He slipped into the outbuilding, roused the pilgrims, and explained what was happening. He opened the rear gate and showed them a way to leave before the armed company arrived.

Xuanzang thanked him. The party went out quietly, taking the bundles and the horse. The old man returned to his room, hoping that kindness would not bring destruction upon his house.

## The second killing

By dawn the robbers discovered the empty shelter. They followed the path of the fleeing pilgrims and caught sight of them when the sun rose. Xuanzang heard the pursuit and begged Wukong to drive the attackers back without killing them. The monkey promised little. He turned and faced the company as it rushed forward with weapons raised.

This time the violence was far greater. The robbers were seeking revenge; Wukong was enraged at being hunted after trying to leave peacefully. Their swords and spears could not stop his staff. He swung it through the crowd. Men fell dead or wounded. Those who understood the danger ran; others kept attacking until they could not rise. The party of thieves was broken, but so were many human bodies.

Wukong asked the wounded men which of them was old Yang's son. They pointed to one in yellow clothing. The monkey went to him, took his sword, and cut off his head. Then he carried the head after his master as proof that the old man's wicked son would trouble his family no more.

Xuanzang nearly collapsed when he saw it. The old Yangs had sheltered them, fed them, and saved them in the night. Xuanzang understood how deeply the father had loved the son despite his crimes. Wukong saw only a threat whose removal completed the rescue. He did not understand why the monk should be grateful for shelter and then condemn the violence committed against the householder's dangerous child.

Bajie kicked the head from the path and covered it with earth. Sha helped Xuanzang stand. The monk's fear had turned to horror and moral outrage.

He began to recite the spell that tightened the golden fillet around Wukong's head.

## Pain, obedience, and separation

Wukong fell to the earth. His eyes bulged, his ears burned, and he rolled in agony. Xuanzang continued the chant beyond what was needed to make him listen. The monkey begged his master to stop. He could have borne another army of weapons more easily than this pain from the band he could not remove.

At last the words ceased. Wukong asked what his master wanted. Xuanzang answered that the disciple must leave. The first two killings had been terrible enough. After being warned, Wukong had killed more men and then deliberately severed the head of the son of the very family who had given them shelter. How many times did a monk have to repeat that protecting life mattered? Xuanzang would no longer travel with someone who refused to understand.

The monkey protested. He had defended the party from danger; he had seen armed men coming to murder his master. His actions had prevented them from being robbed and killed. Why should the man who was saved refuse to acknowledge that fact?

Xuanzang did not deny that the danger was real. But he believed that a creature of Wukong's power had ways to stop a band of mortals without slaughtering them. His discipline required compassion toward human beings, even those who had done wrong. Wukong was treating his strength as permission to judge and execute.

Their argument could not be resolved by another demonstration of strength. The whole problem was the use to which Wukong put that strength. Nor could it be resolved by a demand for obedient silence. Xuanzang had the spell, but each use of it revealed how little he could persuade the monkey without pain.

Wukong was dismissed. He bowed, still suffering from the chant, and rose into the air. The others watched him vanish. Once before, after the affair of the White Bone Demon, Xuanzang had sent him away in anger and later needed him to return. This dismissal, however, followed deaths the monk had seen with his own eyes. Whether their separation could be repaired was no simple matter.

The pilgrim party continued with only Bajie and Sha Wujing beside the white horse. They had lost their strongest protector because their teacher would not accept that the road to sacred scriptures might be cleared by killing people. Far away, Wukong had been freed from immediate responsibility for the journey, but not from the golden fillet or the longing to complete his unfinished work.

The next calamity would make both absences impossible to ignore.
