# Journey to the West: The nine-headed lion

## A battle before the walls

The city gates of Jade Flower were shut. Across the battlements the royal guards stood beside the three young princes, who had so recently been students in a peaceful palace garden. The old prince watched a line of supernatural figures gather beneath the dust rising from the plain. At the center of the company was a lion with nine heads, old enough and powerful enough that the others treated him as their ancestor. Before him walked the Yellow Lion, the thief whose banquet had ended in fire.

Wukong, Bajie, and Sha Wujing went out to meet them. The disciples had recovered their weapons, but the men on the walls could not look at the approaching force without fear. A gold-banded staff was still one weapon against an entire family of lions. Bajie called to the Yellow Lion, mocking his stolen feast and demanding to know why he had come with so many shaggy relatives. The Yellow Lion answered with fury. His cave had burned. His servants had died. He had come to collect payment for those losses.

They rushed together. Rake struck shovel; staff met iron mace and axe. The sounds rolled back to the city. Wukong shifted from one lion to another, shielding Sha when two opponents tried to take him from the side. Bajie fought with great force, but his strength could not be kept at its highest pitch forever. The battle continued far past the hour at which ordinary soldiers would have withdrawn.

At last Bajie was caught by a blow and thrown down. Two of the lions dragged him away. Wukong heard his shout and turned. He had no wish to surrender his brother, but the enemy pressed so closely that he could not simply cut through to the prisoner. Pulling out hairs, he breathed on them and multiplied himself into a crowd of small Wukongs, each with a weapon and a shrill battle cry. The sudden swarm confused the lions. Sha fought beside his brother while the little monkeys surrounded the enemy. Two lion spirits were captured and brought back toward the walls, where the prince's men bound them securely.

There would be no victory feast that evening. Bajie remained in the enemy's camp. The old lion sent a message that if the captured lions were kept alive he would not harm the pig; if they were killed, their captors should expect to lose Bajie in return. Wukong accepted the grim exchange for the night. He told Xuanzang that he meant to bring Bajie back and that the other side needed its prisoners as badly as they needed theirs.

The three princes had seen Wukong split into many bodies and rejoin himself. Their fear could not entirely overcome their curiosity. They asked how such a thing was possible. Wukong explained the spell of bodies outside the body, worked through his hairs. The boys looked at the hair on their own arms and understood that martial training had a depth far beyond the blows and turns they had spent their lives practicing.

## Six mouths and three left empty

At dawn the old lion arranged a new attack. He sent the younger spirits out to occupy Wukong and Sha, then took the sky himself. The city guards saw him descending toward the battlements. The old prince's family and Xuanzang were gathered there, watching the battle. They had been told to remain inside the walls, yet no wall could stop a creature who traveled on black clouds.

The nine-headed lion opened his mouths. One seized Xuanzang. Others took the Jade Flower prince and his three sons. He gathered Bajie as well when he passed over the enemy camp. Six prisoners hung helplessly from six jaws, with three mouths still empty. Then the monster flew away toward Bamboo Joint Mountain, leaving the smaller lions to keep Wukong and Sha occupied as long as they could.

The cry from the battlements told Wukong what had happened. He turned to find the royal pavilion empty. His master had been taken in front of an entire city, while he was fighting to defend it. The monkey's answer was immediate and violent. He made another storm of copies and overwhelmed the lions who remained. Yellow Lion himself was killed. The others were taken captive or fled into the hills. But the triumph did not bring back Xuanzang or the prince and his sons.

The princess consort came to Wukong in tears. She had seen her husband and children carried off by a creature whose jaws could have crushed all of them. Wukong told her that the old lion wanted leverage. He had reasons to keep his captives alive. The Great Sage would go after them, but he would not waste the evening running blindly into a place the enemy knew better than he did.

Sha watched the prisoners in the city. Wukong inspected the mountains, found the cave called Nine-Bend Winding, and called for his master. A blue-faced creature fled across a ravine; Wukong chased it, but the cave mouth was closed and the enemy did not come out. When the old lion did face him, his power proved far greater than that of his descendants. Nine heads could strike in different directions, and a single opening among them was enough to seize an opponent. Wukong and Sha were taken in the struggle and brought inside with the other prisoners.

Bajie, bound among them, was at least relieved to see his brothers again. The relief lasted only until he remembered that there was now no one outside the ropes. Xuanzang's composure had been worn thin by captivity after captivity. The young princes, who had begun by asking for lessons in courage, discovered that courage could not by itself undo a knot or pry open a lion's teeth.

## The master of the lion

Wukong found a way to escape the cave in the night. When he reached the open hillside he summoned the local spirits and questioned them about the creature who had taken the royal family. They answered more cautiously than he expected. The nine-headed lion was no ordinary demon king. He belonged to the Celestial Worthy of Supreme Unity, Taiyi Tianzun, a divine master at the Eastern Pole. The beast had escaped his keeping. In human reckoning it had been wandering in the world for years.

Wukong did not linger to denounce the entire order of Heaven. He asked where its owner could be found, leaped to the master's residence, and presented the case. A holy mount had become the center of a household of predatory lions. It had abducted Xuanzang and the rulers of Jade Flower. The guardian who had lost it must come and reclaim it.

The Celestial Worthy summoned the attendant who should have been watching the animal. The servant appeared bewildered and ashamed. He had found a vessel of heavenly liquor, drunk from it, and fallen into an extraordinary sleep. While he slept, the lion had broken free. The liquor, given by Laozi, could hold him for three days in Heaven. Three celestial days had become several human years below. The master understood at once how the escape had lasted so long without being noticed.

The attendant begged to be forgiven. Taiyi Tianzun was stern, but he promised not to kill him, and set out with Wukong to recover the animal. They crossed the sky to Bamboo Joint Mountain, where the guardians of the road came to greet them. The Celestial Worthy told Wukong to go to the cave and call the lion out. He did not plan a long battle. An animal that had become terrifying in a place without its keeper might still recognize the voice to which it had once been trained.

Wukong shouted until the lion came out in anger. The creature opened its jaws to take him. The Great Sage sprang backward to the high rock on which the Celestial Worthy waited.

"Look who is here," Wukong called.

The master's voice sounded across the hill. The nine-headed lion stopped. Instead of leaping he bowed all four legs to the ground. The attendant caught him by the mane, struck him for the escape, and fitted the familiar trappings to his back. Within moments the same animal that had carried off a prince and a monk was once again a celestial mount. Its master rode away on colored clouds.

Wukong watched them go. He had found his master not by overpowering the nine mouths, but by learning whose creature it was and bringing the right person to the right place. A powerful household above had been careless, and an entire country below had endured the consequences.

## Lessons after captivity

Inside the cave Wukong untied Xuanzang, the old prince, all three sons, Bajie, and Sha. The family came out blinking at the daylight. Bajie did not forgive the lion's den for the indignity of his bindings. He brought dry brush and set it alight, leaving the Nine-Bend Winding Cave blackened behind them.

They returned to Jade Flower. The prince's wife and household ran to meet the rescued family. The city prepared another banquet, this time without fear that an enemy was about to appear over the walls. It might have been enough to leave matters there, but the novel follows the fate of the other lion spirits. The captured lions were killed and their bodies divided among the people of the kingdom. The meat was distributed so that the townsfolk might taste it and steady their courage after the attack. Wukong had stopped the celestial mount, but he did not ask that the demon family's lesser members be treated as guests under protection.

The princes resumed their training. Their first lessons had been full of admiration for the glitter of magical arms. Now they had seen how quickly a demonstration could become an actual war, and how even the greatest fighters could be defeated when an enemy struck at those they were obliged to protect. Wukong, Bajie, and Sha showed them the forms again. The students handled their newly forged copies with a seriousness that had been absent when they first challenged the travelers.

At last Xuanzang pressed for departure. The old prince brought gold and silver in thanks, but Wukong would not accept payment for teaching or rescue. Bajie, who was examining the rips made by lion claws in his clothing, asked for something humbler. His robe needed replacing. The court gave fine new garments to the three disciples, made in the colors and forms they already wore. They accepted with pleasure.

The city accompanied them to the road. There were drums and banners, and householders burning incense as the travelers passed. The three young princes bowed for the last time before their teachers. Wukong had offered them strength, but it would be their task to acquire skill, judgment, and restraint after he was gone. Their father had nearly lost his entire family by permitting the lessons, yet he did not ask that the teachers take them back.

The horse moved west. For a few miles the prince's people followed; then the crowd dwindled behind the four pilgrims. The road to the Buddha's mountain was nearer than before. It was also still open to another kind of deception, one that would soon arrive not with an army but clothed in the shape of holiness itself.
