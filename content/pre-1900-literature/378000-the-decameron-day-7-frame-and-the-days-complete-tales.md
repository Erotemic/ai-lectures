# The Decameron · Day 7: frame and the day’s complete tales

## The Decameron — Day Seven: Dioneo and the Valley of the Ladies

Before sunrise, the servants are already on their way to the valley. The little community that fled the plague in Florence has found another place within its country retreat: a wooded hollow with clear water, trees and a little lake. On the previous afternoon the women discovered it for themselves. Now Dioneo, elected king for the seventh storytelling day, orders the servants to prepare a breakfast and the comfortable arrangements needed for a long afternoon's conversation. For once the day's stories will not be told beside the familiar fountain at the villa.

The ten friends depart early, while only the last bright star remains visible. They hear birds calling as they walk toward the valley. Breakfast is set under trees beside the water, where fish can be seen swimming. After food and song, carpets are spread on the grass. Some have rested in prepared beds. At the appointed hour, with the company settled near the lake, Dioneo asks Emilia to tell the first story. This change of setting is neither random scenery nor a report from the plague outside. It shows how carefully the company structures pleasure. The servants have made the comfortable landscape usable; their labor is part of the fiction even when the well-dressed storytellers occupy the center.

The subject Dioneo has chosen is the tricks wives play on their husbands, whether from desire, fear or the need to escape scrutiny. Its source is worth remembering. During the sixth day, the servant Licisca quarreled with Tindaro about the sexual knowledge of women entering marriage. The queen tried to silence her, but Dioneo publicly sided with Licisca and adopted her remarks as the occasion for the next day's theme. A servant who was not allowed to give a formal tale has nevertheless altered the formal program of the company.

A day of tricks may sound like light entertainment, and much of it is. There will be a lover at a door explained away as a ghost, another hidden in a barrel, a husband fooled by a false drowning, and a man made to believe the evidence of his eyes is an enchantment. But the stories are not interchangeable. Some expose the absurdity of jealous surveillance. Some involve women maneuvering in marriages that give them little protection or liberty. One wife pays a maid to receive the beating that would otherwise fall on her. Another makes her lover submit to a calculated ordeal to prove his devotion. The distinction between ingenuity and injury is part of the day's subject.

The narrator of each tale will shape what an audience finds funny. Emilia presents a clever exorcism; Fiammetta gives an argument against jealous husbands that becomes almost a little legal defense; Panfilo's pear-tree trick makes perception itself unreliable. And the day's last speaker, Dioneo, uses his privilege to move beyond ordinary domestic deception into a tale about a message from the dead.

The valley seems at first to be a world without consequences. What happens in the stories reminds us that this safety belongs to the listeners, not necessarily to the women, men and servants they describe. Dioneo's court can laugh because it is temporarily sheltered. Boccaccio lets us enjoy the mechanics of trickery, but also invites a more exact question: who has the power to make others believe a story, and who bears the risk when that story succeeds?

## The Decameron — Day Seven, Tale 1: Gianni and the Bogey

Gianni Lotteringhi is a successful wool-worker in Florence and a man easily impressed by religious ceremony. His gifts to friars have gained him prayers, chants and positions in a confraternity of singers. He values these distinctions, but understanding another person's intentions is not his great strength. His wife, Monna Tessa, knows this. She has a lover, Federigo, and a country house to which Gianni comes only now and then. The arrangement offers both the opportunity and the danger around which Emilia builds her tale.

Tessa and Federigo devise a signal. An ass's skull placed on a post in a vineyard is turned one way when Federigo may come, another when Gianni is in the house. It is a humble sort of code, adapted to the path the lover must pass. During one visit Tessa expects Federigo to arrive for supper. She has two capons cooked, with eggs and wine. Instead, Gianni returns unexpectedly. Tessa eats a less splendid meal with her husband and has the better supper placed in the garden. But she forgets to tell Federigo about the change of plans.

In the night, a knocking wakes Gianni. Federigo stands outside, expecting the signal to have been correct. Tessa cannot open the door, and she cannot explain the knocking without exposing her arrangement. So she claims that the disturbance comes from a bogey or phantom that has been frightening her. Gianni is alarmed. He has accumulated prayers for every occasion; surely one of them will help. Tessa offers a prayer of her own.

The words do two jobs at once. To Gianni they sound like a solemn exorcism, invoking the sort of religious powers he trusts. To Federigo they are intelligible instructions: the garden contains his dinner, and he should take it and leave. The knocking ceases because the man at the door follows the message, not because a supernatural visitor has been defeated. Gianni receives the proof he wanted, while the audience understands the entirely practical cause of the silence.

Emilia introduces her story as a useful lesson in banishing terrors. The joke depends less on a wife's general cleverness than on her precision about two hearers. She speaks only once, and each man hears a different story. Neither listener needs to be unintelligent in every respect; it is enough that Gianni is afraid and convinced the religious language will work, while Federigo knows the secret context the husband lacks.

There is also a small domestic economy beneath the trick. Tessa's maid prepares and moves the food. The capons and wine, paid for from the household's resources, pass to the guest Gianni does not know he is hosting. The night ends without scandal or punishment. Yet no ghost has been expelled, and the marriage has not been repaired. Emilia's version of victory is a successful management of appearances. The true mechanism remains invisible to the person most relieved by its result.

## The Decameron — Day Seven, Tale 2: Peronella and the Barrel

Peronella lives in Naples with a husband who leaves early each morning to earn a living. Their household is modest: a single useful room, small goods to sell, little time for privacy. When he has gone, she sometimes receives a man named Giannello. One morning, before her husband is due home, the two are together. Then the door sounds. Her husband has returned. Peronella thrusts Giannello into an empty barrel, the great household vessel that ordinarily takes up space rather than concealing danger.

The husband arrives not empty-handed but excited. He has met a buyer for that very barrel and expects to receive five gold florins for it. He thinks this is a fortunate business opportunity. Peronella, with no time to devise a second hiding place, reverses the situation. Five florins? She has already sold the barrel for seven. The buyer, she says, is inside it at this moment, examining it before taking it away. What appeared to her husband as evidence of her infidelity suddenly becomes evidence of her financial cleverness.

Giannello hears his cue. He comes out, plays the demanding customer and complains that the inside is dirty. He will pay the higher price only if the vessel is cleaned. The husband, now afraid of losing a better bargain than the one he brought home, climbs into the barrel to scrub its interior. Peronella bends over the opening and gives brisk instructions about where to rub and how thoroughly to wash. At the same time Giannello takes sexual advantage of the concealment created by the husband's labor. The narrator makes the physical arrangement unmistakable, even as the dialogue presents a brisk scene of commercial negotiation.

At last the cleaning is finished. Giannello pays, takes the barrel and departs. Peronella's husband believes he has helped her complete a successful sale. He has been made to do the work that allows his wife's lover to escape, and he congratulates the household on its fortunate transaction. Money changes hands, but in the accounting of the tale the more important transaction is control of the scene.

Filostrato tells the story quickly. There is no supernatural claim and no elaborate invented biography. Peronella merely makes her first statement fit the visible evidence so well that everyone must act as though her account is true. The husband sees a stranger emerging from a barrel; the narrator has supplied a reason for that stranger to be there. Once the man accepts the role of vendor's assistant, every new action reinforces the false story.

The comedy has a class dimension. These are working people whose small property and earnings matter. The barrel is not a decorative prop. Selling it can noticeably improve the household's means, and that possibility makes the husband cooperate. The wife's command of the situation is brilliant, but the erotic joke depends on turning the husband's trusting labor into his humiliation. Boccaccio asks us to watch a household object become a stage, where the same gestures can mean honest work to one person and deception to another.

## The Decameron — Day Seven, Tale 3: Fra Rinaldo and the Child

Fra Rinaldo has long known a married woman in Siena. Their acquaintance changes when he becomes spiritual sponsor to her child. That relationship, which should bring the friar into the family's life under a special obligation of trust, also gives him a pretext for frequent visits. He begins to press his desires on the mother. She reminds him that his position as the child's godfather creates a close spiritual relationship, and that sexual relations across that bond are forbidden. Rinaldo answers with the shameless invention of a convenient distinction: a friar's own spiritual standing, he implies, should remove the obstacle. His argument is not independent theology. It is a suitor's self-serving attempt to undo a refusal.

Eventually the woman agrees to see him privately, and the two establish their affair. One afternoon he visits with a young companion, who helps keep the household occupied while he goes to the mother's room. The husband unexpectedly returns. Before he can understand what has been happening, the household must produce an explanation for a friar being found so close to his wife.

The wife begins the performance with an urgent claim: their child had been dangerously ill. Fra Rinaldo has saved him through prayers or a remedy for worms. The arrival of the husband is transformed from a threat of discovery into an occasion for gratitude. Rinaldo understands the fiction and reinforces it. His role as godfather, which he had used to gain access to the household, becomes the respectable reason for his presence. The mother and friar speak as though a narrowly avoided tragedy has just made them all indebted to him.

The husband is relieved, thanks the friar and treats the supposed rescue as a miracle. The episode is sometimes told with the husband's desire to commemorate the event by an image of the saint or the healer's intervention. What matters is the direction of belief: he does not simply fail to see evidence; he is invited to interpret a scene of betrayal as evidence of care for his child. A frightening alternative has been supplied, and the husband's love for the boy makes that alternative persuasive.

Elissa's comedy has two targets. The first is a sanctimonious friar who can speak the language of duty while exploiting intimacy and trust. The second is a household order in which the husband takes religious authority as a guarantee of truth. Rinaldo is trusted because he is a friar and a godfather, yet these positions are precisely what allow him to arrange a private encounter. The inconsistency is not accidental to the plot; it is its engine.

The spiritual kinship question also returns at the end of the day, when Dioneo's story about the dead raises it again in a different register. Boccaccio sets the two arguments beside one another without requiring the listener to accept either invented doctrine. The story is not a factual record of a miraculous cure. It is a tale in which the power to describe a child's illness protects adults from being answerable for what they have done.

## The Decameron — Day Seven, Tale 4: Tofano and the Well

Tofano of Arezzo is deeply jealous of his wife, Monna Ghita. Whatever affection may once have existed between them has become a struggle over freedom and suspicion. The more closely he watches her, the more determined she becomes to escape his control. He drinks heavily. One of Ghita's ways of arranging an evening with her lover is to encourage that habit until Tofano sleeps. The marriage has made deception a routine rather than an exception.

One night Tofano decides to investigate. He pretends to be drunk, then waits until Ghita has gone out and locks the house against her return. When she comes back, he stands behind the secured door and refuses to let her in. This is the moment in which he believes he has finally won. He has physical command of the threshold and intends to turn her absence into public disgrace. Ghita begs him to open. He will not.

She then changes the stakes. If he persists, she says, she will throw herself down the well. The well is nearby and dark; a splash would be heard from the house. Ghita takes a heavy stone, throws it into the water and hides. Tofano hears the crash and believes she has carried out her threat. His suspicion gives way to panic. He leaves the house and rushes toward the well to help her.

Ghita has been waiting for exactly this. She slips back into the house, bolts the door and makes herself the person who controls admission. Tofano is left outside, as she was. He demands that she let him in; she loudly accuses him of drinking and behaving disgracefully. Neighbors are now likely to hear her version rather than his. The same public reputation he meant to use as a weapon against her can be turned against him. He is no longer the husband gathering witnesses to an adulterous wife's conduct, but a drunken man causing a scene at the doorstep.

The reversal ends in a negotiated accommodation. Ghita retains far more freedom than Tofano intended to give her, and the husband discovers that the power to lock a door does not necessarily mean the power to control the story told about it. Lauretta treats the wife's resourcefulness as the satisfying achievement. The trick works because it creates a sudden demand on Tofano's genuine concern: he may be jealous and coercive, but he does not want her dead.

That is a disturbing basis for comedy. A false suggestion of suicide makes Tofano vulnerable to fear; the threat of public shame makes Ghita vulnerable to him. Neither spouse can speak honestly without first considering how the other might use the disclosure. The well is an ingenious stage prop, but it is also a measure of the distance between them. Their final agreement ends the immediate conflict, not necessarily the distrust on which it grew.

## The Decameron — Day Seven, Tale 5: The Jealous Husband as Confessor

A married woman in Rimini lives under close supervision by a husband who believes constant vigilance is the proper answer to the possibility of betrayal. He watches her meetings, her movements, even her approach to confession. The wife wants to see a young neighbor, Filippo, but ordinary opportunities have been sealed off. Fiammetta begins her tale with a defense of the pleasures and breathing room that a woman ought to enjoy. A husband who makes every ordinary liberty suspect, she suggests, turns marriage into a kind of imprisonment.

The wife asks to visit a church and confess. Her husband sees a new possibility for intelligence. If he can learn what she tells the priest, he may discover the secret he has been searching for. Disguising himself as a priest, he takes the place where her confession will be heard. But his wife recognizes him. What he regards as a clever hidden inquiry becomes a stage on which she can direct his next moves.

She confesses to loving a priest who visits her at night. The jealous husband believes he has caught the essential truth. He hears religious language combined with an apparent admission of adultery and takes the story literally. He does not ask why his disguised presence should itself be beyond recognition. Instead he determines to catch the nocturnal visitor. At night he guards the front door, waiting for the priest he thinks will arrive by the ordinary route.

Meanwhile Filippo comes by the roof. The lover enters and the wife entertains him while her husband stands below, faithfully watching a place no one needs to pass. For several nights the performance succeeds. When the husband finally reveals that he had been the confessor, the wife is ready with another interpretation. The priest she spoke of was, she says, the man before her: her own husband, dressed as a priest. The statement can be defended by the literal circumstances of the confession. Its evident intention was to mislead.

Fiammetta's narrator has prepared us to see the husband as deserving ridicule for surveillance. Yet the mechanism is more exact than a simple reversal of foolishness. His disguise invites the wife's recognition, and she uses the truth about his own action to protect a false impression about hers. He supplies the position from which he can be duped. Her confession works because confession is expected to yield hidden knowledge, and because he trusts the role he is impersonating more than the person he is questioning.

The tale exposes a contradiction between religious procedure and domestic spying. In the confessional, words are supposed to be spoken for conscience and absolution, not gathered as evidence by a masquerading spouse. The woman's successful escape from scrutiny does not prove that concealment is always liberating, nor that every jealous fear is groundless. It shows how an effort to control another person's account of herself can make a husband surrender judgment over his own conduct. When he discovers the trick, the forms of truth have already been used against him.

## The Decameron — Day Seven, Tale 6: Isabella and the Two Visitors

At her country house Madonna Isabella expects a visit from Leonetto, the young man she loves. She is married, and her privacy is precarious, but she has arranged a space in which they can meet. Another man, Messer Lambertuccio, also pursues her. He is powerful enough that his attention is hard to refuse safely. Isabella finds herself with one man whom she welcomes and another whom she fears, just when her husband is expected to remain away.

The evening collapses into a chain of arrivals. Leonetto comes first and is received. Lambertuccio then appears, pressing his claim. Isabella cannot allow the men to encounter one another openly, so Leonetto hides. Before Lambertuccio can leave, she learns that her husband is riding home. She has only moments to turn the presence of two concealed men into a credible scene.

Her solution is theatrical and exact. She orders Lambertuccio to draw his sword and rush down from the house, looking furious and threatening. The returning husband sees a violent man departing as though in pursuit of an enemy. Isabella then summons Leonetto from hiding and introduces him as the terrified victim of that pursuit: a stranger who has taken refuge in her rooms because Lambertuccio means to kill him. The husband's task now seems perfectly clear. He must offer hospitality and escort an endangered guest to safety.

The more generously he performs that duty, the more securely Isabella's invention works. He has no reason to ask why Leonetto was concealed in the house, because she has already supplied the answer in the form of an urgent rescue. The two lovers gain what they most need: time, an explanation and a respectable exit. Lambertuccio, whose earlier threats helped create the difficulty, is made to lend his temper to the fiction that releases them.

Pampinea's story shows a woman distributing roles to men who have greater obvious power than she does. Lambertuccio provides the visible danger; Leonetto provides the grateful fugitive; the husband provides the moral authority of the host. Isabella takes no public credit, but her rapid account gives each actor a part to perform. The husband becomes an unwitting participant in his wife's concealment, not by being idle but by behaving honorably within the invented circumstances.

The tale belongs to the day of wives' tricks, yet the two visitors are not interchangeable suitors. Leonetto is the lover she chooses. Lambertuccio's importunity creates pressure and risk. Treating all three men as equally amusing rivals would erase the reason Isabella needs more than a witty line. Her ingenuity is a form of navigation through unequal claims on her household and body. The ending is comic because no duel occurs and no one is exposed, but the underlying world remains one in which a woman's safety can depend on knowing exactly what story men will accept.

## The Decameron — Day Seven, Tale 7: Lodovico, Beatrice, and Egano

A young Florentine named Lodovico hears travelers praise the beauty of Madonna Beatrice of Bologna. Without ever meeting her, he becomes determined to see her. He tells his wealthy father that he intends a pilgrimage, leaves his position at the French court and travels instead to Bologna. There he takes the name Anichino and enters the service of Beatrice's husband, Egano, becoming an exceptionally useful retainer. Egano trusts him with household matters. The young man has earned that trust through genuine competence in the service of a concealed purpose.

One day Egano is out hawking and Beatrice plays chess with Anichino. He lets her win. When the other attendants depart, he sighs and finally tells her who he is, why he has come and how completely he loves her. Beatrice responds with affection, and the two arrange a midnight meeting. They appear to have found the private opening they wanted, until Egano returns home and goes to bed beside his wife.

Anichino enters the dark bedroom at the appointed hour and touches Beatrice to wake her. She holds him where he is and wakes her husband. To the terrified lover, this sounds like exposure. Instead, Beatrice tells Egano that his trusted servant has insulted her by proposing an illicit meeting in the garden. She says she pretended to accept, and that Egano can test the man's treachery by disguising himself as his wife and waiting for Anichino in the garden.

Egano accepts. He puts on his wife's clothes and veil and goes down to the appointed place. With the husband away, Beatrice is free to enjoy her meeting with Anichino. Later she sends Anichino to the garden with precise instructions. He finds the disguised Egano, pretends to take him for Beatrice, and angrily strikes him while accusing the supposed wife of betraying her husband. Egano bears the blows because they appear to prove the servant's loyalty. His discomfort becomes the evidence he was sent to gather.

Afterward he returns upstairs, delighted. Beatrice learns from him the details of the performance she has already arranged. Anichino and Beatrice remain free to pursue their love. Filomena's narrator invites laughter at a rich man whose greatest confidence is placed in someone he has allowed close to his own household. The trick is not just disguise. It is the transfer of moral authority: every blow Anichino gives becomes a demonstration of his fidelity to Egano rather than an assault on him.

The opening of the story also deserves notice. Lodovico's pursuit begins with a report of a woman as though she were a coveted marvel. A pilgrimage becomes the cover for a private desire, and honest service becomes an instrument of betrayal. Anichino can display extraordinary self-control and practical intelligence while still exploiting a relationship of trust. The comedy depends on Egano reading actions without knowing their intentions. The listener, given the concealed story first, can see how easily a demonstration of virtue may be arranged by the person most interested in faking it.

## The Decameron — Day Seven, Tale 8: Sismonda and the Thread

Arriguccio Berlinghieri is jealous of his wife, Monna Sismonda. She has a lover, Ruberto, and uses a small mechanical signal to arrange their nighttime meetings. A thread is tied to her toe and runs toward the window; when Ruberto pulls it, she knows he has arrived without a spoken warning. The thread is almost nothing, a length of fiber across a dark room. To Arriguccio, when he discovers it, the thread becomes the proof he has been searching for.

One night he feels the signal and follows its route. He believes he can catch Ruberto and settle the matter by force. While he pursues the man, Sismonda calls a maid and pays her to take her place in the bed. The substitute must remain silent and withstand whatever comes. Arriguccio returns furious, thinking his wife is still in the bed, and beats the woman savagely. He cuts her hair as another mark of disgrace. The servant suffers violence meant for someone else; the money she receives does not make the pain disappear.

Arriguccio goes to Sismonda's family to accuse her, certain that he will have visible proof. But Sismonda has returned to the bed and arranged her appearance. She shows neither wounds nor shorn hair. Her brothers and mother confront the furious husband's account with the unmarked wife standing before them. They are persuaded that he has invented the accusation or become dangerously irrational. Threats and reproaches are directed at Arriguccio. The household he had meant to shame seems publicly cleared.

Neifile's tale is structured as a contest between one kind of evidence and another. The thread tells Arriguccio that someone has used the signal, but not what he will subsequently be able to prove. The beating and haircut should create marks on the body of his wife. Sismonda changes the body that receives those marks. When her relatives arrive, the visible record contradicts his report, and his earlier violence makes him less credible rather than more.

The story is not safely described as a harmless practical joke. A woman with less power, the maid, absorbs the consequences of another woman's plan. A husband's anger produces physical injury. Sismonda's escape from surveillance is real within the fiction, but its cost is displaced onto someone who has limited freedom to refuse. The comic ending asks the audience to enjoy a jealous husband's humiliation while refusing to reckon fully with the injured servant.

This is one reason to read the seventh day's tales individually. Tessa uses a prayer as a code; Peronella uses a barrel; Sismonda uses a second woman's body. Each is ingenious in the technical sense of making one explanation occupy the place of another. But ingenuity is not a complete moral description. The test is also who controls the substitute evidence, and who is required to carry its weight.

## The Decameron — Day Seven, Tale 9: Lydia, Pyrrhus, and the Pear Tree

In a household governed by Nicostratus, a woman named Lydia becomes enamored of a young retainer, Pyrrhus. She wants an affair; he doubts whether she will risk anything to make it possible. Pyrrhus sets three tests, each directed at something Nicostratus values: Lydia must kill his favored hawk, obtain a piece of his beard, and pull a sound tooth from his mouth. These demands are not tokens of harmless devotion. They require injury, destruction and a willingness to turn her husband's trust into an instrument against him.

Lydia carries out the tests with a confidence that astonishes Pyrrhus. She kills the hawk, claiming a justification for the act. She gets the beard hair and the tooth by making Nicostratus's ordinary concerns work against him. When the third condition is fulfilled, Pyrrhus agrees to meet her. Lydia promises an even greater trick: they will have their encounter while the husband watches, and he will not believe what he sees.

The setting is a garden with a pear tree. First Pyrrhus climbs into the branches and loudly reports the impossible sight of Lydia and Nicostratus behaving intimately below. The couple is not doing what he describes. Nicostratus sees that the report is false. Pyrrhus concludes that the tree must possess a strange power: from its branches, it seems to make the observer see sexual acts where none occur. He urges the husband to test the phenomenon.

Nicostratus climbs the tree. This time Lydia and Pyrrhus come together below him in fact. The husband sees them and cries out. They answer with the explanation he has just watched being established. Of course he sees an impossible scene from the branches; that is what the tree does. He may descend and discover nothing, just as Pyrrhus did. Nicostratus is forced to choose between his eyes and the theory he has accepted to explain another man's false report. The lovers have made truth look like a repetition of an earlier illusion.

The deception succeeds. The pear tree can be cut down to end the disturbing phenomenon, but removing the tree does not cure the husband's ignorance. Panfilo's tale is memorable because it manufactures a rule for how evidence ought to behave, then places a real event inside the false rule. The trick does not hide the act. It gives the observer a reason to discount his own correct perception.

Before the famous pear-tree scene, however, there are the hawk, beard and tooth. Those details show that Lydia's loyalty to Pyrrhus is measured by her ability to harm someone else. Desire has become competitive, with one person's body and property serving as signs exchanged between two others. The tale belongs to a long family of garden and tree deceptions that later English readers encounter in Chaucer. Within Boccaccio's sequence, its lesson is not that sight always deceives. It is that an agreed theory can teach a person to misread even what he correctly sees.

## The Decameron — Day Seven, Tale 10: Tingoccio Returns from Purgatory

Two men in Siena, Tingoccio Mini and Meuccio di Tura, are close friends with a shared curiosity about what follows death. No one can return from the grave to give an ordinary account, so they make a pact. Whichever dies first will come back, if it is possible, and tell the survivor about the other world. It is the kind of promise that sounds solemn until the practical question arises: what information would these friends most like to know?

Tingoccio becomes intimate with the mother of his godchild. The spiritual relationship matters. A godparent is joined to the child's family by a religious bond, and sexual contact across that connection is treated as an offense in the moral system the men have learned. The same relation was invoked by Fra Rinaldo earlier in the day, when he used his status as godfather to gain access to a married woman. Tingoccio's affair gives the pact a particular point of inquiry.

Eventually Tingoccio dies. One night, when Meuccio is alone, a voice comes to him, and the dead friend appears. Tingoccio says he is in purgatory and describes various punishments. The apparent miracle is narrated without offering independent proof; it is the premise of Dioneo's fiction. Meuccio listens, asks questions and finally reaches the subject that troubles him. Does Tingoccio suffer a special punishment for having slept with the mother of his godchild?

No, Tingoccio answers; in the place where he now finds himself, such relations do not count for the extra punishment Meuccio imagines. Meuccio is at once astonished and regretful. All the opportunities he has refused from fear of this supposed sin now seem, by the report of a ghost, to have been lost without necessity. The moral and metaphysical question of the afterlife has been converted into a calculation about missed sexual occasions.

Dioneo ends the seventh day with a joke that breaks its apparent rules. The protagonists are not a wife tricking her husband, and their most important deception is not enacted in a household room. Instead, a dead man communicates what the living cannot verify. The device returns to religious authority, which appeared in the first tale as a prayer that was actually a coded instruction. At the end, the claim is even more difficult to test. It arrives from beyond death.

The story should not be mistaken for a report of what medieval Christian theology established about spiritual kinship. It is a bawdy fictional argument built around a supernatural messenger. Tingoccio's testimony is authoritative to Meuccio because Meuccio wants it to be. Desire, which prompted the question, also shapes the answer's reception. Dioneo ends with a laugh, but the structure is exact: a visitor from the other world offers the precise exemption that an eager friend would most like to believe.
