# Journey to the West: The Bull King and the stolen face

## Who set the mountain alight?

The pilgrims could not cross the Flaming Mountains. Wukong had fought Princess Iron Fan, had been blown through the sky, had returned with a wind-stilling pill, and had forced her to surrender a fan. The fan was false. Three sweeps had raised the flames instead of quenching them. Xuanzang, Bajie, and Sha sat near the eastern edge of the burning country while the local Earth God offered them a meal and explained what Wukong must do next.

Find the Bull Demon King, the god said. He was the husband who could persuade Princess Iron Fan to surrender the real treasure.

Wukong had another question. Had the Bull King caused the mountain to burn? Was this some monstrous bonfire deliberately kept alight to hold the road hostage?

"No," answered the Earth God. "The fire came from you."

Wukong stared at him. He had defeated fire spirits, burned demons' caves, and set many things in motion during his long career, but he had never visited this place before the pilgrimage. The Earth God told him to remember Heaven five hundred years earlier. When Wukong was captured and imprisoned in Laozi's Eight-Trigrams Furnace, he had endured its flames until he broke out. In his escape he kicked over the furnace. Burning bricks and embers fell from Heaven into the world below and formed the Flaming Mountains. The god himself had once tended that furnace. He had been held responsible for its loss and punished by being sent down to this scorched country.

The pilgrimage was being stopped by the debris of Wukong's rebellion. The same rebellion had earned him his golden eyes and his prison beneath the Five Elements Mountain. It had also left people who had never seen him dependent on annual petitions to a demon's family for the right to grow food.

The Earth God knew where the Bull King lived. The Bull had deserted his wife and gone to live at Cloud-Rubbing Cave on Mount Accumulated Thunder with a fox woman known as Princess Jade-Face. Her father, an immensely wealthy old fox spirit, had died leaving her an estate. She had taken the Bull Demon King as her husband and paid him handsomely to remain. For two years the Bull had been seldom seen at Princess Iron Fan's home. Wukong looked toward the burning mountain, promised to return, and leapt into the sky.

## Princess Jade-Face

Mount Accumulated Thunder rose from valleys of flowering trees and green pines. Wukong searched its paths for Cloud-Rubbing Cave. A beautifully dressed woman appeared among the rocks carrying a sprig of fragrant flowers. He called out respectfully and asked where the cave might be.

The woman recoiled at his monkey face. When he said that he had come from Green Cloud Mountain, sent by Princess Iron Fan to ask the Bull King to return, her surprise changed into anger. The wife had no right to summon him, she said. Had she not paid for his comfort, his clothes, his meals, and his entertainments? Jade-Face had wealth enough to maintain the Bull King's household, and the other woman should be ashamed to disturb it.

Wukong understood who was speaking. He had asked a question of the very woman he sought. He answered her insults with his own. Money could buy a husband a comfortable cave, he said, but not the right to curse a wife who had been abandoned. He drew his iron staff and drove her toward her door. Terrified, she fled into Cloud-Rubbing Cave and summoned her protector.

The Bull Demon King came out wearing armor and carrying an iron staff. He was furious not only because his concubine had been frightened, but because he had heard that the intruder was Sun Wukong. The two had once belonged to a brotherhood of seven demons and mountain kings. Since then Wukong had accepted a new master and fought old acquaintances along the western road.

Wukong tried the old greeting. He bowed and called the Bull his elder brother. He insisted that he had come for a single favor: lend the true plantain fan, let Xuanzang pass the mountain, and take the fan back unharmed. No property would be lost.

The Bull listed his grievances. Wukong had helped Guanyin seize his son. Wukong had confronted his son's uncle at the Water of Release. Now Wukong had forced the Bull's wife to give up a treasure and frightened the woman with whom he lived. What kind of younger brother would abuse every member of the family and then call for brotherly aid?

"Your son was going to steam my master," Wukong answered. "Guanyin spared him and gave him service. He is alive."

"He is not home," said the Bull. "Nor have you made it possible for his mother to see him."

Their quarrel returned to the price of help. The Bull refused. Wukong refused to go away. Iron struck iron before the cave, then among the clouds. For more than a hundred exchanges neither gained the advantage. The Bull was one of the few opponents on the road whose strength matched Wukong's without a ring, a gourd, or a magical cord to do the fighting for him.

A messenger arrived from a friend: the Bull King had been invited to a feast. He ordered Wukong to stop and announced that he had an engagement. The monkey stared as his old brother put away his weapon, changed into fine clothes, mounted a remarkable golden-eyed beast that could travel through water, and departed. He had an enemy to help, or a guest to entertain. He chose the banquet.

## The banquet under the pool

Wukong followed in the shape of a light wind. The Bull passed over the mountains to a deep lake surrounded by broken stones. A sign identified it as the Emerald Waves Pool. The Bull disappeared beneath the water.

Wukong made himself into a broad crab and slipped down through the clear green depths. Underwater stood a gateway and a splendid palace in which the water did not pour through the open doors. Strange fish, turtles, water spirits, and dragon servants were gathered for a feast. The Bull's golden-eyed mount was tethered near the entrance. Inside, the Bull sat among a dragon king, his kin, and his court. Musicians played. Cups were filled and carried from guest to guest. The host laughed, and the visiting monster who had just refused a pilgrim's plea accepted another drink.

The crab crept toward the hall to hear what was being said. A servant noticed the unauthorized guest. The old dragon ordered him seized and demanded to know why an uninvited crab would scuttle through a noble gathering. Wukong answered in a tiny, apologetic voice. He was merely a lake creature, he said, ignorant of court custom. If they would excuse the offense, he would leave at once. The assembled spirits asked mercy for him. The old dragon relented.

As Wukong backed out, he saw the Bull's mount standing unattended. He had no intention of waiting until the feast ended. Even then the Bull might refuse the loan. But the mount offered a better plan. Wukong untied it, leapt into the saddle, rode up through the water, and changed his form into that of the Bull Demon King.

He came to Plantain Cave as the long-absent husband. The maids opened the doors without question. Their mistress hurried to greet him, grateful and reproachful in equal measure. He had abandoned her for Jade-Face, she said. What did he want after so long away?

Wukong spoke in the Bull's voice. He was concerned about that miserable monkey Sun Wukong, he declared. Had the monkey been at the cave? Had she lent him the fan? The Rakshasi described the terrible encounter: the swords, the first great blast of wind, the second visit, the insect in her tea, and the pain inside her belly. At the end she had given him a fan.

The false Bull feigned outrage. How could she have handed over the family's great treasure to their son's enemy?

"Do not worry," she said, smiling through her exhaustion. "I gave him a false one."

For a moment Wukong nearly betrayed his delight. He turned the conversation toward the genuine fan and asked whether it was safely hidden. His supposed wife told him she had kept it close. Then she ordered food and wine to celebrate the reunion. She was relieved to have him home; he had come to rob her.

Wukong could not leave at once without arousing suspicion. He sat at the table, accepted a cup, and kept to fruit rather than the meat set before him. The princess spoke of the long separation and of the other woman. She sought tenderness from a husband who had been away for years. Wukong answered in borrowed phrases, maintaining a disguise that became more awkward the longer the evening continued. What seemed to the Rakshasi a reconciliation was a trap devised by her son's enemy.

At last Wukong asked how the true fan was stored. Better show it to him, he suggested, so he could check that the monkey had not stolen it after all. The Rakshasi took a tiny object from her mouth, little bigger than a leaf, and offered it to him. This was the fan.

Wukong turned it over, uncertain. It hardly looked large enough to cool a face, let alone eight hundred li of mountain. The princess laughed at him. Had he grown so forgetful with Jade-Face that he no longer knew his own household treasure? Take hold of the seventh red thread on its handle, she told him; speak the proper formula, and the fan would grow to a full twelve feet.

Wukong memorized the instruction. The Rakshasi leaned toward the face she believed was her husband's. The monkey could bear the deception no longer. He put the tiny fan in his mouth, wiped away the Bull's features, and stood before her in his own form. He jeered that she had been deceived by an old enemy. She stumbled back in humiliation and fury as he sprang through the door.

He reached a high hill, repeated the words, and watched the leaf unfold into the splendid true plantain fan. It had woven red cords and a radiance unlike the counterfeit. He had succeeded in taking it. Yet he had neglected to ask how it could be made small again. He had to sling the enormous object across his shoulder and carry it openly toward his master.

## A host finds his mount missing

At the pool beneath the broken stones the feast came to an end. The Bull Demon King reached the gateway and found his golden-eyed mount gone. No servant admitted touching it. Then the dragon's household remembered the strange crab who had wandered into the banquet hall. The Bull immediately understood. The crab had been Wukong, and the monkey had stolen the mount to impersonate him at Plantain Cave.

He mounted the clouds and flew home. His wife was in distress. When he appeared in his own form, she described how the apparent husband had coaxed the real fan out of her mouth and turned into Wukong before fleeing. Her grief over their son had been used against her twice, and now her private longing for her estranged husband had been turned into another opening.

The Bull promised revenge. He did not have his iron staff with him, so he took her two swords and set out after the monkey. He knew that Wukong, delighted by the success of his disguise, would be going back toward the Flaming Mountains. A man who had just stolen a face might be vulnerable to the very same trick.

Above the burning country, the old sworn brothers moved toward one another once more: one with the stolen fan, one with a plan to steal it back.
