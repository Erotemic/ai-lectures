# Journey to the West: The blue ox and the diamond snare

## What a recovered weapon could not settle

On the mountain above Golden Cave, the heavenly officers were waiting. Fire had been tried against the demon and had failed. A flood summoned from the Yellow River had poured across the countryside instead of drowning him. Nezha had lost six weapons; Wukong had lost his own iron staff. And the demon's shining white ring, small enough to wear upon an arm, had taken every weapon brought against it.

Wukong had done one useful thing. He had crept into the cave in a tiny shape, discovered where the stolen objects were stored, and recovered the golden-hooped staff. He emerged delighted, carrying it as though the problem had been solved. But Heavenly King Li and the other officers had their own questions. Where were their swords and spears? Where were the fire gods' instruments? What would they tell Heaven when they came back empty-handed?

"You have your treasure," they said in effect. "What about ours?"

"I have my staff," said Wukong, "and with it I can beat that animal until he lets go of the rest."

Below them the mountain came alive with drums. The One-Horned King was marching out with his followers. Discovering the loss of the staff had not made him hide; it had made him furious. Wukong answered with an eager leap down the slope. The two met with the crack and clang of staff against spear, neither willing to yield. Their fighting went on until the light faded. The monster offered to postpone the contest until morning, but Wukong said that evening was no excuse for a creature who had imprisoned his master. The king withdrew behind the sealed entrance.

The gods congratulated Wukong on his strength. He accepted the praise without pretending it settled the question. A spear could be met by a staff. The ring was different. Wukong wanted to steal it while its wearer slept.

Nezha wondered why he would go into danger at night. Wukong gave him the answer of an experienced thief. The darkness was precisely what made it worth attempting. Someone who intended to slip through a door, lift a treasure, and disappear did not wait for the sun.

## A cricket, a flea, and a bracelet

He shrank into a cricket and slipped through the crack at the entrance. From the corner of a wall he watched the demon's servants eat, tidy their tables, and prepare their bedding. The cave that by day had been a fortress looked at night like a crowded household. Some guards kept watch; others fell asleep; the king gave warnings against another intrusion by the monkey.

Wukong crept into the sleeping chamber. Attendants were helping their master remove his clothing. There, on the demon's left arm, lay the white circle. It was not displayed on a pedestal or left carelessly among his possessions. He pushed it up his arm until it sat tight, then lay down with it still in place.

Wukong became a flea. He crawled beneath the bedding, found the arm, and bit. The demon jerked awake and abused the servants for failing to shake out his bed. He pushed the ring higher. Wukong bit again. The demon rolled and cursed, but did not take the ring off. Its value was too great, and his wariness remained with him even in sleep.

A third attempt would merely tell the king that something more than insects had entered his room. Wukong withdrew. He had found the treasure but could not take it. He turned instead to the armory, where he had earlier heard the neighing of the celestial fire horses.

The door was locked. Locks, to the Great Sage, were not a serious impediment. A whispered charm opened it. Inside he found Nezha's weapons, the fire department's apparatus, and the little hairs that had once multiplied into his monkey army. He blew upon the hairs and made a crowd of small monkeys. One took a blade, another a spear; the rest seized what they could carry. Wukong released the fire horses and set the stored instruments working. Flames raced through the inner rooms.

The cave erupted with shouts. Servants woke and ran into the smoke. The king's household, which had been ready to roast its prisoners in the morning, found itself threatened by the fire. Many lesser demons died in the blaze. Wukong escaped with his company of transformed hairs and their armfuls of recovered spoils. Before dawn he had restored the heavenly officers' property.

For the second time the gods believed the crisis had turned. And for the second time Wukong pressed his luck.

## The circle is still the circle

The demon came out again. Wukong challenged him and, before striking, recited a long account of his own career. He told of the mountain where he had been born, of the teacher who had shown him transformations, of the Dragon Kings, the underworld, and the heavenly battles; of the furnace in which Laozi had tried to reduce him and the Buddha's palm from which he had failed to escape. He was not merely boasting about victories. He was explaining why the king should not think him a common monkey who could be frightened away from a captive monk.

The demon had a sharper interpretation. A creature who had raided Heaven, stolen its medicines and fruit, and fought its armies was, to him, a famous thief. If Wukong could describe himself as a holy protector now, the king could just as easily remind him of the deeds that had brought him under a mountain for five hundred years.

Their weapons clashed. Nezha and the fire officers joined in with the newly recovered equipment. The heavens seemed to have learned nothing from the earlier fight. As soon as so many bright instruments were in motion, the One-Horned King raised the little white circle.

The staff left Wukong's hand again. Nezha's weapons vanished again. So did the devices of the fire officers and other celestial arms. There was no need for the king to beat the attackers one at a time. He simply collected them.

When the demon returned to his cave, he ordered his servants to repair the burnt rooms and prepare to kill the prisoners as part of the rebuilding celebration. Xuanzang's danger was no longer an abstract possibility. Wukong had spent his courage and his ingenuity, and the monster still dictated the terms.

The officers stood in the aftermath, distressed and blaming one another. Wukong thought of one authority he had not yet consulted. The heavenly registers had found no missing official. But the Buddha could look across the world and perhaps identify a creature whose ordinary place was not recorded among the officers of Heaven.

He turned west toward Vulture Peak.

## A hint that would not be spoken plainly

At the Buddha's seat, Wukong gave a precise account. The demon had captured Xuanzang, Bajie, and Sha Wujing. The white ring had taken his staff and celestial weapons. Wukong had won some of them back and lost them again. If the monster was connected with some famous being, he needed to know who owned it.

The Buddha looked out over the world and recognized the enemy. Then he refused to give the name. If Wukong repeated the disclosure, he explained, the owner might make trouble even for Vulture Peak. Instead he offered help: eighteen arhats would take eighteen measures of golden cinnabar sand to bury the demon's feet and hold him still.

Wukong was pleased to have an answer that did not require another hopeless exchange of swords. But as he traveled with the monks, he noticed that there were only sixteen of them in sight. He accused the absent pair of trying to escape the assignment. The Dragon-Subduing and Tiger-Subduing Arhats emerged and said they had stayed behind to receive instructions. Wukong took the explanation for an excuse, and the party continued with the accusation still hanging in the air.

At Golden Cave he lured the king away from the entrance, exchanging blows without trying to win outright. The arhats scattered their strange sand. It fell like a cloud and piled around the demon's legs. He raised one foot, only to find the other sinking deeper. He leaped to the surface, and still the sand climbed. For a moment it appeared that the monster was trapped not by a weapon but by a landscape that refused to let him walk.

Then he threw up the ring.

The sand vanished. Eighteen monks stood with empty hands. Wukong had seen the same disaster in so many forms that it hardly needed explaining. He turned upon the two arhats who had delayed their departure. They now revealed the Buddha's second instruction. If the sand was taken, Wukong was to go to Laozi's palace beyond the thirty-three heavens. There he would find the real clue.

"Then why," Wukong demanded, "did nobody tell me before all this?"

The answer was not satisfying. The great institutions of the universe had sent him from one authority to another while his master remained in a demon's cave. He had obtained a hint, at the cost of another set of treasures.

## The empty stall

Laozi received him with the weary familiarity of someone who had met Wukong at the worst moments of his earlier career. Why had he come to the Tushita Palace instead of protecting his master? Wukong answered by moving from polite inquiry to accusation. There was a trail that might lead straight into Laozi's household, and he meant to follow it.

He wandered through the halls until he came to the cattle stall. The keeper was asleep, and the blue ox was gone.

"Your ox has run away," Wukong announced.

The keeper woke in panic. He had found a pill in the alchemical chamber and swallowed it. The medicine had put him to sleep for seven days. During that long sleep the blue ox had vanished. Laozi examined his treasures. Almost everything remained, except the diamond snare, a hard shining circlet he had fashioned long ago. It was the same ring that had defeated Wukong, Nezha, the fire officials, and eighteen arhats.

Laozi explained its remarkable property. Weapons and many kinds of magical assault could not withstand it. He added that the blue ox would have been harder to retrieve had it also taken his palm-leaf fan. The fan remained in its proper place. At last the apparently invincible demon had a weakness: its rightful master could command both animal and treasure.

Wukong could not resist observing that a beast belonging to such an exalted person had injured so many others. He was not completely consoled by the explanation that a sleepy keeper had accidentally let it slip away. But he needed the prisoners released more than he needed to win an argument about supervision. He brought Laozi back to the mountain.

The sage took his fan and waited upon the heights while Wukong went to the cave entrance once more. This time the monkey was not pretending he could win a contest of strength. He dashed forward, struck the monster on the face, and ran. The king, outraged, pursued. Then a voice called from above: the ox had stayed away long enough, and it must return home.

The One-Horned King looked up. It knew the voice. Laozi spoke a charm and used the fan. The diamond snare flew from the demon's arm into its owner's hand. Another movement of the fan left the creature without its human shape. A blue ox stood where a feared monster had fought. Laozi passed the ring through its nose, tied it with his sash, mounted, and led it back toward the heavenly palace.

There was no prolonged duel at the end. The creature that had resisted armies, fire, water, and magical sand yielded when its own household authority appeared.

## Dinner beside the road

Wukong and the divine allies entered Golden Cave to reclaim their belongings. They killed the surviving lesser monsters, recovered the weapons, and freed Xuanzang, Bajie, and Sha Wujing. The angels and officers returned to their several stations; the arhats went west. The pilgrims collected their horse and baggage and found the road again.

Xuanzang had suffered because he and the others had walked out of a protective circle drawn by Wukong. Now he thanked the monkey for the immense rescue and looked with new respect at the old warning. Wukong had reason to be angry, and sometimes he enjoyed that reason far too much. But for this moment his master's gratitude was enough.

As the four set off, someone called to them from the roadside, offering a vegetarian meal. It was an unexpected sound after so many locked doors, deceptive dwellings, and hungry monsters. Xuanzang hesitated. The meal would prove to have been prepared by the mountain and earth spirits, grateful that Wukong had completed his labor. For a moment, however, the travelers could not know whether a summons to dinner was kindness or another snare.

They had learned that a fine house could hide a cave and a white ring could defeat the armies of Heaven. On the road west, even the simple offer of food required a second look.
