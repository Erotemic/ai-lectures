# Journey to the West: The return of Sun Wukong

## The confession on Flower-Fruit Mountain

Bajie was dragged back to the Monkey King through a crowd of shouting monkeys. They tugged his ears, hauled his robe, and jostled him until he scarcely knew which direction was forward. His clothes were torn. The palace of the restored mountain, which he had admired only a short while earlier, now looked like a place in which he might receive an especially painful beating.

Wukong sat waiting on a rock.

Why, he demanded, did Bajie curse him while walking away? Had he not received a polite welcome, a tour of the mountain, and fruit for breakfast? Did he think the Monkey King could not hear what happened on his own road?

Bajie fell to his knees and denied having said anything improper. Perhaps the monkeys had misunderstood him. Perhaps an echo in the valley had changed his words.

Wukong was not persuaded. He claimed he could lift one ear and hear the conversations of Heaven, lower the other and know what the judges of the underworld were discussing. Bajie's complaints had scarcely been private. He called for large sticks and began specifying a punishment so extensive that Bajie decided denial was no longer a useful policy.

He begged Wukong to consider their master. Wukong asked what good that argument was: it was the master who had dismissed him. Bajie invoked Guanyin, the bodhisattva who had put both of them on the road. At that name Wukong's anger eased a little.

Then tell the truth, he said. What disaster had struck Xuanzang? Why had Bajie come pretending that the monk merely missed him?

Bajie tried once more to maintain the story. Wukong cut him short. Though his body had returned to Water-Curtain Cave, he said, his heart had never ceased following the pilgrim west. He knew very well how the road went. A monk like Xuanzang could not travel long without danger. If Bajie kept lying, Wukong would use the staff rather than the sticks.

Bajie looked at the path beyond the cave, calculating whether he could run. Wukong told him that even three days' head start would not save him.

Finally the story poured out.

Bajie told of the pine forest, the forgotten begging bowl, the golden tower that proved to be a demon's cave, the princess who had been captive for thirteen years and had rescued Xuanzang by sending a letter home. He told of Baoxiang's king, the public promise to retrieve the princess, the fight with the Yellow-Robed Lord, and Sha's capture. He confessed that he had retreated and hidden. He told of the monster's appearance at court, Xuanzang turned into a tiger and chained in a cage, and the white dragon horse's lonely attempt to rescue him. The dragon was wounded, and it was the dragon who had insisted they send for the eldest disciple.

Bajie ended with an appeal: the dragon had called Wukong a loyal and compassionate elder brother. A person who had been freed from five hundred years of imprisonment by his teacher could surely help that teacher now.

Wukong listened without interrupting.

When Bajie finished, the Monkey King stood up. He told the younger brother to stop groveling and rise. There was no need to argue any longer.

He went into the cave, put aside the clothing of his restored court, straightened his traveling tunic and tiger-skin waistcloth, and took up the iron staff. The monkeys gathered around him, alarmed that their ruler meant to depart so soon after his return.

He told them to keep planting trees and repairing the mountain. They must preserve what they had rebuilt. He had a duty known throughout Heaven and Earth: he had undertaken to escort the Tang monk to the western scriptures. When that work was finished, he could return to enjoy the mountain with them. For now he must go back.

It was the answer Bajie had hoped for, but there was a revealing moment before they crossed the sea. Wukong asked Bajie to travel slowly for a moment. The monkey dipped down into the seawater and washed himself.

Why waste time bathing? Bajie demanded. Their master was in danger.

Wukong explained that several days among the mountain spirits had left him smelling of demons. His master preferred his disciples clean. He did not want to be rejected again for bringing the wrong sort of air into the company.

Bajie had come expecting a violent and grudging ally. Watching the monkey wash before meeting his teacher, he understood that Wukong had wanted to return from the first moment he heard what had happened.

## The prisoners at Boyue Cave

The shining gold on the hill was still visible from the sky when the two disciples returned to Wanzi Mountain. Bajie pointed toward the cave. Sha Wujing was there, he said. Their master was farther away, caged in the royal palace. The monster was at court.

Wukong said he knew. He went down alone to inspect the entrance.

Two children were playing outside with curved sticks and a ball. They were boys about eight and ten years old, sons of Princess Baihuaxiu and the Yellow-Robed Lord. They had grown up behind the mountain gates, where servants called their father a king and where the princess had no easy path home.

Wukong seized them.

The boys screamed and struggled. Cave servants ran to tell the princess that a stranger had taken her children. She hurried outside and saw the monkey standing on a high rock with a child in each hand. He threatened to throw them down unless she released the prisoner Sha Wujing.

Baihuaxiu was furious. These were her sons. Whatever quarrel he had with their father, what right had he to seize them? Wukong gave his name and repeated the condition: one freed disciple in exchange for two children. The princess went to the captive immediately.

Sha at first resisted being untied. If the demon returned to find him gone, the princess might suffer for the escape. She told him that his elder brother had arrived and had demanded his release. Hearing the name Sun Wukong was like a draught of cool water after a fever. Sha hurried out, bowed, and thanked him.

Wukong could not resist rebuking him. Had Sha defended him when Xuanzang recited the tightening spell and ordered him away? The brothers were eager to be rid of him at White Bone Mountain; now they were just as eager to have him back. Sha acknowledged that he and Bajie had failed. He did not defend himself with further argument. Please, he said, save our master.

Bajie descended from the clouds and joined them. Wukong sent the two brothers to Baoxiang with the captive children. They were to go above the palace and announce that the Yellow-Robed Lord's sons had been captured, provoking him to return to the mountain for battle. Wukong would remain at the cave so that the struggle did not tear apart the king's city.

Bajie objected with unexpected clarity. If they harmed the boys, it would be Bajie and Sha standing in the palace holding the evidence. The father would demand revenge against them, while Wukong, who had ordered the act, remained out of sight. Was that a fair distribution of danger? Wukong dismissed the objection and ordered them to go.

The princess tried to stop the plan. She had freed Sha as demanded. Wukong had promised her the boys. Instead he told her they were being taken to meet their royal grandfather, and then he pressed her about the years she had spent in the cave. Why had she not escaped sooner? Did she feel no obligation to her parents?

She answered that she had never chosen to stay. The demon had taken her by force, watched her movements, and made the journey home impossible. She had endured because she feared that if she died silently in the mountains, her parents would never learn the truth. The letter had been the first chance she found to reach them.

The Monkey King promised to free her. Then he concealed her in a place away from the cave and transformed himself into her exact likeness. He returned through the entrance, ready to meet her husband.

In Baoxiang, meanwhile, Bajie and Sha carried out Wukong's terrible instruction. They threw the two boys down onto the palace steps. Both children were killed. The courtiers cried out in horror, and Bajie proclaimed whose sons they had been.

Their deaths were meant to draw the Yellow-Robed Lord from the capital, and they would. But the children had not abducted Baihuaxiu, deceived the king, or transformed the monk. No counterspell would bring them back. Whatever Wukong achieved by defeating their father, their deaths would remain.

The Yellow-Robed Lord heard the shouts. He was still heavy with wine from the previous night, and his identity had already become suspect among the court servants. He saw the two disciples in the clouds, one of whom ought to have remained chained in his cave. Rather than fight while unprepared, he returned to Wanzi Mountain to discover whether the children were truly his.

## A wife's face and a hidden treasure

Wukong, wearing Baihuaxiu's appearance, waited inside the cave.

As the Yellow-Robed Lord entered, Wukong began to weep as though grief had left him unable to stand. The monster hurried to comfort the woman he believed was his wife. She told him the sons had been seized and taken to the palace. She could not bear the thought that she might never see them again.

The demon's rage rose. He asked whether she was ill with grief. Wukong said only that the pain in her heart was almost unbearable.

The Yellow-Robed Lord had a remedy, he said. He possessed a precious object that could ease such suffering. There was one warning: when it appeared, she must not flick it with her thumb. Doing so would expose his real nature.

Wukong was delighted. A man who feared his secret being seen had just described exactly how to reveal it.

The demon led his supposed wife into a private chamber and brought a glowing pellet out of his mouth. It was a cultivated inner elixir, small enough to hold in the hand, precious because of years of discipline and magical practice. Wukong took it, pretended to touch it to the place where his heart hurt, flicked it with his thumb, and swallowed it.

The monster grabbed for it. The princess's face vanished. Sun Wukong stood before him, grinning.

The Yellow-Robed Lord staggered backward. Who was this intruder? Where had he hidden Baihuaxiu? Wukong answered that he was the eldest disciple of the monk the demon had imprisoned as a tiger. He had come to recover his master, save the princess, and teach the Yellow-Robed Lord how to welcome guests.

The demon attempted to shame him. Had not his own teacher driven him away? Why would he interfere after such a dismissal?

Wukong answered that a day's quarrel did not erase the obligations of a disciple to a master. Xuanzang had freed him from the mountain and guided him toward a different life. He would not leave him helpless in a cage. He also repeated Bajie's accusation that the demon had spoken ill of the Monkey King behind his back.

The monster recognized Bajie's talent for carrying quarrels from place to place and denied saying any such thing.

They argued until the demon called his guards. Wukong became a fearsome warrior with three heads and six arms, multiplying his iron staff so that he could strike in three directions at once. He broke through the lesser creatures and fought his way out of the cave. The Yellow-Robed Lord followed with his great sword, and the battle climbed into the sky.

For many exchanges neither gained the advantage. Wukong tested his opponent, retreating just enough to invite a reckless blow. When the demon struck at the opening, Wukong changed his guard and swung toward the monster's head.

The Yellow-Robed Lord vanished.

Wukong looked for blood, for a body, for the cloud-trail of someone fleeing. There was nothing. The demon had not escaped in any ordinary direction. Wukong remembered that he had seemed familiar with the Monkey King's name and his earlier life. Perhaps this was not a wild creature of the mountain at all.

He turned toward Heaven.

## The absent star

The guards at the heavenly gates knew the former rebel's reputation and did not trouble him. In the celestial court Wukong asked for a check of the registers. A powerful being had descended to the human realm, harmed the Tang monk, and vanished under his staff. Which heavenly official was missing from duty?

The gods checked the planets, the directional star groups, and the celestial offices. Most posts were properly filled. But among the Twenty-Eight Lunar Mansions, one position was empty: the star known as Kui Wood Wolf, or Kuimu Lang.

The records showed that he had missed several celestial attendance calls. Thirteen days had passed in Heaven. In the human world those days amounted to thirteen years.

The Jade Emperor ordered the remaining star officials to summon him. They found Kui Wood Wolf hiding by a mountain stream, using the water's power to conceal himself. He had been afraid to face Wukong again. Brought before the emperor, he acknowledged leaving his appointed place.

The emperor asked why a celestial officer who enjoyed the splendors of Heaven would steal away to a human mountain and live as a monster.

Kui Wood Wolf offered an explanation in terms of an earlier heavenly romance. Baihuaxiu, he claimed, had once been a heavenly attendant who tended incense in the palace. They had intended to be together, but he had feared acting on the desire in Heaven. She had entered the human world and been born as a princess. He had followed, become the Yellow-Robed Lord, and taken her as his wife. In his account, thirteen years of captivity were the fulfillment of an agreement made before their human lives.

That was the story he told the celestial court. Baihuaxiu had told Xuanzang a different one. She had described being seized, kept far from her home, and unable to leave. She had measured every word in the cave against the possibility of her captor's anger. The star officer's claim about a bond before birth did not make those thirteen years a marriage freely chosen.

The Jade Emperor removed the star's credentials and assigned him to work in the alchemical precinct of the Supreme Lord Laozi, tending the furnace until he could earn reinstatement. He was taken back into the hierarchy from which he had slipped away. Wukong, satisfied that the monster would not return to Boyue Cave, left the heavenly court without the elaborate gratitude its officials might have preferred.

The Monkey King went back to the cave and found Baihuaxiu. He explained that the Yellow-Robed Lord had been a heavenly star official and that his superiors had recalled him. The danger of his return, at least for now, was over.

Bajie and Sha arrived clamoring for their share of demons to fight. Wukong told them the fighting was finished. Sha had the practical answer: then bring the princess to her family. They traveled by magic to Baoxiang, where the queen, the king, and the princess's sisters received her in astonishment and relief.

Baihuaxiu thanked the travelers who had brought her home. The court now knew that its handsome guest had been a demon, and that the beast in the iron cage was not what it appeared.

But someone still had to free the monk.

## The cage opens

The officials led Wukong to the place where the tiger had been confined. To everyone else there was a striped beast, heavy with iron chains, its eyes open but its body unable to obey. Wukong alone could see the monk beneath the spell.

He laughed, unable to resist one more rebuke. His master had called the monkey violent and had wanted a company free of such ugliness. Now the master himself was displaying a rather fierce face.

Bajie interrupted him. This was not the time to win an argument. The monk needed help.

Wukong retorted that Bajie had been eager enough to make the argument when it was the Monkey King's head under the tightening fillet. Sha knelt and asked him to set the matter aside. Wukong answered that he had never intended to abandon his master. He had returned because the monk needed him, not because he required a more persuasive invitation.

He sent for water.

Bajie fetched the purple-gold begging bowl from the luggage, filled it, and handed it over. Wukong pronounced a counterspell and sprayed the water over the tiger. The stripes faded, the animal's form dissolved, and Xuanzang lay before them as a human monk once more.

The chains were removed. Xuanzang opened his eyes, saw his eldest disciple, and asked where he had come from.

Sha recounted the journey across the sea, the fight at Boyue Cave, the discovery of the absent star, the return of Baihuaxiu, and the undoing of the tiger enchantment. Xuanzang thanked Wukong repeatedly. He promised that, when the scriptures were brought home, he would tell the Tang emperor how much of the success belonged to his first disciple.

Wukong did not ask for a formal apology. He asked only that the tightening spell be left unspoken.

The king offered a vegetarian feast in gratitude, signed and returned the travelers' papers, and saw them out of the city. Baihuaxiu had reached her family. The star had been sent back to Heaven. Sha, Bajie, and the dragon horse had remained part of the mission despite their failures and injuries. The group of four travelers and a white horse was complete again.

They turned west together. Wukong took his place at the head of the road, alert for the next mountain. No one had forgotten what had happened at White Bone Mountain or Baoxiang. The pilgrimage did not have the power to undo every cruelty committed along its way. But it could continue, and this time the monk did not ask the monkey to leave.
