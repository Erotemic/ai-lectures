# Journey to the West: The third fan and the end of the fire

## A brother in the road

Sun Wukong was hurrying toward the Flaming Mountains with a twelve-foot plantain fan over his shoulder. It was the genuine fan this time, not the false one that had turned the mountain into a greater blaze. He had taken it by disguising himself as the Bull Demon King, borrowing the Bull's voice, and coaxing Princess Iron Fan into revealing both the treasure and its means of growth. He was pleased with himself. The long family quarrel seemed at last to have been reduced to one clever theft.

Someone came along the road to meet him. It was Bajie, as Wukong thought, panting and complaining that Xuanzang had been waiting too long. The master was afraid his eldest disciple had been killed, Bajie said, and had sent him to help.

Wukong laughed. "You needn't worry, brother. I have the fan."

The newcomer wanted the whole story. Wukong told him about the Bull's banquet at the Emerald Waves Pool, the stolen water-traveling beast, the visit to Plantain Cave as the Bull Demon King, and the trick by which he had obtained the real fan. The supposed Bajie listened with growing satisfaction. He praised his elder brother's cleverness and offered to carry the large, inconvenient object for him.

Wukong handed it over.

The other disciple suddenly began to shrink the fan with a formula Wukong did not know. He reduced it to a tiny leaf, then threw off Bajie's appearance. The Bull Demon King stood before Wukong with the fan. The trickster had been tricked with his own method. Wukong had taken the Bull's face to deceive the Bull's wife; the Bull had taken Bajie's face to deceive Wukong.

"You old ox!" Wukong shouted, reaching for his iron staff.

"You robbed my wife," the Bull replied. "Now you know how it feels to be fooled."

They fought in the sky. The Bull could not blow the monkey away: in the earlier struggle with the Rakshasi, Wukong had swallowed the wind-stilling pill Lingji had given him. The power that had protected his body against the first blasts now remained within him. The Bull put the fan out of danger and took up the swords brought from his wife's cave. Staff and blades rang above the hills. Neither enemy was willing to give ground, and neither could easily force the other down.

Far away, at the place where the pilgrims waited, Xuanzang watched the sky and asked why Wukong had been gone for so long. He had sent no Bajie to meet him. The real Bajie was still standing beside Sha Wujing and the horse. The Earth God warned that the Bull King was a formidable match for their eldest brother. Bajie finally agreed to go and help. The Earth God went with him, accompanied by local spirit troops, while Sha remained to protect their master.

## The cave broken open

Bajie and the Earth God found Wukong engaged in a contest that had lasted deep into the night. Their arrival brought a fresh surge of courage. Bajie demanded to know why the Bull had refused a simple loan and turned the road into a battlefield. Wukong explained that the Bull had imitated Bajie, recovered the fan, and escaped with it.

Bajie was indignant at the use of his face. He was also tempted by the thought of going around the mountain. There were other roads, he argued. Why spend another day getting beaten by a relative who would not help? The Earth God spoke firmly. Xuanzang had chosen the way west, and that was the way the party must open. Returning to the safer paths would not advance the pilgrimage.

Together Wukong, Bajie, the Earth God, and the accompanying spirits assaulted Cloud-Rubbing Cave, the Bull's home with Princess Jade-Face. The Bull came out armed, angrily demanding why the monkey was smashing the doors of a house. Bajie answered with his nine-toothed rake. Wukong brought his staff down from the other side. The old Bull fought both of them, striking and defending with a strength that seemed not to tire.

They battled through more than a hundred exchanges. At length the Bull tried to withdraw into his cave, but the Earth God's troops blocked the entrance. He threw off his armor, abandoned his weapon, and changed himself into a swan. He rose away from the mountain, hoping to vanish in the open air.

Wukong saw him. He told Bajie and the local spirits to finish the attack on the cave and prevent the enemy from finding refuge there. He himself changed into a fierce hunting bird and sped after the swan.

Then the battle became a cascade of bodies. The Bull became a bird of prey; Wukong changed into another. The Bull flew as a white crane; Wukong became a splendid phoenix. The Bull dropped to the earth as a musk deer; Wukong descended as a hungry tiger. The Bull answered with a leopard, Wukong with a lion-like beast, the Bull with a great bear, Wukong with an elephant whose trunk and tusks could seize him. Each creature had a form suited to frightening or catching the last. The two old mountain kings knew one another's gifts too well to be surprised for long.

Finally the Bull abandoned the game of matching animals and revealed his full strength. He became a white bull of terrifying size, with horns like iron towers, a back hundreds of feet high, and a body stretching across the slopes. Wukong enlarged himself in answer. His face rose like a mountain, his mouth widened like a lake, and his staff became large enough to strike a giant. The white bull drove his horns forward; the monkey met them with the iron rod. The hills shook, and the contest drew the attention of the divine protectors who had followed the pilgrimage unseen.

The Bull was no longer fighting only one rebellious monkey. Guardians gathered on every side, blocking the routes he might use to escape. He resumed his smaller form and fled toward Plantain Cave, where Princess Iron Fan had withdrawn after the deception. Wukong and the gathered gods followed and surrounded the cave.

Bajie arrived from Cloud-Rubbing Cave with the Earth God and their troops. His report was grim. They had broken into the Bull's other household; Bajie had killed Princess Jade-Face with his rake, slaughtered many of the demon attendants, and burned the buildings. The wealth, the courtship, and the place in which the Bull had chosen to live were gone. Wukong approved the destruction because it removed another refuge. The novel gives the calamity no gentler result. These are not merely men arguing over a borrowed tool: their dispute has destroyed a household.

## Heavenly reinforcements

The Bull stayed behind the closed door, keeping the fan safe while the siege tightened. But the pilgrims' protectors had called for greater help. The Four Great Vajra Guardians arrived, together with the Pagoda-Bearing Heavenly King Li Jing and his son Nezha, the young warrior of Heaven. The divine forces took positions so that the Bull could not simply turn into a bird, fly north, and start another life beyond Wukong's reach.

When he tried to break out, Nezha confronted him. The prince assumed his three-headed, six-armed form and attacked with divine weapons. He severed the Bull's head, but the creature grew another. Nezha struck again and again; each new head appeared from the wounded body. The defeat could not be achieved by ordinary cutting.

Nezha fastened flaming wheels against the Bull's horns and burned him with a fire that could follow a demon's transformations. Li Jing raised a demon-revealing mirror and fixed the Bull in his true form so he could not escape by changing again. Bound by his nature and surrounded by enemies, the Bull finally cried out that he would submit. He did not want to die.

Nezha ordered him to produce the fan. The Bull said it was in his wife's keeping. A cord was fastened to his nose, and he was led before Plantain Cave. There he called to Princess Iron Fan to bring out the treasure and save his life.

She emerged without the finery she had worn to fight Wukong, looking more like a penitent devotee than a cave princess. She carried the true plantain fan in both hands. Seeing the gods and the captured Bull, she begged for mercy. The fan was hers, and she had used it to sustain herself and the people who depended upon her. Now she surrendered it to the monkey she had sworn to hate.

Wukong took the fan; the heavenly company turned back toward the scorched mountain. The Bull was led away under divine custody. Princess Iron Fan remained alive, but her son was with Guanyin, her husband's second household was destroyed, and the husband himself had been bound. The great victory on the road had a cost visible to everyone who had taken part.

## Forty-nine sweeps

Xuanzang and Sha had waited through the heat, rising and sitting, walking a few steps and returning, certain that something had happened to Wukong. Now the sky filled with banners and divine lights. Sha recognized the warriors bringing the captive Bull. Wukong walked among them holding the true fan. Xuanzang bowed to the gods and thanked them for assisting his journey.

Wukong went to the edge of the fire. He raised the fan and swept it once across the mountain. The blaze fell. Not a flame remained above the ridges. He swept again, and a cooling breeze traveled over the road. He swept a third time, and clouds gathered overhead. Rain began to fall on the burning land.

Xuanzang could breathe. The horse stood quietly. Bajie and Sha watched water strike the stones that had scorched their feet. After so many false promises, the fan did what the villagers had said it would do.

But the Earth God raised a difficulty. The people of the region had known the fan's power for years. They had used it to win crops for a season, only to find the fires returning. Was the mountain truly safe forever, or only until the wind shifted again?

Princess Iron Fan, brought to the place by the Earth God, answered. To remove the root of the fire, Wukong must fan the mountain forty-nine more times. The repeated sweeps would draw out the heat so completely that it would not reignite.

Wukong did as she said. The fan rose and fell through forty-nine strokes. Rain came down heavily on the fire's former home. The Earth God watched the embers die; the drought-struck land received the water it had long lacked. The pilgrims were spared the rain where they stood, while the mountain itself was washed clean.

At first Bajie wanted to keep the fan. After all the fighting, deception, and danger, was it not part of the reward? But Wukong remembered his promise, made before the first quarrel became a war. He had asked to borrow the fan to carry his master through the mountain and had offered to return it. The princess knelt and asked for her property. Now that the root of the fire was gone, even the Earth God urged him to give it back.

Wukong returned it. Princess Iron Fan reduced the great leaf to a tiny size and kept it safely in her mouth. She withdrew to pursue cultivation, while the Bull Demon King remained in the custody of the powers that had subdued him. The local spirit could finally hope that the region would be green without annual bargaining and the submission of petitions.

The pilgrims stayed overnight and crossed the newly cooled mountain the next morning. Underfoot there was damp earth instead of embers. Their clothes no longer steamed; the horse no longer hurried in fear. The road had been opened by a treasure, by help from Heaven, and by the bitter settling of a family grievance. A fire kindled in Wukong's wild years had taken five hundred years to extinguish.

Beyond the Flaming Mountains lay another country, another court, and a monastery with a tower that no longer shone. The monks there had been accused of stealing a treasure they had never touched.
