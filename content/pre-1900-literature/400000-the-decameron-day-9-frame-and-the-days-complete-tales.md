# The Decameron · Day 9: frame and the day’s complete tales

## The Decameron — Day Nine: Emilia and the Freedom to Choose

The company wakes to a day without an appointed subject. Emilia, newly crowned queen, has refused to confine the speakers to a single kind of tale. Their conversations have already ranged through religion, misfortune, thwarted love, tricks and countertricks. Now, after the eighth day's elaborate rivalries, she lets every storyteller choose. This freedom is not the absence of order. There is still a queen, a sequence of speakers, and the expectation that ten stories will carry the afternoon. Only the topic is released.

In the morning Emilia rouses the young women and men and leads them into a nearby wood. Deer and goats move through the trees without their ordinary fear. The narrator offers a striking explanation: during the pestilence, human hunters have ceased to be the constant danger they ordinarily are. The animals move freely not because every creature has forgotten mortality but because the usual human threat has been suspended. The company's escape from Florence offers a related, more fragile sensation. They are alive amid a larger catastrophe, enjoying a space that cannot be guaranteed to last.

The friends gather leaves and aromatic plants, walk, sing and return to the house. Food, music and the labor of their servants maintain the pleasant rhythm of their temporary society. In the afternoon they settle down to stories. Filomena begins with a widow who has two unwanted admirers. The series moves between convents, travelers, painters, an inn and a household where a husband tries to interpret a dream. The narrators return to familiar people, especially Calandrino, whose eagerness to believe bad evidence about himself has already made him a standing comic character.

But open choice does not make the day's contents neutral. One story turns a supposed medical diagnosis into a way of extracting money. Another begins with a girl's attraction and ends in marital anger. Emilia herself chooses a story that expressly defends husbands beating supposedly disobedient wives. Her argument is not a universal judgment delivered by the novel from above. It is a speech by one of its fictional storytellers, heard after many earlier tales exposed the cruel effects of husbands' jealousy and power. The collection makes the audience hold these stories together without providing a single consistent doctrine.

Dioneo, exempt as usual from the fixed speaking order, ends the day by pretending that an absurd sexual encounter is an attempted magical transformation. His joke returns the company to the worldly body after Emilia's moralizing advice. When the crown passes to Panfilo, the last king, the tenth day receives a definite subject: acts of great-heartedness and magnificence. The change alters what the audience expects from the day of storytelling. The storytellers will measure what human beings do with wealth, power and the possibility of giving something away. Emilia's free day gives one final view of their mixed world before the more elevated closing theme.

## The Decameron — Day Nine, Tale 1: Francesca and the Tomb

Madonna Francesca of Pistoia is a widow. Two Florentine men, living in Pistoia after being banished from their own city, insist on pursuing her. They are called Rinuccio and Alessandro. Neither knows that the other is pleading for her favor, and Francesca wishes to accept neither. Their messages have become a burden. A plain refusal has not stopped them, so she devises a demand intended to be too frightening for either man to carry out.

That morning a notorious man called Scannadio has been buried. Francesca knows his appearance inspired fear even while he lived. She sends Alessandro a message: if he wants her favor, he must enter the tomb after dark, wrap himself in the dead man's winding clothes and remain silent, as though he were the corpse, until someone comes to carry him to her house. To Rinuccio she sends a second instruction: enter the same tomb at midnight, lift out Scannadio's body and carry it to her door without speaking. The tasks are separate; the men do not know they are actors in a single invention.

Against her expectation, both agree. Alessandro goes first, terrified by the prospect of being found among the dead. He removes the corpse from its place, puts on the burial cloth and lies down, waiting. His fears multiply in the dark. Rinuccio follows, also uneasy, imagining accusations of witchcraft or a challenge from the dead man's relations. He finds the figure in the tomb, shoulders it, and staggers toward Francesca's house. He has no idea that the burden is a living rival who is trying with immense difficulty not to reveal himself.

Near her doorway the night patrol suddenly appears, carrying light and weapons. Rinuccio drops his burden and flees. Alessandro, unable to maintain his impersonation, gets up and runs as well. Francesca, watching from a window with her maid, sees enough to understand both men's courage and the accident that has rescued her plan. By morning the empty tomb becomes a marvel for the neighborhood. People speculate about supernatural intervention. The two would-be lovers eventually explain what they attempted and ask her to forgive their failure.

She refuses. Her condition was not fulfilled, and she owes them nothing. The audience may recognize that their willingness to undertake the work is evidence of strong feeling. Francesca recognizes something more immediately important: she never offered them the right to decide that their feeling must be returned. A contrived task succeeds by failure. Her stated bargain provides the excuse that ordinary refusal ought to have supplied, and the interruption by civic guards becomes the thing that frees her.

Filomena's tale joins darkness, burial and foolish love to a small problem of social independence. Francesca's cleverness is easier to applaud than the fact that she needs it. The admirers take extravagant risks in hopes of possessing her affection; they do not first ask whether it is welcome. The ending does not reward daring with marriage or sex. It restores a widow's freedom to say no, even though the rules by which she gains that freedom are absurdly elaborate.

## The Decameron — Day Nine, Tale 2: The Abbess and the Breeches

A young nun has found a lover. He visits her cell at night, and their meetings continue until other women in the convent discover the secret. Rather than speak to the young woman, her accusers take the news to the abbess and persuade the superior to conduct a surprise inspection. Their plan has a simple assumption: the authority at the top of the convent can expose a forbidden desire and punish it without having her own desires examined.

The trouble is that the abbess is with a priest in her own bed when the accusation comes. Wakened hurriedly in the dark, she reaches for what she thinks is her veil, places it on her head and hurries to the novice's room. It is not her veil. She has grabbed the priest's breeches, and she wears them like a ridiculous head covering. She arrives with the severe confidence of someone who believes the signs of office are all in place.

The accused nun expects censure. The abbess begins to condemn her conduct, lecturing on chastity and the shame she has brought upon the house. The young woman notices the curious object on the superior's head. At first, she hears the warnings. Then she points to the headgear and asks the abbess to look at what she herself is wearing. The scene turns in a moment. The person meant to represent purity is broadcasting the circumstances of her own interrupted night.

The abbess understands the danger immediately. She abandons her hard line and speaks much more indulgently. The young woman's liaison is not punished. In the comic world of the tale, hypocrisy has been defeated by a garment. The clothing has become evidence stronger than rank, accusation or sermon.

Elissa's narration is not a history of convent life in Lombardy. It belongs to a long tradition of literary jokes in which religious office and bodily appetite collide. The device is familiar because it is effective: a person who has one set of standards for subordinates is exposed as violating those standards herself. Yet it would be too simple to say that the tale abolishes rules or rewards every secret. It is the particular superior's authority that is damaged, not the existence of institutions or vows as such.

The accused nun is rescued by an accident. Had the abbess put on the right veil, the same conduct might have brought harsh consequences. The comedy rests on that imbalance of power. The abbess has the privilege to accuse while expecting concealment; the nun has no comparable protection. For one night, the visible trace of the superior's own activity makes her unable to maintain the distance on which her judgment depends.

## The Decameron — Day Nine, Tale 3: Calandrino Is Pregnant

Calandrino has inherited two hundred pounds from an aunt. To his friends Bruno and Buffalmacco, this represents an opportunity for generous food and wine. To Calandrino, it is the beginning of a new life as a property owner. He talks grandly about buying land, but nothing comes of his negotiations. His friends cannot persuade him to spend even enough to treat them to a meal. They ask a third painter, Nello, to join them in a different sort of campaign.

One morning Nello encounters Calandrino and studies his face with concern. Is he ill? Has he slept badly? Calandrino is alarmed. He goes on and meets another friend who independently tells him he looks sick. The word independently is the whole trick: the men have agreed on what to say. Anxiety spreads through Calandrino more rapidly than any disease. He becomes increasingly convinced that his body is failing because everyone seems to detect the same symptoms.

Bruno and Buffalmacco recommend a medical opinion. Their accomplice is Master Simone, the physician who previously believed Bruno and Buffalmacco's fantasies about a secret society. Here the doctor is no longer merely the victim of a prank; he takes an active part in making one. After examining Calandrino, he announces the impossible diagnosis: Calandrino is pregnant. The frightened man believes him. The physician supplies an explanation suited to Calandrino's prejudices and fears, including a way to blame his wife for the calamity.

The doctor then offers an expensive remedy. Calandrino must provide capons and money for medicine. He obeys, terrified of what might happen if the pregnancy proceeds. The conspirators dine well on the birds, and their supposed treatment produces a guaranteed success. They tell him he is cured. He can rejoice in having escaped a condition that never existed.

The tale is built from four kinds of false authority. Three friends create an appearance of agreement. Their victim translates agreement into proof. A physician gives the fiction a professional name. A paid treatment then supplies an equally false measure of recovery. Once Calandrino feels safe, he has no reason to examine the diagnosis, because the relief itself seems to confirm it.

Filostrato's company laughs at Calandrino's gullibility. A listener can also ask why their laughter is so easy to obtain. His friends exploit real anxiety and confiscate money by persuading him that his body is at risk. They do not only demonstrate his foolishness; they use their knowledge of his fears as a means of getting paid. The episode returns a familiar comic character to the collection while showing the mechanism of a manufactured illness: first invent a threat, then sell the escape.

## The Decameron — Day Nine, Tale 4: The Two Ceccos

Two young men from Siena share a familiar name. One is Cecco, son of Messer Angiulieri; the other is Cecco, son of Messer Fortarrigo. Their friendship hides very different temperaments. Angiulieri is trying to travel responsibly with money and a horse. Fortarrigo loves gambling and has so little control over his impulses that he cannot be trusted with either. They set out together, and their differing ideas of what a journey requires soon become a dangerous problem.

At Buonconvento, Fortarrigo discovers the gaming tables. He loses his own money and then the funds Angiulieri has entrusted to him. He wants his companion to rescue him, but Angiulieri is already weary of the misbehavior. Rather than repair the loss, Angiulieri leaves. Fortarrigo pursues him in the countryside, intent on reversing the situation with one more deception.

He strips down to his shirt and begins crying out that the rider has robbed him. Country people hear an apparently desperate victim accusing a well-dressed man on horseback. They stop Angiulieri and demand an explanation. Angiulieri gives the true account, but his words are placed against the immediately legible picture Fortarrigo has arranged: a nearly naked man and another man with the clothes and horse. The peasants take the wrong side.

Fortarrigo calmly claims his companion's clothes and mount, and rides away. Angiulieri, who had been attempting to escape a dishonest companion, is left barefoot and dressed only in his shirt. There is no ingenious way to reframe the event in time. A stranger's wrong first impression has made the theft possible, and the rightful owner's explanation arrives too late.

Neifile tells a compact story about the authority of appearances. Here there is no enchanted stone, fake medicine or theological disguise. The trick is a visual accusation. The apparent victim has arranged himself to look exactly as an onlooker expects a robbed person to look. The apparent robber possesses the things that a robber would plausibly have taken. The tale does not require the peasants to be malicious; it requires them to trust the image more than the disputed history behind it.

It also makes the reader consider what counts as friendship. The two men are young, alike in name and connected by habit, but likeness supplies no guarantee of fidelity. Fortarrigo uses their shared history to obtain access to money, then uses a fabricated public story to dispossess the man who trusted him. The comic finish depends on a severe injustice. If the ending encourages laughter, it also shows how quickly social judgment can turn against someone who lacks the signs of credibility.

## The Decameron — Day Nine, Tale 5: Calandrino and Niccolosa

Calandrino and his fellow painters are working on a house at Camerata when a young woman named Niccolosa arrives with the owner's son, Filippo. Calandrino sees her washing at the well and imagines a future in which she prefers him to everyone else. He is married to Tessa, a fact he hopes will remain outside the story. He approaches Bruno with a confidence entirely at odds with the evidence: Niccolosa must surely be smitten with him, and someone should help bring her within reach.

Bruno knows more than he admits. He recognizes Niccolosa and soon tells Filippo and the other painters what Calandrino has said. Together they arrange a comedy in which every apparent sign of affection is produced for Calandrino's benefit. Niccolosa plays along with the game. Calandrino reads her carefully managed glances as proof of his irresistible charm. His companions encourage him to talk, sing and admire his own imagined conquest. The more absurd his behavior becomes, the more secure he feels.

Bruno offers something better than ordinary courtship: a written charm. If Calandrino carries the paper and touches Niccolosa with it, she will follow him. Calandrino accepts the prescription, because it promises success without any need to understand her wishes. The actors arrange that Niccolosa will seem to obey the charm. To Calandrino it becomes the most direct possible evidence that magic works and that his feelings are returned.

But there is another audience to be managed. Nello, a relation of Tessa, has an easy way to reach Calandrino's wife. She learns of her husband's attempted adventure and comes to the appointed place. Calandrino is discovered not in the arms of a woman won by enchantment but in the middle of an elaborate practical joke. Tessa's anger and physical retaliation break his confident fantasy. The young woman and accomplices have made him a spectacle rather than a conqueror.

Fiammetta's story repeats the pattern established in Calandrino's earlier adventures. A claim of magic is believable because the supposed beneficiary badly wants it to be true. He imagined that an invisible stone would allow theft without detection; he accepted an impossible pregnancy and paid for a cure. Here he takes a scroll for a device capable of turning another person's desire into an automatic response. The underlying ambition is different, but the habit of belief is the same.

The tale's named woman is not the object of a genuine supernatural power. She is an actor in a staged response; her participation is part of the deception. Calandrino, who thinks the charm would make her follow him regardless of choice, does not understand the stakes of what he is asking for. The laughter of Bruno and the others exposes that failure, while Tessa's arrival recalls the marriage he was so eager to ignore.

## The Decameron — Day Nine, Tale 6: Three Beds and a Cradle

A poor innkeeper near the Mugnone has little room to spare. His household includes his wife, an unmarried daughter named Niccolosa and a baby son. Two young men, Pinuccio and Adriano, arrive late, claiming that their journey has left them no place else to sleep. They are not there by chance. Pinuccio is in love with Niccolosa, who returns his affection, and he has arranged the visit because the cramped house may allow them to meet secretly.

The innkeeper reluctantly makes space. Three beds fit into the single sleeping room, and the baby's cradle stands by the parents' bed. The strangers take one bed. The daughter sleeps in another. Her parents occupy the third. Once everyone appears asleep, Pinuccio moves to Niccolosa. She welcomes him, but the room is not silent for long. A cat knocks something over, and the innkeeper's wife gets up to see what has happened. At almost the same moment Adriano rises for a private necessity and moves the cradle out of his way, leaving it next to the guests' bed.

That small change transforms the room. The wife, returning in darkness, feels for the cradle to find her bed. It is now beside Adriano. She lies down with the wrong man, thinking him her husband, and they have intercourse. Meanwhile Pinuccio, eventually leaving Niccolosa, mistakes his way back and gets into the innkeeper's bed. Believing the man beside him is Adriano, he speaks freely of his success with the daughter. The furious listener is not his friend but her father.

Everything threatens to collapse at once. The father rises in anger and calls out. Adriano and the wife are in the other bed. The wife rapidly understands how the mistaken beds have produced the apparent scandal. To protect her daughter and herself, she moves to Niccolosa's bed and supplies an account in which the daughter has been sleeping with her all along. She insists that the father has confused his own impressions. In the chaos no one possesses a full public record of the night, and her confident story carries the family past the immediate crisis.

Panfilo's tale is often remembered as a farce of displaced furniture. It is more exact than that. The cradle ordinarily identifies the mother's position in a dark room. Moving it destroys the navigational system on which everyone relies. Pinuccio's bedroom confession would have stayed private if he had reached the right bed. The whole story turns upon the difference between knowing where one intends to be and knowing where one actually is.

Its comedy also masks different forms of risk. Pinuccio plans his entry; Niccolosa's willingness is part of the tale; Adriano takes advantage of a woman who mistakes him for her husband. That mistake matters to consent, even if the narrator treats the encounter chiefly as a mechanism for laughter. The mother's skill at crisis management prevents exposure, but it does not make the night's different encounters equivalent. A careful retelling can preserve the elaborate comedy without erasing those distinctions.

## The Decameron — Day Nine, Tale 7: Talano's Dream

Talano di Molese has a beautiful wife named Margarita. Their marriage is marked by quarrels. She resists his requests, and he has learned to expect disagreement whatever the subject. One night, while the couple are staying on their country property, Talano dreams of Margarita walking in a nearby wood. A wolf attacks her. She escapes with her life but suffers injuries to her neck and face. The vision is so vivid that he wakes deeply troubled.

In the morning he tells Margarita about the dream and begs her not to walk in the woods that day. He does not claim the authority of a physician or an eyewitness; he is afraid because the image has been so clear. Margarita receives the warning in the context of their habitual antagonism. She suspects that her husband wants to control where she goes, or perhaps has some other private motive. The more earnestly he warns her, the less willing she is to accept his advice.

She goes to the wood. A wolf emerges, attacks and wounds her in the way Talano feared. She survives, but the marks remain. The narrator presents the dream as a true forewarning and treats the disaster as a consequence of Margarita's refusal to listen. Those around the storyteller agree that the exact correspondence makes it more like a vision than an ordinary dream.

We can hear more than one problem in this short narrative. The dream proves predictive inside the fiction, but its explanation is not established outside that fiction. The injuries are literary events, not evidence for a general claim that dreams foretell the future. The story gives a woman an opportunity to avert harm and then makes her failure to obey the moral of the incident. That is the line Pampinea's chosen example invites her audience to follow.

Yet the marriage's earlier distrust is also part of the mechanism. If a husband's ordinary speech has become a series of commands, a sincere warning may be hard to separate from another attempt at control. Margarita makes a dangerous choice, but her error occurs in a relationship where appeals to obedience have already lost credibility. The narrative takes the warning as true; a reader is free to ask why truth has no reliable channel between these people.

The result prepares for a much more forceful story later in the day. Emilia will use an explicitly didactic account of domestic discipline to argue that a wife ought to submit to her husband's rule. Placed beside that argument, Talano's dream shows how easily a terrible accident can become a lesson about obedience. The wolf is real within the story. The account of what the wolf proves about marriage is told by a human narrator.

## The Decameron — Day Nine, Tale 8: Ciacco and Biondello

Ciacco is famous in Florence for his appetite. Though he has no fortune to support it, he knows how to find his way to the tables of richer men and is quick enough in conversation to make himself welcome. Biondello is another wit of the city. Between them a small trick over dinner becomes a contest in which each man wants not merely to win but to make the other's humiliation visible.

Biondello encounters Ciacco and makes him believe that a fine meal is being prepared for him at the house of a wealthy gentleman. He offers the information in a manner designed to be irresistible to a person who prizes a good table. Ciacco hurries to enjoy the promised hospitality. But the meal is nothing like what he has been led to expect. He discovers that he has become the object of a practical joke and leaves angry, intent on repaying Biondello for the deception.

Ciacco's revenge is more dangerous. He knows the sort of message that will inflame a proud man's temper and contrives for Biondello to deliver it without understanding its effect. The recipient believes he has been insulted or challenged by the messenger. Biondello is beaten. The man who first converted another's desire for food into a moment of public foolishness now becomes the victim of a trick that gives him no time to explain his innocence.

Lauretta invites the listeners to compare a clever response with a cruel one. The first deception produces embarrassment and disappointment. The second recruits the strength and anger of a third party. It does not simply match the injury done by Biondello; it magnifies it. The trickster has found a social mechanism more powerful than his own hands and set it in motion.

Ciacco occupies a position that complicates the social contest. He is a known presence in other people's dining rooms, and the social customs of hospitality provide an expectation Biondello can manipulate. His hunger is real; the audience is invited to laugh at its excess. Yet no listener needs to suppose that wanting a good meal is equivalent to deserving physical violence. The two tricks reveal different prices paid for being made a fool.

Boccaccio allows the stories' narrators to enjoy the ingenuity of practical jokes even when the consequences are unequal. Their laughter is a feature of the framed narrative, not a command that the reader must find all forms of punishment just. In a collection organized around people telling stories to one another, that distinction is essential. One can recognize a perfect mechanism of retaliation while remaining troubled by what the mechanism does.

## The Decameron — Day Nine, Tale 9: Solomon and the Bridge of Geese

Two travelers are headed to Solomon, the king celebrated for his wisdom. Melisso is wealthy and lavish in his hospitality, but says he cannot make anyone love him. Giosefo is angry about his wife, whom he regards as disobedient, and wants to know how he should control her. They are disappointed by the king's answers. To Melisso he gives a single instruction: love. To Giosefo he says to go to the Bridge of Geese. The men depart believing that the famous wisdom may have offered them only riddles.

On the journey home, they come to a bridge crossed by a line of pack animals. One mule stops and refuses to move. Its driver beats it with a stick and persists until it is forced to go forward. Giosefo believes he now understands what Solomon meant by sending him there. The bridge has not furnished an allegory he needs to meditate upon. It has supplied a brutal example he believes he can imitate.

At home Giosefo treats his wife with violence, and the tale represents the change in her behavior as a successful cure for rebellion. Melisso receives a less violent clarification of his own advice. If he wishes others to love him, he must learn to love them, rather than imagining affection can be purchased by display and generosity. The paired answers occupy the same story but do not deserve to be collapsed into one harmless lesson about practical wisdom.

Emilia's introduction makes the intended moral explicit. She argues that women are by nature and custom subordinate to men and that force may be necessary to maintain obedience. Her reasoning provides a key to the tale's use of Solomon. The narrator is telling this story as an endorsement of marital discipline. It would misrepresent the primary text to pretend that the beating is condemned by Emilia or that the final outcome is narratively neutral. The episode is troubling precisely because an intelligent, articulate fictional woman speaks in favor of the husband's violence.

That does not establish Boccaccio's own position as an historical certainty. Earlier days have included wives who escape surveillance, women whose husbands injure them, and farces in which male power is made ridiculous. The same collection can include a story that licenses violence and one that celebrates resisting a controlling husband. Their juxtaposition resists conversion into a single modern slogan about the work's attitude toward women.

The king's first answer is worth hearing beside the second. Melisso learns that love requires an action on his own part. Giosefo learns to impose his will by force. One response asks the questioner to change himself; the other helps him change another person against her will. A collection that has spent so much time exploring the meaning of cleverness now exposes a further problem: a technique can work according to a narrator's measure of success and still fail as an ethic of how to live with other people.

## The Decameron — Day Nine, Tale 10: Dom Gianni and the Mare

Dom Gianni is a priest who supplements his meager income by trading at fairs in Apulia. He owns a mare and travels with his merchandise. A poor trader named Pietro, who has only an ass, becomes his friend. They offer each other hospitality in their small homes. When the priest visits Pietro and his young wife Gemmata, the single bed cannot accommodate all three, so the visitor sleeps on straw beside his mare.

Gemmata wants the guest to be more comfortable. The priest, joking or boasting, says he can change his mare into a woman and back again, and therefore has no need of a better bed. Gemmata takes this incredible claim seriously and asks her husband whether the priest might transform her into a mare. An additional pack animal would be a remarkable advantage to their trade. Pietro, who also wants a better livelihood, eagerly asks Dom Gianni to perform the marvel.

The priest tries to dissuade them. They insist. At last he agrees and makes a condition: no one is to interrupt the ceremony or speak during it. Gemmata is told to undress and stand on all fours. Dom Gianni touches the parts of her body in turn and names the corresponding parts of an animal. Pietro holds a light and watches. The priest's performance is designed to culminate not in genuine magic but in a sexual act under the cover of completing the mare's tail.

Pietro finally recognizes what the supposed tail entails and cries out that he wants none of it. Dom Gianni blames him for breaking the spell. The transformation, he says, was almost complete, and now cannot be repeated. The couple have no new beast of burden; the reader has seen the grotesque mechanism by which a merchant's wish for profit becomes an excuse for a cleric's sexual opportunity.

Dioneo's license within the company shapes the tales he can tell and the risks he can take. He may range beyond the daily theme and regularly uses his privilege to produce a bawdy conclusion. Here he follows Emilia's grave advice about obedience with a comic account of an attempted transformation. He does not deliver a general theory of marriage. He ends by making the audience picture the very bodily act hidden beneath the language of an enchantment.

The tale depends on poverty and belief as well as lust. A second animal would genuinely help Pietro and Gemmata. That desire makes them vulnerable to a claim that would otherwise be laughable. The priest possesses more knowledge of the deception than either of them and exploits the fiction of a precise ritual. The humor need not conceal the inequality of that position. And with the last tale of Day Nine, the company's free choice of subjects has produced one more story in which someone sells power that does not exist and someone else pays for believing in it.
