# Journey to the West: The fish basket and the old turtle

## A prisoner beneath eight hundred li

The river had opened under the pilgrims and swallowed Xuanzang. His disciples had survived on the broken ice, but the man they were sworn to guard now lay imprisoned in a stone coffer at the bottom of Heaven-Reaching River. Above him the cold surface was a patchwork of broken sheets. Below him the water pressed around the fish spirit's palace. The monster had won by offering the travelers a road that turned out to be a trap.

Wukong could leap through the air, but his powers had a weakness he did not like discussing. He did not fight as freely underwater as he did on land. When he entered the water, he could move and use spells, but the breathing and movement needed to remain beneath it made a long battle awkward. Sha Wujing had lived in rivers; Bajie had commanded naval forces in his earlier heavenly life. If anyone could draw the spirit from its halls, they were better suited to the work.

The pig and the river demon descended together, determined to find the prison. Wukong was supposed to remain ready for a fight above the surface. Yet the idea of being absent from the investigation bothered him. He feared his companions might wander through the palace without discovering where Xuanzang had been hidden.

Bajie proposed a little trick of his own. He had been teased enough for his hunger and fat belly; now he could repay a small part of the debt. He told the monkey to prepare for a swift passage underwater and made a movement that promised to take him down. For a moment Wukong seemed to be caught by the joke. Bajie had tried to make the monkey do the work while laughing at his discomfort.

But the trick had a counter-trick. Wukong could leave a figure in his place and change his real body into a creature no larger than a louse. He slipped into Bajie's ear, where he could listen without being seen. The pig went below carrying his elder brother without knowing precisely how close he was. Sha Wujing followed, staff ready, through the dark water.

They found the palace and listened at its walls. The monster's attendants moved back and forth. The captive monk was concealed in a great stone chest, not seated among open prisoners. Xuanzang's voice could be heard from inside it. He wept and called upon his disciples, lamenting that he had been drawn into the river by an illusion of solid ice. He feared his journey would end below the waves, his scriptures never fetched, and the promise he had made to the Tang court left empty.

From Bajie's ear Wukong heard his master. That sound cut through his impatience and mischief. He could not yet reach the coffer, but he knew that Xuanzang was alive. He sent comfort as best he could and made his companions understand where the captive lay.

The question was how to bring the Great King out. If the disciples could force him onto land, Wukong could fight without the difficulties of underwater combat. If he stayed behind his palace gates, the monster could wait while his prisoner weakened.

## The palace that belonged to someone else

The underwater fight did not proceed as the disciples had hoped. Bajie and Sha Wujing challenged the spirit and tried to draw him up. He had a heavy copper weapon and the advantage of his own element. The water itself seemed to protect him. The fish attendant in his palace was quicker at understanding the danger than he was. She recognized the disturbances of Sun Wukong and warned her master that the Great Sage was not an opponent to meet casually on open ground.

Close the gates, she urged. Keep the captive safe behind the stone walls. If the monkey cannot reach the master, the disciples will have to leave or search for a power they have not yet brought.

The Great King listened. The palace closed against the intruders. The three disciples could break many things, but they could not simply tear away the river and lift every roof from the city beneath it. To those inside, shut doors and deep water felt like safety.

The local water beings knew another part of the story. This great palace was not the fish spirit's ancestral home. Nine years earlier it had belonged to an old white turtle, a venerable creature who had lived in the river long before the monster arrived. The intruder had driven the turtle out and made his home into a private fortress. The fish spirit's claim to be the rightful guardian of the river was no more honest than his demand that villagers feed him children.

This information did not yet free Xuanzang, but it changed the shape of the problem. The Great King was an invader in the waters as surely as he was a predator on shore. Someone had tolerated his presence, and someone might have a claim over him.

Wukong understood that direct force was no longer the best hope. The monster had chosen the element in which Wukong was least comfortable and had kept out of reach of the iron staff. There was a being whom Wukong had often summoned to solve problems involving dangerous spirits and obscure divine responsibilities. He would go to Guanyin.

## The bodhisattva with no ceremonial robes

Wukong traveled to the southern sea in a hurry. The island where Guanyin dwelt ordinarily held a world of order and ceremony. Visitors expected attendants, a splendid seat, and the serenity of a bodhisattva whose thoughts were directed toward all suffering beings. But Wukong found her in the midst of a task rather than a ritual.

She had not dressed for an audience. She looked as though she had come straight from private work, and in her hands was a little basket of purple bamboo. She was making it as a fisher might make a trap for a creature in a pond.

Wukong wanted an explanation. His master had been captured in a river. The fish spirit was guarding its underwater palace. Why was Guanyin weaving a basket rather than ordering her attendants to assemble a splendid rescue?

Guanyin answered by continuing her work. The object she was making was the remedy. The Great King of Numinous Response was not an independent ruler of all those waters. He had once been a goldfish kept in her lotus pond, where he listened to sacred teaching and grew in power. He had escaped, taken a place in the Heaven-Reaching River, and made the villagers worship him with offerings of their children. His copper weapon was tied to the lotus life from which he had come.

The revelation brought no comfort to the villagers who had already given up children. A creature from the pond of a merciful bodhisattva had been using her distance from the world as an opportunity. Guanyin could recognize him; she could call him back; the river's families had not possessed either power. For nine years he had occupied the old turtle's home and demanded a yearly tribute.

Wukong pressed her to hurry. He had heard Xuanzang crying in the chest. Guanyin was already prepared. She took the little bamboo basket and accompanied him toward the river, with no need to delay for a procession or formal appearance.

When they arrived, the people gathered on shore could scarcely believe what they saw. A holy figure, without the grand costume they associated with temple images, stood beside the water holding a fisher's basket. The scene was so concrete that witnesses would remember it long after the monster had gone: the bodhisattva at the bank, the basket in her hands, the river before her.

Guanyin began her summoning. The basket went into the water. The fish spirit who had hidden behind the walls of the underwater palace could not hide from its former keeper. Drawn by her power, the goldfish emerged and was caught in the bamboo work as easily as though it had never left the lotus pond.

The Great King of Numinous Response was a fish again. His rank, his warlike helmet, and the terror he had inspired among villages meant nothing in Guanyin's presence. She lifted him away from the river and took him back toward the southern sea.

The capture did not turn the harm he had caused into a harmless prank. The kingdom beneath the river had been disturbed, lives had been spent, and the palace's other creatures had been made part of his predation. The people who survived would still have to live with the memory of the sacrifices. Yet the source of the yearly demand was finally gone, and Xuanzang could be brought up from his prison.

## The home restored to an old turtle

Bajie and Sha Wujing returned to the underwater palace. Its defenses had lost their master. The disciples opened the stone coffer, found Xuanzang alive, and brought him back to air and daylight. His wet clothes were changed; he was warmed and fed. The Chen family, who had feared that their guests had died on the frozen crossing, welcomed him back as someone restored from the dead.

It was a day of crowded gratitude. The village that had prepared to sacrifice two children now had the children alive and the monster removed. People spoke of building an image of Guanyin as they had seen her, with a basket in her hands. In a story full of magnificent heavenly palaces and enormous weapons, the picture of a simple fisher's tool had become the sign of deliverance.

But the eight-hundred-li river still lay between the travelers and the road west. They had tried to cross on ice and been betrayed. A boat large enough for the company and horse was still difficult to find. Wukong could fly, but Xuanzang could not be carried over such a distance as easily as a piece of baggage without creating another danger.

Then the old white turtle appeared.

For nine years he had lived away from the palace stolen by the goldfish. Now the intruder had been taken away, and the great halls beneath the river could belong to him again. The turtle had heard of the pilgrims' struggle. He knew that they had restored a home he could not reclaim himself, and he wished to repay them.

He could carry them across the river on his back. His shell was wide and strong. His knowledge of the currents surpassed that of any boatman on the bank. A journey that might have taken a vessel days could be accomplished by him without putting the travelers on thin ice or leaving them to drift.

Xuanzang was astonished by the offer. He had traveled through rivers on boats and once been ferried across a dark stream by a dragon family. He had never ridden an old turtle across the breadth of Heaven-Reaching River. But the creature bowed his massive head and invited the four pilgrims and the white horse to climb onto his shell. They arranged their baggage carefully and held on.

The turtle entered the water. The vast stream moved around him. Where the false ice road had cracked under their feet, the living shell carried them securely. The near bank diminished behind them, and for the first time the journey across the river continued without deceit.

As they traveled the old turtle made a request of his own. He had practiced and lived for many years in the water, yet he remained in a turtle's body. If Xuanzang truly reached the Buddha in the western paradise, would he ask when the turtle might cast off that shell and take human form?

Xuanzang was grateful for the ride. He promised that he would ask.

The request was small compared with the demands of the monsters they had met. The turtle wanted knowledge, not tribute; an answer, not a feast. Xuanzang readily agreed, perhaps without considering how many questions and worries the western journey would place upon his mind before he came home.

The turtle carried them to the farther shore. The pilgrims dismounted and thanked him. A long stretch of the road had opened, and the children of the Chen family would not be offered to the Great King again. Xuanzang had been rescued from the stone chest. The old turtle had his home back, and he had placed a question in the monk's keeping.

The travelers took up their belongings and went west. Behind them the river continued to flow as it always had, but its people were no longer obliged to mistake a hungry fish for a god.
