# Journey to the West: Lotus Cave and the sky in a gourd

## A woodcutter with a warning

The pilgrims left Baoxiang Kingdom with their traveling papers stamped and Sun Wukong restored to his place at the front of the party. The king and his officers had seen them beyond the gates. Behind them lay the great city, the rescued princess, the iron cage in which Xuanzang had been made to appear a tiger, and the quarrel that had almost ended the pilgrimage.

Ahead, once again, lay mountains.

The road climbed in the bright weather of spring. Willows were green, flowers had opened, and birds called among the trees. For a while the company could imagine that their restored unity would be enough to carry them west. Xuanzang still worried about the great distances. He had left the Tang court with a vow, but a vow did not make the miles shorter, and every mountain seemed to put another wall between him and the scriptures.

Wukong reminded him of the Heart Sutra learned earlier from the monk in the crow's nest. Fear arose from attachments and confused perceptions, he said. A person with work still to do could not reasonably demand the ease of one who had finished it. If the master wished to be free of all exertion, there would be time for that when the mission was accomplished.

Xuanzang listened and urged the horse forward.

The next ridge rose steeply, with ledges above deep gullies and paths almost swallowed by bushes. The air was still when a figure appeared on a grassy slope below. An elderly woodcutter stood beside a felled tree, his ax in hand.

He called out to the travelers to stop.

There were savage demons in this mountain, he warned. They devoured people coming from the east and west alike. Anyone who entered without caution would find the road very dangerous.

Xuanzang froze on the horse. He wanted to know exactly where the demons lived, how many there were, and whether the company ought to turn aside. Wukong went to question the woodcutter. The man repeated his warning but gave little that could help a traveler plot a safe passage. Wukong returned, reassuring his master that even if there were a few monsters, he would deal with them.

By then the woodcutter had vanished.

Xuanzang pointed out the empty slope. How could an old man with an ax move so quickly? Bajie guessed they had met a ghost walking abroad in the daylight. Wukong pretended the man had simply gone back into the forest, but he lifted his eyes and saw a figure rising among the clouds.

It was the divine officer assigned to guard that day's passage, disguised as a woodcutter to deliver a warning. Wukong flew after him and demanded to know why a messenger of the gods had hidden behind an ordinary man's clothing.

The officer admitted that the danger was real. The monsters in the mountain had powerful transformations and should not be underestimated. Wukong would need careful observation, clever strategy, and a close watch over his master. Without those, the road west might end here.

When Wukong returned, the monk was moving slowly among the stones, attended by Bajie and Sha Wujing. The warning troubled Wukong more than he wished to show. Telling Xuanzang everything might make him so frightened that he could go no farther. Telling him nothing might allow the party to walk straight into the mouth of the trap.

Wukong chose a third course: send Bajie ahead to scout.

## The scout who preferred to sleep

Bajie objected. Why should he always be the one made to go ahead? Wukong was the eldest disciple and had the keenest eyes. Sha had a strong staff. The road was everyone's responsibility. If demons waited in the hills, would it not be wiser to avoid them than to look for them?

Wukong replied that Bajie was entirely suited to the task. He had years of experience in Heaven and a weapon fit for the strongest mountain monster. A short patrol would do him good. Xuanzang, persuaded by the performance of confidence, told Bajie to go and return with news.

Bajie set off muttering.

The mountain path rose and dipped. He had barely gone a few miles before his resentment overflowed. He turned around and addressed the absent travelers as though they stood before him. The master was too easily frightened, Wukong too fond of cleverness, and Sha too willing to carry bags while someone else went into danger. All of them wanted to reach the western paradise; why did that require sending a poor pig to inspect a mountain full of hungry demons?

What was the sensible course? Find a quiet place to sleep for a while, then return and report that he had seen nothing troubling. No one would be harmed, and the expedition could proceed as before.

He found a hollow among the grass and spread his rake along the ground like a makeshift pillow. His eyes closed almost immediately.

What he did not know was that Wukong had followed him in the shape of a tiny insect. The monkey had settled just behind Bajie's ear, small enough to be mistaken for a fleck of dust. He heard the complaint and watched the arrangements for sleep.

Wukong changed into a woodpecker and struck Bajie sharply on the lip.

Bajie woke with a howl. He looked around for the creature that had attacked him, cursed the trees, and hurried away. He was now determined to return with a report sufficiently elaborate to excuse the time he had wasted.

He found three large stones and addressed them as if they were his master and two fellow disciples. To the first he bowed and said he had found a mountain made of stone. To the second he explained that the demons lived in a cave made of stone. To the third he described doors plated with iron. He rehearsed answers to increasingly difficult questions, promising himself that if anyone asked exactly how many nails were in the doors, he would say the excitement had prevented him from counting them.

The real Wukong, once again disguised as an insect, heard the entire performance.

He flew back ahead of Bajie and told Xuanzang to expect a very confident liar. The master scolded him for insulting his brother without proof. Wukong announced the lie in advance, down to the invented stone mountain, stone cave, iron door, and uncounted nails.

Bajie arrived shortly afterward, trying to remember his own rehearsal. He told Xuanzang that the mountain was full of monsters, though the monsters had been most polite. They had offered him food, called him an honored guest, and promised to escort the pilgrims safely through their territory.

Wukong began asking for details.

What was the mountain called?

A stone mountain, Bajie replied.

And the cave?

A stone cave.

The doors?

Iron-plated doors, firmly nailed.

And precisely how many nails were there?

Bajie could not remember.

Wukong finished the answer for him. Bajie stared. He had heard no footsteps behind him, had seen no shadow on the stones, and had been quite sure he was alone.

When Wukong described the sleeping place, the peck on the lip, and the private rehearsal, Bajie begged forgiveness. Wukong threatened to strike him with the iron staff. Xuanzang, now convinced the patrol had been a sham, still asked that the brother be spared. The staff was too heavy for a lesson in honesty. They needed everyone alive to cross the mountain.

Wukong agreed not to beat him, on condition that he go back and actually inspect the road.

Bajie went. This time every noise frightened him. A tiger crossing the path must be Wukong in disguise, he thought. A tree that fell in the wind was surely another prank. When a crow called above him, he shouted up that he had already confessed and would not tell any more lies. Yet Wukong had not followed. Bajie's conscience was now doing the monitoring without the monkey's help.

## The two kings of Lotus Cave

The mountain had a name, Pingding Mountain, often called Flat-Top Mountain. Within it stood Lotus Cave, ruled by two demon brothers: the Gold Horn King and the Silver Horn King.

Gold Horn had heard about the pilgrim from the Tang and knew more than a casual mountain predator. The monk was said to be the incarnation of an exalted disciple whose long discipline had made his body capable of granting longevity to anyone who ate it. Silver Horn asked why they should keep practicing difficult alchemical methods if such a feast would make them immortal at once.

Gold Horn urged caution. The pilgrim was protected by formidable disciples. Better first to identify the company correctly. He produced a picture of the travelers and described them by name: the white-horse rider, the monkey, the pig, and the dark-faced staff-bearer.

Silver Horn took a detachment of demons and went out to find them.

Bajie met the patrol in a narrow place on the road.

He tried to pass as an ordinary traveler. There was nothing about him, he hoped, that demanded an inquiry into his spiritual commitments. The demons lifted the picture and compared it with the large creature standing before them. Silver Horn pointed out a round body, great ears, and a long snout. Bajie tucked his snout against his chest as if that might conceal it.

The monster demanded to see his face. Bajie protested that he had been born this way. Why drag out a man's private misfortunes? When the demons threatened to use a hook, he let the snout emerge and hoped they would be satisfied with the sight.

They were. The picture named him: Zhu Bajie, one of the Tang monk's disciples.

Silver Horn drew his Seven-Star Sword and attacked. Bajie answered with the nine-toothed rake and a speech about its celestial craftsmanship. For a while the two fought almost evenly. Bajie could have continued against one opponent. Silver Horn ordered his followers to join in, and the hillside crowded with attacking demons.

Bajie tried to retreat. He caught his foot in creeping vines, stumbled, recovered, and was tripped again by a lesser demon crouching low to seize his ankles. Several others threw themselves onto him. They gripped his ears and bristles, his legs and tail, and carried him into Lotus Cave.

Silver Horn announced to Gold Horn that he had captured one of the pilgrims.

The elder brother looked at Bajie and was disappointed. This was not the monk they wanted. Bajie immediately agreed. A worthless captive should certainly be released; there was no sense keeping a fellow who could not satisfy their requirements.

Silver Horn had another idea. If Bajie could not grant immortality, his flesh might still make something for the larder. He had him thrown into a clear-water pool so that his coat could be soaked and cleaned. Later, the demon proposed, they could salt him and dry him like meat for the winter.

Bajie protested that he had joined the wrong sort of establishment. These were not monks seeking wisdom, but traders in preserved pork.

He remained in the pool, miserable and helpless, while Silver Horn went back to the mountain.

## The compassionate master and the wounded stranger

Xuanzang, waiting for the overdue scout, again grew anxious. Wukong suggested that Bajie was merely taking his time. The monk worried that they might lose him altogether. Wukong advised moving forward and meeting him farther along the road.

Silver Horn saw the pilgrims approaching from a high place. He watched Wukong draw his staff and perform a few practiced strokes to clear the way. The iron bar flashed in the sun. The monkey moved with the effortless control of someone who had spent centuries using that weapon. The demon's followers pressed for a battle, but Silver Horn recognized the danger. A forceful attack would probably fail. He needed a way to make the pilgrims carry him into their own company.

He sent his servants away and transformed himself into a gray-haired Daoist priest lying wounded beside the road.

When the travelers came near, the old man cried out for help. Xuanzang immediately stopped the horse. A fellow religious practitioner was hurt. How could he ride past?

The stranger said he belonged to a monastery farther west. He and a disciple had been returning from a ritual when a tiger attacked. The animal dragged away the younger man. The priest had stumbled onto stones and injured his leg. Blood ran down his foot. He could not walk back to his residence.

Xuanzang felt that the obligation was plain. Buddhist and Daoist robes might differ, but the duty to preserve life was common to both. The old priest needed help.

Wukong saw the deception immediately. He told the traveler what he was: a mountain demon using a wounded man's appearance to approach the Tang monk. The stranger protested that he was a harmless old priest. Xuanzang, alarmed by the harsh accusation, told his disciple to be kind. Had they not learned how easy it was to mistake appearances? It was not for travelers to leave an injured man on the road because his face displeased them.

The monk offered his horse. The supposed priest said his leg was too badly hurt to sit astride it. Xuanzang suggested that Sha carry him. The stranger recoiled from Sha's face and claimed to be frightened by it. Then Wukong must carry him, said Xuanzang.

Wukong consented with a smile that did not reach his eyes.

He told the old priest to warn him if he needed to relieve himself. Being a demon was no excuse for soiling a pilgrim's robe. The monk rebuked him for speaking rudely to a wounded stranger. The priest climbed onto Wukong's back, and they started west.

Xuanzang and Sha soon went ahead into a hollow. Wukong lingered behind with the stranger. Once his master was out of sight, he considered tossing the demon onto the rocks. Then the weight on his shoulders changed.

## Three mountains on one back

Silver Horn pronounced a spell and summoned Mount Sumeru onto the monkey's shoulders.

The weight was immense. Wukong shifted it toward one side and laughed at the trick. A burden carried squarely was easier than one hung off-center, he told the creature. If this was all the monster could manage, they might as well go on.

Silver Horn summoned Mount Emei onto the other shoulder.

Wukong adjusted again. He was famous for his strength. Carrying two mountains slowed him, but he could still move. He began to follow the monk down the slope.

The demon summoned Mount Tai above him.

The third mountain struck with crushing force. Wukong's legs buckled. The ground took his weight, the earth groaned, and the monkey was pinned beneath the rocks. Blood ran from his mouth and nose. The creature who had once escaped from the Five Elements Mountain with the help of a monk now lay under a mountain spell while that same monk walked away unprotected.

Silver Horn sprang into the air and pursued Xuanzang.

Sha Wujing drew his staff and tried to defend his master. The demon met him with the Seven-Star Sword. Their weapons struck back and forth until Sha began to tire. Silver Horn seized him under one arm, snatched Xuanzang from the horse with the other, hooked up the baggage, and even caught the horse by its mane. He carried the whole company back to Lotus Cave in a rush of wind.

Gold Horn rejoiced at the sight of the captured monk, but he was more cautious than his brother. Had they also secured Sun Wukong? The monkey was too powerful to leave outside while they cooked his master. Silver Horn said that three mountains held Wukong fast. Still Gold Horn ordered the others guarded and delayed the feast until they could be certain.

They lifted Bajie from the water, hung the brothers and their master in different parts of the cave, and tethered the white horse. The luggage went into storage. In a few moments a party that had left Baoxiang whole was reduced to one free disciple, and that disciple was under the weight of three mountains.

Gold Horn asked how best to dispose of him.

Silver Horn suggested sending two quick-witted servants with magical vessels. One was called Jingxigui, the Sharp-Witted Ghost; the other was Linglichong, the Clever Insect. The names promised quick minds. The task they were about to undertake would test that promise.

They were given a purple-gold red gourd and a white jade bottle. To capture Wukong, the demons explained, one need only turn a vessel downward, call his name, and wait for him to answer. If he answered, he would be drawn inside. A sealing charm would then turn the prisoner into liquid. There were other treasures in Lotus Cave, but two would be enough for this errand if the servants used them properly.

The two set out confidently.

## The mountain opens

Under the mountains Wukong lamented his own miscalculation. He had recognized the false priest yet obeyed Xuanzang's instruction to carry him. Once again the monk's compassion had become an opening for a monster. Once again Wukong was separated from the master he had promised to protect.

He remembered the first mountain under which he had been trapped, the one the Buddha had set upon him for his rebellion against Heaven. He had lain there five hundred years before Xuanzang arrived with the power to lift the seal. Now he had scarcely returned to his place beside that monk before another mountain crushed him to the ground.

His distress brought the local mountain gods, earth gods, and heavenly protectors to the scene. The gods had helped move the mountains in response to Silver Horn's spell, without recognizing the victim beneath them. When they learned that the Great Sage Equal to Heaven lay pinned under the rocks, they were frightened. He might blame them for imprisoning him.

They promised to restore the mountains to their proper places if he would forgive them. Wukong agreed. The pressure lifted, and he leaped free, shaking dust from his clothes. Then he ordered the gods to present their legs for a beating.

They protested. Had he not just promised to forgive them?

Wukong complained that the demons had grown powerful enough to command local gods as servants. The earth gods admitted that they were forced to serve at Lotus Cave in shifts. Wukong understood that this was not an ordinary band of hill robbers. Here were monsters whose spells reached the machinery of the mountain itself.

A glow approached along the path. The gods said it came from magical treasures carried by the demon servants. Wukong asked what kind of people the kings favored. Practitioners of Daoist alchemy, they answered, especially wandering adepts.

Wukong smiled. If the demons liked Daoist masters, he could supply one.

He transformed into an elderly religious wanderer, with hair arranged high, patched robes, and a small instrument for marking rhythm as he walked. He settled at the side of the path, just where two servants would be certain to pass.

When the Sharp-Witted Ghost and the Clever Insect arrived, Wukong stretched out his staff to trip them. They fell over it and sprang up ready to scold the stranger. Wukong greeted them as fellow practitioners and explained that a junior ought to bow to an elder. Their anger turned to curiosity. What mountain did he come from? He said he was an immortal from Penglai, one of the legendary island realms of the blessed.

The servants immediately apologized. They had not recognized a senior immortal. Would he perhaps help them gain immortality themselves?

Wukong offered to make a worthy follower into an immortal, then asked where they were going. The pair said they served the kings of Lotus Cave and had been sent to capture the monkey Sun Wukong.

Wukong asked which monkey.

The one escorting the Tang monk, they said. Their second king had trapped him under three mountains. Now they would place him inside one of their magic vessels, seal it, and make him dissolve.

Wukong asked to see the treasures. The servants handed him the gourd and the bottle. For a moment he considered running off with both. He could escape in a single leap. Then he decided that stealing them outright would look bad. The Monkey King preferred to outwit his opponents while letting them believe they had made an excellent bargain.

He returned the treasures and produced a gourd of his own.

It was large, gleaming, and red-gold, shaped from one of Wukong's hairs. The servants admired its appearance but asked what it could do. Their vessels could hold a thousand people, they said. What could the old wanderer's gourd contain?

The whole sky, Wukong replied.

They stared at him. A vessel that held people was one thing. Holding the sky was another. They asked him to prove it.

If he could really pack the heavens into his gourd, they would trade their two treasures for his one. Wukong agreed.

## The sky in a gourd

To make his boast persuasive, Wukong had to borrow more than a monkey hair. He sent a divine messenger to the Jade Emperor with an extraordinary request: would Heaven allow its light to be hidden briefly while he convinced two demons that he had stored the sky in a vessel?

The emperor was not pleased. The Monkey King had already caused enough disruption in the celestial realm. How, the emperor asked, could anyone put the sky in a gourd? It was not a blanket to be folded or a stone to be lifted.

Nezha, the young divine warrior, proposed a practical answer. The sky itself need not be moved. A great dark banner kept by the northern deity Zhenwu could be unfurled across the heavens and hide the light of sun, moon, and stars. To witnesses on the ground, the world would go suddenly black. They would think the old wanderer had done what he claimed.

The emperor allowed it. Nezha borrowed the banner and waited above the southern gate of Heaven.

Below, Wukong told the two servants that they should watch closely.

He tossed the false gourd into the air. Because it was made from a hair, it drifted lightly upward. He muttered a spell. Above the mountains Nezha spread the dark flag.

Day disappeared.

The change was total. The sun vanished, the shadows became darkness, and the demons could no longer see their own hands. The Sharp-Witted Ghost reached for his companion. The Clever Insect demanded to know where the old immortal had gone. Wukong, standing directly beside them, answered calmly that the sky had gone into the gourd, so of course there could be no daylight left outside.

The servants were terrified. If this was the edge of the world, they might step into the sea and fall for days. Wukong encouraged their misunderstanding, telling them to stand perfectly still. They begged him to let the sky out again.

He made a show of performing another spell. Nezha rolled up the flag. The sun returned as abruptly as it had vanished. The mountain was bright and familiar. The two demons were overwhelmed by admiration.

A treasure that could hold a thousand men was useful, they agreed, but a treasure that could swallow the heavens was beyond price. They exchanged the purple-gold red gourd and the jade bottle for Wukong's hair-made imitation.

The monkey, pleased with the bargain, even proposed drawing up a written contract so that no one could later complain of having been cheated. There was no brush or paper, the servants said. They would swear an oath instead. Whoever regretted the bargain could suffer illness through all four seasons.

Wukong accepted. The oath was made. The servants departed delighted with their new possession, confident they had traded up from two vessels that held human beings to one that could hold the sky.

The Monkey King leaped into the clouds and thanked Nezha for his part in the deception. The divine warrior returned the borrowed banner to its keeper. Wukong turned to watch the two small figures moving along the path below, carrying a gourd that would cease to be wondrous as soon as its maker wished.

Inside Lotus Cave his master and brothers remained prisoners. There were more treasures yet to account for, and two kings who had every reason to grow suspicious when their servants returned. Wukong held two of their weapons, but he had not recovered the people he had come to save.

The battle for Lotus Cave was only beginning.
