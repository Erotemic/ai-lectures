# Jane Eyre I: the complete story

## Jane Eyre — Story: Gateshead and the Red Room

A child sits apart from the family that has taken her in. Outside, rain blows against the windows. Inside, Mrs. Reed and her children gather comfortably, but Jane Eyre is excluded. She has found a book with pictures of distant, cold places. Its images give her a little world she can inhabit without asking anyone's permission. Then her cousin John Reed finds her, calls her dependent and inferior, and strikes her with the book. Jane fights back. The adults punish Jane.

That reversal tells us what kind of world Charlotte Brontë is going to examine. Jane is an orphan in the household of her dead uncle's widow. Her uncle had asked his wife to care for her. But being a relation, being a child, and being entirely without money give Jane no secure claim to affection. The cousins treat the house as theirs. Mrs. Reed reads Jane's anger as a defect of character rather than as a response to what has been done to her. Jane is sharp-tempered, proud, and sometimes frighteningly vehement. The narrative does not ask us to pretend she is a little saint. It asks us to attend to the difference between her behavior and the system of favoritism in which it occurs.

Mrs. Reed orders Jane confined in the red room. It is the room where Mr. Reed died, furnished solemnly and seldom used. The room's cold grandeur weighs upon a child already afraid that nobody will defend her. In the mirror she glimpses a small, strange figure. She imagines her dead uncle returning to judge the broken promise made at his bedside. She calls out, terrified, but Mrs. Reed refuses to believe the terror is real. Jane loses consciousness.

The red room is more than a Gothic setting. It puts a dependent person inside the family memorial, under the authority of the dead benefactor whose protection she can no longer invoke. The adults have decided what Jane is: an ungrateful burden. Because they control the doors, their interpretation has immediate physical force. Yet this is also Jane's own retrospective account. The grown narrator remembers the child's sensations with precision, while trying to understand the machinery of her humiliation. The distance between the frightened child and the woman who can tell the story will become one of the novel's great resources.

Jane recovers in the care of Bessie, a servant who can be kind but is not consistently able to protect her. Mr. Lloyd, an apothecary summoned after her collapse, treats Jane's misery as more than a medical episode. He asks why she is unhappy and suggests that school may offer a change. Jane does not imagine school will be easy. She imagines that a different place might at least give her a chance to become someone other than the person the Reeds insist she is.

The candidate school is Lowood Institution, administered by the Reverend Mr. Brocklehurst. When he comes to interview her, Mrs. Reed tells him that Jane has a tendency to deceit and must be watched. Brocklehurst speaks sternly about sin, humility, and the punishment awaiting wicked children. He is concerned that a child's character should be subdued before she learns to expect comfort. He hardly knows her. The accusation will follow Jane into the next house because the adults who judge her have access to institutions, while she has only her voice.

Before Jane leaves, her anger finally takes an articulate form. She tells Mrs. Reed that she is not deceitful, that she has been treated cruelly, and that she will not pretend gratitude. The victory is real but brief. Once the speech ends, Jane is still a small girl with no money, still going to a school chosen by somebody else. Brontë refuses to make a bold declaration magically solve material dependence. At the same time, the words matter. Jane has rejected an account of herself that an adult was trying to make permanent.

Later readers often call this a coming-of-age novel. But its first act is almost a legal and moral dispute over testimony. Who may describe the child? Who will be believed? Can a person denied status become the author of her own story? The book's first-person voice is the answer in progress. Jane's life will include love, religious arguments, hunger, work, and temptation, but all those later scenes begin with this: a child in a locked room, refusing to accept that someone else's convenience determines her worth.

## Jane Eyre — Story: Lowood, Helen, and Miss Temple

Jane arrives at Lowood during winter. The girls rise early, wash in cold rooms, and eat food so badly prepared that hunger becomes part of their education. Clothes and shoes fail to keep out the weather. The school claims to train poor girls in Christian modesty. In practice, the lessons often confuse humility with deprivation. To survive here Jane must learn habits she did not learn at Gateshead: endurance, study, friendship, and a way of judging religious language by the conduct of those who use it.

The first person to interest her deeply is Helen Burns, a pupil who reads while others play and bears punishment with what Jane finds almost impossible patience. Helen is scolded by Miss Scatcherd for untidiness and lapses of attention, although Jane can see both her intelligence and her goodness. Jane wants Helen to resist, to protest when an accusation is unfair. Helen thinks that retaliation may reproduce the harm. She speaks of a soul whose value cannot be measured by the insults of one teacher. They disagree without losing their affection. The novel lets a child put serious ethical questions to another child.

A different model appears in Miss Temple, the superintendent. She is not merely gentle. She creates spaces where accuracy and justice are possible. She offers food when the girls' meal is inedible; she questions what she has been told about pupils instead of accepting an accusation from authority as proof. When Mr. Brocklehurst visits, he rebukes the institution's small comforts and deplores the girls' hair as evidence of vanity. At the same time, members of his own family appear richly dressed. Brontë makes the contrast visible without requiring a lecture from the narrator. A language of sacrifice protects the comfort of those with power.

Brocklehurst publicly displays Jane and calls her a liar before the school. He intends the shame to isolate her. Jane is devastated because she knows what it means to be assigned a false identity. Helen consoles her, but Helen does not declare that society's opinion is harmless. The pain is real. Miss Temple then hears Jane's account, makes inquiries, and publicly clears her name. This correction matters because it is an act of responsible authority. It also reveals the danger of a system in which a child's reputation can be destroyed by one adult's unexamined word.

Lowood's conditions soon become deadly. Disease spreads through the school. The sick are separated, staff and supporters begin to recognize the institution's failures, and its regime is eventually reformed under new oversight. But the epidemic has already taken its toll. Helen Burns is dying, not of the fever that has spread among many girls, but of consumption. Jane finds her in the night and lies beside her. They speak of death and the possibility of another life. Helen answers with trust; Jane still has questions. When morning comes, Helen is dead.

The force of the scene does not come from making the girls' faith identical. Jane loves Helen, and grief cannot be resolved by instantly adopting Helen's theology. Helen's willingness to forgive remains both a sustaining example and a challenge to Jane's instinct for self-defense. Later in the book another devout character will ask Jane to renounce her personal desires in the name of a mission. We will need to remember that religious conviction has already appeared in several forms here, not as one uniform command.

For eight years Jane stays at Lowood: six as a pupil and two as a teacher. Under Miss Temple's guidance she acquires skills, discipline, and a measure of calm. When Miss Temple marries and leaves, Jane realizes that her serenity has depended partly on the presence of a trusted person. She does not despise her work, but she wants a larger experience of the world. She advertises for a position as governess. The letter she receives offers a post at Thornfield Hall, teaching a little French girl.

This is a quiet but decisive movement. At Gateshead Jane's future was decided for her; at Lowood she has learned how to make herself employable. Now she takes a practical step toward an unknown household, rather than waiting for a rescuer. The novel has not made her rich, and it has not made her free from the demands of employers. It has given her a small earning power and a habit of judgment. Those will be tested when her new employer becomes the person she loves.

## Jane Eyre — Story: Thornfield and Its Secrets

Thornfield Hall initially offers Jane a startling kind of peace. Mrs. Fairfax, the housekeeper, welcomes her warmly, and her pupil Adèle Varens is lively, French-speaking, and sometimes difficult to keep attentive. Jane designs lessons and settles into the work of a governess. She lives within a great house, but not as a member of the family who owns it. She is educated enough to converse with its master and paid to remain useful to a child. That intermediate position will make every later scene at Thornfield a negotiation of proximity and distance.

The owner, Edward Rochester, is away when Jane arrives. Mrs. Fairfax knows his habits but does not claim to understand him completely. The house contains rooms and passages that Jane has yet to know. From an upper storey she hears a strange laugh and is told it belongs to Grace Poole, a servant who sews there. A plain explanation is offered, but the sound belongs to a different register from the children's lessons and Mrs. Fairfax's household conversation. In a Gothic novel, a hidden noise invites a hidden story. In this novel, a hidden story also raises the question of who is permitted to know what happens inside a rich man's house.

One winter evening Jane walks toward the village to post a letter. A horse and rider come along the road; the horse slips on ice and throws its rider. Jane helps the brusque stranger to his feet. Only after she returns does she discover that he is Rochester. She has met him first as a person in a difficulty, not as the employer whose money supports her. That accidental reversal shapes their early conversations. Rochester is older, wealthy, at times rude and manipulative. Jane can be shy, but she does not flatter him. He discovers that she will answer a question directly, even when it would be easier to produce the polite answer an employer expects.

Rochester examines Jane's drawings and asks about her past. His interest feels more serious than ordinary social courtesy. Yet he alternates candor with evasion. He tells her fragments of his history with Adèle's mother, Céline Varens, and explains why he has undertaken responsibility for the girl without offering a simple guarantee of biological parenthood. A painful or compromising past is part of his self-presentation. He wants Jane to understand that he is not a conventionally admirable gentleman. At the same time, he maintains control over how much she hears.

Jane's own inner life becomes more expansive here. When she stands high in the house looking across the fields, she reflects that human beings, including women, need action and the exercise of their powers. She cannot accept that her small daily duties should exhaust her desires. Brontë does not make the longing contemptuous of Adèle or of teaching. It is a claim that useful labor need not be the entirety of a person's mental existence. Jane can love her pupil and still want a world beyond the schoolroom.

Then a fire begins in Rochester's chamber during the night. Jane wakes to a suspicious sound, discovers flames spreading around his bed, and throws water over him and the burning curtains. Rochester survives because she acts. He orders her to keep the incident private, disappears to investigate the upper floor, and later supplies an explanation involving Grace Poole. Jane sees that Grace has not been dismissed. The explanation and the master's behavior do not fit neatly together.

Jane begins to love Rochester, and she also begins to distrust her own interpretation of his interest. He talks with her in a way that makes difference of age and rank temporarily disappear, then behaves with a freedom that only the master of the house possesses. He can summon her and dismiss her. He can withhold information she cannot discover easily. She must bear the consequences of a feeling she has no right to expect him to reciprocate.

Thornfield is therefore not simply a romantic castle with an eerie attic. It is a workplace in which affection crosses boundaries that wealth and privacy maintain. A governess hears a laugh, saves her employer's life, and receives partial explanations. The reader sees Jane's courage and curiosity, but not what Rochester has chosen to hide. The mystery is already a moral problem before anyone names it.

## Jane Eyre — Story: Visitors, Gateshead, and the Masquerade

Rochester returns to Thornfield with an aristocratic party. The company includes Blanche Ingram, accomplished, handsome, and apparently the woman Rochester intends to marry. Jane must be present in the drawing room at times because Adèle is there, yet she occupies neither the position of guest nor that of family. She watches people who can discuss governesses with condescension while a governess listens. She also watches Rochester seem to court Blanche. In Jane's mind, the conclusion is painful and plausible: a man who owns a great estate is about to choose a woman of his own class.

Rochester encourages this assumption. He performs the role of suitor, drawing out Blanche's pride and exposing the importance she attaches to money and status. But the result is not simple proof that Jane ought to trust him instead. He is staging a social experiment without explaining it to the person most deeply affected. He knows more than Jane does about his intentions. Jane has to interpret his performances from a vulnerable position. The gap between what he knows and what she knows is increasing just as their intimacy appears to deepen.

A strange visitor arrives at the party disguised as a fortune-telling woman. Several young ladies consult her. When Jane speaks to the visitor, the conversation becomes invasive: the supposed fortune-teller reads her guarded emotions and presses for signs of love. Jane resists the performance, and Rochester finally reveals that he himself has worn the disguise. The scene can be playful in recollection, but it is also another experiment in unequal access to the truth. He can hide his identity and interrogate her feelings. She has had no equivalent opportunity to question him honestly about his plans.

Another visitor makes the hidden story darker. Richard Mason arrives from the West Indies. Rochester reacts with visible distress. During the night, a scream sounds in the house, and Jane is summoned to attend Mason, who is bleeding from a wound in an upper room. Rochester orders Jane to remain silent while he goes for medical help. Mason has been attacked, and yet Rochester insists on secrecy about the person who attacked him. He arranges for Mason's departure. Jane's courage and discretion earn his gratitude, but they do not bring her closer to a full account. Readers can remember the strange laugh attributed to Grace Poole, the fire, and now this wound. The incidents are connected, although their explanation is withheld.

Meanwhile the past calls Jane away. News comes that her cousin John Reed has ruined himself and died, and that Mrs. Reed is gravely ill. Jane travels back to Gateshead. The adult daughter of Lowood is not the child who left it. She can now see her aunt's household without having to accept its judgment as final. She attempts a reconciliation; Mrs. Reed remains hardened. But the visit reveals a withheld letter from Jane's paternal uncle, John Eyre, who had wished to adopt her and provide for her. Mrs. Reed falsely told him that Jane had died. Even after years have passed, the old household has continued to affect Jane's material prospects through its control of information.

Jane returns to Thornfield with a mixture of relief and foreboding. She thinks Rochester will marry Blanche and send her away to work elsewhere. She knows that loving him cannot give her a claim over him, and she would rather find another position than become an object of pity. What she does not know is that Rochester has been devising a different outcome, while concealing the obstacle that may destroy it.

This portion of the novel is built out of masquerades: the drawing-room courtship, the fortune-teller's costume, the official explanation of nocturnal violence, and the aunt's lie about a dead child. They differ in gravity, but all force Jane to make choices with incomplete evidence. A story about finding a voice must also become a story about discovering when another person's speech cannot be trusted.

## Jane Eyre — Story: Proposal, Wedding, and Flight

In the garden at Thornfield, Rochester tells Jane that she must soon leave because he is to marry Blanche Ingram. Jane has prepared herself to hear this, but preparation cannot prevent her grief. Pressed to speak, she breaks through the class distinctions that have confined their conversations. She insists that her lack of beauty, money, and connections does not mean she possesses less feeling or less soul. Her words turn a scene of apparent dismissal into a declaration of equal human standing. Rochester then says that he has not intended to marry Blanche: it is Jane he loves and wants to marry.

Jane is happy, incredulous, and wary. She has been made to believe in an approaching separation, and now the future is suddenly reversed. She asks questions. Rochester must repeat himself. Eventually she accepts the proposal. That night a storm splits the great chestnut tree in the garden. The event is not a mechanical prediction that the marriage must fail. It is an image that makes it impossible to hear the lovers' promise without a note of divided life. The novel has been teaching us to look closely at gaps between visible happiness and concealed facts.

As the wedding approaches, Rochester tries to adorn Jane with jewels and expensive clothes. Jane resists the transformation. She does not want to become a doll who displays the wealth of the man she is about to marry. She insists on continuing the daily role and identity she knows, even while she longs for the new life. Mrs. Fairfax, startled by the sudden engagement, worries about disparities of age and fortune. Her warnings may be conventional, but they make a point Rochester's ardor cannot erase: Jane's social and economic dependence has not vanished because a rich employer has declared love.

The house offers one more warning. On the eve of the wedding, Jane wakes to a figure in her room. The stranger examines her bridal veil, puts it on, and tears it. Jane faints. Rochester attributes the event to Grace Poole, as he has tried to account for earlier disturbances. His explanation asks Jane to set aside the alarming face she remembers. He tells her enough to soothe her without telling the truth.

At the church the marriage ceremony is interrupted. A lawyer named Briggs and Richard Mason assert that Rochester already has a wife. The claim is not a rumor from a jealous rival. Rochester admits it. Bertha Mason, whom he married years earlier in Jamaica, is alive and is kept under supervision in the upper rooms of Thornfield. He takes the party to see her. The concealed person behind the laugh, the fire, the wound, and the torn veil is his first wife. Bertha appears through Jane's terrified description and through Rochester's bitter account of his earlier life; the narrative grants her no comparable opportunity to tell her history.

Rochester explains that his father and brother had helped arrange a wealthy marriage, that he discovered after the wedding what he describes as Bertha's serious illness and violence, and that he has suffered in the marriage ever since. Those are his claims and his interpretation of events. They explain his resentment; they do not make his wife legally nonexistent or give Jane the information she needed to consent to a marriage. His proposed ceremony would have been bigamous. The lover who speaks to Jane as an equal has hidden a fact that places her future at risk.

Rochester urges Jane not to leave him. He offers a life together abroad, asking her to disregard the legal impossibility of marriage. She loves him and is desperately tempted. Brontë does not make the temptation trivial. Jane understands what abandoning him will cost her. She also understands that remaining as his dependent companion, after being deceived about his existing marriage, would violate her own sense of self-respect. She refuses a relationship whose terms are being set by Rochester's desire and resources. Her decision is not a declaration that she feels nothing. It is a decision that feeling must not extinguish conscience.

Before dawn Jane leaves Thornfield without a farewell. She has very little money and no secure destination. The person who has narrated a long struggle for independence now chooses a freedom that may kill her. This is a devastating pivot: the grand proposal cannot count as the end of Jane's education. The test of her autonomy arrives when love and financial survival point one way, and her own judgment points another.

## Jane Eyre — Story: Moor House and an Unexpected Family

Jane leaves Thornfield with her decision intact and almost nothing else. She takes a coach as far as her money permits, then discovers that she has lost the small bundle containing her remaining possessions. She wanders through an unfamiliar district, seeks food and shelter, and encounters the humiliation of asking strangers for help. Some refuse or distrust her. The story does not soften the consequences of acting on principle. An independent decision does not produce instant financial independence. Jane is near starvation before she reaches a house whose occupants may help her.

The house is called Moor House, or Marsh End. Its inhabitants are Diana and Mary Rivers and their brother, St. John Rivers, a clergyman. The sisters are sympathetic, but St. John is initially suspicious of a stranger who will not tell him her full history. Jane calls herself Jane Elliott. She wants shelter and work, not disclosure of a past she can scarcely bear to name. The household eventually accepts her, and she begins to recover. Unlike Thornfield, where warmth depended on a rich employer's favor, Moor House offers an unexpected friendship with women who share Jane's intellectual interests.

Diana and Mary give Jane conversation, reading, and a sense of belonging. Their brother is more difficult. St. John is disciplined, ambitious, and determined to enter the missionary field in India. He sees life in terms of duty and vocation. He admires in Jane qualities of self-command and competence that could make her an effective assistant, but he is disinclined to treat private happiness as a sufficient reason for action. We have met religious conviction before in Helen Burns and Miss Temple. Here its vocabulary may demand a very different sacrifice.

St. John helps Jane find a position teaching at a small village school. The work is modest, and Jane at first experiences its loss of social and intellectual range as painful. Nevertheless, she takes it seriously. She learns the names and capacities of her pupils, recognizes effort, and gains the respect of families who had little reason to expect a stranger to care. Brontë does not turn every rural girl into a charming emblem of simplicity. It matters that Jane's new life consists partly of ordinary, competent work. She is rebuilding an existence she can sustain without Rochester's money.

St. John's own future is complicated by Rosamond Oliver, a wealthy and beautiful young woman whose affection for him is clear. He is attracted to her but refuses the possibility of marriage because it seems incompatible with his intended mission. Jane observes the struggle and sees his ability to discipline himself almost to the point of denying part of his own humanity. What she admires in his will is inseparable from what she fears in its severity.

Then a coincidence of names reveals an inheritance. St. John has come across Jane's real identity; the uncle who left her a letter years ago, John Eyre of Madeira, has died and left her a fortune of twenty thousand pounds. Just as important, Jane discovers that Diana, Mary, and St. John are her cousins. Their mother was related to her father. Jane has suddenly acquired more money than she has ever imagined possessing and a family whose affection she is free to return.

She decides to divide the inheritance equally among the four cousins. Each will receive five thousand pounds. This is not a vow of poverty. It is a deliberate redistribution that makes the new bond more than a matter of gratitude owed by a helpless visitor. The sisters' prospects improve; Jane keeps enough to live without selling her future to an employer or husband. The same woman who could once be called a dependent has become someone able to provide for others and choose where she belongs.

For much of the novel money has traveled in the hands of people who can conceal letters, appoint teachers, or prescribe a marriage. Now it enters Jane's hands. The change does not decide whom she will love, but it alters what a love decision can mean. She no longer has to choose between moral judgment and starvation. A second proposal is coming, however, and it will test a different kind of independence: whether the language of duty can exert as much pressure as the language of passion.

## Jane Eyre — Story: St. John, Ferndean, and the Ending

St. John Rivers has made Jane an offer that is also a demand. He wants her to marry him and accompany him to India as a missionary's wife. He does not pretend to be in love with her. He believes her discipline and intelligence would make her useful in the labor to which he has pledged his life. Jane is willing to work beside him as a sister or colleague, but she refuses marriage without mutual love. St. John will not accept the distinction. In his view, a proper religious vocation can require the sacrifice of personal desire; in hers, a vow as intimate as marriage cannot be made merely for efficiency.

The force of his argument frightens Jane partly because she respects him. Rochester offered passion and concealed the obstacle to lawful marriage. St. John offers lawful marriage and would extinguish the passion and companionship Jane believes marriage ought to contain. The alternatives are not symmetrical, but both men ask her to submit a central part of her life to their designs. Jane experiences the pressure as a temptation to surrender her own inward judgment in the name of holiness. Her survival at Thornfield required her to say no to a man she loved. Here she must say no to a man who speaks confidently of moral obligation.

At a moment of desperate uncertainty, Jane hears Rochester's voice calling her name, although he is far away. She answers. The novel does not give an external witness who can explain how this occurred. Jane later learns that Rochester experienced a corresponding cry. A reader can take the episode as a spiritual communication, as a form of narrative coincidence, or as a scene deliberately placed between religious conviction and private longing. What matters to the plot is that it breaks St. John's hold on her deliberation. Jane decides to learn what has happened to Rochester, not merely to return unquestioningly to his former house.

She travels back to Thornfield and finds it a ruin. Bertha Mason set fire to the house and died after leaping from the roof. Rochester tried to rescue those inside and was severely injured. He has lost a hand and his sight. He is living away from Thornfield at Ferndean, a secluded house in the woods. The discovery is terrible, not a tidy lesson inflicted by providence so that a proud man may deserve marriage. A woman has died, a household has been destroyed, and Rochester's injuries are permanent consequences of a disaster connected to years of confinement and concealment. None of that should be reduced to a convenient punishment that frees the lovers.

Jane goes to Ferndean. The reunion is affectionate, awkward, and intimate. Rochester cannot at first believe Jane has truly returned. She has come voluntarily, with resources and a family of her own; she has not been sent back as a governess. He is no longer the employer who could arrange her future, but he remains a man with a history of deception and a need for care. Jane can now describe her choices without having to bargain for shelter. When he again asks her to marry him, there is no hidden wife. Their legal and economic circumstances have changed.

Jane narrates the marriage in a sentence famous for putting the decision in the woman's own voice: 'Reader, I married him.' Ten years later, she reports that they have lived together happily. Rochester has regained enough sight in one eye to see their first child. Diana and Mary have married, and Adèle has been placed in a better school and remains loved by Jane. Jane's inheritance and friendships have not disappeared into her marriage. The ending tries to imagine love as daily companionship rather than as a dazzling rescue.

Yet the novel does not end with the newlyweds. Its final pages return to St. John, still working in India, accepting the possibility of an early death in service of his calling. Jane admires his commitment even though she could not join him as his wife. Why close on his religious expectation rather than the happiness at Ferndean? Perhaps because the book has never offered one approved way of living. Helen Burns, St. John, Rochester, and Jane have made radically different judgments about suffering, duty, love, and fulfillment. The first-person narrator can tell us where her path led without claiming that every difficult moral question has been settled.

Jane began inside a family that denied her right to speak for herself. She ends not merely loved but able to tell the story of decisions she made and their consequences. That is why the novel's account of marriage depends so much on all the chapters before the wedding. Jane does not become a person because Rochester chooses her. The union can be meaningful to her because she has already fought to become a person who can choose.
