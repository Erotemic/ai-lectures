# The Decameron · Day 1: frame and the day’s complete tales

## The Decameron — Story: Florence, the Plague, and the Ten Storytellers

It is Florence in the year 1348. Before Giovanni Boccaccio lets anyone tell a story about love or money or a well-timed joke, he asks us to walk through a city in which ordinary life is coming apart. A deadly pestilence has arrived. People fall ill, families disappear, and the habits by which a community cares for its sick and buries its dead begin to fail. Physicians have no reliable remedy. Some people withdraw into careful seclusion; others throw themselves into pleasure; others leave. The bodies of the dead are carried away with less ceremony than anyone would once have thought possible. This is Boccaccio's literary account of the Black Death, not a modern epidemiological report. He records both physical symptoms and the social terror that makes neighbors turn away from one another.

Then he asks us to enter a church: Santa Maria Novella. Seven young women have gathered there. Their names, as the book gives them, are Pampinea, Fiammetta, Filomena, Emilia, Lauretta, Elissa, and Neifile. Pampinea proposes an escape. Why remain in Florence, exposed to danger and despair, when they could withdraw for a time to a well-supplied country estate? The others ask practical questions. Is it safe or seemly for women of their station to travel alone? Will people condemn them? A proposal to save one's life must still negotiate custom and reputation.

Three young men arrive: Panfilo, Filostrato, and Dioneo. They know the women and agree to join them. Servants will accompany the group, prepare food, and maintain the household. The seven women and three men, the company sometimes called the brigata, leave the damaged city for gardens, fields, and rooms that can be kept in order. The difference is deliberate. Outside, burials and ordinary obligations have collapsed; inside, the company will plan its days and keep a shared rule. Boccaccio does not present a world without class distinctions: the servants' labor makes the privileged storytellers' leisure possible.

Pampinea proposes that they appoint a ruler for each day. The ruler will decide the shape of their leisure, where they gather, and eventually what kind of stories they tell. The office passes between women and men. They may sing, walk, dance, dine, rest in the heat, and tell stories when the day is cooler. To protect their enjoyment from becoming chaos, the rules are as important as the pleasures. The book will count ten storytelling days within roughly a fortnight. Friday and Saturday are not storytelling days in the group's schedule; the days are not simply ten consecutive evenings.

For the first storytelling day Pampinea herself is queen. She gives the ten narrators freedom to tell on any subject. Later days will bring specified themes, but the first opens a wide door: religious hypocrisy, clever answers, hospitality, vanity, danger, and the strange relation between what people are and what other people believe. Every speaker will tell a tale; they will also listen, react, and choose the next ruler. Dioneo will acquire a special privilege of speaking last and sometimes departing from the theme. The tales belong to a book with its own society, not to an anonymous pile of anecdotes.

Boccaccio's proem addresses women whose loves and restrictions leave them in need of distraction and consolation. That dedication belongs to the world of the book: it is a rhetorical gesture by an author, not proof that every story expresses a single moral view. The company will disagree through its tales. A joke that saves one person may injure another. A happy ending may depend on an injustice the teller does not regret. And yet the act of gathering, listening, and making a livable day out of a broken time is itself an answer to the opening catastrophe. The first voice belongs to Panfilo, and his tale concerns a man so thoroughly dishonest that his last confession becomes his greatest deception.

## The Decameron — Day One, Tale 1: Ciappelletto and the False Saint

Panfilo begins with a warning: people who pray to God through a saint cannot always know whether the saint they invoke deserved the name. That thought prepares a story in which a scoundrel becomes the object of devotion. A wealthy merchant, Musciatto Franzesi, needs a hard man to collect debts for him in Burgundy. He chooses a notary known as Ser Ciappelletto. If the narrator's account is to be believed, Ciappelletto is expert at lying under oath, stirring quarrels, committing violence, and treating every ordinary standard of honesty as an obstacle to his work. He is not one more sinner who happens to have made mistakes; he has cultivated deceit as a profession.

In Burgundy he stays with two Florentine brothers who lend money. He becomes dangerously ill. The brothers discover that their guest's life has reached a crisis which threatens their household. If he dies without confession, the church may refuse him burial. If he makes a truthful confession to a friar, the enormity of his offenses might make the priest reject him, with the same damaging result. The brothers already work in a trade that exposes them to public suspicion. They dread the violence a scandal might bring upon their property. From his sickbed, Ciappelletto hears their problem and promises to solve it.

A respected friar is summoned. Ciappelletto speaks as if the last hours of his life have awakened his conscience. He claims to have lived with astonishing purity. His supposed sins are almost absurdly small: he once failed to return a trifling excess payment promptly, he may have observed a holy day imperfectly, he once spat in a church. As the friar tries to reassure him, Ciappelletto grows more distressed. At last he weeps over the terrible secret of having cursed his mother when he was a child. The friar is overwhelmed by such delicate remorse. He has asked a man to reveal the gravest offenses of his life and received, instead, a performance carefully calibrated to make ordinary human faults appear unbearable.

The brothers listen from the adjoining room. They know the entire scene is a sham. Their laughter is restrained only by the danger of discovery. The friar, who has no access to the same information, hears a man so evidently contrite that he gives him absolution. Ciappelletto requests burial in the friars' church. He soon dies, and the brothers arrange the solemn funeral. The friar tells the townspeople that an extraordinary holy man has passed among them. Details of the invented confession become proofs of sanctity. The body attracts visitors, the grave attracts candles and petitions, and the sinner is honored as San Ciappelletto.

A simple comic ending would leave the whole matter at the friar's gullibility. Panfilo will not quite do that. He wonders whether people who pray sincerely, even through an unworthy intermediary, might still be heard by God. Ciappelletto's deception cannot undo the possible sincerity of those who seek help. That is not a declaration that the fraud was harmless. It makes a harder question: if a religious community builds its judgments on performances it cannot verify, how much of the institution's authority belongs to truth and how much to convincing testimony? The book's first saint is made by storytelling, and the reader knows the story behind the story.

## The Decameron — Day One, Tale 2: Abraham in Rome

Neifile tells of two friends in Paris. Giannotto is a Christian merchant who wants his friend Abraham, a Jew, to join his religion. He presses him with repeated arguments and encouragement. Abraham, unwilling to accept a new faith merely on another man's recommendation, announces that he will travel to Rome and look at the people responsible for governing the Christian church. He will judge the religion by what he sees at its center.

Giannotto is alarmed. He knows stories about the greed, vanity, sexual misconduct, and bargaining that flourish around the papal court. The journey, he fears, will be disastrous for his purpose. If Abraham wants an example of holy Christian life, Rome is not where Giannotto would send him. But Abraham is firm. He leaves, sees the behavior of the clergy for himself, and returns. Giannotto asks how the visit went, expecting to hear that his friend has rejected the whole proposal. Abraham's report is devastating. The men whose office should demand exemplary conduct seem, in his telling, occupied with worldly appetites and mutual corruption.

Then comes the reversal. Abraham says he wishes to become a Christian. Giannotto can scarcely believe it. Abraham explains that a religion with leaders so harmful to its own supposed values should have collapsed long ago if it were maintained by human management alone. Its survival, despite the behavior of those charged with sustaining it, persuades him that it must have divine support. Giannotto's worst evidence has become, by Abraham's reasoning, the strongest possible argument for conversion.

It is a feat of comic logic, but we should hear both its sharpness and its limits. The tale is not Boccaccio's proof that religions are equally valid, nor an independent account of what medieval Jewish travelers thought of Rome. It is a Christian conversion story that places words in the mouth of an imagined Jewish merchant. Within that framework, the Jewish character is observant, intellectually serious, and able to draw a surprising conclusion. The clergy, not Abraham, bear the main force of the satire. The story's claim about divine preservation belongs to its speaker and narrative design; a listener does not have to accept it as a conclusion of history.

Neifile's story also deepens the question raised by Panfilo. In the first tale, a false confession made a false saint, and the sincerity of worshippers was left unresolved. Here, defective churchmen unexpectedly become witnesses to the strength of the religion they fail to practice. The author uses the difference between institutional claims and institutional conduct as comic material. Giannotto gets the result he desired, but not through the arguments he expected to succeed. The friend who inspected the center of authority has judged it corrupt and then chosen to join it. The paradox is the story, and its discomfort is part of the pleasure and the argument.

## The Decameron — Day One, Tale 3: Melchisedech and the Three Rings

Saladin, a ruler with pressing financial needs, hears that a Jewish moneylender named Melchisedech possesses great wealth. He could ask for a loan directly; instead he considers a more profitable trap. Summon the man, demand an answer on a question that will offend someone powerful whatever he says, then use the offense to seize his money. The question is which of three faiths—Judaism, Christianity, or Islam—is true. Saladin counts on having the authority to punish an answer he dislikes.

Melchisedech recognizes the danger. No speech that simply praises his own faith, or dismisses the ruler's, will be safe. Rather than claim to settle the question, he offers a story. A rich man has a beautiful and precious ring which he intends to pass to the son he favors most. For generations this ring has served as the token by which a father identifies his chosen heir. At last the ring comes to a father who loves three sons equally. Each son has reason to hope he will inherit. Each has received assurances he believes. The father secretly has two convincing copies made, and at his death each son receives a ring.

Now all three possess what appears to be the family treasure. Each can tell a plausible story about his right to it. But the rings are so alike that no observer can reliably say which is the original. So it is, Melchisedech says, with the religions whose followers each believe that the true inheritance has descended to them. He has given Saladin an answer without choosing a target for punishment. A demand for certainty has been met by a parable about the limits of available evidence.

Saladin understands that his planned test has failed. Instead of taking offense, he admires the speaker's intelligence and abandons the trick. He explains that he needs money. Melchisedech lends it, and the ruler repays him honorably, rewarding the prudence that saved him. The danger to the Jewish merchant's wealth has been turned into a relationship grounded, at least by the end, in open negotiation rather than intimidation.

The rings do not establish that the three religions have identical teachings. That is not how a parable works. They expose the difficulty of settling a question by asking a vulnerable person to make a declaration under threat. The most important change in the story is not that every disagreement disappears, but that Saladin stops using unequal power to force an answer. His capacity to recognize wit becomes an element of his greatness. Melchisedech's eloquence protects his life and fortune.

This story has a long afterlife, including later versions that give the ring parable a more explicit argument for religious toleration. Here it remains shaped by danger, wealth, and the uneven position of the speakers. The first two stories of the day put Jewish and Christian figures into tense conversation; the third brings a Muslim ruler into the scene. All three ask whether a person's official standing reveals the truth about that person's judgment. Melchisedech survives because he can tell a story that Saladin would rather admire than punish.

## The Decameron — Day One, Tale 4: The Monk and the Abbot

Dioneo's turn takes us from theological puzzles to the practical problem of an authority who is guilty of the same conduct he punishes. At a monastery, a young monk becomes involved with a woman and brings her secretly into his room. The abbot notices enough to suspect what has happened. He stations himself where he can watch, hoping to catch the young man in an act that will justify severe discipline.

The monk, however, detects that he is being watched. He knows that simple denial would be hopeless if the abbot entered and found the woman. He also knows that the superior may have temptations of his own. So he creates a small opportunity. Pretending to leave for a necessary errand, he offers the abbot the key to his room. The abbot thinks he now has the evidence he needs. But when he opens the door and finds the woman, his own appetite takes command. The person who came to inspect a fault commits it himself. The young monk, who has been hiding where he can see the encounter, learns exactly what he needs to know.

Later, the abbot prepares to reprimand the monk. The monk does not confess in the manner the abbot expects. He begins talking as if he has lately discovered that religious discipline permits what he had previously believed forbidden. His superior has, after all, supplied the example. The abbot understands that the monk knows the truth. A public prosecution would expose the prosecutor. He softens, drops the threatened severity, and the young man escapes the penalty.

A reader could call this hypocrisy exposed, but Dioneo's story is stranger than a simple moral victory. No one has suddenly become virtuous. The monk's success does not depend on innocence or repentance; it depends on information. The abbot's ability to punish depends not only on the rules but on his reputation as a person entitled to enforce them. Once the monk can threaten that reputation, the apparent hierarchy bends. The young man has used the superior's own behavior as a kind of leverage.

The scene is comic because timing matters: a key, an empty corridor, a hidden observer, and an authority moving from suspicion to indulgence. It is also another tale in which private facts and public judgments diverge. The institution remains standing, but the story has shown what happens behind one of its doors. Dioneo, who enjoys the more bodily and disorderly possibilities of storytelling, has drawn laughter from the collapse of solemn certainty. A listener can laugh at the embarrassment while still seeing the broader question: what becomes of rules when the people charged with administering them have an interest in keeping their own breaches secret?

## The Decameron — Day One, Tale 5: The Marchioness and the Hens

The Marquis of Monferrato has set out for the crusade. He praises his wife so warmly that the French king grows curious about a woman he has never met. Curiosity becomes an ambition to seduce her. The king arranges his journey so that he may visit Monferrato while its lord is absent. When word arrives that the royal guest intends to dine, the marchioness has very little time to prepare and less room to refuse the visit. She knows what the king wants, and she knows the danger of treating a ruler's wishes as an ordinary social request.

She chooses hospitality rather than confrontation. There will be food, ceremony, and every outward mark of courtesy. But she orders a meal composed of hens, presented in several dishes and preparations. A great lord expects abundance and variety; here he receives repeated versions of the same bird. The king notices. Making a joke that he perhaps hopes will become an opening for flirtation, he asks whether the place has no roosters at all.

The marchioness answers that although women may differ in dress and rank, their nature is not so different from place to place. He need not seek novelty here. The jest is delicate enough to be spoken at a royal table, but clear enough to put a boundary before the king. He understands both that she has seen through his purpose and that she means to protect her marriage. He takes the rebuke without pressing her, praises her wisdom, and departs.

No sword is drawn, no formal accusation made, and no public scandal needed. The marchioness uses the duties of hospitality to protect herself from the person enjoying them. The very food meant to welcome him becomes the language by which she refuses him. Her reply also relies on a social convention that her guest is expected to recognize: an honorable king ought to respect a woman's declared fidelity rather than exploit her husband's absence. Whether actual women at court could always rely on such recognition is another question; this is a story about wit succeeding against unequal power.

Fiammetta's tale is short, but its small action changes the mood after the monastery. The monk defended himself by exposing the abbot's double standard. The marchioness must prevent an injury before it occurs. Her intelligence works through restraint. She reads a danger correctly, prepares a response with ordinary household materials, and lets the offender discover the meaning himself. That is why the meal matters. The story could report merely that a clever woman refused a king, but Boccaccio gives the refusal a form that the king must taste, joke about, and finally understand.

## The Decameron — Day One, Tale 6: The Inquisitor and the Broth

A prosperous Florentine has drunk enough wine to let a careless joke escape him. He declares that his wine is fit even for Christ. The remark is heard by an inquisitor, a friar entrusted with pursuing religious offenses. In this man the authority of religious judgment is coupled with a powerful appetite for money. The joke supplies him with a pretext to summon the layman and threaten serious consequences. A frightened person is more likely to purchase relief than to argue about theology.

The layman pays, and the threatened proceeding is reduced to a penance. He is instructed to attend services and perform the acts assigned to him. He might have disappeared from the story as one more person intimidated by official power. But when he sits in church, he listens carefully to a biblical promise: what is given in charity will be returned a hundredfold. What does that mean, he begins to wonder, for people who give very little and speak so confidently about their eternal reward?

The friars feed the poor from the remains of their broth. The layman watches the distribution. When he meets the inquisitor again, he expresses concern on his behalf. If the religious community is to receive one hundred times the broth it gives away, the good friar may face a disastrous flood in the next world. The calculation is ridiculous, of course, but it exposes a disparity that had been hidden by pious language. People whose tables are plentiful offer scraps to others, then imagine generous credit awaiting them for their charity.

The inquisitor realizes that the man he threatened has understood the terms of their encounter rather better than he had hoped. He cannot easily punish a joke drawn from the sermon he has helped to authorize. The man is ordered away, and that is precisely what he wanted. The balance of force has not vanished: the inquisitor still occupies an office with dangerous powers. But an argument made inside the institution's own language has limited what that power can do in this meeting.

Emilia's story resembles the Marchioness's in the technique of the reply. Neither speaker says simply, 'You are corrupt' or 'You intend to harm me.' Each constructs a situation in which the powerful person must understand the accusation without hearing it in those words. The marchioness uses a dinner; the layman uses a sermon and a bowl of broth. The results are comic, but the stakes are social. The characters who command the language of morality are not necessarily the ones behaving well. The small man survives by listening more closely to their words than they do themselves.

## The Decameron — Day One, Tale 7: Bergamino and the Generous Abbot

At the court of Messer Cane della Scala, entertainers and learned visitors expect to be welcomed and rewarded. Bergamino has come with that expectation. He is a skilled teller of stories, a man whose livelihood depends on people who enjoy his company. But something has changed in the lord's disposition. He has become reluctant to spend. Days go by, and Bergamino is left without the customary kindness. His own funds diminish while the wealthy patron sits surrounded by the forms of magnificence.

Bergamino knows that a direct request might fail or humiliate him. He tries a tale instead. Once, he says, a celebrated poet named Primasso came to visit the Abbot of Cluny. The abbot was renowned for hospitality; no worthy visitor should go away hungry. Primasso came in modest clothing and was not recognized. The abbot, taking the visitor for an insignificant poor man, withheld food. Primasso had brought three loaves against the uncertainties of travel. He ate them one by one as he waited, and the spectacle eventually drew attention. When the abbot learned who his guest was, shame overtook him. The man he had excluded was a poet whose reputation deserved honor; the abbot corrected his error with a generous welcome.

The point reaches Messer Cane. Bergamino has not said, 'You are refusing to feed a man worth entertaining.' He has invited the court to consider an abbot who nearly disgraced himself by failing to recognize such a guest. The patron recognizes the likeness between past and present. He rewards Bergamino and restores the hospitality for which he had been admired.

This is a story about a story. We hear Bergamino's predicament, then the little account of Primasso, then watch the first story change because of the second. The embedded narrative gives the courtier a safe instrument of complaint. He can place his criticism in another person's mouth and another institution. The host who accepts the analogy can repair his behavior without being ordered to do so by a dependent man.

Yet the story also makes a distinction between charity and reputation. Did the abbot become generous because he discovered the hungry person had a famous name? Would feeding an obscure visitor have been less worthy? Boccaccio's courtly world often praises liberality because a noble household earns distinction by receiving distinguished guests. Bergamino secures his place by using that code. He is clever, and his cleverness works, but the remedy is not a society in which nobody depends on the judgment of the rich. It is a society in which the rich can be reminded, through art, of what their own professed honor requires. Filostrato ends with a lord who has listened to a story and found himself within it.

## The Decameron — Day One, Tale 8: Paint Generosity on the Wall

In Genoa lives Ermino de' Grimaldi, a man whose immense fortune has won him notice and whose reluctance to spend it has won him a second, less flattering reputation. He has wealth enough to make a splendid house. What he lacks is the openhanded spirit that would make a visitor glad to enter it. He meets a cultivated man, Guglielmo Borsiere, who knows how to speak to people of standing without blundering into a quarrel.

Ermino wants advice on decoration. He has built a fine hall and asks what he should have painted upon its walls. A merchant with money might expect suggestions of famous ancestors, victories, pleasing landscapes, or the virtues that wealthy families like to claim. Guglielmo proposes something more pointed. Have the painter represent Generosity, he says, since Ermino has never seen it. The answer is brief enough to leave almost nothing for further explanation.

The wealthy man is stung, but he is not beyond embarrassment. He recognizes that the polished joke has told him exactly how he appears to people outside his countinghouse. A hall can proclaim greatness through expensive images; it cannot compel anyone to believe its owner possesses the virtues represented there. Ermino begins to change his conduct. The story gives him a future in which he becomes known for liberality rather than only for possession.

Notice the difference from Bergamino's previous tale. Bergamino needed an entire nested anecdote to persuade his patron to treat a neglected guest better. Guglielmo compresses the work into one sentence. Both stories make reputational desire a tool of correction. The rich man wants to be thought magnificent; a speaker who can describe the distance between that aspiration and actual conduct has a means of influence.

The tale is also about public art. What should be painted on a wall? A picture can teach, flatter, boast, remember. Guglielmo suggests that it can also become a mirror, revealing by its very presence a virtue absent from the person who ordered it. In this setting, language accomplishes what an image was supposed to accomplish. The courtier's answer supplies the portrait more vividly than any painter could.

Lauretta's story takes little time to tell, and that economy is essential. If Guglielmo delivered a long sermon, Ermino might hear accusation rather than invitation to change. A single thought places him at the center of a joke that he can survive only by improving. The laughter is not a substitute for social judgment; it delivers it. A day that began with a master of lies creating holiness by false testimony now gives us an instance of a person becoming better because a truthful sentence reaches him at the right moment.

## The Decameron — Day One, Tale 9: The Gascon Lady and the King of Cyprus

A woman from Gascony has traveled to the Holy Land and is passing through Cyprus on her way home. There she is assaulted and humiliated. She seeks a hearing before the king, the person whose office should make redress possible. But she learns that the ruler has become famous for tolerating insults to himself and failing to protect those who need him. Appeals for justice accomplish little. His own passivity makes the law appear weaker than it ought to be.

The woman does not have an army, an influential family nearby, or a means of forcing the king to listen. What she does have is the ability to describe his disgrace in terms he cannot easily set aside. When she appears before him, she asks not for punishment of her attackers but for instruction. She wishes, she says, to learn how he has become so skilled at enduring injuries without feeling them. Perhaps he can teach her to bear her own wrong, since he seems to have learned to tolerate far worse insults directed at his royal person.

The king hears what the question means. She has presented his apparent patience as cowardice. The speaker who was meant to beg at the throne has made the throne itself the subject of judgment. The rebuke awakens him. He changes his ways, provides redress, and rules thereafter with greater courage. The tale moves from the personal injury of an unprotected traveler to the public responsibility of a sovereign.

Elissa's story is not a model for an actual assault victim's obligation to make a clever speech. That is not the kind of claim a literary anecdote can sustain. Its particular fiction imagines a woman whose language succeeds where formal channels of justice have failed. In other tales of the day, witty reproach protects marriage or frees a person from official pressure. Here it makes authority act, at least according to the tale's ending, as authority should.

There is a sharp distinction between being patient with injury to oneself and tolerating wrongdoing against those one is meant to defend. The woman exposes that distinction by asking the king to teach her his habit. He cannot answer without admitting what his conduct has meant to others. His transformation is swift because the tale is short: a sentence produces a new ruler. In life, the institutions of justice do not change quite so tidily. In Boccaccio's fiction, a well-aimed remark can turn a court inside out and make a prince hear his own reputation as another person hears it.

## The Decameron — Day One, Tale 10: Master Alberto and the Young Widow

Pampinea ends the first day with an accomplished physician from Bologna, Master Alberto. He is elderly and respected for his learning. One day he falls in love with a young widow whose beauty attracts much attention. His admiration is visible enough to become a source of amusement among the woman and her friends. They invite him into a courtyard, serve refreshments, and ask with pointed politeness why a man of his age should presume to desire someone so much younger, especially when many fine young men seek her favor.

Alberto understands the attack beneath the gracious welcome. He could be angry, or he could accept the implication that love is reserved for a particular age. Instead he replies with a joke about a common meal. He has seen women choose the green leaves of a leek and discard the better part. If people sometimes choose what looks less promising, perhaps he has reason to hope that a woman who rejects attractive younger suitors might prefer him. He adds that age may diminish bodily vigor without erasing judgment, feeling, or the understanding that comes with experience.

The widow recognizes the balance of courtesy and rebuke. Her question had made the old man an object of public amusement. His answer has made her preferences, and the carelessness of her assumptions, available for scrutiny. She grants him the respect she had not given him at first. The scene ends not in a romance but in a restoration of dignity. A man who has been made to look ridiculous has used language to show that he still commands his own intelligence.

The tale can be heard in several registers. The leek is a comic image and not a serious theory of why people fall in love. Alberto's reasoning turns a social embarrassment into a possible invitation. Yet he never claims that the widow owes him affection. He wins the contest of wit, not possession of the woman's person. The exchange also relies on conventions about sex, age, and desirability that may be familiar without being binding truths. The joke works because the assumptions are present in the room and can be turned back upon those who spoke them.

Pampinea finishes her reign as she began it: by arranging a setting in which people can speak, receive a reply, and make their judgments in one another's presence. Across the day's stories we have seen false testimony, conversion, religious controversy, hypocrisy, royal intrusion, clerical greed, courtly dependence, and the power of brief rebukes. These tales do not agree on one doctrine. But they repeatedly ask whether status can be trusted as a sign of merit, and what a person of lesser force can accomplish through better words. The next day, under Filomena, the question changes. Fortune will lift and overturn lives so violently that cleverness may be only one part of how anyone survives.
