# The Decameron · Day 3: frame and the day’s complete tales

## The Decameron — Story: Day Three, the New Garden and the Pursuit of Desire

After two days of stories, the ten members of Boccaccio's company do not simply proceed to a third evening. Their ruler, Neifile, has set aside Friday and Saturday for religious observance, personal care, and rest. She has also arranged a move. The famous ten days of storytelling are not consecutive, and the retreat itself keeps changing shape as the company moves between carefully staged spaces.

On Sunday morning the company walks through countryside alive with birdsong to another residence. The new house stands on a modest rise. Its rooms are richly furnished; its courtyard holds fresh water and provisions. A garden stretches beyond it, with trellised walks, roses, jasmine, and fruit trees. At the center a fountain sends water into a marble basin; ingenious channels conduct the overflow through the grounds and eventually toward working mill wheels. Boccaccio describes beauty and practical management in the same breath. The garden is cultivated, watered, and economically productive, and the guests compare its delight to an earthly paradise.

The travelers sing, eat, rest in the shade, read, play games, and gather again around the fountain. Outside their refuge, Florence still suffers from plague. Inside, supplies are brought and meals prepared by servants. The company's pleasant order depends upon labor that its members do not all perform. That division should remain visible when we describe their freedom to spend the afternoon telling stories. Their life is not a record of the whole society; it is the imagined life of a privileged group attempting to defend a patch of civilization against a surrounding catastrophe.

Neifile's theme is now more deliberate than the first day's open invitation and different from the second day's reversals of Fortune. The speakers are to describe those who have obtained something they longed for by ingenuity, persistence, or exertion, or who have recovered something they lost. This gives us a series about intention. Someone wants an object, a lover, a change in circumstances, or the restoration of a relationship. We will learn what that person is willing to do and how other people's wishes come into conflict with the plan.

Filostrato, who begins, tells of Masetto, a young man who gains entry into a convent by pretending he cannot speak. The apparent difference between a sacred vocation and bodily appetite will become a recurring comic resource. But later tales will make the same machinery less playful. A woman manipulates a confessor to arrange her own affair; a jealous wife is deceived into approaching the wrong man; an abbot turns a husband's imagined journey through the afterlife into a means of control. In each, somebody has a plan, but success for one participant need not be good fortune for another.

The day also includes longer romance-like accounts of Tedaldo's return and Gillette's campaign to reclaim her marriage. Dioneo closes with a deliberately provocative tale about a young seeker of religious life and the hermit who teaches her an earthly meaning for spiritual language. The register ranges from resourceful comedy to conduct that deserves more than laughter.

A garden is an apt setting for this day. The company has transformed uncultivated movement into a sequence of shaded paths and fountains. Their characters likewise try to direct unruly desires into chosen ends. Yet gardens can be enclosed, and the person who controls a gate may not be the person who must live inside. Boccaccio asks us to listen to both the clever action and its distribution of power.

## The Decameron — Day Three, Tale 1: Masetto at the Convent

In a village near Florence, a young farm worker called Masetto hears news from a man named Nuto who has left his job at a convent. The garden is beautiful, the work plentiful, and the pay poor. Nuto speaks bitterly of the young nuns who continually find fault with how things are done. Masetto hears something different in this complaint. He imagines that living among the women may prove more rewarding than farming for the people he already knows.

The convent needs a gardener, but Masetto fears that the women will not hire a healthy, attractive young man. He travels there with his tools and pretends to be unable to speak. A silent laborer, the convent's steward reasons, will bring less risk of gossip and will be easier to manage. Masetto is employed. He digs, carries, and plants. The nuns assume that their new worker cannot tell anyone what occurs within their walls.

Two of the younger nuns begin to wonder whether a man who cannot speak might nevertheless offer them an experience forbidden by their vows. They experiment with the idea and draw Masetto into an intimacy concealed from the rest of the community. The knowledge spreads. Others also seek him out, and eventually the abbess herself participates. Masetto's disguise has achieved its intended end almost too effectively. The work of tending the garden becomes the lesser part of his work; secrecy and the competing demands made upon him leave him exhausted.

At last, when alone with the abbess, he begins to speak. Rather than expose the convent, he says he has recovered his speech and explains that he cannot continue under the conditions they have made for him. The abbess is shocked, then finds a way to preserve appearances. The sisters agree to tell their neighbors that the prayers of the convent have miraculously healed a mute gardener. Masetto is given a more secure position as steward, and the entire arrangement continues behind the institution's respected public face.

Years pass. Masetto eventually returns home with wealth and children, whom he has not had to support or raise in the usual way. Filostrato presents him as someone who got more than he sought by trusting his cleverness and energy. The last joke depends on the gap between the convent's public story and what actually occurred there. People celebrate a holy miracle of recovered speech while the audience knows that Masetto was never mute.

But notice how much depends on the storytellers' assumptions. Filostrato begins by mocking the belief that religious clothing can make human desire disappear. That is part of the story's argument: a nun has not ceased to be a human being because she has entered a convent. At the same time, the social arrangements that make everyone believe the gardener cannot speak also make it harder to recognize his ability to refuse. Masetto has engineered the deception, yet he must eventually declare a limit to the demands placed upon him. A comic fantasy of unlimited opportunity turns into a negotiation over labor and bodily capacity.

The joke also turns on class. The young man is valuable because he can do difficult outdoor work, but he is admitted only when those above him regard him as safely powerless. Silence makes him seem manageable. He uses that imposed expectation as cover. The story is not a report on convent life; it is an anticlerical novella that invites an audience to doubt the perfect correspondence between religious profession and human behavior. Within the day's theme, Masetto gains what he desires, but the way he acquires it leaves the convent's celebrated holiness standing on a fabricated account.

## The Decameron — Day Three, Tale 2: King Agilulf and the Groom

King Agilulf of the Lombards rules from Pavia. One of the servants who tends the royal horses falls in love with the queen, Theodelinda. The difference in rank between them is enormous, and he cannot approach her openly. He watches instead. He notices how the king dresses and how he passes from his own room to hers at night, carrying a torch and a staff. The groom studies the little signals by which the royal household recognizes authority.

He arranges a similar cloak, staff, and light. When night comes, he imitates the king's silent journey to the queen's chamber. A sleepy attendant admits him. The queen believes her visitor is her husband; the groom takes advantage of that mistake and returns to his lodging before anyone raises an alarm. Shortly afterward the real king visits. The queen's surprise at his second appearance gives him reason to suspect that somebody came before him in his name.

Agilulf is angry, but he reasons that a public accusation would disgrace the queen and spread a story he cannot undo. He does not believe she knowingly betrayed him. Instead he investigates with extraordinary care. Entering the servants' sleeping quarters, he finds the man whose heart is beating faster than his fellows' and secretly cuts a patch of hair from his head. The mark will allow the king's officers to identify him later without a scene.

But the groom wakes enough to understand what has happened. He takes a pair of shears and cuts the hair of the other sleeping servants in the same way. When the king returns with his officers, he sees a room full of men bearing the same mark. To seize all of them would be to acknowledge that his private discovery has failed and invite the scandal he wished to prevent. He chooses a different remedy: a guarded rebuke to the entire group, making clear that the offense is known and must not be repeated.

Pampinea's tale is built from two almost wordless scenes. First the groom borrows the king's bodily gestures and gets through a door. Then the king tries to turn a bodily sign into proof of an individual crime. The groom defeats the sign by making it common. In neither scene does rank operate by abstract command. It works by repeated habits, recognizable clothing, servants who know when to admit a visitor, and the confidence with which a ruler expects a mark to identify his subject.

The result may be called a triumph of quick thinking, but we should not mistake its circumstances. The queen agreed to receive her husband; she did not knowingly agree to receive the groom. His impersonation violates that distinction. Boccaccio's teller emphasizes the groom's resourcefulness and Agilulf's restraint, not the queen's point of view. The story is therefore a useful case for asking who counts when we praise a clever plan. One person's successful disguise is another person's loss of knowledge about what is happening.

The king, for his part, makes an illuminating calculation. He might punish the whole household to maintain an appearance of absolute authority. He instead recognizes the limits of his evidence and the damage that publicity could cause. The tale ends with an uneasy balance: a servant has escaped detection, a king has avoided an open scandal, and the queen's mistaken encounter remains unaddressed by those who know what really occurred. Prudence and justice have not proved identical.

## The Decameron — Day Three, Tale 3: The Lady, the Friar, and the Message

A married woman in Florence longs to meet a young man who lives nearby. She cannot simply invite him into her house without endangering her reputation. The person who can carry information between two people without causing suspicion is a friar, and the friar she chooses takes pride in his spiritual seriousness. Her plan depends on that pride.

She goes to confession, but instead of admitting her desire, she complains that the young man has been pursuing her. She presents herself as a woman distressed by unwanted attention and begs the friar to admonish him. The friar believes her immediately. He seeks out the young man and delivers a severe warning, describing in detail the woman who says she is troubled by him. For the young man, who had not understood the situation, the warning becomes a revelation that she is interested in him.

The woman soon returns to confession with fresh distress. The young man, she says, has dared to send presents or attempt new approaches. Again the friar passes her complaints along. She is using the very language of refusal to transmit an invitation. Because the friar is certain he knows the moral positions of the people in the story, he cannot recognize that he is the one being led.

At last she tells him that the young man has supposedly discovered how to enter her garden at night and may climb into her house. She makes sure the warning contains a workable route. The friar hastens to reprimand the young man again, and this time he unknowingly supplies the practical directions the lovers need. When the husband is away, the visitor arrives by the appointed way. The woman welcomes him. The sequence of confessions has produced exactly the encounter she wanted without any direct written declaration from her.

Filomena's pleasure in the trick comes from its repeated reversal. Every time the friar tries to prevent an illicit relationship, he strengthens it. He thinks he is stopping a rumor; he is confirming interest. He thinks he is guarding a household; he is explaining how its defenses may be crossed. The religious office gives his words weight, and that same weight makes his mistaken intervention useful to someone else.

Yet the woman is the most skilled interpreter in the story. She understands the friar's habits, the young man's likely reactions, and the conditions of her own confinement. Her speech is carefully directed at two audiences: the confessor who hears an accusation and the neighbor who will hear an invitation. The audience knows both meanings at once. This is dramatic irony built into everyday conversation, with no magical device and almost no physical adventure.

We should be precise about what is being mocked. The teller does not show that confession is inherently foolish. She shows a man whose certainty about a penitent's sincerity prevents him from asking how his own intervention may affect the world. The story also refuses to idealize domestic respectability: the husband's absence is the condition of the successful encounter, and reputation matters more than any discussion of the marriage itself. The lady gains what she desired by finding a way to make an institution speak on her behalf without the institution realizing what it has said.

## The Decameron — Day Three, Tale 4: Fra Puccio’s Penance

Fra Puccio is a devout Florentine householder with an interest in securing the salvation of his soul. His title does not mean that he is a cloistered monk; he has attached himself to religious practices while continuing to live at home with his wife, Isabetta. As his attention turns toward penance and instruction, her life becomes narrower. He is absorbed in exercises of piety while she is left alone with ordinary needs and a marriage that offers little companionship.

A priest called Dom Felice, recently returned from Paris, is received warmly by Puccio. Felice sees that Isabetta is attractive and that her husband will follow a confident religious teacher. He devises an exercise that sounds both solemn and demanding. Each evening Puccio is to stand in a prescribed posture, with his arms spread in the form of the cross, reciting prayers and maintaining his discipline for hours. His reward, according to Felice, will be a shortened road to blessedness.

Puccio is delighted. The ceremony gives his yearning for holiness a concrete task. It demands physical effort, regularity, and unquestioning obedience. He is so eager to follow it that he does not ask why his instructor should need such intimate knowledge of the household's evenings. While Puccio remains busy with the posture and the prayers, Dom Felice visits Isabetta and begins an affair with her. Each night the husband believes he is advancing toward paradise; the priest is using the same hours for his own pleasure.

The comedy depends on the terrible exactness with which the instructions work. A perfectly obedient pupil has become easier to deceive than a negligent one. Felice does not need to force his way past a locked door. He arranges for the person who might guard it to be occupied by an activity that looks virtuous. The husband's body is present in the house but mentally and physically committed elsewhere.

Isabetta, however, is not a mere object in the arrangement. She has desires and frustrations that are important to the story. Still, the opportunity is provided by a priest exploiting the religious trust of another person. The tale allows enjoyment of the trick while also letting us see the professional and emotional asymmetry on which it relies. Dom Felice presents himself as a guide to eternal happiness, but his competence is exercised chiefly in arranging a temporal affair.

Panfilo's narration joins several of the day's concerns. People seek something earnestly; the desire may be admirable in itself; the means chosen may defeat the goal. Puccio's wish to become a better Christian is not the joke. The joke is that he accepts an arbitrary routine as a guarantee of holiness and lets a more worldly man define its terms. He has confused the possession of a rule with the exercise of judgment.

For a listener encountering medieval religious life, it is worth retaining the difference between the tale's satire and actual theological teaching. Boccaccio has not documented a church-approved shortcut to sanctity. He has invented a credulous man and an opportunist who speaks in the vocabulary of spiritual discipline. The invented posture exposes the distance between authoritative-sounding advice and moral credibility. A person can follow every instruction and still have failed to ask who benefits from the instruction's design.

## The Decameron — Day Three, Tale 5: Zima and the Palfrey

In Pistoia, a young man known as Zima is in love with the wife of Messer Francesco Vergellesi, a knight who is preparing to leave for a public office in Milan. Zima is admired for his elegance, and he owns a fine riding horse, a palfrey. The knight wants the horse but is unwilling to pay what it is worth. Zima offers a bargain: the palfrey is his if he will allow Zima to speak privately with his wife in the knight's presence.

The husband accepts. Perhaps he is so sure of his wife's obedience that he regards the interview as an empty concession. He instructs her to listen but not to respond. The men have negotiated an occasion in which her silence will make the husband's acquisition look risk-free. She is placed before Zima like someone whose presence can be exchanged as a favor.

Zima begins to speak. He tells the woman of his long devotion and asks her to offer him some sign that he is not hopelessly pursuing a dream. She obeys her husband's instruction and says nothing. Zima then performs the tale's extraordinary turn: he begins to answer on her behalf. In an imagined reply he gives voice to the sympathy and permission he hopes she will feel, explaining that after her husband has left for Milan, a pair of towels placed in a window could signal that the garden gate is open. He has made a one-sided declaration into a complete dialogue, supplying both question and answer.

The knight, delighted by what he takes to be his wife's steadfastness, praises her silence and goes away with the palfrey. Zima complains, in a final witty exchange, that a conversation with a stone is not what he paid for. Meanwhile the wife has heard the proposed plan, and she has also heard how insistently he imagines her responding. After the knight departs for Milan, she considers the attention she has received, her husband's absence, and her own wishes. She chooses to place the towels in the window. Zima sees them and enters by the indicated route; the pair begin an affair.

The story turns on an important distinction. The husband's permission for Zima to speak is not permission for the wife to answer. Nor is Zima's invented reply evidence that she has actually consented to a meeting. At the moment he gives his imaginary speech, it remains a fantasy designed to persuade. The decisive act occurs afterward, when she privately decides whether to signal him. Boccaccio's plot is constructed so that the husband thinks he has purchased a horse for nothing, while the wife receives an opportunity to act that he did not intend to grant.

Elissa is interested in the power of language to create an event. The double dialogue is a verbal invention: Zima rehearses the future he wants until the woman can imagine entering it. The palfrey becomes a visible marker of the husband's greed and overconfidence, while the towels at the window become a private sign of a decision that only the two lovers understand.

This is not simply a story about eloquence conquering resistance. A person who is ordered not to speak has to be offered something more than an opportunity to answer aloud. Here the husband treats her as trustworthy because he believes she has no independent voice, and his certainty enables the very choice he believes he has ruled out. Zima gains his desire, but what makes the story more than a clever sale is the wife's eventual action after the bargain has ended.

## The Decameron — Day Three, Tale 6: Ricciardo’s Deception of Catella

In Naples, Ricciardo Minutolo desires a married woman called Catella. She loves her husband, Filippello, intensely and rejects Ricciardo's approaches. The important fact is not merely that she is married. She has made her own refusal plain. Ricciardo learns that she is jealous of her husband, and he decides to use that vulnerability instead of respecting the answer he has received.

First he makes a show of transferring his attention to another woman. People begin to believe he has abandoned his pursuit, and Catella allows him into ordinary social company again. At a gathering near the sea, he hints that Filippello has arranged an affair with Ricciardo's own wife. Catella presses him for details. Ricciardo pretends to be reluctant, exacts a promise of secrecy, and then tells her that the lovers will meet in a particular bathhouse the following day. He offers a plan by which she can catch them without being seen.

Catella's jealousy supplies the urgency he needs. She goes to the indicated place, where the rooms are darkened and her visitor is expected. Ricciardo has arranged to occupy the position she thinks belongs to Filippello. She enters believing she will confront or expose her husband and, in the darkness, encounters Ricciardo instead. Only later does she learn whom she has been with. The trick has transformed her mistrust of Filippello into an opportunity for someone who already knows she does not want him.

Fiammetta tells the deception with the elaborate timing of a comedy: rumors planted in public, a whispered confidence, a concealed room, and the revelation after the trap has worked. The narrator's frame treats Ricciardo's success as the attainment of a difficult desire. Catella is distressed, and Ricciardo attempts to manage the consequences by controlling what she can say about the incident. The story's conventions encourage an eventual accommodation, but the path to that accommodation has been arranged without her informed choice.

It is worth stating the central difference without euphemism. Catella's consent, if the scene is described in those terms, was based on a false belief about the man's identity. Ricciardo secured access by impersonation and deception. Her earlier refusal remains a refusal. A reader can observe Boccaccio's narrative technique without approving the act that the technique makes successful.

The story also shows why jealousy is such a powerful engine of intrigue. A person preoccupied with another's possible betrayal may neglect evidence about the supposedly helpful informant. Ricciardo provides Catella with a story that confirms her fear and a route to act on it. The more intensely she wants to know what her husband has done, the less she asks why Ricciardo has brought the knowledge to her.

Compare this with the woman who used a friar as an unwitting messenger. In that case the initiator manipulated an institution to contact a desired partner. Here a man manipulates a woman's fear to overcome a stated refusal. Both plots celebrate ingenuity at the level of construction. They are not ethically interchangeable. The difference is important precisely because Neifile's theme groups them together under the attractive language of obtaining what one desires.

## The Decameron — Day Three, Tale 7: Tedaldo Returns as a Pilgrim

Tedaldo degli Elisei is a young Florentine whose relationship with a married woman, Ermellina, has come to an abrupt end. She has withdrawn her favor and refuses even to hear him. A friar's counsel has helped her resolve to abandon the affair. Tedaldo cannot persuade her otherwise. He leaves Florence and spends years away, carrying his disappointment from place to place. When he finally comes home, he does not arrive as the familiar man his friends remember. He wears the clothing of a pilgrim and moves through the city without announcing his name.

He discovers a bewildering piece of news. The people of Florence believe that Tedaldo has been murdered. A body resembling his has been found, and because the dead man's identity was misunderstood, Ermellina's husband Aldobrandino has been accused of the killing. The man's own brothers have helped bring the charge. Aldobrandino faces death for a crime he did not commit, while the supposed victim is walking the streets alive.

Tedaldo might simply reveal his identity and put an end to the mistake. But the story gives him a more elaborate task. His return creates an opportunity to repair the relationships he left behind, win Ermellina's affection again, and halt the prosecution before it becomes irreversible. Retaining his pilgrim's disguise, he gains access to Ermellina. He speaks as though he were a religious guide with knowledge of her suffering and makes her recount how she once dismissed Tedaldo.

The conversation becomes a long reproach. He attacks the friar whose advice separated the lovers and argues that her rejection was unjust. The account is remarkable because the man who claims spiritual authority is in fact the man asking for renewed intimacy. His words are not neutral counsel. Tedaldo presents his interpretation of the past as a judgment she ought to accept. Ermellina, grieving and afraid for her husband, promises that if Tedaldo were alive she would welcome him again. Only then does the pilgrim show her a ring she once gave him and remove his disguise.

Their reunion cannot be allowed to distract from the prison. Tedaldo goes to Aldobrandino, reassures him, and asks that he forgive the dead man's brothers for pursuing a charge they sincerely believed to be true. He then discovers the real circumstances of the killing and brings forward the evidence needed to save the innocent husband. Florence's mistaken story is corrected. A man is spared an execution, the brothers cease their hostility, and the household can come together without an unresolved murder accusation dividing it.

Emilia's novella is one of the day's longest and most intricate. It moves through exile, disguise, erroneous identification, judicial danger, recognition, and reconciliation. The first question is a classic romance question: how will a rejected lover regain access to someone he desires? But the story enlarges that question until it concerns the lives of other people. Tedaldo's presence is necessary not only to restore a romance but to prevent the law from punishing an innocent man for the wrong man's death.

Notice how much weight the tale places on testimony. The same city that can mistake a dead stranger for Tedaldo is prepared to convert its mistake into a criminal conviction. Sight, hearsay, old quarrels, and the apparent plausibility of an accusation are not sufficient. Tedaldo's return forces the evidence to be revisited. Yet his own method of recovering Ermellina's favor involves another claim to authority: the pilgrim's disguise allows him to speak with a borrowed moral status. He is right about the identity of the dead, but that does not make every reproach he directs at Ermellina an impartial moral truth.

The tale ends with social peace and a discreet continuation of the affair. Boccaccio has composed a success story in which a lover can solve a judicial crisis and emerge better placed than when he departed. The complexity of the plot keeps us from treating desire alone as a sufficient explanation. Tedaldo's wish starts the story, but the life of Aldobrandino gives it urgency beyond his wish. The success worth recognizing is not simply that he gets the woman back; it is that a false story, once accepted by a community, is made answerable to what actually happened.

## The Decameron — Day Three, Tale 8: Ferondo’s Purgatory

An abbot in Tuscany enjoys a reputation for holiness. One of his acquaintances, a prosperous but unsophisticated farmer named Ferondo, has a wife the abbot wants. The obstacle is not simply Ferondo's presence. He is intensely jealous, so his wife lives under close supervision. The abbot realizes that if he can convert the husband's fear and religious trust into obedience, he may get the opportunity he desires.

When Ferondo's wife comes to confess, she speaks of the miserable life created by her husband's jealousy. The abbot seems to understand. He proposes an astonishing cure: Ferondo must spend a period in purgatory and return chastened, no longer inclined to guard her every action. The plan is framed as a spiritual service. The wife hesitates, but the abbot combines promises of relief with his own demands for her affection. He is not the impartial confessor she has been led to expect.

The abbot gives Ferondo a powerful sleeping powder disguised in wine. To those watching, the farmer appears to die. He is buried with the proper rites. At night, the abbot has the body removed from the grave and carried into a secluded room, where Ferondo eventually awakens. A monk instructed by the abbot tells him that he is in purgatory, suffering punishment for his jealousy. Ferondo, who does not know about the drug, cannot imagine another explanation for his surroundings.

Days and nights are transformed into a theatrical punishment. Food comes at intervals; blows and warnings reinforce his conviction that he must repent. In the outer world the abbot visits the widow he has manufactured, and their relationship continues while Ferondo is imprisoned. The priest has not merely made up a tale about the afterlife. He has arranged people, rooms, medicines, clothing, and ritual so that the subject of his experiment experiences a convincing false world.

When enough time has passed, another dose of the powder makes Ferondo appear unconscious. He is placed back in his tomb and later recovers in the presence of astonished villagers. He describes his period in purgatory and returns home professing a new humility. The public interprets his reappearance as a miracle. The abbot's authority increases; the trick is credited to divine intervention rather than to human design.

Ferondo's wife gives birth to a child fathered by the abbot, whom Ferondo accepts as his own. The teller closes with a world in which nearly everyone believes the account the abbot has constructed. He has achieved his desire, Ferondo is less jealous, and the household continues. The final arrangement is comic within the tale's scheme, but it is also sustained by profound misinformation about an experience of captivity and punishment.

Lauretta draws her comedy from the resemblance between a miracle story and a well-executed theatrical production. Where other characters forge letters or impersonate lovers, this abbot creates an environment. He knows what Ferondo expects purgatory to feel like, uses that expectation, and supplies the cues that keep belief intact. He also knows that the community will supply its own interpretation when a dead man appears to walk home.

This is not a description of Catholic teaching about purgatory. It is a satire on a man who invokes such teaching to benefit himself. The monk's false authority is the instrument of the wrong, not evidence that ordinary believers are obliged to accept untested claims about the afterlife. The scene allows us to ask how a story becomes credible when everyone around it is already committed to the conclusion.

It also asks what becomes of the person whose experience has been manufactured. Ferondo believes he has endured punishment that taught him a moral lesson. The audience knows he has endured a trick. Whether he emerges kinder does not cancel the abbot's exploitation of his trust. In the day's language of gaining a desired object through ingenuity, the abbot is successful. In the language of justice, success is a very different question.

## The Decameron — Day Three, Tale 9: Gillette of Narbonne

Gillette is the daughter of a physician from Narbonne, and she has learned something of her father's medical knowledge. She loves Bertrand of Roussillon, a nobleman whose rank places him above her in the marriage hierarchy. When the king of France suffers from an illness that his doctors cannot cure, Gillette approaches the court and offers a remedy. She asks no ordinary payment. If she succeeds, she wants the king to grant her Bertrand as her husband.

The king at first doubts that a young woman can accomplish what his physicians have failed to do. Gillette accepts the risk of failure and applies the treatment she has inherited. The king recovers and keeps his promise. He summons Bertrand and orders the marriage. Gillette has attained the rank and husband she sought, but the transaction has made something painfully clear: Bertrand does not want to marry her. He complies with royal command while regarding the match as beneath his station.

After the wedding, Bertrand leaves. He goes to Florence instead of remaining with Gillette, intending to pursue a military career and conduct his own affairs. He sends a message setting conditions that appear impossible: only if she possesses a particular ring he keeps close and bears a son by him will he accept her as his wife. The conditions are designed to make a legal bond ineffective in everyday life. Gillette has gained the formal title of wife without being welcomed into a shared household.

She does not remain at home waiting for him to reconsider. She governs his estate well enough to win the affection of its tenants, then travels to Florence under conditions that allow her to investigate his life there. She learns that Bertrand is courting a young woman of limited means. With the young woman's mother, Gillette arranges a substitution. The expected lover agrees to give the woman gifts; Gillette supplies the resources and secretly takes her place during their meetings. Bertrand gives up the ring and, without recognizing his wife, is intimate with her. Gillette conceives twins.

She returns with the children and the ring, presenting both before the man who declared them the conditions of their marriage. Bertrand can no longer deny that the terms he himself proposed have been met. Boccaccio concludes that he accepts her and comes to love her. Neifile gives us a wife who has pursued a seemingly hopeless objective by medical skill, household administration, negotiation, secrecy, and a carefully arranged encounter.

The shape of the story traveled far beyond the *Decameron*. Readers of Shakespeare's *All's Well That Ends Well* will recognize the healing of a king, an unwanted marriage, the ring, and the substitution of one woman for another. Shakespeare changes and expands the dramatic setting, but the troubling core remains. The heroine's competence is real, and the union she wins is not the result of mutual desire at the outset.

There is a reason to resist treating Gillette's plan as proof that determination can make love just. The king uses political power to assign Bertrand a spouse. Bertrand uses status and absence to humiliate the spouse. Gillette then obtains an intimate encounter by arranging for him to mistake her identity. The narrative's legal and dynastic logic celebrates that the right wife has borne children, but a contemporary account of consent would not equate compliance with that logic and informed agreement.

At the same time, Gillette is far more than the passive prize of a powerful man. She diagnoses an illness, earns a royal reward, rescues a neglected estate, makes alliances with women outside the aristocratic household, and turns Bertrand's own words into proof. Her actions demonstrate how narrow the ordinary channels of power are for someone of her birth. She must operate within the system that frustrates her to secure any meaningful position inside it.

Neifile has chosen this as her ninth tale because it makes the day's theme almost literal. A thing long desired is gained, appears lost, and is recovered through a sequence of exertions. What persists beyond the ending is the problem of how much personal freedom a romance may sacrifice to produce its promised happy conclusion. The story's power lies in our ability to admire Gillette's intelligence while continuing to question the bargain she has been forced to make with a world of unequal rank.

## The Decameron — Day Three, Tale 10: Alibech and Rustico

For his final story, Dioneo turns the company's attention from elaborate Florentine intrigues to a young girl living near Gafsa in North Africa. Her name is Alibech. She has heard people speak of the Christian God and wants to learn how she might serve him. She leaves her family's home and travels into a remote region where religious hermits have withdrawn from ordinary society. Her motives, in the story, are sincere. Her knowledge of the language and customs of these ascetics is almost nonexistent.

The first hermits she encounters understand that a young visitor presents a temptation and a responsibility they do not want to undertake. They send her farther into the wilderness. Eventually she reaches a man named Rustico, who decides to receive her. He tells himself that this is an opportunity to test his spiritual strength. But he is not immune to the same desires that occupy so many characters in the book.

Rustico soon begins to manipulate Alibech's ignorance. He gives ordinary sexual desire an invented religious vocabulary, calling an intimate act a way of putting the devil into hell. Because she believes she is being taught a duty owed to God, Alibech takes his explanation at face value. Dioneo tells the story as a bawdy comedy in which the novice becomes so eager to practice the supposed discipline that Rustico can no longer keep up. The repeated phrase becomes a joke about a teacher who loses control of the lesson he invented.

Eventually news reaches the wilderness that Alibech's family has died and that she has inherited substantial property. A young man named Neerbale comes to find her, takes her back to town, and marries her. In conversation with other women she innocently repeats the vocabulary Rustico taught her. Those who understand its ordinary meaning laugh, and the language of the hermitage is exposed as a private euphemism, not a recognized practice of worship.

It is important to be clear about the characters' different knowledge. Alibech is a child or very young adolescent in the story. Rustico is an adult religious man who understands what the phrase conceals and exploits her lack of knowledge. We can analyze the comic mechanism without joining the teller in treating that exploitation as harmless. The girl has sought religious instruction, and the person she finds substitutes his own interests for the instruction she asks for.

The tale nonetheless offers one of Boccaccio's sharpest demonstrations of what words can do. A person who knows what an action is may rename it in the terms another person trusts. The hearer can then take the renamed action to be precisely the virtue she came to practice. This is the dangerous inverse of the third tale, where a woman used the vocabulary of complaint to convey a desired invitation. Here the deceived person is not directing the message; the supposed instructor controls the definition.

Dioneo is permitted to end a day with material that pushes against the common theme. He has found a story in which the acquisition of a longed-for good—service to God—gets mixed with a private, fraudulent lesson. The sequence of women and men gathered in a luxurious garden will laugh at the hermit's failed control and the girl's innocent descriptions. Their laughter is part of the literary evidence of what the novella expects from its audience. It need not dictate the judgment of everyone who reads it.

Day Three has offered us a full gallery of schemes. Some restore the innocent, some embarrass the powerful, some open a space for choice, and others take that space away. The technique is often the same: study another person's expectations and make them serve your plan. Dioneo closes not with a simple rule for conduct but with an account of how easily a powerful speaker can transform the vocabulary of virtue into an excuse for personal gratification.
