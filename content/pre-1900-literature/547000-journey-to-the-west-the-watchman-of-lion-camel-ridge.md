# Journey to the West: The watchman of Lion-Camel Ridge

## A warning beside the mountain

After Yellow Flower Temple, the western road passed from the fading heat of summer toward the first cool days of autumn. The nights lengthened. Leaves stirred along streambeds and the mountains took on the blue, hard outlines that distant mountains wear when the air is clear. For a little while the pilgrims had room to remember how pleasant an ordinary day could be. Xuanzang rode, Bajie muttered about his hunger, Sha Wujing kept the baggage secure, and Wukong looked ahead for dangers that were not yet visible.

Then a high mountain barred the road. Its cliffs rose beyond the clouds, and the path climbed toward the stone as though it might end there. Xuanzang grew apprehensive. Was there truly a passage through a place so steep? Wukong assured him that people found tracks over mountains and boats across rivers. If there were no road, they would find a way. The master urged his horse upward.

On a slope stood a white-haired elder with a staff and a string of prayer beads. The old man called down to the travelers to stop. Demons lived ahead, he said, and these demons had eaten people without number. Travelers would be wiser to turn back. Xuanzang was so frightened that he lost his balance in the saddle and fell onto the grass. Bajie helped him while Wukong stared at the stranger.

The master was not eager to send Wukong to question the man. He thought his disciple's alarming features and quick tongue might frighten away a source of useful information. Wukong offered a solution. He changed into a handsome young monk, his face neat, his movements decorous. Xuanzang approved at once. Bajie complained that the monkey had made himself more attractive than any amount of rolling about could make a pig.

The young monk approached the elder with flawless courtesy. Where exactly did these monsters live? How many were there? What could they do? The old man answered in the fashion of someone who understood how little courtesy would protect a traveler from a great demon. The three rulers of Lion-Camel Ridge commanded a force of lesser creatures counted in the tens of thousands. Their connections reached into courts and palaces beyond the mortal world. Even creatures who called themselves immortal showed them respect. A brave pilgrim might still walk straight into a slaughterhouse.

Wukong's questions became sharper. He wanted the name of each enemy and the nature of each weapon. Bajie, listening from the path, shouted across the distance that such a frightening account was grounds for giving up the mountain altogether. Xuanzang prayed for the wisdom to proceed. And when Wukong challenged the old man, letting his own face show through the young monk's appearance, the stranger vanished into the air. His warning had been delivered. From above he revealed himself as Taibai Jinxing, the Gold Star, a heavenly visitor who had come to warn the pilgrims, not to obstruct them.

## The bell in the grass

Wukong told the others to wait and went forward alone. From a height he surveyed a silent valley. No demons marched below him; no guards challenged him. Perhaps, he thought, the Gold Star had exaggerated. Then he heard a curious rhythm from the farther side of the ridge: a clapper beating, a bell jingling, and a voice reciting instructions as though a solitary soldier had been sent to patrol an empty fortress.

A tall little demon approached, carrying a flag marked with an order, a bell at his waist, and the wooden clapper in his hand. He was repeating the command that every patrolman was to beware of Sun Wukong. The monkey was clever, he warned himself aloud; he could turn into a fly. Wukong, who had already taken the form of a fly and settled upon the fellow's cap, nearly lost his composure. How did this small guard know the name of the intruder on his head? Had he seen him? Should Wukong strike before being discovered?

Then Wukong listened a little longer. The demon knew nothing. He was merely reciting orders supplied by a distant superior, as a guard might repeat the words on a watchman's placard. Wukong decided he would learn more by playing along. He flew off to a branch, took the shape of another watchman, and approached the patrol from behind, clapping and jingling in precisely the same rhythm.

The true guard turned suspiciously. He did not recognize this supposed colleague. Wukong declared that they belonged to the same household. What household? The cave. What work did the newcomer perform? Wukong said that until recently he had tended the kitchen fires. The guard pointed out a defect in this answer: fire-tenders did not receive mountain patrols. Each servant had his assignment, and the kings did not permit such casual changes. Wukong replied that he had tended the fire so well that the kings promoted him. The guard observed that the stranger's mouth was too pointed for a familiar cave servant. Wukong discreetly rubbed the pointed mouth into a more acceptable shape. The guard found this much more troubling than the first explanation.

Every patrolman possessed a badge, he said. Wukong must show his. The monkey had reproduced the flag, bell, clothing, and clapper, but he had overlooked this small administrative object. He asked to see the guard's badge first. There it was, a gilt token bearing the patrol name Little Wind-Driller. Wukong turned away, plucked a hair, and produced a superior-looking badge labeled Chief Wind-Driller. His rank, he said, was newly established. He oversaw the whole forty-strong patrol unit and expected his subordinates to treat him accordingly.

The guard apologized immediately. A senior officer had appeared in front of him, and the lack of resemblance to familiar officers was now evidence of promotion rather than fraud. Wukong took the opportunity to demand a payment for the new superior. The guard promised that the whole patrol would contribute when they met the others. Wukong accepted the promise and joined the march. He had passed the first gate by exploiting the guard's fear of an order he dared not question.

## Three kings and a kingdom eaten

As they walked, Wukong drew out his companion's knowledge. Their first master was a great blue-maned lion, a beast with a mouth so immense that he boasted he could swallow an army. Their second was a yellow-tusked elephant whose trunk could seize or crush an opponent. The third was a vast roc, a great bird of prey with powers of flight and a dangerous treasure: a bottle containing the two energies of yin and yang. A captive shut inside that bottle would not leave it alive. The eldest two alone would be difficult. Together with the third, they were waiting specifically for the monk traveling west with three disciples.

The patrolman lowered his voice when he spoke of the lands beyond the mountain. On the far side was Lion-Camel Kingdom. Years before, the third ruler had descended upon its people, devouring the population and leaving the place to creatures of his own kind. The mountains had become the road to a kingdom of demons rather than an accidental nest beside a pilgrimage route. The force described by Taibai Jinxing was not an exaggeration invented to frighten travelers. The old world of ordinary houses, farms, and markets had been overturned here.

Wukong asked questions as though the answers would help him serve the kings, not destroy them. Where did the watchmen stand? How were they assigned? How many men did each gate command? His companion answered with the indulgence of a man who enjoys explaining his own importance to a newly promoted colleague. Wukong had learned more than he could have learned by smashing a few demons near the entrance. He had also heard enough to know that he could not leave the whole force behind his master's horse.

When the pair reached a lonely place, Wukong ended the questioning. The guard who had been so talkative and so obliging did not survive the encounter. Wukong took his badge and patrol equipment and assumed his appearance. This was the hardest edge of the monkey's method. He could make a joke of promotion, extort imaginary fees, and turn a flag into a prop; yet when he wanted to move unrecognized through the fortress, the unfortunate messenger became a disguise he could wear more safely than a mere invention.

## A rumor runs through the army

At the first outpost a crowd of little demons recognized the returning patrolman. What had he seen? Did the monk's terrible disciple really approach? Wukong answered with the solemnity of an eyewitness. He had found Sun Wukong sitting upon a ridge beside a huge iron staff, sharpening the weapon on stone. The staff was thick as a beam, the monkey tall as a tower. He had promised to smash the kings and all their guards, beginning with those stationed near the gate.

A rumor need not be believable in every detail to succeed. It need only sound like the thing its audience already fears. The guards had heard the warning to watch for Wukong. They knew the numbers of the king's troops. They had not expected to be placed at the front of an attack by someone famous for defeating armies. The false patrolman added an argument from self-interest: there would be little meat from one pilgrim to divide among tens of thousands of demons. Why should the guards die merely to fill the stomachs of their three masters?

The response was immediate. The creatures looked from one face to another, agreed that none wished to be first under the iron staff, and scattered. Wukong moved to the next position and repeated the story with greater force. At the second gate he said that he had seen the staff shining like polished metal and heard the Great Sage vow to kill every defender. Those who survived their own fear helped spread it. By the time he reached the inner approaches, whole groups of demons had abandoned their places. A force that might have overwhelmed a single traveler had been weakened by a tale told to its own soldiers.

Wukong was delighted. In his earlier life he had frightened Heaven by actually wielding his staff. Here he had frightened a demon army with nothing but an imitation watchman's voice. He could hear clappers falling silent and footsteps disappearing down the slopes. He wondered whether the three kings themselves might be persuaded to surrender without a blow.

But the plan was dangerous in another way. The moment a fleeing guard reported that the same watchman had appeared in two places, or that Little Wind-Driller had not been promoted at all, the disguise would collapse. Wukong kept the stolen badge close to his chest, walked with the confidence of one authorized to pass, and made for the deep cave where the three rulers lived.

There the paths grew darker and the outer defenses gave way to a den that seemed to swallow the hillside. Wukong knew the strength of the lion's jaws and the elephant's trunk only from stories. He knew of the roc's yin-yang bottle only from a patrolman's fearful account. He had made thousands of lesser creatures scatter, but the three powers who commanded them still awaited him inside. He stepped across the threshold to find out whether his lies had bought an opening or carried him directly into their hands.
