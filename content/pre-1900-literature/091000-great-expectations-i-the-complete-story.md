# Great Expectations I: the complete story

## Great Expectations — Story: The Marshes and Satis House

A small boy stands in a graveyard on a winter evening and reads the names on the stones. His own name is Philip Pirrip, but as a child he could only pronounce it as Pip. His parents are dead, and so are five little brothers. From the shapes of the inscriptions he makes up imaginary pictures of the family he cannot remember. Beyond the churchyard lie the flat marshes of Kent and the dark river. At this moment a man leaps out from among the graves, seizes Pip, and demands food and a file. The stranger is a convict, cold, hungry, and afraid of being caught. He threatens the child with a frightening accomplice who may or may not exist. Pip believes him and goes home carrying a secret.

Pip lives with his much older sister, Mrs. Joe Gargery, who has raised him, as she repeatedly reminds the household, at considerable inconvenience to herself. Her husband Joe is a blacksmith, patient, physically powerful, and gentle with the boy. He does not make his affection conditional on Pip's performance. The sister rules the household with a mixture of labor, anger, and punishment. At Christmas Pip steals bread, cheese, brandy, and a pork pie, and takes a file from Joe's forge. On the marsh he encounters a second escaped convict, whose terrified behavior suggests a quarrel between the fugitives. Pip finds the man he promised to help and watches him eat with desperation. The stolen food is a crime committed by a frightened child to keep a frightening adult alive; Dickens does not ask us to imagine that either the fear or the hunger is trivial.

At the family Christmas dinner, adults who relish correcting the child make him feel guilty even before the theft is discovered. Soldiers arrive with handcuffs needing repair. Joe assists them, and the men search the marsh. They capture both convicts after a struggle. The first, hearing the accusation of theft, claims he took the food from the Gargery house himself. He protects Pip without announcing that he is doing so. That act is easy to miss at the time. It will matter enormously when the boy grows into a young man who thinks he has left the marsh behind.

Pip is then summoned to the wealthy, decaying house of Miss Havisham. Her rooms have been left in a state of interrupted ceremony. Curtains block the daylight. She wears a wedding dress long past its intended occasion, and a wedding feast waits beneath dust and cobwebs. She has stopped her own life at the hour when she was abandoned before marriage. Her adopted daughter Estella is beautiful and proud, and she mocks Pip's coarse hands and heavy boots. Pip has never thought so intently about what his hands and boots signify. He returns to Joe's forge feeling ashamed of the very world in which he has been loved. This is a turning point, not because Estella has discovered an objective truth about him, but because her language becomes the language in which he begins judging himself.

At Satis House Pip is made to play cards, walk Miss Havisham in circles, and endure the elaborate imbalance between a lady who gives commands and a child who has little choice about accepting them. He encounters her quarrelsome relations, who visit with expectations of their own inheritance, and the boy Herbert Pocket, with whom he fights awkwardly in the garden. Estella permits Pip to kiss her after the fight, a moment he invests with far more meaning than it can bear. The boy mistakes access to a wealthy house for progress toward a different social identity. He supposes, without evidence, that Miss Havisham is educating him for Estella.

Elsewhere in the village, Joe's forge is alive with work, but the promise of a working life does not satisfy Pip's new ambition. Biddy, who learns quickly and helps him learn, sees his change. She asks what he truly wants and listens while he tries to explain that becoming a gentleman would make Estella stop despising him. He notices, but does not fully consider, that Biddy's judgments of people are kinder and often more accurate. The home he now describes as coarse contains people with talents that his new social scale cannot measure.

Miss Havisham eventually arranges a meeting at which Pip is to be bound as Joe's apprentice. She provides a sum of money for the arrangement, not the romantic adoption Pip has imagined. Joe accepts the practical conditions. Pip is wounded by the fact that his destiny appears to be the forge after all. He cannot bring himself to tell Joe that the prospect of spending a lifetime with him has begun to feel like a humiliation. There is no sudden revelation yet, only the slower violence of a child learning contempt for the conditions of his own survival.

For now the great expectation is mostly an expectation in Pip's mind. He has met a criminal who sheltered him, a blacksmith who loves him, a rich woman who is trying to use a child to settle a grievance, and a girl educated not to trust affection. He organizes them into a fantasy about rank. Dickens lets the older Pip narrate these events with an awareness the younger Pip lacked. The story's most important education has begun, and its first lesson is one the young hero has not recognized: the ability to feel ashamed is not the same as the ability to distinguish what deserves shame.

## Great Expectations — Story: Apprenticeship and London

Pip becomes Joe's apprentice, but his imagination continues to dwell at Satis House. He sometimes slips away to ask after Miss Havisham, and he measures his ordinary working life against a world he has misunderstood. Mrs. Joe is violently attacked at the forge and is left severely disabled, unable to speak normally and dependent on care. Suspicion touches Orlick, a resentful laborer who has quarreled with Joe and Mrs. Joe, but the household cannot establish who assaulted her. Biddy moves in to nurse her. Pip's own attention wanders between guilt, fear, and hope of escape. He is not the detective of the incident so much as an uneasy witness who keeps finding ways to turn away.

A London lawyer, Mr. Jaggers, arrives with astonishing news. Someone has provided Pip with a considerable fortune, subject to conditions. Pip is to go to London, receive the education of a gentleman, and expect further financial advancement. The benefactor's identity must remain secret, and Pip must keep the name Pip. To the boy, the unspoken identity seems obvious: Miss Havisham must be making him suitable for Estella. Jaggers does not confirm this guess. The very absence of information gives Pip's desire space to construct its favorite explanation. Joe is happy for the opportunity even as he understands that Pip is preparing to leave. Pip is already practicing the distance of someone newly rich.

On his arrival in London, the city is less magnificent than Pip anticipated. Jaggers's offices are crowded by criminal cases and anxious clients. The room seems to carry the smell of human trouble. Pip meets the lawyer's clerk Wemmick, who speaks practically of money, professional efficiency, and the difference between what is useful and what is sentimental. He is guided to Barnard's Inn and discovers that the young man who will share his rooms is Herbert Pocket, the very boy he once fought in Miss Havisham's garden. Herbert is generous, poor by the standards of a fashionable young gentleman, and willing to explain manners without humiliating his friend.

Herbert gives Pip a more accurate account of Miss Havisham. As a young woman she was deceived by a man who courted her for money and then abandoned her on her wedding day. Her half brother was implicated in the scheme. The grotesque frozen wedding is not a strange aristocratic custom but the memorial to a private catastrophe. Herbert has also heard that Miss Havisham brought Estella into her home to be raised. Nothing in the story establishes that she intends Estella to marry Pip. He has mistaken his own longing for the plan of another person.

Pip studies with Herbert's father, Matthew Pocket, whose learning and domestic life offer a less theatrical variety of gentility. He becomes acquainted with Jaggers's threatening manner and the lawyer's housekeeper, Molly, whose scarred and unusually powerful hands are made a spectacle at dinner. He also comes to know Wemmick's private home at Walworth, an affectionate little castle of personal inventions, where the clerk cares for his elderly father. Wemmick's tenderness is so unlike his office performance that Pip must learn to read the separation. The novel is filling London with people who are more complicated than their occupational labels.

With an allowance to spend and no requirement that he earn it, Pip accumulates debts. He buys things partly because he supposes they suit a gentleman, and he helps Herbert do the same. They make elaborate lists of obligations and congratulate themselves on their responsible accounting without actually reducing what they owe. The comedy hurts because both young men are capable of recognizing their foolishness while continuing it. The expectation of wealth has loosened their attachment to consequences.

Joe visits Pip in London, dressed uncomfortably and painfully aware of the manners expected around him. Pip is embarrassed by Joe's speech and clothing. Joe perceives the embarrassment and leaves as gently as possible, unwilling to cause more discomfort. The meeting marks one of Pip's moral failures. Joe has made no demand beyond the chance to see someone he loves, yet Pip behaves as though an honest worker's presence is a threat to his own standing. It is difficult to overlook the contrast with the convicts and clients at Jaggers's office, where the genteel legal world gains income from bodies and lives that polite society would prefer not to see.

Pip continues his hope of Estella. She is sent to London and draws the attention of wealthy men. At Richmond and in their meetings he reads signs of future affection into her familiarity, although she repeatedly warns him that she cannot love as he wishes. Miss Havisham takes pleasure in hearing of men who pursue Estella and are wounded. The young woman is not simply a cold object set before Pip; she is a person whose emotional education has been distorted by a guardian with a grievance. Pip nonetheless insists that Estella's refusal cannot be final, because his own imagined story requires something else.

When Pip turns twenty-one, he receives control of a regular income through Jaggers. He asks about his benefactor and learns nothing. He quietly uses some money, with Wemmick's help outside the office, to advance Herbert's career in a merchant business. This is one of Pip's most admirable actions, partly because it does not advertise itself as an admirable action. Then comes an evening at his London rooms, in a storm, when a stranger climbs the stairs. Pip sees a rough man, older, weathered, and excited. The visitor calls him by name. A buried memory is about to overturn almost everything Pip thinks he knows about his rise.

## Great Expectations — Story: The Benefactor Returns

The stranger standing in Pip's rooms is the convict from the marshes. His name is Abel Magwitch. After capture and transportation overseas, he has spent years laboring and gaining money in Australia. He arranged for Jaggers to turn that money into Pip's expectations. Miss Havisham was not the benefactor. Pip's fortune comes from a man he once helped with stolen food and a file. Magwitch has crossed back to England in defiance of the law, because he wants to see the gentleman he believes he has made. For the older man, Pip's cultivated manners and elegant rooms are a triumph he can hold in his hands. For Pip, the discovery first brings disgust and terror. The money he spent so freely is suddenly connected to the criminal world he has taught himself to despise.

Magwitch speaks with affection. He does not initially understand why Pip recoils. England's system of transportation has made his return a grave legal risk, and he knows it, but the hope of seeing Pip has overcome caution. Pip arranges a hiding place and asks Herbert for help. From this point the story changes its direction. Pip must try to protect the man who has protected him, and that effort gradually makes the abstraction called a convict into a person. It also places Pip in danger of being implicated in the harboring of a returned prisoner.

Magwitch tells a history that complicates Pip's understanding of criminal guilt. As a young man he was poor, frequently punished, and vulnerable to exploitation. He entered a criminal partnership with Compeyson, a smoother man with an appearance that played well in court. Compeyson presented as a gentleman while Magwitch looked like the sort of person a court was prepared to condemn. When they were tried, the contrast in manner and dress helped Compeyson receive a lighter sentence. Compeyson was also the man associated with the scheme that ruined Miss Havisham's wedding. What Pip had treated as two separate stories of crime and aristocratic suffering now touch each other.

Pip fears that Compeyson has learned about Magwitch's return. The men need a way to get Magwitch secretly out of England. They move him from Pip's rooms to lodging by the river under another name, and Herbert's fiancée, Clara Barley, becomes part of the household that shelters him. Pip and Herbert practice rowing to make a river escape look ordinary. Wemmick sends a warning in language careful enough to avoid implicating the lawyer's office. Everyone must navigate a world in which open honesty can be dangerous, while concealment may serve genuine care.

At the same time Pip's romance with Estella reaches a painful end. She intends to marry Bentley Drummle, a wealthy man Pip knows as brutal, dull, and arrogant. Pip begs her not to choose him. Estella insists that Pip has misunderstood what she has already said: she was never offering the feelings he imagined. Their argument at Satis House is not a contest that Pip can win by pleading better. He has to confront the difference between his long investment in a promise and her refusal to make that promise at all. Miss Havisham's apparent distress now begins to exceed the satisfaction she expected from using Estella against men.

Pip returns to speak with Miss Havisham about the harm done and to request help for Herbert. She has the means to aid the young man, and eventually agrees. She asks forgiveness for the manipulation of Estella and of Pip. In the course of his visits the emotional arrangement at Satis House becomes easier to name: a woman injured in one relationship has made another person into an instrument for injuring others. Pip begins to understand the injustice without pretending the remorse repairs it automatically. Nor does his understanding absolve him of using Estella's coldness as a reason to disregard the people who offered him ordinary affection.

Another hidden story gradually emerges. Pip recognizes features of Estella in Jaggers's housekeeper Molly. He learns that she once stood trial for murder and that Jaggers defended her. Through conversations with Herbert, Jaggers, and Wemmick, Pip reconstructs the possibility that Molly is Estella's mother and that Magwitch is her father. The clues bind the respectable young woman to the criminalized lives society treats as an opposite class. The family history is less important as a surprise than as a challenge to the idea that moral character can be read from birth, wealth, or manners.

Meanwhile Pip's old village troubles persist. Mrs. Joe has died after her long incapacity. Orlick remains hostile, and Pip's suspicion of him has not become decisive public proof. Pip travels between London, the village, and the river, with plots of money, revenge, romance, and escape crossing one another. He begins to see that there was never a single line carrying him toward the gentleman Miss Havisham supposedly intended him to be. The inheritance that seemed to sever his past has drawn him into the past's unresolved obligations.

By the end of this movement Pip has another conception of what a gentleman might be. Magwitch is rough and capable of frightening behavior, but he has spent years working to give Pip a chance at life. Joe is an uneducated craftsman, but his conduct is generous. Compeyson looks gentlemanly and exploits that appearance. Jaggers is an educated professional whose methods can conceal both power and care. Pip has to decide whether his own moral education will remain a lesson in appearances, or become an ability to respond when someone vulnerable stands before him. The coming attempt to take Magwitch downriver will test that decision more severely than any London dinner.

## Great Expectations — Story: Fire, Escape, and Forgiveness

Pip is now caught between two forms of danger: the public danger of Magwitch's return and the private dangers he has never settled at home. Miss Havisham, who has admitted the harm in her training of Estella, sits too close to the fire. Her old clothing catches alight. Pip rushes to smother the flames and is badly burned himself. She survives the immediate accident but later dies from her injuries. It would be too neat to say the fire cures her or allows Pip to forgive her without residue. It turns a house kept in suspended time into a place where consequences can no longer be held at bay.

A letter summons Pip to a lonely place on the marshes. It is a trap set by Orlick, who resents Pip and acknowledges having attacked Mrs. Joe. Orlick binds him and threatens to kill him. Pip is rescued by Herbert and others who have followed his movements. The sequence gives violent physical shape to something already present throughout the novel: old injuries do not vanish because Pip has acquired wealth, and neglected conflicts do not become harmless through distance. He survives partly because the friends he has made refuse to leave him alone.

The planned escape for Magwitch proceeds despite the risks. Pip, Herbert, and Startop take a boat down the Thames and aim to transfer Magwitch to a vessel that will carry him away. The river that once marked the edge of Pip's childhood becomes a route through the machinery of trade, law, and flight. Before the plan succeeds, another boat intercepts them. Compeyson has been watching. A struggle erupts; Compeyson disappears into the water and is drowned. Magwitch is injured and captured. The body of the man who once dominated him through social polish has vanished, but the law's power over the returned convict remains.

Pip attends to Magwitch in custody and later at his bedside. The affection that the young Pip once accepted fearfully has become an affection he can choose. The authorities confiscate Magwitch's fortune, and Pip no longer stands to inherit it. That loss matters financially, but the novel's deepest change is not that the young man has learned to be poor. It is that he can now sit beside a dying prisoner without shame. Pip tells Magwitch that the daughter he thought lost is alive, a beautiful woman whom Pip has loved. He tells the man she is loved. Magwitch dies, and Pip is left to live with the knowledge that a wealth he had despised at its source was given with great tenderness.

Pip's debts are still real. His body is weakened by injury and illness, and he faces arrest for what he owes. In delirium he is cared for by Joe, who comes to London, settles the debts, and tends the sick man without using the occasion to reproach him. Pip wakes to the ordinary generosity that he had long considered beneath his new status. He tries to speak his remorse. Joe, whose language is plain, does not turn the moment into an account demanding payment. The relation between them has changed because Pip can finally perceive how badly he behaved, even though Joe had not stopped being kind.

Pip goes home intending to ask Biddy to marry him, only to discover that Joe and Biddy are about to marry each other. Their union is not a trick or a penalty inflicted on the hero. Biddy has her own judgment, and Joe has found a companion who loves him. Pip blesses their marriage and leaves to work abroad with Herbert in the trading firm that his earlier secret assistance helped support. He must earn a living and learn the differences between honorable work, comfortable status, and the spending power he once mistook for distinction. Years pass. The novel's broad account of maturity is quiet: Pip's expectations have ceased to be a substitute for action.

The ending that most readers encounter brings Pip back to the ruined site of Satis House. He meets Estella, whose marriage to Drummle was deeply unhappy and whose suffering has altered her. They walk together from the place where their childhood misunderstandings began. The published final sentence imagines that Pip sees no shadow of another parting. This is not an explicit account of a wedding or of a settled happy marriage. It is a hopeful or ambiguous image of possible companionship, and the way one hears it depends on the wording of the edition.

Dickens also drafted an earlier ending. In that version Pip meets Estella by chance in London. She has suffered in marriage, then remarried, and their meeting ends in separation. The two conclusions assign different degrees of comfort to a reader who has followed Pip's learning. We will compare them later without pretending that the unpublished version was the conclusion Victorian readers received when the story first appeared in 1861. For now the published narrative has brought Pip from the graveyard and forge through wealth, shame, and the river back to the possibility of seeing another person without a fantasy imposed on them.

The child in the churchyard imagined what his dead family looked like because the stones offered only fragments. The young gentleman in London did something similar with his benefactor and with Estella. He made elaborate stories from partial signs and then treated those stories as guarantees. The older narrator has learned that generosity can arrive in an ugly coat, that refinement can hide cruelty, and that love does not become truthful simply because it is long cherished. The cost of these lessons is severe. The achievement is not that everything turns out well, but that Pip's way of judging people is no longer simply borrowed from those who made him feel small.
