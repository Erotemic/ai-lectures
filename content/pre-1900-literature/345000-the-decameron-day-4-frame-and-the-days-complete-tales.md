# The Decameron · Day 4: frame and the day’s complete tales

## The Decameron — Story: Day Four, Boccaccio’s Defense and the Goslings

Before the fourth day's ten tales begin, the book's author interrupts the book. Until now, we have listened to a company of young women and men in a carefully ordered retreat. Their rulers have selected themes, their members have told stories, and the frame narrator has moved us between meals, gardens, dances, and moments of rest. Suddenly the person addressing us is not one of the ten participants. Boccaccio speaks as the maker of the collection, answering people who object to the sort of work he is writing.

He has heard several criticisms. He is too interested in pleasing women. He ought not, at his age, to devote himself to love stories. A serious writer should keep company with the classical Muses rather than speak so often of the concerns of living women. He might do better to earn his bread than to compose idle tales. Some critics even complain that the events he recounts did not happen in the way he says. The objections mix morality, social prestige, literary hierarchy, economics, and the question of factual accuracy.

Boccaccio replies with a story of his own, though he insists it is only part of a story and does not belong among the company's hundred. A Florentine man named Filippo Balducci loses his beloved wife and is overcome with grief. He withdraws from the world with their little son. On a mountain, he brings the boy up to know prayers, fasting, and heavenly things while keeping him from the sight of ordinary society. For years the father alone goes into Florence to obtain supplies.

When the son is about eighteen, he persuades his aging father to take him on one of these journeys. The city astonishes him. He sees buildings, animals, and the busy signs of life that his father has never described. Then they pass a group of young women returning from a wedding. The son asks what they are. The father, afraid to name the attraction he hopes to prevent, calls them goslings and instructs him to look away. The son finds them more captivating than anything else in Florence and asks to take one home.

The father is defeated by the very ignorance he has cultivated. He can refuse the request, but he cannot prevent the son's immediate interest. Boccaccio uses the episode to argue that desire is part of human nature, not something one can abolish by silence, isolation, or changed labels. If a young man raised in seclusion is drawn toward women upon first seeing them, why should an author who has known them throughout his life be ashamed of writing to delight them?

His defense is affectionate but not free of limitations. The women are praised as an audience and as beautiful objects of desire; we can ask how much authority they are granted as readers and how often they are made into the subjects of male argument. Still, the claim that vernacular prose addressed to women deserves literary space is significant. Boccaccio does not apologize for failing to write Latin epic. He is defending a public for stories and an artistic medium in which practical experience, appetite, humor, and social observation belong.

The interruption also plays with levels of fiction. The author answers critics of stories that are not yet finished within the book. We need not infer an exact publication sequence from this rhetorical pose; the scene may be part of the work's designed dialogue with its readers. His example, moreover, raises the count of embedded stories beyond a simple one hundred if we treat every narrated episode equally. He explicitly marks it as outside the company’s numbered tales, protecting the ten-by-ten architecture even as he adds another narrative.

We then return to the company and to its new ruler, Filostrato. His theme is radically different from Neifile's successful acts of ingenuity. He asks for loves that ended unhappily. The garden remains beautiful, the guests remain protected, but their stories will bring hidden murders, punitive fathers, deaths by grief, jealous revenge, and lives that cannot be repaired by a final clever reply. The writer who has just defended pleasure now asks his listeners to endure sorrow. The book is testing not only what a story can offer but how much its chosen audience can be asked to feel.

## The Decameron — Day Four, Tale 1: Ghismonda and Guiscardo

Tancred, Prince of Salerno, loves his only daughter Ghismonda with an attachment that has become possessive. She has been married and widowed; her father is reluctant to let her leave his household again. Ghismonda is intelligent, proud, and no longer willing to accept a life in which her father's comfort determines the whole course of her own. She falls in love with Guiscardo, a servant of the court. His birth is humble, but his character and judgment impress her more than a list of ancestors could.

The lovers arrange meetings through a secret passage into her chamber. Ghismonda knows that the prince's pride of rank makes the relationship dangerous. Guiscardo is equally aware that a ruler who regards his daughter as an extension of himself may not distinguish love from disobedience. They continue to meet, keeping their affair hidden within the apparently secure architecture of a princely house.

One day Tancred enters his daughter's room while she is absent and conceals himself there, intending to see her when she returns. He falls asleep and awakens to find Guiscardo with her. He remains hidden and watches. The scene forces him to witness not only an affair but the existence of a private life in his daughter that he has refused to imagine. He leaves unnoticed, and his response is not to speak with her. He has Guiscardo seized.

When Tancred confronts Ghismonda, his language mixes paternal tenderness and injured authority. He declares himself shamed that she has chosen a man of low birth. Ghismonda refuses to accept the premise. Human worth, she answers, is not made by inherited titles. Nature gives the same basic capacities to people born in different conditions; virtue makes distinctions that rank cannot guarantee. She has loved Guiscardo deliberately and does not ask to be treated as an ignorant child. Her argument is one of the most forceful speeches in the *Decameron*: she contests both her father's right to arrange her feelings and his belief that a servant's humanity is less valuable.

Tancred hears the speech but does not change course. He orders Guiscardo killed. The murdered man's heart is placed in a golden cup and sent to Ghismonda. A vessel made precious by its material is made to carry proof of the prince's violence. Ghismonda understands immediately. She laments Guiscardo, speaks of the courage he deserved from a ruler, and prepares poison. She pours it into the cup and drinks it, choosing death rather than life under a father who has destroyed the person she loved.

Tancred arrives too late to undo what he ordered. Ghismonda dies before him. He has lost the daughter he meant to keep, and the tragedy ends with the burial of the lovers together. Fiammetta has begun Filostrato's day with a sequence in which authority produces exactly the loss it was designed to prevent.

The story asks us to listen closely to Ghismonda. She is not presented only as the beautiful object over whom two men struggle. She has identified Guiscardo's merits, arranged her own love, and articulated a social argument with startling clarity. The scene in which she speaks to her father is not a minor interval between arrest and death. It exposes the irrational part of Tancred's defense: he calls the relationship shameful because Guiscardo is low-born while treating the murder of the lover as an exercise of princely dignity.

The golden cup gathers the contradictions of the household into a single object. Gold measures inherited wealth and visible position; the heart measures a person the prince has deemed disposable. Sending the heart is a performance of power, but it also confirms Ghismonda's account of her father's cruelty. The prince intended a warning and created a relic of the man she values. That reversal makes her final act legible as protest, even as the death remains tragic rather than a solution.

Boccaccio's first tale of this day leaves no clever escape. The lovers have been discovered and punished; Ghismonda's reasoned defense fails to restrain coercive power. Her words nevertheless outlive that failure in the reader's judgment. A story can end badly for its characters while giving its audience a clear view of the argument that should have prevailed.

## The Decameron — Day Four, Tale 2: Fra Alberto and the Angel

After the death of Ghismonda, the company is shaken. Filostrato has promised tragic love stories, and Fiammetta has delivered one without relief. Pampinea takes the next turn and changes the immediate tone while retaining the day's theme of love ending badly. Her central figure is not a noble lover destroyed by family pride. He is a swindler who has discovered that a religious costume can provide a profitable second identity.

A man from Imola called Berto della Massa has acquired such a reputation for dishonesty that nobody trusts him. To escape his past, he goes to Venice and enters a religious community under the name Fra Alberto. There he adopts the signs of severe devotion, speaks humbly, and becomes known as a holy friar. The performance is successful precisely because his audience does not know the history he has left behind.

He meets a vain married woman, Madonna Lisetta, who believes herself exceptionally beautiful. Alberto flatters her by claiming that the angel Gabriel has fallen in love with her. The visitor will descend from Heaven, he says, and must be welcomed with proper secrecy. He arranges the encounter himself by appearing in the guise of the angel. Lisetta accepts the supposed distinction as proof that her beauty has reached beyond the human world.

The secret cannot survive the pleasure of telling it. Lisetta boasts of her heavenly admirer, and news spreads to relatives who are less willing to accept the story. Their anger makes Alberto's position dangerous. To escape an immediate discovery, he leaps into a canal and reaches the house of a man who offers shelter. The rescuer soon learns that the visitor is the fugitive friar everyone is discussing. Instead of protecting him, he devises a public spectacle.

The man disguises Alberto as a wild creature and leads him through Venice for a crowd's amusement. The false angel is reduced to an object of laughter and abuse. Eventually other friars recognize him, take custody of him, and confine him. The person who used spiritual dignity as a means of access is now stripped of every external sign of that dignity.

Pampinea has constructed a vivid counterpoint to the preceding tragedy. Guiscardo's low rank did not prevent him from behaving honorably; Fra Alberto's religious rank does not guarantee honest conduct. Ghismonda's father judged a man's worth from social position; Lisetta judges the truth of a claim from the flattering prestige of its alleged source. Both judgments fail, though their consequences differ greatly.

The comedy also has a recognizable geography. Venice's canals and streets matter: Alberto's flight is not complete when he escapes through a window, and the crowded city can turn a private secret into a public procession. His last disguise is not chosen as freely as the first. He had used the friar's habit and the angel's authority to direct events; another man now puts him in costume for the public to inspect.

We should not flatten the episode into a claim that all clerics are corrupt or all believers gullible. The satire relies on a specific impostor and a specific form of vanity. It asks how deception grows when one person wants advantage and another wants a flattering miracle to be true. Lisetta's credulity is comic in the narrator's eyes, but Alberto is the one who knowingly uses religious language to obtain an intimate relationship under false pretenses.

In the full architecture of the day, Pampinea has given the audience a pause without abandoning the topic. Love ends in disgrace and imprisonment rather than in a noble death. The result changes the emotional temperature and reminds us that the *Decameron* can turn the same abstract theme through many genres, from heroic tragedy to street farce.

## The Decameron — Day Four, Tale 3: The Three Sisters in Crete

Lauretta tells a story in which almost every attempt to conceal a crime produces another. In Marseille a wealthy merchant has three daughters, Ninette, Madeleine, and Bertelle. They are loved by three young men: Restagnon, Foulques, and Hugues. Restagnon has noble blood but little money; the other two have inherited fortunes. The six conspire to leave their families, pool their resources, and build a shared life in Crete, away from the scrutiny that has made their affairs difficult.

At first the plan appears successful. They travel together, settle in comfortable houses, and enjoy the freedom they had imagined. But they have not abolished jealousy, ambition, or the danger of private violence. Ninette comes to believe that Restagnon has become involved with another woman. The story does not settle whether her suspicion is justified. In her anger, she procures poison and kills him.

The death cannot remain private. The woman who supplied the poison is arrested for another matter and reveals what she has done. The Duke of Crete has Ninette seized, and she faces execution. Her sister Madeleine sees no legal way to save her. The duke has already expressed interest in Madeleine, and she offers to yield to him on condition that he release Ninette and keep the exchange secret. The bargain saves the imprisoned sister but places Madeleine at the mercy of a powerful official using the threat of punishment to obtain what he wants.

Foulques learns enough to suspect how Ninette survived. He demands an explanation from Madeleine. When she tells him, he kills her in anger. He then flees with Ninette, who does not know he has murdered her sister. Their disappearance leaves the third couple in an impossible position. Hugues and Bertelle are arrested on suspicion of complicity and, under pressure, are drawn into the machinery of accusation. They avoid execution through money paid to their guards and escape to Rhodes, but they lose their wealth and prospects. There they live in misery and die.

One killing, then another, and then the collapse of the remaining household: the tale is designed as a chain, not as six separate biographies. It starts with an agreement to share property and risk. Once suspicion enters the house, the common life has no mechanism to withstand it. Neither private intimacy nor shared prosperity can prevent one person from treating another as an obstacle to be removed.

Lauretta's narrator attributes the tragedy above all to rash anger, and her framing speech reflects medieval gender assumptions about women's emotional nature. We need not adopt those assumptions to follow the story's logic. Ninette acts violently, but the duke and Foulques also turn their power and anger against women. Madeleine's bargain is made under the pressure of an execution threat, and her attempt to save a sister becomes the pretext for her own murder. Anger is not confined to a particular sex; it is given different opportunities by the distribution of power.

The setting extends Boccaccio's world beyond the Italian mainland. The families' commercial wealth makes travel possible; Crete is not an empty romantic island but a place with its own ruler and law; Rhodes becomes the last refuge of people who have lost what they once carried with them. Geography is part of the descent. Each movement seems to offer escape from one jurisdiction or set of consequences, but the fugitives bring their decisions with them.

The ending is severe because nothing restores the first promise. A community built for pleasure has dissolved into murder, detention, disappearance, and poverty. The tale challenges Day Three's faith in ingenuity: every clever evasion now produces a higher cost. The difference between making a plan and making a livable world has become impossible to ignore.

## The Decameron — Day Four, Tale 4: Gerbino and the Princess of Tunis

Gerbino is a young Sicilian nobleman, the grandson of King Guglielmo, known for courage and splendor. Reports of his qualities reach the daughter of the King of Tunis. Through messengers, gifts, and letters, the two form an attachment before they have met. Their love is nourished by descriptions and promises, a relation established across the water by speech rather than shared daily life.

The princess is promised to the King of Granada. Her father prepares a ship to carry her to the marriage. Hearing rumors of Gerbino's passion, the King of Tunis asks Guglielmo to guarantee that the vessel will pass safely. The Sicilian king gives his word and sends his glove as a physical pledge. The diplomatic meaning is clear: the ship will not be attacked by those subject to the king's authority.

The princess sends Gerbino a message announcing her departure and urging him to prove his love. He knows that his grandfather has made the promise, but he arms two galleys and goes to sea in the hope of intercepting her. He persuades his men with language that joins love to the prospect of valuable plunder. For himself, he says, he seeks only the woman; the rest of the ship's wealth may be theirs. The expedition is therefore an act of passion and an armed venture with material rewards.

The ship appears. Its crew displays Guglielmo's glove and invokes the pledged safe conduct. Gerbino dismisses the token and demands the princess. The defenders refuse. A fierce battle follows, in which arrows and stones fly across the water. Unable to take the ship quickly, Gerbino tries a more destructive tactic, using a burning vessel to force the crew's surrender.

The defenders decide that their king's daughter will not become Gerbino's prize. They kill her before his eyes and throw her body into the sea. Gerbino boards in a rage and kills many of the people who opposed him. He can recover her body and bury it, but not restore her life. The expedition he imagined as a rescue has made her death possible.

The King of Tunis demands an accounting. Guglielmo has given his word. Though he loves his grandson, he judges that the breach of safe conduct cannot be excused by private passion. Gerbino is arrested and executed. The old king chooses the preservation of a political pledge over the life of his heir. Elissa ends with a second loss produced by the first: the princess has died in the attack, and Gerbino dies because he ordered it.

This is a tragedy about competing obligations. The lovers use the language of a promise to each other; the rulers use the language of a promise between kingdoms. Neither kind of promise is trivial, but Gerbino assumes that desire can annul the public undertaking and that force will be able to repair the difference. It cannot. His courage, so attractive in reports carried across the sea, becomes the means by which he defeats the very possibility of being with the woman he loves.

We should also notice how little freedom the princess possesses at either end of the story. Her father arranges her marriage, and Gerbino plans to seize her vessel. She sends a request for help, but once fighting begins, the men controlling the ship decide what to do with her body. The poem-like aura of long-distance love must not conceal the violence of the institutions surrounding it. She is neither a diplomatic gift nor a military prize from her own point of view.

Boccaccio places the glove at the center of the conflict for a reason. A small object stands for an agreement larger than any individual. Gerbino scorns it as irrelevant to his urgent feeling. The result shows why a pledge that protects travelers can matter more, not less, when emotions run high. The tale asks whether courage without fidelity to a just promise deserves praise when others must bear its costs.

## The Decameron — Day Four, Tale 5: Lisabetta and the Pot of Basil

In Messina, three wealthy merchant brothers live with their sister Lisabetta. A young man named Lorenzo works in their business. Lisabetta and Lorenzo fall in love and begin a secret relationship. The brothers discover it. They do not respond by listening to her or examining Lorenzo's character. They fear the shame that a liaison with a dependent employee might bring upon their family and trade connections.

They ask Lorenzo to accompany them on a journey outside the city. There they murder him and conceal his body. When they return, they explain that he has been sent away on business. Lisabetta asks repeatedly where he has gone. The brothers give evasive answers and eventually rebuke her for asking too much. Her grief is joined to uncertainty: she has no proof that the man she loves is dead, and the people who know have taken control of the account she receives.

Then Lorenzo appears to her in a dream. He speaks of his murder and shows where his body was hidden. Lisabetta goes to the place with a trusted woman. They uncover the remains. Unable to carry the whole body away, she takes his head and brings it home. She places it in an earthenware pot, covers it with soil, and plants basil above it. The plant grows under her constant care and tears. She visits it, waters it, and remains beside it, expressing a grief that her household has refused to recognize openly.

The brothers notice the attachment and take away the pot. When they discover what it contains, they are frightened of exposure and flee. Lisabetta, deprived even of the last object through which she could remain connected to Lorenzo, declines and dies. Her mourning cannot be managed into silence without destroying what is left of her life.

Filomena has given us a story of great economy. There is no long pursuit, mistaken court judgment, or series of elaborate disguises. The brothers' crime happens quickly. Most of the tale unfolds afterward, as a hidden killing is translated into the daily labor of tending a plant. The pot of basil is at once an ordinary household object and an improvised memorial. Nothing in its outward appearance tells strangers what it holds, but for Lisabetta every leaf belongs to the person she has lost.

The dream is equally important. Boccaccio does not invite us to reconstruct a modern forensic investigation. In the literary world of the novella, Lorenzo's appearance supplies the knowledge the brothers have suppressed. Dreams in the *Decameron* do not always have the same meaning or reliability. Here the dream gives grief a place to search and makes the concealed crime accessible to the victim's beloved.

The brothers' motives connect love to commerce and rank. Their sister's sexuality is treated as something that might diminish the standing of a merchant household, while Lorenzo's position as an employee counts against him. The brothers are able to travel and bury a body because they possess the practical authority and resources of employers. Their secrecy is not a private feeling; it is enforced by the means available to prosperous men.

Later readers often remember the basil plant more vividly than the murder. That is part of the novella's achievement. Violence is converted into an image of patient nurture; the hidden head becomes the root of an almost sacred attachment. But the tenderness is not consoling. Unlike the successful reversals of Day Two, no fortune will reunite these lovers. Unlike Ghismonda, Lisabetta does not deliver a public argument against family pride. She makes a private space of mourning, and even that is taken from her. Her story ends by showing what happens when the living are denied permission to remember the dead on their own terms.

## The Decameron — Day Four, Tale 6: Andreuola and Gabriotto

In Brescia, a young woman named Andreuola loves Gabriotto, a man who has won her affection but does not possess the rank and wealth her family might prefer. They are secretly married. Their relationship is not a passing flirtation: they have made a commitment to one another, even though they cannot live publicly as husband and wife. A walled garden offers them a place to meet, and for a time their love is sheltered by the secrecy they have chosen.

One night Andreuola dreams a warning. An ominous presence seems to snatch Gabriotto away. She awakens frightened and tells him of the dream when they next meet. Gabriotto tries to reassure her, then tells a dream of his own. He has imagined a strange animal or dark creature entering his body, leaving him unable to defend himself. The two dreams make the garden feel less secure. The lovers have entered the story expecting the future of marriage; their dreams suggest that time may be running out.

As Gabriotto finishes recounting his dream, he is seized by violent pain and dies in Andreuola's arms. The narrative gives an immediate bodily cause for the catastrophe; we should not replace the episode with a modern medical diagnosis that the text cannot support. Nor does Boccaccio simply announce that the dreams were supernatural predictions. Their dramatic effect comes from being followed by a death that makes ordinary reassurance suddenly powerless.

Andreuola has almost no time to mourn. She knows that the discovery of her secret husband in the garden will invite suspicion. With the help of a faithful servant, she wraps Gabriotto's body with care and prepares to carry it back toward his family. The women are stopped by watchmen and taken before the city's magistrate. A dead man, a secret relationship, and two women moving through the streets at night provide enough material for the authorities to imagine a crime.

The magistrate questions Andreuola. She tells the truth: Gabriotto was her husband, and he died suddenly during their meeting. The magistrate believes he may use her fear and isolation to impose his own desires. When he tries to force himself upon her, she resists. The man charged with judging her conduct becomes another person who must be resisted. She maintains her account even though her grief has left her with little protection.

At last her father learns what has happened and intervenes. Gabriotto receives an honorable burial, and the obstacle to acknowledging the marriage falls away only after the husband is dead. Andreuola refuses a new marriage and enters a convent. For her this is not the comic convent of Masetto's story. It is the place she chooses after the life for which she secretly married has ended.

Panfilo's tale is concerned with the distance between love and social recognition. The marriage was real to the lovers, but its concealment makes Andreuola vulnerable when Gabriotto dies. She is treated as suspicious not because evidence establishes guilt, but because public authorities are ready to assume the worst about a woman outside the expected arrangement of her family. A magistrate's office does not automatically make its holder just.

It also matters that she does not abandon Gabriotto's body. In the previous tale, Lisabetta preserves a fragment of Lorenzo's remains because her brothers have denied her a burial and a public act of grief. Here Andreuola tries to bring the whole body into the open and is nearly punished for doing so. These two stories sit beside each other in the fourth day's sequence, making the treatment of the dead a measure of how much freedom the living possess.

Day Four's sorrow need not always involve deliberate murder. Gabriotto's death is sudden, almost arbitrary. The further injuries arise because grief takes place inside a world of reputation, suspicion, and unequal power. Andreuola cannot restore him. What she can preserve is the truth of their commitment and her right to decide what remains of her life.

## The Decameron — Day Four, Tale 7: Simona and Pasquino

Simona lives in Florence and earns her living by working wool. Pasquino, a young man of equally modest circumstances, earns his living in the same world of labor. They meet through ordinary work and fall in love. In other stories, a wealthy family may stand between lovers, or a ruler may decide whose marriage is politically useful. Here the couple is poor, and their happiness is made from time stolen from the obligations of daily employment.

One afternoon they arrange an outing to a garden. They sit together and share their companionship. Pasquino reaches for a leaf of sage, rubs it against his teeth or gums, and almost immediately collapses. He dies before Simona can understand what has happened. The suddenness makes her grief frightening to people who arrive afterward. She was the one person beside him when he fell. The world is ready to suspect that she poisoned him.

Simona is brought before a magistrate. She insists that she did nothing to harm Pasquino. She describes the garden, the herb, and the instant in which he became ill. But her explanation sounds improbable to the men who question her. They demand a demonstration. In the garden she shows them where the sage grew and, to make the account plain, takes a leaf and handles it as Pasquino had done. The same fatal effect follows. Simona too collapses and dies.

The officials now have a startling answer to the charge against her. The sequence of events is no longer merely her unsupported account. Repeated in front of witnesses, it shows that some danger lay in the plant. When the garden is examined, the tellers find a large poisonous toad beneath the sage. The tale supplies its own cause for the poisoned leaves; we should not turn this scene into a modern botanical or toxicological claim. The toad is part of the novella's explanation of the two deaths.

Emilia's story is brief, almost severe. Two people seek nothing more elaborate than an afternoon together. Their love does not demand political overthrow, a stolen ship, a disguised identity, or a father's consent. Their happiness is destroyed by a hazard they do not recognize and by the limits of other people's understanding of what has happened. Unlike several characters in Day Three, Simona cannot solve the problem with an ingenious plot. Her attempt to make the truth visible becomes the act that kills her.

The manner of proof is important. As soon as Pasquino dies, Simona's grief is treated as potentially criminal. She has no obvious powerful advocate. She is a poor working woman, and the legal encounter assumes that she must answer for an event even she does not understand. The magistrate cannot inspect her memory. He asks her to reproduce a physical action. What might have cleared her name while she lived becomes confirmation only when she dies.

The tale also changes the register of gardens in the *Decameron*. At the opening of this third storytelling day, the company admired a carefully watered garden, a controlled refuge against a deadly world. Here a garden seems to offer a small private paradise to two laboring lovers. Its familiar herbs are not necessarily safe; a place built for pleasure contains something no one has noticed. Both gardens are products of human plans, and neither grants mastery over chance.

The deaths of Simona and Pasquino do not constitute a grand moral judgment. They are not punished for betrayal or for breaking an oath. Their love simply ends, and their society reaches the truth too late. That is another way for love to end unhappily, and Boccaccio allows the story's shortness to convey the abruptness of the loss.

## The Decameron — Day Four, Tale 8: Girolamo and Salvestra

In Florence a young man named Girolamo, heir to a prosperous household, grows up alongside Salvestra, the daughter of a tailor. Their affection deepens into love. Girolamo's widowed mother considers the attachment unsuitable. Wealth and family reputation suggest to her a more advantageous marriage, and she resolves to separate the young people until the feeling fades.

Her strategy is patient. She arranges for Girolamo to travel to Paris on business for several years. Distance, she believes, will do the work she cannot accomplish through direct persuasion. The scheme succeeds in one narrow sense: while he is away, Salvestra marries another man. But the mother has mistaken absence for forgetfulness. Girolamo returns still attached to the life he imagined with her.

He finds that the woman he loves has a husband and a home of her own. Rather than accept the new situation, he searches for an opportunity to speak with her privately. He gains access to her house and pleads for a revival of their relationship. Salvestra is distressed and refuses. She tells him that their old love cannot now be acted upon without injuring the commitments she has made. Her refusal is not a theatrical device intended to make him try harder. It is a boundary that the story makes clear.

Girolamo insists that she owes him one last proof of kindness. He asks to lie beside her for a moment, promising not to touch her. She reluctantly permits him to rest near her. What follows is not the reconciliation he hoped for. He dies there, without violence and without any sign that she has changed her mind. Salvestra first fails to recognize the death, then confronts the terrifying discovery that a man has died in her home under circumstances nobody will easily explain.

She seeks help from her husband, who is startled but responds with practical care. They carry Girolamo's body back toward the house where he belongs. His family and neighbors gather for the funeral. Salvestra watches the mourning from within the crowd and is overcome. She goes to his body, and there she too dies. The lovers are reunited only in death, and the arrangements made to prevent a socially inconvenient marriage have helped lead to the loss of both lives.

Neifile's narrative is about the limits of the schemes families devise for their children. Girolamo's mother understands commerce, reputation, and the usefulness of travel. She believes the heart can be managed as a household account: send the boy away, wait, and return him to a more profitable arrangement. The plan leaves out the persistent inner experience of the person being moved. The tale asks readers to recognize the destructive difference between a prudent calculation and a just one.

Yet the story also gives Salvestra a position that should not disappear into the lovers' final tableau. She has married someone else; she declines Girolamo's proposal to resume their affair; she is frightened by what happens when he will not leave. The tragic force of his death does not convert her earlier refusal into a mistake. Her later death is represented as overwhelming grief, not as evidence that she secretly agreed to abandon her marriage.

The ending turns a private attachment into a public spectacle. In the bedroom, the lovers cannot resolve their past. At the funeral, family and community must confront what the separation has produced. There is no clever speech to reverse the damage, no sudden inheritance to make the union possible, and no successful escape to another city. The story's final image is one of shared burial, an intimacy that can be acknowledged only when it no longer threatens the family's plans.

Among the fourth day's tragedies, Girolamo and Salvestra show how a decision can cause harm without being designed as a murder. The mother believes she is protecting a household. The lover believes he is asking for one final kindness. Each acts from an intelligible desire, and yet the outcome is irreversible. The gap between intentions and consequences has become the day’s central subject.

## The Decameron — Day Four, Tale 9: The Two Guillaumes and the Eaten Heart

The setting is the south of France, in the world of knights, castles, and songs of courtly love. Guillaume de Roussillon and Guillaume de Cabestaing are friends and companions in arms. But Cabestaing falls in love with Roussillon's wife, and she returns his affection. For a time the affair remains concealed. The language of courtly devotion offers a way to imagine the relationship as ennobling, but it provides no protection when the husband's jealousy becomes violent.

Roussillon learns of the love between his wife and his friend. He resolves not merely to separate them but to exact revenge. Taking advantage of the trust of friendship, he arranges an encounter in which he kills Cabestaing. He then orders that the dead man's heart be removed. In the hands of a servant and a cook, the heart becomes the ingredient of a dish prepared for the wife who had loved him.

The woman eats the meal without knowing what it contains. Her husband watches and only afterward reveals what he has done. He has planned a punishment that would make her participate unknowingly in the destruction of her beloved. The disclosure is the real object of the cruelty: he wants her to carry the knowledge of what he has compelled her to do.

She answers with a declaration of fidelity to the dead man. Having eaten what her husband has served her, she says she will take no other food. She then throws herself from a high window and dies. The husband's victory has become the loss of both people whose love he wished to extinguish. Their bodies are eventually buried together. The story closes not with reconciliation but with the public recognition of a bond that the husband's violence could not erase.

This is one of the *Decameron*'s most disturbing tales, and its architecture is very exact. The murder happens out of the wife's sight; the meal arrives as if it were an ordinary gift; the explanation comes only when the husband is sure she cannot refuse what has already happened. The difference between consent and an act performed without knowledge is central. She did not choose to eat the heart. Roussillon makes an intimate bodily act into an instrument of domination by withholding its meaning until it is complete.

The use of a heart as literal food belongs to a widely circulating medieval story pattern sometimes called the eaten-heart motif. Boccaccio's version draws on the traditions associated with troubadours and chivalric romance. Those literary associations matter: the names and surroundings evoke a remembered culture of refined love, yet the actual acts are murder, humiliation, and suicide. A vocabulary of nobility is no guarantee of noble conduct.

The husband's cruelty also challenges the romantic ideal of loyalty among armed companions. The men share a social rank and a tradition of mutual trust. The betrayal of that trust allows the attack. We have seen other kinds of treachery in this day, especially relatives killing a lover to protect standing. Here the murderer converts his wife's supposed wrongdoing into a license for unlimited violence, even though his punishment destroys what he claims to defend.

We should treat the episode as a literary narrative, not as a verified chronicle of a real historical crime. The emotional violence is stylized, and different medieval versions of the motif have different names and details. What remains constant is the deliberate transfer of horror from the murderer to the person who must learn what has been eaten.

Filostrato, whose name and role in the company already suggest a troubled relation to love, tells the ninth story of the fourth day. It returns the listeners to uncompromising tragedy after several stories that mixed sorrow with surprise and circumstance. The ending offers no practical lesson capable of making the loss acceptable. It leaves the audience to judge a man who believes that possession of his wife entails the right to decide what she may love, know, and consume.

## The Decameron — Day Four, Tale 10: Ruggieri, the Chest, and the Sleeping Draught

After nine stories of love frustrated, endangered, or destroyed, Dioneo claims his customary freedom to end the day with a story of a different kind. The company has heard of secret marriages, an execution at sea, a pot of basil, and a dish whose meaning was concealed from the person eating it. Dioneo's own tale also features secrecy and a body that seems dead. But in his hands these ingredients produce a farce in which the feared death proves temporary.

In Salerno, an elderly physician named Master Mazzeo della Montagna has a much younger wife. She takes as her lover a man called Ruggieri da Jeroli, whose reputation is not especially good. Their meetings must be concealed from her husband. One day the physician prepares a powerful sleeping draught for a patient who is to undergo treatment. The medicine is left where Ruggieri can find it during a visit, and he drinks it without knowing what it is.

The effect is alarming. Ruggieri falls into a deep, motionless sleep. Neither the wife nor her servant can wake him. Believing he has died, the two women panic. They fear that the discovery of a man's body inside the physician's house will expose the affair and invite a charge of murder. Their next decision is improvised rather than wise: they put him into a large chest that stands near the house and arrange to remove the apparent corpse from sight.

During the night, two moneylenders who have been looking for a useful chest steal it. They carry it away and bring it into their own lodging. When Ruggieri recovers consciousness, he finds himself in a strange room. His efforts to leave bring him into a fresh calamity. The people whose property surrounds him take him for a thief. The man who was mistaken for dead is now accused of entering a house to steal.

The accusation gathers force because every witness knows only one stage of the story. The doctor's wife knows that Ruggieri collapsed after drinking from the wrong vessel. The people who moved the chest know that it held what appeared to be a corpse. The moneylenders know they acquired the chest by theft but cannot publicly admit that fact. The officers encounter a confused young man in a room where he has no evident right to be. Each interpretation is plausible from a small angle and false when treated as the whole account.

The wife's servant eventually provides the crucial explanation. Her account of the mistaken death and the sleeping draught makes sense of the locked chest and the bewildered prisoner. Ruggieri escapes execution, and the moneylenders' own conduct becomes part of the comic reversal. What looked like the prelude to an irreversible tragedy is revealed as a chain of concealments, misunderstandings, and lucky delays.

Dioneo is not pretending that everything in the story is admirable. The lovers deceive a husband; they dispose of a body they have not verified to be dead; the chest is stolen; judicial authorities initially misunderstand the evidence. Yet a life is saved because somebody can finally reconstruct the sequence of events. The story's exuberance comes from the widening gap between how events appear and what actually happened.

The sleeping potion gives the fourth day a deliberate counter-image to its earlier deaths. Gabriotto died suddenly and was carried away by Andreuola; Ruggieri seems to die suddenly and is hidden away by his lover. Andreuola's truth was met with suspicion; Ruggieri's defense also seems impossible until a witness tells the whole story. But Dioneo's dead man wakes. His comic privilege allows a return from the edge of tragedy that the other narrators have withheld.

As the company prepares to leave Filostrato's day behind, its ruler passes authority to Fiammetta. The next day will be devoted to love that reaches happiness after troubles and reversals. Dioneo has already moved the listeners toward that brighter prospect. His concluding tale does not undo the nine previous losses. It shows instead that the arrangement of stories can change how an audience feels about the same hazards. A chest can be a coffin or the hiding place of a man who will stand up again. The next story will decide which.
