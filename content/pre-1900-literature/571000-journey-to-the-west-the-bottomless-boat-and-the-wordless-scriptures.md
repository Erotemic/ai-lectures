# Journey to the West: The bottomless boat and the wordless scriptures

## At the foot of the true mountain

They came from Kou Hong's restored household with the road before them and the scriptures still beyond reach. The western country had grown green and quiet. Pine trees stood above the hills. Flowers grew along the paths. Here and there a traveler could hear the sounds of people reading sacred texts, and in places where another land might have greeted strangers with suspicion, a house invited monks to rest. Xuanzang had walked toward this country for fourteen years. He knew what it was to see a distant mountain from a gate at dawn and fail to reach it by night. But he could feel that the road had changed. It was drawing toward a center.

One afternoon they approached tall buildings rising above the landscape. The roofs seemed to meet the light before the sunlight reached the road below. Xuanzang looked at them with delight. Wukong, who had known mountain spirits to build false monasteries and demons to dress themselves as gods, examined them for a moment and said that the place was genuine.

"You bowed to every false holy place that frightened me along the way," he told his master. "Now you stand before a true one and remain on your horse."

Xuanzang dismounted at once. A figure was waiting at the gate, bright and composed, carrying the equipment of a Daoist immortal. He addressed the monk as the scripture pilgrim from the Tang. Wukong recognized him as the Golden-Crowned Immortal of Jade-True Monastery, the guardian at the foot of the Buddha's mountain. The immortal laughed when he saw how old the promise had become. Guanyin had told him to expect the travelers long ago, he said, and he had watched the seasons pass while they did not arrive. What had taken them so long?

Xuanzang could have answered by naming every river and demon between Chang'an and this gate. Instead he thanked the immortal for keeping watch. The guardian invited them inside, provided food, and had warm water prepared for their bath. The washing mattered. They had crossed deserts and caves, survived imprisonments, endured roadside hunger, and slept in smoke-filled rooms. Here the last dust of the journey could be washed from their skin before they approached the Buddha.

That night they rested. In the morning Xuanzang wore his treasured robe, cap and staff. The immortal inspected him and smiled. Yesterday the traveler had arrived worn from the road; today he looked ready to stand before a great court. Wukong declared that he knew the route onward, but the immortal pointed out that the monkey knew it by flying through the clouds. Xuanzang had come on foot, and the proper path began through the monastery's rear gate. They followed the guardian out, and at last saw the radiance above Vulture Peak, the mountain of the Buddha's teaching.

Xuanzang bowed as soon as the summit appeared. Wukong laughed and told him not to wear out his knees before they got there. A mountain could be seen long before it could be climbed. The guardian sent them onward, and the disciples led their master toward the final ascent.

## The bridge that could not be crossed

Only a few miles later they came to a river wider and fiercer than the streams they had crossed near their old enemies. Its current ran between banks too steep and bare for easy passage. Across it lay a narrow bridge formed of a single log. The inscription beside it named the crossing Cloud-Reaching Ford.

Xuanzang stared at the log, then at the water boiling below. He had been told that he was approaching the Buddha's home. Had the immortal mistaken the way? Why would the final passage be more terrifying than any earlier bridge?

Wukong stepped onto the log and crossed in a few swinging strides. He turned and waved. Bajie looked down the whole length and refused. He had spent years walking beside gorges, but no amount of sanctity was going to persuade him that a smooth round trunk over a furious river was a proper road. Sha agreed that a different route would be welcome. Wukong ran back and tried to pull Bajie onto the bridge. The pig lay flat on the ground, clinging to earth with all four limbs. He would rather give up Buddhahood than step onto that thing, he said, and Wukong could explain the decision to the Buddha himself.

Xuanzang was still searching the bank when a boat came upstream. Its boatman called to the pilgrims to come aboard. Relief turned almost immediately to alarm: the vessel had no bottom. One could look straight through its frame and see the water. Xuanzang could not imagine a less promising way to cross. The boatman assured him that the vessel had carried beings for ages and that it would not sink. Wukong understood something his master did not. The ferryman was the Buddha of Deliverance, and the impossible craft was the proper crossing for the pilgrim who could not walk the log.

"Get in," Wukong said. "It will hold."

Xuanzang hesitated until Wukong gave him a firm push. The monk stumbled into the water and was hauled aboard by the ferryman. He protested, shaking his sleeves and trying to dry his feet. Wukong helped the others aboard. The white horse and the baggage followed. Then the boat moved, borne over the same current that had seemed ready to swallow them.

A body floated past in the stream. Xuanzang shrank back, thinking some drowned traveler had appeared alongside them. Wukong told him to look again. The body was his own. Bajie and Sha recognized it too, calling out in amazement while the boatman laughed. It was not a fresh death laid in their path, but the former mortal body that Xuanzang was leaving behind at the ford. The traveling monk who had been cold, hungry and frightened could see the shape of his life moving away in the water. He had not become someone else by forgetting that life. He had crossed with it.

On the far bank the boat and ferryman vanished. Xuanzang was light in a way he had never known. He thanked the companions who had guided him across, and Wukong answered that their help had gone both ways. They had protected the master, but through the master's pilgrimage they had also found a path toward their own release. Even Bajie was quiet for a moment. Then he adjusted his burden and followed the others toward the gate.

## The audience at Thunderclap Monastery

The mountain above the ford opened into buildings and courtyards that seemed to contain more light than the sky. There were trees and flowers, birds and animals, halls and stairways reaching up the slope. The pilgrims passed groups of monks and lay worshippers, who greeted the arriving travelers and directed them inward to the central court. Wukong had once marched into Heaven to demand titles and offices. Now he went in beside a master carrying letters from a human emperor.

The great gate was guarded. Word was passed from gate to gate and hall to hall that the scripture pilgrim had arrived. The Buddha summoned an assembly of bodhisattvas, arhats, guardians, and monks. Xuanzang entered with his disciples and their horse. He bowed, presented the travel document, and explained his mission: the emperor of the Great Tang had sent him west to request scriptures that would be brought back for the benefit of the people.

The Buddha received the petition and spoke of the suffering caused by human wrongdoing, ignorance, deception and greed. He named the collection of teachings held in the mountain's library, divided into thirty-five sections and many thousands of scrolls. It had been preserved so that people could study how to free themselves from suffering and guide their conduct. The scriptures were not merely trophies to mark a journey's end. They were the very reason the journey had been undertaken.

Xuanzang asked permission to carry them east. The Buddha told two senior disciples, Ananda and Kasyapa, to take the pilgrims to the repository, give them a meal, and select suitable texts for transmission. The men led them away from the assembly through halls of shelving and cabinets. The scale of the place was almost beyond Xuanzang's imagination. After years spent saving a robe from thieves and searching caves for a missing horse, he stood before the treasure he had dreamed of whenever his courage faltered.

The two guardians asked a question.

"What have you brought us?"

Xuanzang thought they meant the travel credentials or the offerings he could make on behalf of the emperor. He told them that the party had come a very long distance and carried no costly gifts. The road had taken everything they had been able to spare. They had arrived with their lives, their vows, their baggage, and their petition.

The guardians were displeased. They asked for a present in exchange for the scriptures, and when the pilgrims offered nothing, they made it plain that they expected compensation. Wukong immediately proposed returning to the Buddha and complaining. Bajie and Sha urged him to be calm. The librarians then brought the selected scrolls, and the four travelers loaded them without understanding that they had been given no written teaching at all.

They descended the mountain with the bundles on the horse and on the shoulders of Bajie and Sha. Xuanzang bowed to the holy company as they passed. He believed he had received the thing for which he had crossed the world.

## A wind that scatters white pages

An old Buddha, Dipankara, had been watching from the upper rooms. He understood what Ananda and Kasyapa had done. The pilgrims had been given the wordless scriptures, blank pages that could be defended as possessing a truth beyond written signs but would be useless as the public teaching the Tang court had requested. The monks could not carry silence back in boxes and expect scribes to reproduce it for the people. Dipankara sent a guardian named White Hero after the departing party to make the mistake visible.

The guardian followed them as a violent wind. Its force tore the bundles from the horse and flung them into the air. Wukong sprang after the fleeing figure. The guardian, afraid of coming within reach of the iron staff, broke open the bundles and scattered their contents onto the road. Wukong turned back to save the scrolls instead of pursuing him.

Xuanzang knelt to gather the loose sheets. Sha opened a roll, examined it, and stopped. He passed it to the master. Bajie opened another. Wukong unrolled a third. There were no characters on any of them. Every surface was white. Every treasured name and title in the repository had become a bundle of paper on which nothing was written.

Xuanzang sank into despair. Had he traveled all these years to return to the emperor with blank paper? He imagined explaining that he had seen the Buddha, yet had no text to offer the people who waited for it. There had been false monasteries and demons pretending to teach. Now he had come to the real mountain and been sent away with pages he could not read.

Wukong knew whom to blame. The guardians had demanded gifts, he said, and when they received none they had supplied this worthless-looking set of scrolls. He insisted they go back and complain in front of the Buddha himself. Bajie agreed wholeheartedly. Sha folded the scattered blank sheets with care, though he knew they would not be the books they took home. The four went back up the mountain.

## The last payment

Wukong entered the great hall with an anger that had once shattered the gates of Heaven. He laid out the matter before the Buddha: after all the dangers of the pilgrimage, his disciples had demanded payment and handed over blank scrolls when none was offered. They had made a mockery of the emperor's petition and the travelers' labor. He asked the Buddha to correct the wrong.

The answer was not the simple rebuke he expected. The Buddha acknowledged that the guardians had demanded a gift. He also maintained that teaching ought not be transmitted lightly or obtained without an appropriate exchange. He told a story of scriptures once recited for a wealthy household in return for a measured quantity of gold and remarked that the payment had been small. Even the blank scriptures, he said, had their value for those able to understand them. But the people in the eastern lands required written words. The guardians would now supply written volumes.

Wukong was scarcely satisfied. His master had been asked to obtain public teachings for people who would never see this mountain, yet an argument about compensation stood between him and the books. Xuanzang had no money. His most precious object was the purple-gold begging bowl bestowed by the Tang emperor when he left Chang'an. It had fed him along the road. He had taken food in it from the hands of strangers, seen it survive storms and thefts, and carried it as a tangible link to the man who had entrusted him with the mission.

He took the bowl from Sha and placed it in the guardians' hands.

"This was given to me at the start," he said. "I have nothing more fitting to offer. Please give us the written scriptures, so that we do not betray the purpose for which I came."

Ananda accepted it. Other attendants in the library laughed at the demand for gifts and openly mocked the guardians. But neither returned the bowl. This time the scrolls were brought out with writing on them. Xuanzang told his disciples to inspect each volume before it was packed. Bajie unfolded one, Sha another, Wukong another. There were characters on every page they examined. The pilgrims received five thousand forty-eight scrolls, selected from the thirty-five categories of the collection. The figures were carefully recorded, for the objects had to be entrusted to people who would copy and preserve them in the eastern lands.

The four returned to the Buddha's hall to give thanks and receive instructions for treating the scriptures with care. Wukong looked at the bundles with relief mixed with a bitterness that did not disappear merely because the right texts had been obtained. Xuanzang had surrendered the bowl, but he held the scriptures. Sha arranged the burden. Bajie made certain that the food for the next part of the road was also packed.

As they departed the mountain once more, Guanyin spoke to the Buddha of the time remaining in the mission. Fourteen years had passed since she began searching for the scripture pilgrim, but the reckonings of Heaven and the scriptures called for a more exact number of days. Eight days were still needed to complete the sacred count. The Buddha sent eight great guardians to carry the travelers east and return them to the mountain after they delivered the texts.

The men who had once trudged from inn to inn were lifted on a heavenly wind. Xuanzang still had the traveling papers; Wukong still attended him, though the mountain's audience had been achieved. Bajie and Sha held the scriptures tight. The long westward road was behind them, yet their work had changed rather than ended. The texts would have to reach the emperor, and the journey's final reckoning had not yet been made.
