# Journey to the West: The shaved court and the hidden mist

## Four monks in a box

At dawn the ruler of Dharma-Destroying Kingdom sat before ministers who had all lost their hair. His own scalp felt strange under his crown. The queens, the palace women, the officials, and the guards had awakened in the same condition. The king had ordered thousands of monks killed because of an oath he had made; now his household looked as though it had been given the outward mark of the very people he despised. The officials could not explain how it had happened. They could only kneel and ask pardon for presenting themselves in such a state.

Before any answer could be found, the commander of the city guard requested permission to show the king property recovered from robbers during the night. There was a large chest and a splendid white horse. The thieves had abandoned both when the soldiers caught up with them. The chest was so heavy that it seemed likely to contain treasure. The king ordered it opened in the audience court.

The lid rose. Bajie sprang out first, delighted to have fresh air and room for his body. The officers recoiled at the sight of a great snouted figure rising from their supposed loot. Then Wukong helped Xuanzang from the chest, and Sha Wujing brought out the bundles. Four monks stood where the court had expected silver. Bajie caught sight of the white horse held by an officer and demanded it back. The officer needed no second invitation to release the reins.

The king rose from his throne. A day earlier, finding four monks might have meant the completion of his vow and the order for their deaths. Today he came down the steps to greet them. He wanted to know where they had come from and why holy travelers had been locked in a box. Xuanzang explained that he had been sent from the Great Tang to obtain scriptures in the west. He had heard of the king's terrible promise, disguised himself as a merchant, and taken lodging within the city. Thieves had carried away the chest in which he slept, and soldiers had brought him to court.

The king looked from the monks to the rows of shaved officials. He confessed the vow openly. A quarrel with a monk had angered him so much that he had promised to put ten thousand monks to death. He had continued to kill in order to finish what he called a religious undertaking. But now the whole court had been made to look like the objects of his hatred. He feared a reckoning that no sword could prevent. He asked Xuanzang to accept him as a disciple and offered the treasury of the kingdom in repentance.

Wukong refused the treasure. If the king wished to honor the monks, he could stop killing them, exchange the pilgrims' travel documents for passage, and permit them to leave. The name of the kingdom should change as well. It had been called Dharma-Destroying Kingdom, but a ruler who had discovered the danger of destroying the Dharma could call his realm Dharma-Honoring Kingdom instead. The king accepted the new name and arranged for the pilgrims to be received and escorted out.

As they rode away, Xuanzang praised Wukong's method. Sha asked how he had found barbers willing to work through the night on every official in the kingdom. The monkey explained about the sleeping insects and magical razors. They laughed at the marvel of it. Yet beyond their laughter remained the dead. Nine thousand nine hundred ninety-six monks did not return because a king had changed his mind. The chapter lets the travelers pass under royal protection, but its earlier count gives the comedy a shadow that cannot be shaved away.

## The mountain behind the feast

The company had scarcely left the city when a mountain rose across their path. Its wooded slopes were handsome, but a mist moved above the ridges and the air smelled of danger. Xuanzang halted the horse. He was grateful to have escaped a human ruler, yet he was not ready to trust a lonely mountain. Wukong tried to steady him with words about cultivating a clear mind. The goal was not far away because a traveler could see it; the true journey was made by carrying sincerity through fear. He repeated the thought that the spiritual mountain must be sought within one's own heart as well as beyond the borders of the world.

Xuanzang accepted the teaching and rode on. Soon a wind moved through the branches. Then a fog rolled in so thick that the paths and rocks disappeared. It did not resemble the light mist of an ordinary summer morning. The monk asked what wind could make the air so dark. Wukong rose into the clouds to discover the cause. He found a great creature sitting beside a precipice, surrounded by lesser monsters and sending out gusts and vapor to confuse travelers.

The monkey could have dropped upon the creature with his iron staff. Instead he considered his reputation and his brother's habits. Bajie was a capable fighter when the need was clear; he was also fond of food, rest, and complaints. Wukong decided to let the pig lead the encounter. The difficulty was persuading him to march into danger willingly. He came down to Xuanzang and announced that the fog was no demon's doing after all. A village ahead, he said, was steaming large pots of rice and making white flour cakes for hungry monks. The drifting vapor was only cooking smoke.

Bajie heard the word rice and moved close. Had Wukong already eaten? Wukong said he had tasted a little but found the vegetables too salty. Bajie replied that he would endure any quantity of salt if the servings were large enough. He asked permission to go ahead and obtain fodder for the horse, then see whether the villagers might provide something to eat. Xuanzang was delighted that Bajie had suddenly become so thoughtful of the animal. Wukong hid his smile.

Before Bajie left, the monkey added another difficulty. The pious villagers served only handsome monks. Bajie looked at his own face, sighed, and used one of his transformations. He became a short, thin monk with a wooden fish, muttering a prayer in the uncertain manner of someone who had never learned the words. Then he went into the fog in search of a banquet.

## The feast that wanted to eat him

The monsters had set their ambush across the road. When the little monk came singing along, they closed around him and caught at his robe. Bajie begged them not to pull his clothing. He had been told that monks were to be fed here and expected to reach the food without a fight. The demons laughed. This was indeed a place where monks were fed, they said, but only after the monks themselves had been steamed and served. He had arrived as the meal.

Bajie felt his stomach turn. He cursed Wukong for sending him here and resumed his own shape. His rake came sweeping from his belt; the lesser demons stumbled back, shouting in surprise at the great ears, snout, and tusks that replaced the harmless little monk. Their master came forward carrying an iron club. He demanded the traveler's name. Bajie straightened and recounted his former glory in Heaven, his dismissal, his life at Gao Village, and the obligation that now bound him to the pilgrimage. He was Zhu Bajie, the repentant former Marshal Tianpeng, and anyone who meant to eat him would have to fight for the privilege.

The demon did not shrink away. Club struck rake. Bajie drove hard enough to force his opponent backward, but the creature had a band of warriors ready to surround him. Soon the pig was fighting in several directions and wondering whether any of this would end with a meal. Above the trees Wukong watched. He had left a hair-made copy of himself near Xuanzang so that the monk would not see him depart. When Bajie's strength began to fail, the true Wukong shouted from the cloud and dropped into view.

Bajie took heart at once. He was no longer a solitary pig among hungry enemies; his elder brother had come. With a furious sweep of the rake he pressed the demon back. Wukong did not even need to fight for long. The sight of the Monkey King was enough to send the lesser monsters scrambling toward their caves. Bajie returned in triumph, covered in sweat and mud. Xuanzang asked whether he had found grass for the horse. Bajie looked at Wukong as though no injury could be so great as having to answer that question.

He told the whole story: the false feast, the demons, and the elder brother who had rescued him after placing him in danger. Wukong, standing beside Xuanzang, denied having left at all. The monkey had made a double, Bajie protested, and there was no use pretending otherwise. Xuanzang could not see through the trick, but he understood that the road was dangerous. The party must pass the mountain together.

Wukong appointed Bajie to lead with his rake. If the monsters returned, the pig would meet them first and earn the credit. Bajie grumbled that soldiers often returned with wounds even from victorious battles, but accepted the post. Sha followed with the packs. Xuanzang rode between his companions. Wukong kept his eyes on the trees, expecting the demon to make another attempt.

## The divided plum blossom

The monster had withdrawn to his den shaken by the encounter. Among his retainers was one who had once lived on Lion-Camel Ridge and escaped when Wukong destroyed its demon army. That survivor knew what the Monkey King's name meant. The master wanted to eat Xuanzang to prolong his life, but the knowledge that Wukong guarded the monk had put fear into him. Another underling proposed a cleverer method than attacking the four travelers together.

The plan was called the divided plum blossom. Three lesser demons capable of changing shape would disguise themselves as their master and lie in wait along the road. One would challenge Bajie, the next Wukong, and the third Sha Wujing. As each disciple rushed after a threatening figure, the true monster would remain above the trees. When Xuanzang was left alone, the master would be taken without having to defeat a single protector.

The plan worked because it used each disciple's sense of duty against him. Bajie saw an enemy break from the grass and hurried forward with his rake. Wukong saw another monster approaching the horse and raced to intercept it. Sha believed the third would reach Xuanzang unless he stepped between them. All three fought fiercely, each certain that the others were guarding the master. Their opponents retreated farther from the road.

Then the true monster descended from the mist. He seized Xuanzang from the saddle in one enormous hand and carried him into the mountain. The horse reared. The road fell silent. By the time the disciples recognized that they had been separated, their master had vanished again.

Inside the demon's den, the captor demanded that the cooking fires be lit. The wiser of his servants urged patience. Wukong would come looking for the monk. If they ate him at once, the monkey might destroy the entire mountain in revenge. They should bind Xuanzang in the rear garden, keep him alive, and wait until the disciples gave up searching. Then the banquet could be prepared without danger. The monster approved this plan and ordered the prisoner tied to a tree.

Xuanzang struggled against the ropes. Nearby another captive spoke to him: a woodcutter who had been seized days before. The woodcutter had an eighty-three-year-old mother at home who depended upon him. He was afraid to die because she would be left without anyone to feed or bury her. Xuanzang spoke of his own duty to the emperor and to the souls for whom the scriptures were meant. If he died here, he said, the journey and its purpose would fail. The woodcutter answered that his single household mattered to him as deeply as an empire mattered to the monk. They looked across the shadowed garden and wept together.

Outside, Wukong defeated the last decoy and hurried back. He found the horse, the road, and no master. Bajie and Sha returned from their own fights, equally bewildered. The four had entered the mist together; the three protectors had emerged without the one person they had sworn to guard. Wukong took up the reins and began searching the mountainside, calling Xuanzang's name into the fog.

The concealed den and its prisoners lay somewhere ahead. The disciples did not yet know where.

## Source and edition note

Original English scene-first retelling of *Journey to the West*, selected Chinese witness chapter 85, “心猿妒木母　魔主計吞禪.” [Chinese primary text, Wikisource](https://zh.wikisource.org/wiki/%E8%A5%BF%E9%81%8A%E8%A8%98/%E7%AC%AC085%E5%9B%9E). Dialogue and descriptive transitions are independently composed. The opening finishes the Dharma-Destroying Kingdom episode and names the reformed kingdom **Dharma-Honoring Kingdom (欽法國)**. The chapter closes with Xuanzang captive beside a woodcutter whose mother is 83, while the disciples are still searching the foggy mountain. The false-head deception and rescue belong to chapter 86.
