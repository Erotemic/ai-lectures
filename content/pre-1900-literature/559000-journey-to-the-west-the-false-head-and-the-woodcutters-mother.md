# Journey to the West: The false head and the woodcutter's mother

## The place called Linked-Caves

Sun Wukong searched the mist until he met Bajie running toward him, still angry about having been appointed the foremost fighter. The pig had defeated the monster who fought him, only to discover that the master was missing. Sha Wujing came from another direction with the same story. Wukong looked at their faces and understood the plan. Three fighting figures had drawn the three disciples apart; a fourth, concealed in the clouds, had taken Xuanzang from the middle of the road. Their strength had been divided as neatly as petals stripped from a plum blossom.

The monkey blamed no one for long. The master could not have been carried far, and arguing would only give the demons time to eat him. The three followed the mountain paths, scanning the slopes for an entrance. After a long search they saw a stone gateway half hidden beneath a cliff. Its inscription named Hidden Mist Mountain and Linked-Caves Den. The place was surrounded by flowering trees, clear water, and a landscape as attractive as any immortal retreat. Yet the door was shut, and there were armed creatures moving behind the cracks.

Bajie took the rake and smashed a hole in the stone door. "Bring out the Tang monk," he shouted, "or I will dig the rest of your house into the valley!" Within, the demons panicked. Their master had been hoping the three disciples might lose the trail. Now a pig had begun destroying the gate, and the Monkey King stood behind him. A scout looked through the broken opening and recognized Bajie. He reported that the pig was not much to fear. The danger was the monkey with the staff.

Wukong shouted his own challenge. The creature calling himself South Mountain King could either release the monk or answer for him. Inside, a clever servant proposed an answer neither honest nor brave. If the disciples were shown Xuanzang's head and told he had already been eaten, perhaps they would go away. The monster agreed. His servants were sent to make a head that would satisfy the eye of a grieving disciple.

## Two heads and a grave

The first head was carved from a willow root. Blood was smeared across it, and an attendant brought it to the opening on a tray. He addressed Wukong as Great Sage with the greatest respect and said that the monk had been torn apart by hungry servants. Only the head remained. Bajie began to cry before he had even seen it properly. Wukong demanded that it be thrown out.

The wooden head struck stone with a hollow knock. Wukong lifted it, turned it, and threw it down again. A real head, he said, would not make the sound of a block of wood. He smashed the imitation and showed his brothers the root inside. Bajie was outraged, but also relieved. The demons had not proved Xuanzang dead; they had only demonstrated the extent of their deceit. Wukong raised his staff and threatened to break the gate altogether.

The servant hurried back to report that the Monkey King knew the difference between a dead man and a piece of timber. The monster asked how to obtain a real head. That question revealed the cruelty of the den. There were human remains among its provisions, and the servants were able to bring out the head of a person already killed. They removed the hair and skin so that the features would be harder to recognize, set the head on the tray, and offered it as the true remains of the Tang monk.

This time the sound on the stone was different. Wukong examined the head and could find no quick proof that it was false. He had been sure he could distinguish a trick from evidence. Now the very knowledge that had exposed the willow root made the second offering more convincing. Bajie wept openly. Sha lowered his staff and cried. Wukong, who had faced the armies of Heaven without fear, looked at the bloody thing and felt the road fall away beneath his feet.

Bajie asked that they bury it before the heat corrupted what remained. He carried it to a slope where the ground was soft, dug with his rake, and made a mound. He placed willow branches at its sides in place of trees and arranged stones as offerings. Wukong rebuked him for presenting pebbles as food for the dead. Bajie answered that he had nothing else. A gesture could still express grief when no proper funeral objects were at hand. Sha knelt beside the mound, unable to believe their master had come to this.

But grief did not settle what should happen next. Wukong was not prepared to leave the monster in peace. He told Sha to remain by the grave and keep watch over the horse and luggage. He and Bajie would force their way into the den, kill the murderer, and avenge the monk. The irony was not yet visible to them: they were preparing to avenge a death that had not occurred, beside the grave of someone whose name they did not know.

## The South Mountain King

The monster came out with troops at his back. He called himself South Mountain King and boasted of his years of power. Wukong answered with a recital of his own earlier deeds, from Flower-Fruit Mountain to the rebellion in Heaven and the long service to Xuanzang. Bajie heard the familiar account and did not interrupt. There was a time for mocking the monkey's pride; they were not in that time now.

Clubs struck staffs. Rake met spear and sword. The demon's lesser troops pressed around the two disciples, and their master fought fiercely enough to force Wukong to use more than a casual blow. Then the monkey pulled hairs from his body and changed them into copies of himself. Iron staffs seemed to appear on every side. The monster's vanguard could not protect the front and rear at once. One great gray wolf, serving as an iron-backed captain, was struck down; the others retreated toward the den in panic. Bajie ran after them and shouted that this was what came of preparing a monk for supper.

The South Mountain King escaped and ordered the entrance filled with stones and earth. When Bajie tried to break through, the wall no longer shifted. The attackers had won the field but not reached the prisoner, and the prisoner they believed in was only a corpse. Wukong turned back to the little grave. Sha was still kneeling there, tending the poor offerings. Bajie joined him in mourning again, cursing the demon between sobs.

Wukong could not keep still. A den that had a front gate surely had another way out, he reasoned, for water and smoke and servants. He walked around the slope, listening. Near a stream he found an outlet running beneath a wall. He became a water rat, slipped through the narrow passage, and entered the inner court. There he saw servants laying strips of human flesh in the sun. For a moment he believed the second head had truly belonged to Xuanzang. He suppressed his fury and changed into a winged ant to pass unseen through the rooms.

From a beam in the main hall he heard a conversation that changed everything. The servants were laughing that the three disciples had believed the false evidence. They had seen Bajie bury the head and Wukong mourn it. The true Tang monk, they said, was still tied in the garden. The monster was waiting for the mourning to finish and the disciples to leave before he ate his captive. Wukong felt relief so fierce that he nearly fell from the beam. Then he flew toward the garden to find out with his own eyes.

## The men beneath the trees

Two figures were bound to separate trees. One was Xuanzang. The other was a woodcutter, thin with hunger and fear. Wukong dropped his disguise and whispered to his master. Xuanzang looked up, hardly believing the sight. The monkey told him about the heads, the grave, and the battle, but urged him not to shout. Wukong had the answer now and wanted to end the danger before exposing the hidden route by which he had entered.

He returned to the main hall as an insect and scattered enchanted hairs among the demons. They became sleep-bringing insects that crept into noses and ears. The lesser creatures began nodding where they stood. Their master fought off drowsiness for a time, rubbing his face and shaking his head. Wukong sent a second spell against him. At last the South Mountain King lay snoring with his men.

Wukong returned to the garden and untied Xuanzang. The monk immediately pointed to the other captive. The woodcutter had been held longer and feared that his aged mother would die waiting for him. Wukong cut his ropes too. The three escaped by the watercourse and crossed the stream. Behind them the entire den slept, ignorant that its most precious intended meal was climbing into the daylight.

On the hillside Sha heard a voice calling his name and thought grief had made him hear a ghost. Bajie rose from the mound with his eyes wet. Xuanzang stood before them alive. The pig began to laugh and cry at the same time. Sha bowed and embraced his master's hands. Wukong explained that the head in the grave had belonged to another victim, not to Xuanzang. Bajie, suddenly angry at having mourned before a false relic, dug up the grave and smashed the remains with his rake. Xuanzang stopped him. Whoever had died, he said, deserved burial. The creature who used the head to deceive them was guilty; the dead person was not. Bajie gathered what was left and buried it again.

The woodcutter watched the brothers and their master with astonishment. His captivity had been as real as Xuanzang's, and his need of rescue had not involved scriptures or royal promises. He had an old mother and a poor hut. Now, for the first time in days, it was possible that he would return to her.

## Fire and the way home

Wukong had not finished with the den. He went back by the hidden passage, bound the sleeping South Mountain King securely, and carried him out before the others. Bajie demanded a chance to strike the monster who had made them believe their master dead. Wukong pointed out that the rest of the den remained occupied by creatures who had killed travelers and kept human bodies for food. The woodcutter showed them where dead reeds, branches, and dry brush could be gathered. They piled fuel within the den; Wukong set it alight; Bajie fanned the flames with his enormous ears. The fire swept through the hidden chambers. The novel gives no gentler account of what happens to the lesser monsters inside.

The king awoke in bonds and heard his home burning. Bajie struck him with the rake. When the body fell, it assumed its true form, an animal spirit with the patterned coat of a leopard. For a moment the creature who had filled the mountain with wind and mist lay still, no longer grand or titled. Wukong turned away. There would be no celestial official descending to claim this beast as an escaped mount. The pilgrims had defeated it themselves.

The woodcutter asked the company to come to his home. His mother would want to thank the people who had saved him. The hut stood among trees at the edge of the mountain. From a distance they could see an elderly woman at the doorway, grieving and looking down the path. When she recognized her son she ran toward him. He fell at her feet, and she held him as though the whole mountain might try to take him back.

The son explained how he had been bound to a tree, how the Tang monk had been imprisoned beside him, and how the three disciples had brought them both out. The mother thanked the travelers repeatedly. She had little wealth and no feast fit for important guests, but she could offer food from the hillside. They sat together at a rough table and ate wild vegetables prepared with the care of a family that had expected never to eat together again. Even Bajie, who usually commented on every plain meal, spoke first of their host's generosity.

When it was time to leave, the woodcutter guided them back to the main road. He said that the country of Tianzhu was not impossibly distant now, less than a thousand miles away by the road he knew. Xuanzang heard the estimate and wondered how many such narrow escapes remained between them and the scriptures. He thanked the woodcutter for the meal and asked him to care for his mother. The man bowed, holding his staff, and returned up the hill.

The pilgrims went west. A king had renounced killing monks; a monster had been killed for seeking to eat one; and an old woman had received her son back alive. These were not the same kinds of rescue. The journey contained room for all three, and it was the last one that left the gentlest image behind: a poor house in the mountains with its door open and a family gathered around a table.

## Source and edition note

Original English scene-first retelling of *Journey to the West*, selected Chinese witness chapter 86, “木母助威征怪物　金公施法滅妖邪.” [Chinese primary text, Wikisource](https://zh.wikisource.org/wiki/%E8%A5%BF%E9%81%8A%E8%A8%98/%E7%AC%AC086%E5%9B%9E). Dialogue and narrative transitions independently composed. The chapter distinguishes the wooden false head from the genuine human head falsely attributed to Xuanzang, then ends after rescue of the woodcutter, defeat of the leopard spirit, the woodcutter's reunion with his mother, and the travelers' departure from the mountain.
