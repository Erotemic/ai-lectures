# Journey to the West — chapters 40–45: reading and evidence guide

**Prepared 16 November 2026.** Source companion to five original English narrative manuscripts, `514000`–`518000`, covering **six consecutive chapters** of the selected later-transmitted Chinese-language *Journey to the West*. Narrative dialogue is original English retelling, not quotation from a modern English translation. Red Boy, the Black Water River, the dragon courts, and Chechi's rain contest are **fictional events** in the novel, not events in the historical life of Xuanzang.

## Chapter-by-chapter continuity and fidelity

### Chapter 40 — a restored king and a dangerous rescue

- Chapter 39 left the Wuji kingdom's rightful ruler alive but did not yet narrate his departure ceremonies. Chapter 40 has him resume the crown after initially refusing in gratitude, with Xuanzang refusing to remain and rule. Departure is a new scene, not retrospective summary of a nonexistent prior scene.
- The demon, **Red Boy / the Holy Infant King**, is the son of the Bull Demon King and Rakshasi. He first scouts the party and later feigns being a victim bound to a pine tree. Both the child's voice and physical presentation are manipulated; Wukong is suspicious even when Xuanzang sees a captive boy.
- Wukong tries carrying the seeming child, senses his increasing weight, and strikes the abandoned shell after Red Boy leaves it. This act is seen by Xuanzang as violence toward a child, preserving the conflict over compassion and perception rather than making the monk a willing conspirator.
- Red Boy creates the storm that abducts Xuanzang. After Wukong and Bajie threaten to abandon the mission, **Sha Wujing** reminds them of the pledge. The local gods identify the demon and the cave. Wukong's confidence that the son must respect his father's **old sworn brother** is hope, not a fact the story vindicates.

### Chapter 41 — a fire that water fails to quench

- The claimed uncle/niece bond gives Wukong no automatic authority: Red Boy rejects the claim and meets the monkey in combat. The five fire carts are arranged for a specifically named **Samadhi True Fire**. Wukong can endure flame heat but is incapacitated by smoke affecting his eyes after the earlier Laozi furnace episode.
- Sha Wujing proposes the dragon-rain tactic. The four Dragon Kings' rain is not enough; the novel distinguishes rain summoned without a full celestial weather order from the true magical fire. A torrent can knock Wukong into the stream without extinguishing the demon's flame.
- Wukong's apparent death and **Bajie's revival work** are extended dramatic events; do not quietly rewrite them as a harmless scorch. The disciple calls first for his master when consciousness returns.
- Wukong is too weak to fly to Guanyin, so Bajie goes. Red Boy anticipates the route and impersonates **Guanyin** to lure him into a sack. Wukong then infiltrates Fire Cloud Cave disguised as a bundle and fly. The chapter ends with the demon's six servants dispatched to invite the Bull Demon King, giving Wukong a new plan.

### Chapter 42 — counterfeit father, the vase, and the knife lotus

- The false Bull Demon King is exposed by Red Boy's question about **the child's birth date and hour**. Wukong knows the oath of brotherhood, not intimate family details; the disguise ends in failure.
- Guanyin reacts strongly to the misuse of her image. Her pure vase is thrown to sea and carried back on a giant turtle. Wukong's failure to lift it shows how its contents and purpose differ from a normal jar of water. She borrows **thirty-six blades** through Muzha, also known as Hui'an, before making her trap.
- The local mountain spirits receive directions to remove threatened living creatures. Water from the vessel transforms the setting near Fire Cloud Cave. Red Boy attacks the apparent bodhisattva on her lotus throne; she disappears, leaving him to claim the throne, whose petals turn back into blades.
- The child initially submits to Guanyin, then attacks again as soon as the blades vanish. The novel distinguishes a reluctant promise from a genuine change of conduct. Guanyin places **five rings**—head, wrists, and ankles—on him and recites their spell. **Do not move his final departure or Xuanzang's rescue into chapter 42**; they open chapter 43.

### Chapter 43 — water, rescue, and family privilege

- Guanyin claims the subdued Red Boy as **Good Wealth Boy**, a name associated with the Sudhana figure in Buddhist traditions. The child is brought to follow her; this should not be conflated with the historical religious narrative. The prisoners are then rescued from the cave by Wukong and Sha Wujing.
- Xuanzang's reaction to the ominous river provokes Wukong's reminder of the **Heart Sutra**; the conversation is about fear during a real journey, not a lecture on abstract doctrine.
- The **Black Water River** boat is offered by the monster who usurped the genuine river god's underwater palace. Xuanzang and Bajie are taken; Sha Wujing battles the usurper, then the displaced god explains the political situation.
- The monster is **a nephew of the western-sea Dragon King Ao Shun**, through Ao Shun's sister and her husband (the dragon beheaded by Wei Zheng in the earlier novel). The palace is not a property grant to the nephew. Wukong seeks redress from Ao Shun, who dispatches **Prince Mo'ang** rather than pretending the family has no responsibility. Mo'ang arrests his cousin, the captives are freed, and the river god opens a dry road through the water.

### Chapter 44 — the captive monks and the temple comedy

- Chechi's three royal Daoist masters—**Tiger Strength, Deer Strength, and Goat Strength**—have gained favor after relieving a drought. Buddhist institutions are suppressed. The remaining **five hundred** captive monks are described as survivors of much larger losses, including exhaustion, disease, and attempted self-destruction. They have been preserved by protecting spirits yet continue to suffer. Both rescue and coercive religious power remain central to the scene.
- Wukong poses as a Daoist, offers an elaborate familial pretext to free the men, and then **kills the two supervising Daoists**. The manuscript should not erase this choice or pretend the monks' fear of retaliation is irrational. Wukong distributes protective hairs and later confronts the royal authorities.
- At the great Daoist temple the brothers impersonate the **Three Pure Ones** and eat the offerings. Bajie takes their original statues to the **privy**. When the priests demand divine medicine, the disguises culminate in a bodily-function joke. It is vulgar by design; reducing it to a harmless misunderstanding loses the novel's humor and the priests' grievance.

### Chapter 45 — weather as a contested authority

- At court a minister invokes the treatment due foreign envoys carrying Tang's travel documentation. The three honored Daoist masters accuse the travelers of the deaths and the temple outrage. Neither side starts from neutral ground.
- A local petition for rain becomes a state-sponsored test. Tiger Strength's rituals produce genuine attempted action by heavenly weather officials. Wukong **intercepts and commands** those officials to withhold the rain, then arranges for rain to fall when Xuanzang prays. It is not that the Daoist never had any access to the celestial apparatus.
- Wukong's staff signals wind, clouds, rainfall, and clearance. At the end he calls the **four dragon kings to appear visibly** so the court can assess whom they obeyed. The three masters remain resistant. The subsequent tests of **chapter 46** are an intentional unresolved boundary for this batch.
