## Documentary sources

- [Project Gutenberg, *Anna Karenina*, translated by Constance Garnett](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/1399/pg1399-images.html), numbered as ebook 1399. A translated reading text, not an exhaustive witness to Tolstoy's Russian revisions.
- [Bibliothèque nationale de France, *Anna Karenina* uniform title record](https://catalogue.bnf.fr/ark:/12148/cb11970672t): Parts I–VII appeared in *Russkii vestnik* from 1875–1877, Part VIII separately in 1877, with the first complete definitive book edition dated 1878.
- [Wikisource, Garnett translation catalog](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Anna_Karenina_(Garnett)): a secondary access route to the Garnett translation, derived from Project Gutenberg rather than separately collated against 1901 print.

## Important interpretive limits

1. **Anna's thoughts are not external evidence of Vronsky's intentions.** Suspicion and jealousy are experiences narrated from within, but cannot automatically settle questions of his conduct.
2. **The husband's legal advantage is significant, but legal generalizations require care.** Russian marriage and custody rules are historically specific, and the novel's events alone are not a comprehensive legal history.
3. **Anna's death is not the novel's final event.** Part VIII is part of the work; it discusses aftermath, politics, and Levin's faith. Its separate periodical publication is bibliographically relevant.
4. **Levin's views do not transparently equal Tolstoy's complete philosophy.** Similarity between the author and the landowner is important; narrative judgments remain open to analysis.
5. **The novel presents agricultural life after emancipation of the serfs**, but the landowner's perspective does not stand in for peasant testimony or a complete social history.
6. **Characters have multiple Russian forms of address.** Avoid conflating Alexei Alexandrovich Karenin with Alexei Kirillovich Vronsky, or Konstantin Levin with his brother Nikolai Levin. Anna's son Seryozha and daughter Annie are distinct.
7. **Careful discussion of suicide does not turn tragedy into a moral reward or inevitability.** Anna is shown under severe psychological and social pressure, but assigning a modern psychiatric diagnosis would require evidence not contained in the novel.
8. **English translation changes register and syntactic texture.** Analyses of exact diction should name the translator and, when necessary, compare Russian wording.

## Possible future enrichment

A philological expansion could compare the serial installments with the 1878 book, examine the specific dispute over Part VIII using contemporary documentary correspondence, and collate narrative examples in the Russian and several English translations. Source anchors in this pack provide a route to passages and provenance, not that completed research.
