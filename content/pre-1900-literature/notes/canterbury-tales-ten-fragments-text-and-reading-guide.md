# Geoffrey Chaucer, *The Canterbury Tales*: tale map, source guide and unfinished design

This section contains **28 story-focused lectures (275000–302000)** and **9 critical
studies (303000–311000)**. Every surviving one of the **twenty-four tales** has
an individual story treatment, with the long Knight’s Tale in two parts. The
General Prologue, the Wife of Bath’s Prologue and the Retraction receive their
own lectures. The Cook’s and Squire’s Tales **remain unfinished**. Chaucer’s
storytelling contest and round-trip pilgrimage never reach the promised final
supper in the surviving text. These lectures do not supply invented endings.

## Reading the texts without resolving their tensions prematurely

- **The text is incomplete and its order is an editorial issue.** Surviving
  manuscripts differ. The ten fragments and individual tale links provide a
  more reliable description than pretending Chaucer finalized the whole work.
  The Host’s promised homeward journey and dinner never occur in an extant
  narrative. Treat the Retraction as a transmitted textual conclusion rather
  than evidence of a fully finished and fixed narrative plan.
- **Individual speakers are not simply Chaucer.** The pilgrims’ personalities,
  social positions, speeches and prior quarrels matter. A Pardoner can preach a
  true lesson for fraudulent ends; a Knight can tell a complex romance without
  the story making Emily’s wishes the source of every decision.
- **Genre matters.** The Knight’s courtly romance, Miller’s and Reeve’s fabliaux,
  Constance’s hagiographic ordeal, the Wife’s Arthurian tale, the Monk’s
  tragedies, and the Nun’s Priest’s beast fable invite different interpretive
  expectations. The long prose Melibee and Parson’s discourse are integral,
  not silent gaps to skip because they are not verse narratives.
- **Coercion is not consent.** A retelling of Griselda, Virginia, Dorigen, or
  the Wife’s knight should not describe threats and legal vulnerability as
  naturally admirable or pretend that medieval storytelling agrees unanimously
  on women’s freedom.
- **The Prioress’s Tale contains a blood libel.** Its accusation that Jews
  murder a Christian child for religious reasons is **false** and belongs to a
  medieval antisemitic tradition that caused persecution. It must not be
  retold as an independent historical report. The history of Jewish expulsion
  from England in 1290 matters. Critical disagreement over Chaucer’s narrative
  intent does not erase the story’s harmful content.
- **Dates and biography:** Chaucer’s career in royal and civic administration
  is historically documented in part, while the dating and order of many
  individual tales are reconstructed, not directly recorded as one dated
  compositional calendar. His death is usually assigned to 1400.

## Four close-reading exercises

1. Compare the **Knight’s** and **Miller’s** approaches to love, property,
   and comedy. Identify a passage that works differently once the teller’s
   social role and the previous tale are remembered.
2. Compare **the Wife’s Prologue** with the **Clerk’s Tale** and **Franklin’s
   Tale**: who can demand obedience, who can revoke a promise, and under what
   conditions would a promise genuinely be free?
3. Read the **Pardoner’s Prologue** immediately before the Pardoner’s Tale.
   Explain why an effective moral warning does not establish the speaker’s
   honesty. Compare with the Canon’s Yeoman’s fraudulent demonstrations.
4. Read the **Prioress’s Tale** as narrative rhetoric: who receives an
   individual voice, whose actions are simply asserted, which claims are
   fantasies rather than history, and how does the miracle form encourage
   selective compassion?

## Scope of review

These are original retellings and interpretive lectures built with the
referenced primary-text access, university editorial guides, and selected
historical scholarship. Specific story structures and consequential
historical claims were checked against those references, not against a
complete line-by-line collation of every manuscript witness. No film, song,
image asset, or recording is included. Word counts are descriptive only.
