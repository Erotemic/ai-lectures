The third day's ruler is **Neifile**, and its organizing subject is people
who gain a desired object through effort and ingenuity, or recover something
lost. The fourth day's ruler is **Filostrato**, whose theme is love ending
unhappily. **Dioneo** normally speaks last, and his established privilege to
depart from the assigned theme helps explain the ending of IV.10.

## How to read Day Three

Day Three opens in a new residence and a new garden. This change of setting is
not disposable scene painting: the ten story tellers have resources to choose
where to live, to separate private play from urban disaster, and to employ
servants to sustain their orderly household. The garden's fountain and channels
reconcile aesthetic delight and agricultural management. The stories that
follow also examine efforts to turn desire into planned outcomes, but the
outcome may be good for the planner and harmful to another person.

- **III.1, Masetto:** track who hears whom, how a pretense of muteness alters
  the convent's estimation of a laborer, and how Masetto finally sets a limit
  on expectations placed upon him. The tale's account of convent sexuality is
  anticlerical comedy, not evidence that a particular convent behaved this way.
- **III.2, the groom and Queen Theodelinda:** distinguish a clever tactic
  from a defensible act. The queen believes she is admitting her husband;
  the groom exploits that belief. The king's clipped-hair test concerns
  identity evidence, privacy and the security of a royal household.
- **III.3, the lady and confessor:** listen for the difference between
  *reported* speech and a trustworthy account. The woman uses a friar as
  an unwitting messenger; the apparent moral complaint is a set of directions.
- **III.4, Dom Felice and Fra Puccio:** religious exercises are promised as
  a path to immediate purification and are used as a way to distract the
  husband. The satire concerns a particular elaborate fraud, not a general
  refutation of spiritual discipline.
- **III.5, Zima's gift:** the horse is material leverage in a conversation
  where the woman's husband treats attention to his wife as negotiable. What
  does Zima's one-sided exchange make possible, and whose agreement is missing?
- **III.6, Ricciardo and Catella:** deception depends on false identity and
  falsely manufactured jealousy. A fictional confession made under fraud is
  not evidence of freely chosen desire.
- **III.7, Tedaldo:** the returned lover disguises himself as a pilgrim and
  manipulates multiple accounts of events while also rescuing an innocent
  accused man. What kinds of evidence could distinguish an effective plan
  from an honest argument?
- **III.8, Ferondo:** the abbot orchestrates an apparent death and an invented
  experience of purgatory. This is an elaborate abuse of institutional and
  religious power, even when narrated as successful comedy.
- **III.9, Gillette of Narbonne:** the account expands into a romance of cure,
  marriage under pressure, and the fulfillment of an impossible-sounding
  condition. The wife's ingenuity does not settle every question about consent
  and bodily substitution. Compare the tale with the later *All's Well That
  Ends Well* tradition without assuming every detail is identical.
- **III.10, Alibech and Rustico:** the hermit consciously disguises sexual
  conduct as religious teaching for a young novice. Read the story as satire
  of manipulative authority and double meaning; it should not be sanitized
  into a freely informed courtship.

## How to read Day Four

At the opening of the Fourth Day, **Boccaccio interrupts the fictional
storytellers** and directly answers criticism of his work. He is writing
in prose, in the Florentine vernacular, and for a readership that includes
women. He tells his own little story about a sheltered young man who sees
women for the first time and is told they are "goslings." The anecdote is
an argument about the difficulty of abolishing desire merely by controlling
language or isolation. It is not one of Filostrato's ten tales, nor should it
be numbered IV.1.

- **IV.1, Ghismonda:** a father's claim to guard honor destroys an adult
  woman's life; her sustained argument about virtue and rank must be heard
  before the catastrophe, not merely summarized afterward.
- **IV.2, Fra Alberto:** a professed angel is an impersonation and a means of
  coercive access; the Venice street farce differs in style from noble tragedy.
- **IV.3, three sisters:** an attempt to secure freedom through flight
  degenerates into jealousy, killings and political manipulation. The duke's
  bargain with Madeleine is made under threat of her sister's execution.
- **IV.4, Gerbino:** contrast a lover's private ideal with a ruler's glove of
  safe conduct and the daughter's actual vulnerability between marriage
  diplomacy and military seizure.
- **IV.5, Lisabetta:** the basil pot, dream and concealed remains create
  a form of mourning forbidden by merchant family authority. What does a
  remembered object allow its owner to say without speech?
- **IV.6, Andreuola:** a secretly married woman seeks to return Gabriotto's
  body to his family; the magistrate's attempt at coercion turns a supposed
  search for truth into an abuse of legal office.
- **IV.7, Simona:** the deaths around the sage plant are part of a fictional
  toxicological explanation. Compare the initial accusation against a poor
  working woman with the demonstration she is required to perform.
- **IV.8, Girolamo:** a mother sends away her son to interrupt an attachment
  across social classes. Salvestra's new marriage and refusal matter even as
  the tale ends with a double death.
- **IV.9, two Guillaumes:** the eaten-heart motif circulates through medieval
  romantic narrative. A literary plot about imposed knowledge is not a
  verified biographical record of the purported lovers.
- **IV.10, Ruggieri:** the sleeping draught, stolen chest and mistaken charge
  supply a comic counterpoint to the day's repeated deaths. Compare the
  mistaken body removal here with Andreuola's treatment in IV.6.

## Guide to discussion

1. When does ingenuity amount to achievement, and when does it simply shift a
   cost to somebody with less power? Compare III.1, III.2, III.6 and III.9.
2. In the frame, Neifile's garden is designed as a refuge from disorder.
   How do III.1 and IV.7 transform the idea of an enclosed garden?
3. Compare the use of religious discourse in III.4, III.8, III.10 and IV.2.
   What makes each deception plausible, and who gets to define its meaning?
4. The author's goslings story has a young man who has never met women.
   How does it comment on restrictive education and on the intended audience
   of Boccaccio's prose? What does it *not* prove about every woman's wish?
5. Compare Ghismonda's long public case with Lisabetta's silent care for the
   basil. Whose family claims the right to control a relationship, and how?
6. Both IV.6 and IV.10 involve a supposedly dead young man being moved in
   secrecy. Which facts change the outcome, and how does the genre change
   what the audience expects?
7. What is the moral weight of a reader's enjoyment of an ingenious plot when
   a participant within that plot has been deceived, coerced or deprived of
   essential information?

## Scope and textual caution

These lectures are original, self-contained narratives with interpretive
commentary. The plot, roles, day numbering and consequential turning points
were reviewed against the primary Rigg text and day/tale index. This is not an
Italian critical-edition collation, a medical reconstruction, a medieval
legal history, or an assertion that each narrative actually occurred.
The story tellers' literary voices must be distinguished from historical
Boccaccio and from the opinions of the lecture authors. Spoken word counts
measure transcript contents; no fixed lecture-length rule is imposed.
Everything in this installment is text, with no image or audio binaries.
