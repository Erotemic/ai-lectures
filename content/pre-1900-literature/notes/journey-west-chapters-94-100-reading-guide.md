# Journey to the West: chapters 94–100 — final reading and evidence guide

**Scope:** The seven concluding original-English narrative manuscripts, `567000`–`573000`, cover selected Chinese-witness chapters 94–100, bringing the expanded retelling to **all 100 chapters in 85 playable manuscripts**. Each manuscript is designed for uninterrupted narration, with original English dialogue and scene transitions; sidecar evidence records identify fictional narrative events, not historical facts about Xuanzang or the Tang dynasty. No fixed duration target was imposed.

**Primary text:** [*Xiyou ji*, Chinese Wikisource chapter index](https://zh.wikisource.org/wiki/%E8%A5%BF%E9%81%8A%E8%A8%98). Literary background: [*Journey to the West* (Wikipedia)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Journey_to_the_West), [Wu Cheng'en (Wikipedia)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wu_Cheng%27en), and [Xuanzang (Wikipedia)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Xuanzang). The selected online Chinese text is a later transmitted witness, **not a leaf-for-leaf facsimile of the 1592 Shidetang edition**; authorship is traditionally attributed to Wu Cheng'en but debated. The chapter-nine River Child material is edition-sensitive, as documented in the [source and edition map](journey-west-expanded-retelling-source-map.md).

## Chapter 94 — A court wedding and Wukong's bee (`567000`)

Primary source: [西遊記/第094回](https://zh.wikisource.org/wiki/%E8%A5%BF%E9%81%8A%E8%A8%98/%E7%AC%AC094%E5%9B%9E).

- **Opening continuity:** The supposed princess of Tianzhu has thrown her embroidered marriage ball at Xuanzang in chapter 93. The capital now expects him to become her husband. Wukong understands the danger and insists that they play along until he can examine her closely.
- Bajie, Sha, and Wukong are summoned to court. The king orders a banquet in his gardens; the disciples' appearances and conduct supply the humor while the real situation remains frightening for Xuanzang, who is bound by monastic vows.
- The court sets a wedding date. Xuanzang repeatedly asks his disciple not to abandon him, and Wukong offers private reassurance while speaking publicly as though accepting the king's plan. The **supposed princess wants the three disciples sent away**, fearing their ability to see through her.
- The disciples receive their travel credentials and appear to depart. Wukong secretly leaves a hair-body double with Bajie and Sha; he becomes a **bee** and slips into the palace.
- **Exact chapter-ending boundary:** As the king and the frightened Xuanzang approach the wedding, the bee alights near his ear and says that Wukong is here. The woman is still treated as a princess, and her actual supernatural identity **is not yet disclosed**. That revelation belongs to chapter 95.

## Chapter 95 — The lunar Jade Rabbit and the recovered daughter (`568000`)

Primary source: [西遊記/第095回](https://zh.wikisource.org/wiki/%E8%A5%BF%E9%81%8A%E8%A8%98/%E7%AC%AC095%E5%9B%9E).

- The bee's proximity allows Wukong to scrutinize the impostor. She recognizes the threat and flees; the bridal celebration breaks into a confrontation.
- The impostor fights using a **mortar pestle** associated with her lunar identity. Wukong pursues her until **Taiyin, the lunar goddess**, appears to reclaim the escaped **Jade Rabbit**. The story gives the rabbit a grievance and an explanation for her imposture; the narration does not make that explanation permission to imprison another person.
- Wukong recalls the woman hidden behind the monastery wall from chapter 93. The king and queen travel to **Gold-Spreading Temple**, and the room is opened in their presence. They identify their **actual daughter**, dirty and distressed after extended captivity, and reunite with her.
- Royal aid to the temple and Wukong's advice about cocks clearing dangerous centipedes connect the personal rescue to the passage of other travelers through the region. The reconstituted family thanks the monks; a celebration and generous farewell follow.
- **Closing boundary:** The pilgrims depart Tianzhu's capital and resume the western road. The princess affair is resolved here; the next chapter begins with the **Kou household**, not a further marriage complication.

## Chapter 96 — The household that counted to ten thousand (`569000`)

Primary source: [西遊記/第096回](https://zh.wikisource.org/wiki/%E8%A5%BF%E9%81%8A%E8%A8%98/%E7%AC%AC096%E5%9B%9E).

- After leaving the royal city, the party encounters a prosperous house bearing a public statement of pious hospitality. The elderly owner, **Kou Hong**, has vowed to feed **10,000 visiting monks**, and his count is **9,996** when Xuanzang and his **three disciples** arrive. Their four places finish the vow.
- Kou Hong and his household give them food and shelter. Bajie's appetite and willingness to linger contrast with Xuanzang's concern that pleasure in another family's home could prevent him from reaching the scriptures.
- The women and sons of the household wish to offer further hospitality in their own names. Their goodwill is not denied, but the pilgrims cannot extend a vow from host to host forever. The narrative distinguishes generosity from a demand to prolong the guests' stay.
- Kou Hong arranges a grand farewell, with banners, feasting, and neighbors lining the road. This public procession establishes a story reason for outside observers to notice his wealth.
- **Exact chapter-ending boundary:** The monks have departed, and the rich farewell has attracted attention. The **violent robbery and Kou Hong's death occur in chapter 97**, not in this chapter's celebration.

## Chapter 97 — The real robbers, false charges, and Kou Hong's return (`570000`)

Primary source: [西遊記/第097回](https://zh.wikisource.org/wiki/%E8%A5%BF%E9%81%8A%E8%A8%98/%E7%AC%AC097%E5%9B%9E).

- Bandits attack the Kou household after seeing the procession and kill **Kou Hong** during the robbery. His wife and sons, already upset by the guests' departure and confused about the event, accuse the monks of conspiring with the thieves.
- The pilgrims encounter the **actual robbers** and recover the stolen valuables. Officials then mistake their possession of those goods for evidence of guilt, arresting them. The distinction between a helpful recovery and possessing incriminating property is essential to the injustice.
- Wukong resorts to nighttime interventions and supernatural pressure to find a way out of the judicial trap. He descends to the underworld, where **Kshitigarbha** and the authorities permit Kou Hong's return from death.
- The resurrected host supplies testimony about the murder, relieving the pilgrims of the false accusation. The family and magistrates have to face the difference between their assumptions and the robber's actions. The resolution is **not** a story in which the party killed or robbed its host.
- **Closing boundary:** The misunderstanding and death are resolved, and the pilgrims return to their journey. Chapter 98 begins with arrival near **Spirit Mountain / Thunderclap Monastery**, not another Kou-household trial.

## Chapter 98 — The bottomless crossing and two sets of scriptures (`571000`)

Primary source: [西遊記/第098回](https://zh.wikisource.org/wiki/%E8%A5%BF%E9%81%8A%E8%A8%98/%E7%AC%AC098%E5%9B%9E).

- The party reaches the real western holy mountain and passes **Jade-True Monastery**. A dangerous **single-log bridge** stands before them; Xuanzang refuses to cross it. A **bottomless ferry** then receives him, and he sees his former mortal body drifting away, showing the transformation that will frame the last chapters.
- Before the Buddha and the scripture library, Xuanzang asks for the texts he has traveled to obtain. **Ananda and Kasyapa** request an offering. The pilgrims have little to give and are handed scrolls that turn out to be **wordless**, a meaningful but unacceptable substitute for texts that can be studied and copied back in Tang territory.
- A heavenly emissary associated with **Dipankara** scatters the bundles so that the travelers discover their blank condition. The disciples return to complain, and the Buddha offers an explanation concerning wordless truth and the value assigned to the written collection. The narration retains the practical and moral tension of the requested payment rather than removing it.
- Xuanzang surrenders his **purple-gold begging bowl**, an imperial gift from the journey's beginning. This time the party inspects the scrolls and receives **5,048 written scrolls** drawn from the library's categories. Eight heavenly guardians are charged with returning them east in time.
- **Exact chapter-ending boundary:** The party is lifted from Spirit Mountain by the heavenly escort carrying written scriptures. The **old turtle, the wet volumes, and the eighty-first ordeal occur in chapter 99**, not here.

## Chapter 99 — Eighty-one ordeals and a forgotten question (`572000`)

Primary source: [西遊記/第099回](https://zh.wikisource.org/wiki/%E8%A5%BF%E9%81%8A%E8%A8%98/%E7%AC%AC099%E5%9B%9E).

- Guanyin's record totals **eighty** ordeals. An **eighty-first** is needed for the sacred count, and the guardians leave the pilgrims beside **Heaven-Reaching River**, where they had earlier met the Chen family.
- The **old white turtle** who transported the pilgrims in chapter 49 reappears and asks whether Xuanzang delivered its promised question to the Buddha: how long it must remain a turtle before achieving a changed form. Xuanzang has **forgotten** to ask. The turtle throws them, their baggage, and the scriptures into the water.
- The party retrieves and spreads the scrolls to dry. Pages of **the Sutra of the Buddha's Life** stick to a stone; separating them damages part of the writing. The old turtle's grievance and the imperfections of the transmitted text remain visible even after formal success at Spirit Mountain.
- The Chen family recognizes the pilgrims and brings them to **Life-Saving Temple**, erected to commemorate their earlier rescue of the family's children. The children have grown, and the neighborhood once governed by a child-sacrificing river spirit is free of that ritual.
- The villagers want to keep feeding the visitors. Remembering how hospitality delayed them at Kou Hong's house, the pilgrims leave by night, but the **eight guardians lift them into the air** before any new earthly ordeal begins.
- **Exact closing boundary:** The party is flying **east**, not yet arriving at Chang'an. Imperial delivery and the final religious appointments occur in **chapter 100**.

## Chapter 100 — Chang'an, final appointments, and the missing fillet (`573000`)

Primary source: [西遊記/第100回](https://zh.wikisource.org/wiki/%E8%A5%BF%E9%81%8A%E8%A8%98/%E7%AC%AC100%E5%9B%9E).

- The eight guardians carry the party and their scriptures to **Chang'an**, where Emperor **Taizong** receives Xuanzang and hears of the completed pilgrimage. The emperor writes a **preface praising the Buddhist teaching** and orders that the scriptures be copied and distributed.
- The celestial guardians cannot leave the pilgrims at court forever; they return them to the Buddha's mountain to complete the mission. **The imperial delivery is real within the fiction**, but the supernatural itinerary is not historical documentation of the historical Xuanzang's actual journey.
- The Buddha grants five **different** appointments: **Xuanzang, Candana Merit Buddha**; **Wukong, Victorious Fighting Buddha**; **Bajie, Cleanser of Altars** (not a Buddha); **Sha Wujing, Golden-Bodied Arhat**; and **the dragon-horse, Eight-Section Heavenly Dragon Horse**, restored to dragon form.
- Wukong asks to have the tightening fillet taken off, thinking that his new status should end Xuanzang's ability to control him through pain. Xuanzang tells him to touch his head. **The fillet is already gone**, without a loosening spell. This closes the constraint imposed after Wukong's early liberation in chapter 14.
- **Final boundary:** The appointments and the vanished fillet conclude the novel. This retelling closes there too. No new demon confrontation, next pilgrimage, or later reunion is invented as a sequel.

- [*Journey to the West* (Wikipedia)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Journey_to_the_West) and [*Xuanzang* (Wikipedia)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Xuanzang) are orientation references, not replacements for citing individual Chinese chapters.
