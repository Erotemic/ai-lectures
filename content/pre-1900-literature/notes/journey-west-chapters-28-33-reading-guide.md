## Chapter-by-chapter scene and continuity checks

### Chapter 28 — A mountain recovered, a master lost

1. Wukong's return is not a cheerful reset: Flower-Fruit Mountain was devastated during Erlang's campaign, and most of the old monkey host is dead or scattered. The small surviving group must tell its king what has happened while he was imprisoned or absent.
2. Human hunters have preyed on the survivors. Wukong answers with a devastating, lethal magical wind. His action is no mere threatening display: people die. He subsequently calls for supplies, rain, trees, and renewed settlement. Both destruction and rebuilding belong in the narration.
3. Without Wukong, Bajie is assigned to seek food but falls asleep. Sha goes to find Bajie; Xuanzang waits with the horse and baggage. Xuanzang then leaves the road after seeing a gold-topped building he believes may be a sacred site.
4. The apparent temple is Wanzi Mountain's Boyue Cave. Its occupant, the Yellow-Robed Lord, captures Xuanzang. Bajie and Sha locate the cave and begin an inconclusive battle against its ruler. Chapter 28 ends before their rescue succeeds.

### Chapter 29 — Baihuaxiu's letter and the second defeat

1. Baihuaxiu identifies herself as the third princess of Baoxiang, carried away from a mid-autumn celebration thirteen years earlier. She has been forced to live in the demon's cave and has borne two sons. Her words and later behavior describe captivity, not a freely chosen romantic interlude.
2. The princess writes home and secures Xuanzang's freedom, then skillfully persuades the Yellow-Robed Lord to desist. The successful first liberation is hers, not simply Bajie and Sha's martial victory.
3. The king's grief is met by ministers and generals who prefer to put the burden on the unusual visiting pilgrims. Bajie boasts and demonstrates his size-changing powers before the court; Sha accompanies him back to Boyue Cave.
4. In the second battle the disciples lack the hidden help that had aided them earlier. Bajie abandons Sha and hides. Sha, now captured, tells a protective lie to avoid exposing Baihuaxiu's secret letter. The demon believes the lie and later goes to Baoxiang disguised as a handsome husband.

### Chapter 30 — An invented tiger and the courage of the horse

1. At court, the disguised Yellow-Robed Lord claims to have rescued the princess from a tiger, then identifies Xuanzang as the tiger disguised as a monk. A spell makes the innocent pilgrim appear as a tiger to the king and ministers; he is imprisoned in an iron cage.
2. The dragon horse is a participant, not an inert animal. Recognizing the disaster, he returns to dragon form and then disguises himself as a performer in the palace. He asks to dance with the monster's sword and tries to strike; the demon wounds him with an iron candlestick.
3. The dragon reaches the stable, becomes the white horse again, and appeals to Bajie directly. Bajie first wants to disband the party; the dragon insists that Wukong is needed. Bajie goes to Flower-Fruit Mountain and tries to avoid confessing that he needs the monkey.

### Chapter 31 — What it costs to come back

1. Bajie eventually tells the truth; Wukong agrees to return. He bathes before meeting Xuanzang again, bringing forward the tension between his old mountain life and his pilgrim duty.
2. Wukong captures the two sons of Baihuaxiu and the Yellow-Robed Lord. He orders Bajie and Sha to throw them down before the palace so their father will return in rage. **Both boys are killed.** A faithful account must not erase their deaths as an incidental prank or turn their mother into someone who consented to it.
3. Disguised as Baihuaxiu, Wukong tricks the demon into showing a hidden cultivated inner elixir, takes it into his own mouth, fights him, and then pursues the mystery of the demon's disappearance to Heaven.
4. Heaven's officials determine that the missing star officer is Kuimu Lang, the Kui Wood Wolf. He claims that Baihuaxiu had been his beloved celestial partner before her human birth. This is his self-exculpatory **testimony** to the Jade Emperor, not independent confirmation that her captivity was voluntary. Her earlier letter and words resist that interpretation.
5. The star officer is punished administratively and recalled. Wukong brings Baihuaxiu home, removes Xuanzang's tiger enchantment, and rejoins the party. The reconciliation is real but does not magically erase the harms along the way.

### Chapter 32 — The wrong scout and the wrong traveler

1. A warning on Flat-Top Mountain prompts Wukong to send Bajie to investigate. Bajie immediately arranges to sleep and concocts a false report; Wukong follows secretly and exposes the lies, a comic continuation of the earlier forest incident.
2. At Lotus Cave, the Gold Horn and Silver Horn Kings learn of the travelers. Silver Horn defeats and captures Bajie. The kings want Xuanzang for the supposed immortality granted by his flesh.
3. Silver Horn becomes a wounded elderly Daoist. Xuanzang's compassion obliges Wukong to carry him despite Wukong's knowledge that the stranger is a demon. This exposes a tension already present in the White Bone Demon affair, without making Xuanzang stupid or Wukong infallible.

### Chapter 33 — The burden of mountains and the false sky

1. Silver Horn uses his powers to weigh Wukong down with Mount Sumeru, Mount Emei, and Mount Tai. The last immobilizes him. Silver Horn then captures Sha Wujing and Xuanzang; the captives wait in Lotus Cave, where Bajie is also held.
2. Gods whose mountains have been commandeered answer Wukong's call and lift the weight. He tricks the demon servants Jingxigui (Sharp-Witted Ghost) and Linglichong (Clever Insect), who carry a purple-gold red gourd and a white jade bottle capable of imprisoning opponents.
3. Wukong creates a hair-made false gourd and asserts that it can hold the sky. Nezha secures a vast dark heavenly banner and covers the apparent sky, then lifts it again on cue. The two servants believe the feat and exchange their real treasures for Wukong's imitation.
4. **Exact stopping point:** Wukong has acquired the gourd and bottle by deception; the treasure-carriers leave pleased with the fake gourd. The monks remain in the cave. Do not narrate chapter 34's negotiations and consequences as though they happened in chapter 33.

## Notes on retelling and tone

- Preserve comedy: Bajie's sleep-and-alibi routine, Wukong's pointed comments, the absurdity of a gourd that appears to swallow the sky, and heavenly officers' irritation with the Monkey King. Preserve the human stakes in the same narrative rather than substituting comedy for consequences.
- Do not treat Kui Wood Wolf's heavenly-romance defense as a factual override of Baihuaxiu's own testimony; it is important who speaks each claim and before which authority.
- Wukong remains both a formidable rescuer and a character capable of lethal overreaction. The killing of the hunters and of the princess's children is visible in the original narrative and should remain visible.
- The sidecar anchors label these **fictional narrative events**; these pages are evidence of what the transmitted literary story says, not historical evidence for magical or court events.
- Relevant background: [*Journey to the West*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Journey_to_the_West), [Sun Wukong](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monkey_King), [Wu Cheng'en](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wu_Cheng%27en). For historical travel, use the separate World History lecture on Xuanzang, not the novel as documentary biography.
