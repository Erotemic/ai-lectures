## Text and publication

- **First publication:** 1866 in *Russkii vestnik* (*The Russian Messenger*) across the year; a separate novel edition appeared in 1867. The [University of Toronto Libraries exhibit](https://exhibits.library.utoronto.ca/exhibits/show/cp150/translations) documents the magazine and separate edition. The work has six parts and an epilogue, which is not a detachable happy ending.
- **Reading text:** [Project Gutenberg ebook 2554](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/2554), translated by Constance Garnett. Exact discussions of translated words should identify Garnett and, where philologically consequential, be compared with the Russian.
- **Translations and reception:** The [Toronto exhibit](https://library.utoronto.ca/exhibit/crime-and-punishment-150-global-contexts) documents multiple translation, adaptation, and critical traditions. A list of adaptations does not independently prove a particular interpretation correct.

## Interpretive and factual controls

1. **Two victims:** Raskolnikov kills Alyona Ivanovna and the unexpectedly returning Lizaveta. A reduction of the crime to the planned death of a pawnbroker erases the second murder.
2. **The theory:** The extraordinary-person article is a character's claim, not a moral permission granted by the narrator. It does not prove Raskolnikov's precise motive at every point; his explanations shift.
3. **Property:** The stolen goods are hidden rather than used to achieve the altruistic outcome previously imagined. This is significant for assessing the economic justification.
4. **Nikolai:** The painter's confession is false; the reader knows the murderer, but Porfiry must still interpret evidence within the narrative's procedural world.
5. **Luzhin:** Sonya's supposed theft is a fabricated accusation; a witness exposes the planted banknote. Keep reputation and guilt distinct.
6. **Svidrigailov:** He acquires information by eavesdropping, attempts to coerce Dunya, eventually releases her, and dies by suicide. Subsequent financial help to others does not justify the coercion.
7. **Porfiry:** His psychological conviction and legal proof are not identical; the investigation is not a modern forensic criminal procedure.
8. **Sonya:** Her relation to Lizaveta and independent moral judgment matter. Her devotion should not be universalized into a moral obligation for all survivors of violence to stay close to offenders.
9. **Confession versus repentance:** Raskolnikov confesses to Sonya, then later to police. In Siberia he continues to defend his pride for a long time. The novel indicates a beginning of moral transformation, not an entirely completed one.
10. **Names:** Transliteration and diminutives vary. Rodion Romanovich Raskolnikov, Avdotya Romanovna ('Dunya'), Sofya Semyonovna ('Sonya'), Porfiry Petrovich, and Dmitri Prokofych Razumikhin are distinct people; a changed form of address is not a new person.
11. **Historical and medical claims:** The novel represents a specific nineteenth-century legal and social setting. Do not assign modern diagnostic labels to the protagonist as though the text settled a clinical assessment.
12. **No comprehensive variants collation:** No claim is made that every 1866 installment, 1867 printing, later Russian edition, and English translation has been compared line by line.

## Further philological work

A future edition-oriented study should compare Garnett with a defined Russian critical edition and additional translations on representative scenes: the extraordinary-person article, Sonya's Lazarus reading, the interview with Porfiry, and the final confession. Interpretive claims remain open to re-evaluation.
