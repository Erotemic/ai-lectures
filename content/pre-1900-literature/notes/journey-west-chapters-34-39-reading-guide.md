## Chapter-level continuity checks

### Chapter 34 — five treasures in motion

- The two servants try the exchanged fake gourd, cannot swallow the sky, and watch it disappear **on the mountain before they return**. Wukong follows them to Lotus Cave as a fly and overhears the next errand.
- The golden rope is with the nine-tailed mother at Yalong Cave, not in Lotus Cave at first. Wukong impersonates a messenger, bows to the mother despite his humiliation, kills her, and travels to the kings in her form. Bajie notices the monkey's tail and ruins the impersonation.
- Silver Horn's tightening/loosening formulas enable the rope to reverse itself against Wukong. The staff-file and hair-double escape are important stages, not a one-stroke victory.
- Wukong plays with the name Sun Xingzhe to impersonate multiple supposed brothers. Even under another name, he answers the genuine magic gourd and gets trapped. His furnace-hardened body resists dissolution, and he escapes as an insect and reclaims the true vessel.

### Chapter 35 — rescue and its celestial sponsor

- The third name is **Xingzhe Sun**. Silver Horn uses an imitation gourd and cannot capture him. The monkey uses the real gourd and Silver Horn's own reply to trap him. Gold Horn mourns while Bajie makes reckless jokes about the king's plight and almost gets steamed.
- The plantain fan here **summons fire** and is connected to Laozi's alchemical furnace. It should not be confused with later episodes involving Princess Iron Fan. Wukong's hair soldiers are vulnerable to the flames; many ordinary demon soldiers die.
- Wukong steals the jade bottle, then the fan; Gold Horn withdraws to his mother's relatives. The disciples eat and sleep in the liberated Lotus Cave **before** Fox Aqi and Gold Horn return with their force. Bajie kills the fox uncle, and the jade bottle takes Gold Horn.
- Laozi claims all five treasures as his property and reconstitutes the two demons as his gold and silver furnace attendants. He says Guanyin requested them three times to stage the ordeal. The attendants' restoration does not undo the deaths in the demon families or the risk to the monks.

### Chapter 36 — the Baolin interlude

- The vast-world-as-house exchange makes the journey's enormous scale both spiritual and comic; Bajie would prefer turning home.
- The senior monk's earlier encounters with exploitative wanderers explain his refusal of shelter. Wukong **smashes a stone lion**, rather than attacking a person, to win lodging; Xuanzang objects to intimidation.
- Night brings the moon, Xuanzang's homesickness, the disciples' religious/alchemical metaphors and Bajie's parody. Xuanzang sits up with his old scripture after the others retire, preparing the next chapter's ghost visit.

### Chapter 37 — the ghost and the prince

- Drought was five years before; the Daoist rainmaker gained the king's trust for two years and then drowned him in the eight-sided palace well. Three years of imposture have followed.
- A jade **gui** ceremonial tablet remains physically after Xuanzang's dream. The ghost cannot merely be taken on faith, and the prince must be questioned without a public accusation.
- Wukong uses a white rabbit to draw the prince toward Baolin Temple, and a lacquered oracle box to make the prince hear the accusation. The prince initially assumes the monks stole the jade tablet. The first chapter ends with him sent **privately** to ask his mother whether her husband changed three years earlier.

### Chapter 38 — the body retrieved

- The queen reports her husband's sudden emotional and intimate estrangement and remembers a dream of the drowned king; she has also been separated from her son by the palace routine. The prince returns without betraying the meeting.
- The hunt is disguised as a success so that his absence will not invite suspicion. The story of the Well Dragon King is not ordinary treasure hunting: Bajie expected money and discovers a preserved body.
- The king's body is kept unchanged by a face-preserving pearl; the Water/Well Dragon King retains the pearl. Bajie must carry the corpse back from the palace garden. Angered by Wukong's tricks, Bajie claims Wukong can revive the man and persuades Xuanzang to use the tightening spell. **Do not perform the actual resurrection in chapter 38**.

### Chapter 39 — medicine, proof, and the lion

- Laozi has a reason to be suspicious after Wukong's past theft of pills. Wukong bargains absurdly from a thousand pills down to one, hides that one in his cheek, and returns to give it to the king. Xuanzang diagnoses the need for breath; Wukong supplies it.
- The revived king enters his former city as a plain-clothed baggage carrier, and Bajie deliberately assigns him the heavier load. The prince delays an attack in the palace by appealing to the dangers of harming an envoy from Tang.
- The impostor flees on hearing Wukong's disguised confession, then returns looking **exactly like Xuanzang**. The identification method belongs to Bajie: have the true monk recite the special tightening spell, despite the pain that gives Wukong.
- Manjushri identifies the monster as **his blue-haired lion** and says its three-year punishment was retaliation for the real king having immersed Manjushri (in disguise) for three days and nights. Wukong explicitly challenges the cost of the punishment, including what happened to the queen. Manjushri claims the lion was castrated and therefore had not slept with her; this is his answer, not a reason to erase the deception or the family's separation.
- Chapter 39 closes with Manjushri taking the lion away. It does **not** narrate the subsequent details of the court and the pilgrims leaving Wuji; those belong in chapter 40.
