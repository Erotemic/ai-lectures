# Journey to the West: Maitreya's melon trick

## The Lord who could not leave his mountain

Wukong flew south toward Mount Wudang, the last of the night's despair still with him. Behind him, Xuanzang and the other disciples were bound in the false Little Thunderclap Temple. Beside them lay the star officers and guardian spirits who had already attempted a rescue. The demon's white cloth bag had swallowed a celestial army twice. Wukong had escaped only by remembering, in time, that the bag was more dangerous than the mace.

At Wudang he passed courts and gates bright with lamps, banners, and the movement of hundreds of divine officers. The mountain belonged to the Lord of True Martial, who had once left a royal house for religious practice and gained responsibility for subduing evil spirits. Wukong paid the expected respects and described what had happened at Little Thunderclap: the false Buddha, the golden cymbals, his escape through Kang Jinlong's horn, and the bag that had carried off gods as though they were no weight at all.

The lord did not doubt that Wukong needed help. He spoke of earlier campaigns against dangerous beings and of the powers granted to him by the celestial administration. Yet he was not free to march his forces anywhere he pleased. He had no new order from the Jade Emperor. To send a full host on his own authority might put him in conflict with the very court he served. He could not leave Wukong empty-handed, however. He lent two divine commanders, Turtle and Snake, and five mighty dragons. They would travel with the monkey and test the monster's strength.

Wukong thanked him and led the company back to the counterfeit temple. The demon appeared with his mace. Turtle and Snake attacked from one side, the dragons from another, and Wukong struck from above with the iron staff. For a time Yellow Brows was pressed into defending himself against a coordinated assault. Then he reached toward his belt.

The monkey recognized the gesture and shouted his warning. But his new allies did not understand how a piece of cloth could be more dangerous than an armed enemy. The bag opened and took them all. Wukong alone darted out of reach. He saw the Turtle and Snake commanders and all five dragons vanish into the white folds. Another rescue had made the circle of prisoners larger.

The duty spirit assigned to the day's watch came to him as he struggled with frustration. Wukong spoke of the shame of going back to Wudang to report that the borrowed troops had been captured. The spirit proposed another source of aid: the Great Sage State Preceptor Bodhisattva at Xuyi, near Sizhou, whose disciples had conquered troublesome water spirits. Wukong did not like asking for help again, but he could not pretend that one failed plan had exhausted the universe. He turned toward the Huai River.

## Little Zhang and the four generals

The Great Sage State Preceptor's sanctuary stood by busy river country, where trade boats crossed beneath hills crowned with towers and temples. Its lord came out to greet Wukong, accompanied by a youthful-looking disciple called Little Zhang, or Prince Zhang. The monkey explained the danger from the golden cymbals and the cloth bag. He asked the bodhisattva to bring the skill that had once subdued the Water Mother and save Xuanzang.

The bodhisattva would have liked to go, but the river needed guarding. Flood season was upon the region, and a recently captured water ape might become troublesome if its jailer left. Instead he sent Little Zhang and four divine generals. Wukong had learned enough to worry about the outcome, but the prince and his companions looked eager to fight a foe whose name had begun to spread through Heaven.

They arrived at Little Thunderclap with spear and swords. Little Zhang challenged Yellow Brows to come out. The monster mocked him for leaving his master's protection and risking his life on a stranger's pilgrimage. The prince replied with the story of his own past: a royal child who had fallen ill, sought a master, renounced a kingdom, and learned how to subdue monsters. He had come, he said, not to win an argument about reputation but to finish an obligation to those imprisoned inside.

The battle was fierce. Prince Zhang's spear flashed against the short mace; the four generals hemmed the demon in; Wukong hammered at the gaps in the defense. The monster's skill still held. When he felt the pressure grow, he slipped the bag from his waist once more. Wukong shouted to be careful. The prince and generals heard the warning, but they did not know what action it required. In a moment they too were swallowed, dragged into the temple, and bound in the underground chambers.

Wukong was alone again. He had tried strength, wit, heavenly officers, divine dragons, and a prince famed for overcoming water spirits. Each effort had made the captives more numerous. He sat above the temple, no longer sure whether another request for help would be an act of loyalty or only a way to condemn more allies.

Then a bright cloud descended and a broad, smiling figure called his name. It was Maitreya, the future Buddha. Wukong dropped to his knees. Maitreya said he had come for the creature occupying Little Thunderclap. The monkey asked immediately who the demon was and where the terrible bag had come from.

Maitreya answered without disguising the truth. Yellow Brows was his own young attendant, charged with striking the ceremonial bell or sounding stone. On a day when Maitreya had gone to another assembly, the attendant had stolen several treasures and run away. The spiked mace was actually a temple striker. The impossible bag was Maitreya's acquired, or Later-Heaven, bag, known as the human-seed bag. The golden cymbals were also his.

Wukong stared at him. So all this suffering had begun because a holy teacher had left his valuables where an unruly attendant could take them? Maitreya admitted the failure of supervision. He also said that the pilgrims' sequence of trials had not yet ended. That was little comfort to the monkey, but the owner of the bag finally stood beside him.

## A very ripe melon

Maitreya did not summon an even larger army. He proposed a melon field. At the bottom of the slope he would set up a small hut beside a plot of growing melons, pretending to be the gardener. Wukong would lure Yellow Brows there, turn into a large ripe melon, and wait. The demon, thirsty from fighting, would ask for food. Maitreya would offer the disguised monkey. Once inside the monster, Wukong could convince him that no army had yet managed to defeat him by force.

The plan sounded outrageous even to Wukong. What if the demon never followed? What if he simply used the bag again? Maitreya took the monkey's open hand and marked a single character in the palm with sacred moisture. It was an injunction, a prohibition. Wukong was to keep his fist closed until the fight began, then open it before the demon. The command would keep Yellow Brows from relying on the bag and make him pursue the apparent retreating opponent.

Wukong went back to the temple alone. Yellow Brows emerged laughing. He had gathered up so many rescuers that he could scarcely believe the monkey had returned without an army. Wukong challenged him to a contest of weapons. Why, he asked, did a self-proclaimed Buddha need a sack to defeat a single fighter? Could the monster withstand even one hand of the Great Sage's strength?

Yellow Brows took the bait. He agreed to fight without the bag. Wukong opened his fist, letting Maitreya's prohibition take hold. They exchanged blows. Then Wukong pretended that the fight was turning against him. He ran toward the melon patch with Yellow Brows in pursuit, vanished among the vines, and changed into a fruit so ripe that its smell was sweet in the warm air.

Yellow Brows looked for the fleeing monkey and found none. Instead he saw an old gardener tending melons beside a small hut. The demon asked who owned the field. The gardener said it was his. Were any melons ripe? Certainly. The monster was thirsty and demanded one. Maitreya picked up the fruit that was Wukong and held it out.

Yellow Brows opened his mouth to bite. Wukong slid down his throat and began to fight from within. The demon dropped the melon, clutched his belly, and rolled between the rows, crying that he was dying. The monkey kicked, punched, and turned somersaults inside him. All the formidable power of the bag had no use when the enemy was already in his stomach.

Maitreya let the disguise fall away. The gardener became a smiling Buddha. Yellow Brows recognized his master and collapsed to his knees, begging for forgiveness. Maitreya took the white bag and the ceremonial striker. He called to Wukong to show mercy and come out. The monkey wanted a little more punishment for the figure who had tormented so many prisoners, but eventually emerged from the demon's mouth. Before he could strike again, Maitreya had folded the offender into his own bag.

The attendant, muffled within, complained that Wukong had destroyed the golden cymbals. Maitreya told him that broken gold could still be recovered. He returned to the temple with Wukong, gathered the scattered pieces, and restored the cymbals by a breath and a spell. Then he departed with the treasures and the boy who had misused them. The smiling master had solved with a garden and a melon what all those armed companies could not.

## The cost of the rescue

Wukong remained in the courtyard. The demon's lesser followers had begun to flee. He pursued them and killed them, one after another, until hundreds lay dead. Their shapes returned to those of birds, beasts, shrubs, and wild spirits. The principal offender was going home alive in a Buddha's custody; many of those who had served him would not.

Only then did Wukong untie Xuanzang, Bajie, and Sha. Bajie had been hanging hungry through several days of imprisonment. He bolted toward the kitchen, discovered the meal the monsters had prepared before the final fight, and ate before thanking anyone. With food in him, he carried bowls back for his master and Sha. It was a comic scene after an ordeal that had frightened them all, and it brought the starving prisoners back to ordinary bodily needs.

Wukong led Bajie to the underground cells. They opened the doors and loosened the cords of the divine officers, the star spirits, Turtle and Snake, the dragons, and Little Zhang's company. Xuanzang thanked each for the effort made on his behalf. Wukong sent the celestial guests back to their appointed stations. Their embarrassment at having been captured was no smaller than his gratitude.

They rested until the next morning, fed the white horse, and repacked the traveler bundles. Before departing, the pilgrims set fire to the splendid buildings of Little Thunderclap. The false temple burned: doors, roof beams, halls, and the high seat where Yellow Brows had played the Buddha. What had resembled a sanctuary from the road became a smoky shell.

The real Thunderclap was still far away. The lesson was not simply that demons may imitate sacred things, for the genuine teacher's own treasures had made the deception possible. Nor was it that a larger force guarantees a better outcome. Wukong had asked for help many times and seen every force swallowed. The rescue came when Maitreya admitted whose runaway attendant he faced, took responsibility for the stolen equipment, and arranged a trick suited to the creature's appetite and pride. The pilgrims stepped away from the ruins, exhausted, alive, and again uncertain how many more mountains stood between them and the scriptures.
