# Journey to the West: The return, the appointments, and the vanished fillet

## The road that had become a wind

At Life-Saving Temple by Heaven-Reaching River, the Chen family woke with food ready for the monks and found their guests gone. The rooms were empty, the horse was gone, and the scriptures had been packed and carried away. No servant remembered opening the gate in the night. The people gathered before the images of the four pilgrims and brought their prepared offerings there instead. Their rescuers had passed through a village for a second time, and now only the statues remained. The prayers and festivals would go on long after the travelers had left the road.

The travelers themselves were high above the earth. Eight heavenly guardians bore them east, keeping the scriptures secure and the companions together. The long pilgrimage had lasted fourteen years. They had left Chang'an as men who did not know what lay beyond the next pass; now they crossed ranges and rivers in moments. Yet their destination was not the Buddha's mountain. They were carrying the written teaching to the people for whom Xuanzang had promised to seek it.

The wind approached Chang'an. Near the city stood a tower built by the Tang emperor to watch for the return of the scriptures. The emperor had not forgotten his monk-brother. Year after year he came to the place and looked toward the west. When a fragrance moved over the tower and the sky filled with auspicious color, the heavenly guardians called to Xuanzang to descend. They told him that they would remain out of sight, since ordinary people might be overwhelmed by so direct a manifestation. They suggested that the disciples need not all go down. But Wukong answered that someone must bear the scrolls and lead the horse; their master had not suddenly become a porter merely because his journey was complete.

Bajie approved. He also objected to any suspicion that he would forget the mission in the pleasures of the capital. He had carried the burden through too much heat and cold to abandon it within sight of the palace. Sha was already tightening the straps on the scripture packs. The four descended with the horse beside the tower, where the emperor and his officials could see them.

Taizong came down to meet the returned traveler. The monk he had sent away so many years before knelt before him. The emperor raised him, calling him his younger brother in their bond of mission and faith. He looked at the three disciples, whose forms were startling beside the orderly ranks of officials. Xuanzang introduced them as the companions he had received along the way. The emperor offered a royal mount and brought the company toward the city. The iron staff, the nine-toothed rake, the carrying pole and the white horse passed with them through the streets, not as invaders or captives, but as the guards of the books that had crossed the world.

## The pine trees facing east

The news of the return spread. At the monastery where Xuanzang had once lived, the pine trees had turned their branches toward the east. The monks remembered a promise he had made before leaving: when the branches faced that way, he said, they would know he was coming back. Some doubted the sign. Others ran toward the city gate with their robes scarcely arranged. Before long they heard the crowd crying that the scripture pilgrim had returned and the emperor himself was bringing him through the streets.

Xuanzang entered the court with the disciples and their packs. The emperor had him seated and asked what scriptures he had obtained, and how the long journey had ended. The monk reported the truth, including matters that might have been easier to leave unsaid. He told of the Great Thunderclap Monastery, the Buddha's assembly, and the library's guardians, Ananda and Kasyapa. He explained how they had asked for a gift and, finding the travelers empty-handed, had first handed them the wordless scriptures. A celestial messenger had scattered the bundles, revealing the blank leaves. After the party returned to complain, the Buddha had defended the propriety of exchanging something for the teaching while ordering that written volumes be given. Xuanzang had surrendered the purple-gold begging bowl the emperor had placed in his hands at their first farewell.

The emperor listened to this account of a sacred mission completed through a dispute about payment. He did not receive the bowl again. Instead he received the books: thirty-five groups of scriptures, from which five thousand forty-eight scrolls had been selected. Xuanzang explained that the numbers had been checked before departure and that the texts were to be guarded, copied and taught. He did not claim that every page was without damage. He had carried them across water, storm and a final interruption by the old turtle, and some pages had adhered to the rock on which they were dried.

The emperor was moved. He ordered the works treated with reverence, and he offered to write an introduction honoring their arrival. The writing would place the teachings within the world of the empire and explain why the journey had been undertaken. He called it a preface to the sacred teaching. Xuanzang thanked him for a gift different from the one he had lost at the scripture repository: not a vessel for receiving food, but words that would accompany and frame the scriptures when other people read them.

Taizong then asked Xuanzang to recite some of the texts. The monk replied that such a recitation should take place in a monastery rather than in the audience hall. One of the officials named a suitable temple with a pagoda. The emperor agreed, and the court made ready to go there. Xuanzang urged that copies be prepared so the scriptures could travel beyond the treasury. The original volumes should be preserved, he said, but the teaching could fulfill the mission only if people were able to encounter it. The emperor gave orders to the scribes and to those responsible for arranging the copying.

For the first time the scriptures had a future that did not depend on one monk remembering his vows. There would be readers, teachers, copyists and students. The journey could be handed on in the form Xuanzang had sought.

## The summons above the pagoda

The imperial procession reached the temple. A platform was prepared. Officials carried volumes carefully, and the emperor came to hear the scriptures proclaimed. Xuanzang ordered his disciples to keep the dragon-horse and the baggage in readiness. Wukong stood close enough to guard him. Bajie and Sha arranged the burdens they had carried so many times across the road.

Xuanzang took his place on the platform and opened a book. He was about to begin when the sky filled with a fragrance like the one that had announced their arrival. The eight heavenly guardians called down from above. They had been instructed to bring the pilgrims east and then return them west before the appointed time was complete. The moment for recitation had come, but the remaining time for their report to the Buddha was shorter still.

"The scriptures have been delivered," the voices said. "Come back with us."

There was no debate in the court. The three disciples, the white horse, and then Xuanzang rose toward the wind. The book was left with the people below. The emperor and his officials bowed as the figures climbed into the clouds, leaving the scrolls behind them. Their appearance in Chang'an had been brief enough for the people to wonder afterward whether the sight had happened exactly as they remembered, yet the texts were present and the emperor's order to copy them remained.

The court continued the work. Monks were asked to read, ritual assemblies were arranged, and scribes prepared copies for wider distribution. The emperor could no longer question the traveler who had stood before him, but the mission was not undone by the pilgrim's departure. The books had arrived. That was the task Xuanzang had been sent to perform.

## Before the Buddha, again

The guardians carried the company back across the sky. They arrived at the Buddha's mountain within the allotted eight days, the final journey completed without the years of walking that had brought the pilgrims west. They entered the great hall to report that the scriptures had been delivered to the Great Tang and the instructions fulfilled. The Buddha received the report and called the companions forward, each by name.

He addressed Xuanzang first. The monk who had crossed the world had been known, in an earlier existence, as Golden Cicada, a disciple who had failed to honor the teaching and had been sent down to the human world. The years of pilgrimage had not erased that beginning; they had changed what it meant. His vows, endurance and success in obtaining the scriptures now led to a new appointment. He was named the Buddha of Candana Merit.

Wukong was called next. The Buddha recalled the stone monkey's uprising against Heaven, the banquet ruined, the mountain under which he had spent five hundred years, and the long years when he had guarded his master. The old name Great Sage Equal to Heaven had been a title Wukong had taken for himself in defiance. The new one came at the end of service and correction. He was appointed the Victorious Fighting Buddha.

Bajie listened closely. He had been a celestial marshal and a punished offender, and he had walked the road with a burden on his shoulders and a thousand complaints in his mouth. The Buddha praised the work he had done but said that his appetite and inclinations had not been entirely mastered. He was appointed Cleanser of the Altars, an honored attendant who would receive and clear the food offered in religious ceremonies.

Bajie's protest came at once.

"They become Buddhas," he said, indicating Xuanzang and Wukong. "Why am I the one who clears the altars?"

The Buddha reminded him of the many people making offerings throughout the world and of the endless food that his new duties would bring him. It was a post fitted, with a certain celestial humor, to the disciple who had never understood why the others were content with so little at meals. Bajie considered that answer. His appetite had caused him trouble from the first days of the pilgrimage, yet now it had been given a place in the order of things. He accepted the post, though no one could be certain he was entirely satisfied.

Sha Wujing came next. He had once been the Curtain-Raising General in Heaven, punished and sent to the River of Flowing Sand, where travelers had died at his hands. He had crossed the world carrying the baggage and defending his master without making himself the center of every quarrel. He was appointed a Golden-Bodied Arhat. His labor had been quiet, but the Buddha named it as part of the victory.

Last came the white horse. The dragon prince had been condemned for an offense in his own family and given a life of service under the appearance of a horse. He had borne Xuanzang through danger and carried scriptures back toward the Tang. He was awarded the rank of the Eight-Section Heavenly Dragon Horse. He was taken to a transforming pool on the mountain. There he shed the horse's body and rose in his own dragon form, scales gleaming, whiskers moving in the bright air. He curled around one of the great columns near the gate. The animal who had walked step after step while the companions argued above him was finally seen for what he was.

## The circlet that was gone

The appointments had been spoken. The party that had come west with wounds, hunger, longing and guilt stood among the Buddha's company, each with a place and a name. There was no further demon to defeat and no more travel paper to be stamped. But Wukong had one last question for his master.

He touched the top of his head.

"Now that I am a Buddha, must I still wear the tightening fillet? Will you still recite the spell when I displease you? Give me the loosening spell, and I will break the thing so that it cannot be used on another."

Xuanzang looked at the disciple he had once tried to control by pain. The circlet had been put on Wukong after the monkey killed men, defied orders, and refused to understand that his strength could not be its own justification. The spell had hurt him many times. Wukong had never forgiven it, and perhaps the journey would have been less cruel if they had found another way to teach him. Yet it had accompanied their long struggle to travel together.

"There is no need to remove what has already gone," Xuanzang said. "You have become a Buddha. Feel your head."

Wukong ran his hand over his hair. The fillet was no longer there. He searched around his brow, then touched it again. The thing he had feared and resented had vanished without a loosening spell. He did not have to smash it. He did not have to bargain for its removal. A bond that had once made him obedient through suffering no longer defined him.

He looked at Xuanzang. The monk had crossed the world because he believed the scriptures could help people he had never met. The monkey had crossed it because he had been freed from a mountain and given a task he had not wanted. They had argued and parted, found one another again, saved each other's lives, and finally stood together at the mountain they had named for years without being able to see it. Their fellowship had become something neither the staff nor the spell could wholly explain.

The Buddha's assembly turned toward prayer. Beyond the halls the mountains rose in clear light. The dragon rested about the pillar. Sha stood at ease. Bajie thought of the responsibilities, and perhaps the food, that awaited him. Xuanzang and Wukong were no longer a pilgrim being protected and a prisoner being controlled, but two beings who had reached the end of their appointed journey.

The tale closes here, not with a final blow from Wukong's staff or another cry from a demon cave, but with the scriptures delivered and the companions restored to their places. The road had been made of rivers, arguments, hunger, mistaken judgments, rescue, mercy and loss. Some of its promises had been kept late; one to the old turtle had not been kept at all. The scriptures were written and carried home, though some pages had been torn on a stone. And the iron ring that had gripped the Monkey King's head for so much of the journey was gone.
