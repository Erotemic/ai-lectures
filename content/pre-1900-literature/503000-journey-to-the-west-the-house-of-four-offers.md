# Journey to the West: The house of four offers

When the travelers left Flowing-Sand River, they were three disciples and a master, counting the horse separately. If the horse had been asked, it might have objected to that accounting. It too had been a dragon once. Every member of the company, apart from Xuanzang, had worn another shape, held another position, and entered the pilgrimage under the weight of a mistake.

Sha Wujing, who had once stood in a heavenly court and later eaten people on the riverbank, now worked quietly among his new companions. He learned where they kept the bowls and bedding. He listened while Bajie argued about the weight of the packs, and while Wukong answered questions that nobody had quite asked. Sha had not been with them long enough to judge which quarrels mattered and which were merely part of the daily music of their journey.

Autumn colored the woods. Red leaves gathered on the heights, and the air grew sharper toward evening. A flock of birds passed overhead on its own long journey. Xuanzang watched the light decline and asked where they would sleep.

Wukong, who could sleep upon a branch and eat what grew around him, answered that a religious traveler should not expect a comfortable roof each night. A patch of earth, a little shelter, and the sky would serve.

Bajie stopped walking. Wukong's view of hardship, he pointed out, was easier to maintain when he was not carrying the luggage. He described the bundles almost item by item: the lengths of cane, the ropes, the coverings to keep the rain off, the staff, and the other objects that had to cross each hill on somebody's shoulder. Was he a disciple or a pack animal? There were three of them, yet the burdens kept finding their way onto his back.

Wukong replied that he had appointed himself to the safety of the master. Bajie and Sha would look after the luggage and the horse. Any neglect could be corrected with a tap from the iron staff.

Bajie objected to government by iron staff. And what of the horse? It carried only one man. Why should it not carry a little more?

That led to a fresh explanation of the white horse's former existence as the dragon prince of the Western Sea. Wukong recounted the offense for which the prince had been condemned and Guanyin's intervention. He was not a common animal available to haul whatever Bajie chose to pile on him. He had accepted the task of bearing Xuanzang. Each creature had his own assigned labor.

Sha was startled. Was the gentle horse truly a dragon?

Wukong assured him it was. Bajie, unwilling to abandon the argument, began praising what dragons could do: raise storms, move clouds, fly over mountains. Why then did this one walk so slowly?

Wukong said he could remedy that complaint. He raised his staff in a playful threat. The horse, which understood the monkey perfectly well, bolted.

Xuanzang clutched the reins. The horse galloped along the rocky path and up a rise, leaving Bajie to stumble after it under his load. When the monk finally brought the animal under control, he turned on Wukong with a frightened rebuke. Did his disciple really think a dangerous road was the place for such jokes?

Wukong smiled and said he had merely followed Bajie's suggestion that the horse should move faster.

Bajie arrived breathless, not at all grateful to have been shown his point made absurd.

It was on the rise where the horse stopped that they saw the house.

Its walls were clean, its roof sound, its entrance welcoming. Tall pines and bamboo framed the grounds. A little bridge crossed a stream. Even the autumn flowers along the wall appeared well tended. There were no carts in the yard, no chickens to scatter, and no farm laborers coming home. The place had the stillness of a household between occupations, yet it promised the three things that travelers most wanted after a weary day: supper, sleep, and a door that closed against the night.

Xuanzang insisted that they ask permission before entering. Wukong, impatient with the wait, stepped inside to see whether anybody was there. He saw polished furniture, a room arranged for guests, and a household prosperous enough to maintain servants. Then a woman appeared from behind an inner door and demanded to know who had entered a widow's house without leave.

The monkey bowed and apologized. His party was traveling from Tang China to seek scriptures in the western land. Could the owner spare them a night's lodging?

The widow's anger passed. She invited the master and the other disciples to enter, welcomed them to her hall, and ordered tea. A young servant brought fine cups. Bajie studied the costly furnishings with the keen attention he usually reserved for a cooking pot. When the woman herself came forward, elegantly dressed and still beautiful despite her years, his attention sharpened again.

The lady told them her family's story. Her birth surname was Jia; she had married into the Mo family. Both her husband and his parents were dead. She had inherited extensive land, livestock, stores of rice, fine garments, and precious metals. There was more wealth here, she said, than she and her three daughters could enjoy. The daughters were grown: Zhenzhen, Aiai, and Lianlian. All were educated, capable at household arts, and unmarried.

There was, however, an awkward problem. A stranger who married the widow might expect her to give up the property, and she did not wish to leave it. Nor did she wish her daughters to marry away and abandon their mother's house. Then four traveling men had appeared at her gate, as though arranged by fortune itself.

She proposed a solution. The monks could abandon the difficult road, let their hair grow again, marry into the household, and enjoy its wealth. She and the three daughters would each have a husband, and the travelers would exchange hardship for comfort.

The house was very quiet after she spoke.

Xuanzang did not answer. He stared at the floor as though silence might make the proposal disappear. He had left his country, accepted an imperial charge, and sworn to seek the scriptures. A marriage, a household, a new collection of obligations: these were not merely rewards offered to a weary man. They were another life, one that would replace the life he had promised to live.

The widow took his silence as a reason to explain further. The household possessed herds, stores, land, and rooms enough for everyone. There would be no want of fine clothes in winter or cool garments in summer. She described the pleasures of meals and family celebrations. She named the daughters' ages and virtues. Why endure dangerous mountains, uncertain lodging, and monsters when all this was ready to hand?

Bajie shifted in his chair. He could scarcely sit still.

When the monk continued to say nothing, Bajie touched his sleeve and urged him to be courteous. The lady had made a generous offer; surely it deserved some answer.

Xuanzang found his voice. He rebuked Bajie for forgetting the vows they had made. They had not crossed mountains and rivers in search of wealth or wives. A person who had entered a religious life could not simply change his purpose because a more comfortable arrangement came along.

The lady countered that household life also had its merits. There were pleasures and kindnesses, families to care for, lands to cultivate. Why should this company despise the ordinary affairs by which people lived? Xuanzang conceded that wealth and household joys could be good for those who chose them. But those were not his vows. He had committed himself to something else.

The widow's manner hardened. She had not offered them insult, she said. Why should a guest answer generosity with a lecture? If the master would not marry, perhaps one of his disciples would. Had he authority to refuse on behalf of every man in the room?

Xuanzang turned to Wukong. Would he stay?

Wukong laughed. Whatever else he had been before the pilgrimage, he had never imagined settling down as a husband in a human household. If the question must be asked, Bajie looked more suitable.

Bajie protested at once that nobody should make a decision in haste. There were matters to discuss, arrangements to consider. The protest sounded less like a refusal than a man trying to conceal his eagerness by speaking solemnly.

Xuanzang turned to Sha. Sha's answer was direct. Guanyin had rescued him from his former existence and guided him to the monk. He had only just begun to repay that mercy. He would carry the loads, guard the party, and go to the west, even if the road cost him his life. He would not exchange the mission for a wealthy marriage.

The widow rose angrily. She went behind the inner screen and closed the door. No one came back with the promised meal. No servant appeared to show them a bed. The house that had offered abundance became a splendid empty hall in which four travelers sat hungry.

Bajie complained. Xuanzang might have answered more tactfully. One could accept a meal and a night's rest without deciding whether to marry. There had been no need to close every possible door with a sermon.

Sha suggested that Bajie might stay and become the household's son-in-law after all.

Wukong promptly agreed. What an arrangement it would be! Bajie could have the estate, the daughters, the storerooms, and a lifetime of fine meals. His former connection with Gao Village could be explained away, if he did not mind the trouble of changing households again. The monkey promised, with very little sincerity, that he would not tell on his brother.

Bajie accused them all of hypocrisy. Surely he could not be the only one who saw the advantages of such a household. Yet whenever he tried to speak plainly, the others made a fool of him.

He rose with an air of injured duty and announced that he would tend to the horse.

The horse was already secure outside. There was very little tending to be done.

Wukong watched him go and, with the quiet efficiency of a creature who could become almost anything, transformed himself into a tiny insect. He flew after Bajie.

The pig did not head toward the stable. He hovered near the inner part of the house until the widow appeared. His manner changed at once. He bowed, softened his voice, and explained that his master was extremely devoted to his religious ideals. Wukong could be stubborn, and the newcomer Sha had hardly spent a month among them. But he, Bajie, had some understanding of ordinary life. He could work land. He could mend buildings, carry great weights, raise rain if crops were dry, and turn over a field with the same rake that served him in battle. He would make a valuable husband.

The lady asked whether he needed his master's approval.

Bajie hesitated only long enough to decide that he did not. His master was not his parent. Marriage, he said, was a matter he could choose for himself.

The lady told him she would consult her daughters. She slipped behind the door and left him standing there.

Wukong flew back, resumed his own shape, and reported everything to Xuanzang and Sha, with a delight that made it clear he intended to enjoy the story for a long time. When Bajie returned leading the horse, his brothers asked how the grazing had gone. Not well, he said. There had been little grass.

Wukong asked whether there had at least been someone to talk to near the stable.

Bajie lowered his head. He understood that his private negotiations had already become public entertainment.

At that moment the inner door opened. Lamps came into the hall. The widow emerged with three richly dressed daughters, each graceful and lovely. She instructed them to greet the visitors. Bajie stared with an expression in which surprise, pleasure, and calculation competed for his face.

The widow announced that she was prepared to take Bajie as a son-in-law. The question now was which daughter he should marry.

Bajie found this a difficult problem only because he could think of too many desirable answers. If choosing one would offend the others, he said, why not marry all three and preserve harmony in the house?

The widow refused. One man could not claim three daughters simply because he was unable to make up his mind. She had another method. He would cover his eyes with a cloth and reach for a bride as the young women passed. Whichever he caught would become his wife.

Bajie agreed eagerly. His eyes were covered. He heard laughter and the rustle of silk, and began reaching into the air.

The daughters seemed to be everywhere. A sleeve brushed his hand. A step sounded at his left shoulder. He lunged and embraced a pillar. He swung toward another noise and struck the wall. He staggered across the hall, tripped over a threshold, and grabbed at empty space. Each time he thought he had a hand upon a bride, she slipped away. Soon he was sweating and bruised. The fine wedding ceremony had become a performance in which the groom supplied the comedy.

At last the widow removed the blindfold. Perhaps, she said, her daughters were too shy for such a game. Each had made an embroidered garment. If Bajie could wear the garment made by one daughter, that daughter would be his bride.

Bajie proposed trying all three.

One was enough for the test. The lady presented a beautiful shirt stitched with pearls and ornament. Bajie pulled off his outer robe and slipped the new garment over his body. He began to adjust it.

Then the garment tightened.

The embroidered fabric became binding cords. They caught his arms and chest, twisted about him, and dragged him to the floor. He struggled, cursed, and tried to wrench himself free. The more he fought, the more tightly the cords held. The widow and her daughters vanished.

He was alone, trussed in a marriage garment that had turned into a trap.

When dawn came, Xuanzang opened his eyes and found himself lying not in a splendid hall but beneath pine and cypress trees. The screens, halls, painted beams, servants, and storerooms were gone. The whole mansion had disappeared. Sha was alarmed. Wukong looked almost amused. He had seen the auspicious clouds around the house when they arrived and had suspected that something other than a human household was at work.

A note hung from an old tree. Sha fetched it down. The message disclosed that the widow and her three daughters had been manifestations of four holy figures: the Old Mother of Mount Li, Guanyin, Manjushri, and Samantabhadra. They had come to test the travelers' resolve. The master and the faithful disciples had kept their direction. Bajie had followed a promise of comfort and been caught by it.

Xuanzang bowed in gratitude. He did not seem pleased that divine instruction had chosen this method of arriving, but he accepted the warning. Wukong was still thinking about the absent pig.

Then a shout came from deep among the trees.

Bajie was calling for help. He had apparently spent the remainder of the night suspended by the cords, with no bride, no house, and no dignity left to recover. He promised loudly that he would not do it again.

Wukong suggested leaving him to enjoy his marriage a little longer.

Xuanzang refused. The foolish disciple was still their companion. He had accepted Guanyin's summons, and he had strength they needed. They would rescue him and continue the pilgrimage together.

So the four travelers had acquired a new sort of lesson. A choice could appear as a generous invitation, and a refusal could cost a comfortable night's rest. But for Bajie, at least, an invitation he had thought irresistible had proved impossible to catch. The party went into the trees to find him, carrying not a punishment from Heaven but the prospect of teasing from Wukong for every mile yet to come.

The identity note names the Old Mother of Mount Li, Guanyin, Manjushri, and Samantabhadra. The episode is a religious literary trial; its treatment of household life, sex, and religious discipline belongs to the novel's worldview, not to a modern empirical claim.
