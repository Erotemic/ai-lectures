# War and Peace II: crowds, historical causes, social order, and literary form

## War and Peace — How Tolstoy Makes a Crowd Legible

A reader approaching War and Peace for the first time may think the problem is remembering everybody's name. A more productive question is how a novel with so many people teaches us to care about individual minds without losing sight of the crowd. Tolstoy moves from a salon in which many people speak past one another to a single person noticing a face, a button, the smell of a coat, or the sound of a horse. Then he widens the frame again until one person's understanding seems hopelessly small. This motion, rather than any exhaustive family tree, is the book's principal method of orientation.

Look at the opening Saint Petersburg party. The political talk sounds large and authoritative. Yet Tolstoy allows a person to be revealed by a pause, an arrangement of the face, or a privately calculated compliment. The conversation has public subject matter, but the narration shows how much of it is social work: identifying allies, maintaining prestige, finding a marriage prospect, avoiding the wrong kind of frankness. Pierre's awkward contributions interrupt the performance. The scene is funny because readers can hear both what the speakers intend and what their conduct betrays.

The technique also works in intimate life. Natasha at her first ball is not given an essay about insecurity. Her experience develops through what she notices and what she imagines others notice in her. Andrei's encounter with the enormous sky at Austerlitz rearranges his scale of value without requiring an external narrator to state an ethical theorem. These scenes have different textures because the characters are not interchangeable devices for delivering Tolstoy's opinions. The person who interprets the world changes what counts as foreground.

Tolstoy sometimes enters a mind with extraordinary closeness, then pulls away to describe actions whose consequences the person cannot know. Pierre's agricultural reform looks encouraging to Pierre because people stage his tour effectively. A larger narrative frame exposes what he has failed to inspect. At Borodino, Pierre's bewilderment among artillerymen offers a view no headquarters report can replace; at the same time, Tolstoy argues that no single eyewitness can account for the battle as a whole. Proximity reveals the reality of sensation and the limits of interpretation at once.

Another technique is recurrence with variation. Andrei wants greatness in 1805, turns toward domestic withdrawal after Lise's death, and gradually becomes responsive to new life. His pride returns when Natasha breaks their engagement, then changes again when he sees Anatole wounded. Pierre looks for a system in Freemasonry, an impossible single deed in his plan to kill Napoleon, and finally a livable rhythm during imprisonment. Neither man develops along a straight line. The return of old impulses allows Tolstoy to show that character is persistent without being fixed.

Time itself expands and contracts. A ballroom minute can occupy many pages. Months of administrative work can pass quickly. The central day of a battle appears through intersecting moments and moving vantage points, while years of marriage are condensed in the epilogue. Do not confuse the relative amount of prose given to an event with its objective length. The novel's duration follows the human importance of what is being experienced and the analytical pressure Tolstoy wants to place upon it.

There is a risk in speaking of Tolstoy's narrator as omniscient. The novel often claims enormous authority, including historical authority, but it repeatedly demonstrates the failure of other people's claims to know causes. Its narrator is both a maker of vivid inner lives and an argumentative presence whose judgments can be tested. When the narrator tells us how battles work, we should ask how the adjacent scenes support or complicate that claim. When it makes declarations about women or nations, we should distinguish the book's governing philosophy from facts established by the invented world.

A useful listening exercise is to choose one event and follow it at three scales. Take Moscow's abandonment. At the scale of command, it is a military decision after Borodino. At the scale of a household, it is an argument about whether carts carry goods or wounded men. At the scale of a person, it is Natasha insisting that the injured strangers be given space. All three are true descriptions of the same period. A historical novel becomes powerful when it lets the descriptions test one another, rather than asking one vocabulary to exhaust the event.

## War and Peace — Napoleon, Kutuzov, and the Problem of Historical Causes

War and Peace is not merely set amid historical events. It actively disputes the way historical events are explained. Tolstoy is skeptical of narratives in which Napoleon wills an invasion and millions of people therefore move, or in which Kutuzov makes one wise decision and an entire national fate follows. He asks what it would mean to explain the movement of armies without treating command as a magical force. The novel's historical essays can feel like interruptions until we realize that this dispute has already been enacted by nearly every important scene.

At Austerlitz, officers arrive with a plan so intricate that its presentation becomes an event in its own right. A plan gives the impression of an all-seeing intelligence. The ensuing battle supplies confusion, delay, misheard instructions, and people acting on assumptions they cannot verify. Tolstoy is not claiming that plans never matter. He is arguing that a plan cannot encompass the reactions of the people and the conditions through which it must operate.

Napoleon is repeatedly shown issuing commands after events have acquired a momentum beyond his effective reach. That portrayal is a deliberate artistic and philosophical intervention. It disputes the image of the sovereign military genius as a singular origin of history. But the intervention must not be treated as a neutral record of Napoleon's actual capacities. Historians can study administrative resources, orders, communications, contingencies, and strategic decisions in greater detail than Tolstoy's dramatic argument allows. The novel asks a sharp question about explanations; it does not end historical research.

Kutuzov is the counterfigure. He often seems passive to characters who crave decisive action. Tolstoy values his ability to recognize a collective movement already under way and to resist the vanity of controlling it by force of personality. After Borodino, the choice to yield Moscow becomes an example. Tolstoy invests Kutuzov with sympathy because he can accept a painful necessity without claiming to have manufactured the nation's courage. This should not be reduced to the proposition that old men are wise and young men rash. The distinction is between sensitivity to circumstances and worship of one's own intention.

Pierre supplies a smaller experiment. He resolves to kill Napoleon, believing that a single act by a single man could change the direction of history. Yet his actual experiences in Moscow are saving a child, helping a woman, being captured, and learning from prisoners. The contrast between imagined world-historical agency and concrete ethical action is precise. A grand intention may be morally dramatic while practically empty; an action whose effects are local may possess the substance that the grand intention lacks.

Tolstoy often compares history to phenomena that require attention to extremely many elements. His later philosophical discussion presses toward a view in which collective action cannot be reduced to the simple sum of famous intentions. It would be a mistake to translate that insight mechanically into modern mathematical models as though the novel supplied equations that have been scientifically validated. Its mathematical language is part of an argument about scale and explanation. It invites thought, not the uncritical adoption of a new technical theory.

We can also question Tolstoy from within the world he depicts. His serfs do not have the same latitude as landlords. Generals and emperors can command resources and impose harms no single soldier can. A theory that corrects the exaggeration of great men's intentions might still underestimate structured inequalities of power. Recognizing that no ruler can determine every outcome does not imply that all participants wield equal influence. Tolstoy's material often makes this distinction clearer than his most sweeping formulations.

A close reader should compare an authoritative passage in the second epilogue with a scene of people laboring on the ground. Ask what the scene adds to the abstract proposition: uncertainty, resistance, physical conditions, local incentives, courage, terror. Then ask what the abstract proposition makes newly visible in the scene. The best way to read Tolstoy's philosophy is neither to skip it as an unfortunate appendage nor to worship it as proven history. It is an ambitious attempt to make the scale of lived experience count in an account of collective change.

## War and Peace — Pierre and Andrei: Two Unfinished Moral Educations

Pierre Bezukhov and Prince Andrei Bolkonsky are friends partly because each sees in the other a possibility he lacks. Pierre has openness and appetite without clear direction. Andrei has discipline, intelligence, and critical distance without much willingness to rejoice. Their paths through the novel are therefore not parallel routes toward the same doctrine. They expose two ways that a person can become alienated from ordinary life: by wanting too many things without choosing well, and by treating ordinary things as too small to deserve attention.

Pierre's inheritance is a laboratory of unearned power. He suddenly possesses enormous wealth, and society reorganizes around him. The marriage to Helene is produced almost as efficiently as a financial transaction. The duel with Dolokhov reveals that Pierre's dislike of violence does not keep him from participating in violent rituals when honor is made to seem compulsory. In each case, his main difficulty is not lack of fine moral ideas. It is the interval between approving of an idea and being able to act upon it when other people press him.

Freemasonry gives Pierre a language for improvement. The impulse to help his serfs is genuine. Yet his estate tour shows that people whose livelihood depends on his opinion may arrange scenes to satisfy him. He cannot convert good intention into just outcomes while remaining ignorant of conditions. This is a lesson in the ethics of information, not a dismissal of moral aspiration. Pierre needs to learn how he knows that help has actually reached someone.

Andrei's great early error is differently organized. He wants to distinguish himself so completely that his wife and unborn child become impediments. At Austerlitz, wounded under the sky, he sees the disproportion between the immense world and his ambition. But revelation alone cannot teach him how to live. He withdraws, suffers Lise's death, and becomes an expert in disillusionment. Natasha's vitality attracts him because it cannot be reduced to a proof or system. Yet when she betrays their engagement, he makes his pride the final judge of her person.

Both men repeatedly confuse a change in feeling with a finished moral transformation. Pierre joins the Masons as though adopting a structure were the same as learning the practice. Andrei turns away from military glory as though renouncing one ambition would banish self-importance. Tolstoy keeps returning them to situations where an old weakness takes a new form. This is why the friendship matters: each man witnesses the other's inadequacy without ceasing to regard him as someone worth speaking to.

At Borodino, Andrei's wound and the sight of Anatole in the hospital lead him to a kind of forgiveness that would previously have seemed contemptible to him. The compassion he feels does not claim Anatole was innocent. It recognizes suffering without letting hatred settle the whole relation. Pierre's comparable education emerges in captivity. Platon Karataev cannot repair Pierre's social world, but his manner of meeting everyday necessities breaks Pierre's fascination with an all-encompassing theory of life.

We should resist reducing these transformations to labels: Christian love, peasant wisdom, humanism, fatalism. The novel uses religious and moral languages, but it continually tests them through who feeds someone, who stays near the sick, and who accepts an inconvenient truth. The practical actions do not automatically justify the unequal society that permits them. Pierre can become kinder in prison and still emerge a rich landowner with political influence. Andrei can become capable of forgiveness without living to practice it for many years.

The men also have different endings. Andrei dies, leaving an unfinished relationship with his son and Natasha. Pierre survives and forms a household with Natasha, then becomes politically involved. The dead man's life is not a discarded prototype for the survivor's happy ending. Andrei's thoughts shape the choices and memories of those who remain. Pierre's growth is not sealed against renewed pride or error. In 1820, when he speaks confidently of political reform, the question returns: how can the man who learned humility in captivity act in the world without again confusing righteous purpose with effective knowledge? That question keeps his education alive beyond the final page.

## War and Peace — Natasha, Marya, and the Costs of Domestic Ideals

Natasha Rostov and Princess Marya Bolkonskaya begin from different positions in the novel. Natasha is young, expressive, rich in family attention, and increasingly attractive to society. Marya is older, pious, financially secure but constrained by a severe father who uses education as a form of discipline. Their lives intersect through Prince Andrei, but their friendship becomes more important than that one connection. Reading them together shows both what Tolstoy values in care and what he takes for granted about women's permitted futures.

Natasha's first ball offers a remarkable account of social exposure. She has entered a world in which a young woman must be seen in order to enter its system of marriage. Her longing for a dance is not frivolous. It is entangled with access to recognition, future security, and an adult self. Andrei's attention gives her happiness, but it also binds her future to decisions controlled by him and his father. The one-year delay highlights a difference in power disguised as proper procedure.

The affair with Anatole makes this imbalance more dangerous. Anatole is already married and conceals the fact. His family and friends are capable of arranging a girl's ruin as entertainment. Natasha responds recklessly to his attention, and the novel permits the consequences to be painful. Yet a criticism that simply calls her immoral would ignore the unequal information and social consequences. A man can leave the scene. Natasha must remain in the society that has witnessed her disgrace. Pierre's tenderness allows him to see Natasha without reducing her to the scandal.

Marya's story raises a different question: what happens when a woman is trained to treat self-denial as her most reliable virtue? At Bald Hills she must care for a father who diminishes her and expects gratitude for the privilege of obeying him. Her religious convictions sustain her, but do not erase her anger or longing for affection. The moment she is endangered by the invasion, she is discovered and protected by Nikolai Rostov. It may seem like a romantic rescue. It is also an occasion to ask how much control Marya possessed before and after her protector arrived.

During Andrei's final illness, Natasha and Marya learn a practice of mutual care. One knows him as a former beloved, the other as a brother. They do not need to agree on a complete explanation of his dying to remain with him. The women are at their most consequential when they attend to someone who can give them neither status nor promised happiness. Tolstoy renders this attentiveness with seriousness that conventional histories of war often reserve for command and combat.

The epilogue is both moving and contentious. Natasha is absorbed in marriage and motherhood, and the narrator presents her domestic devotion as a culmination of her capacities. Some readers hear a profound depiction of changed desires; others hear the confinement of a woman who once sang, danced, and seemed to promise a broader life. The two readings need not cancel one another. A character can freely love her children and still inhabit a social imagination that restricts what kinds of fulfillment the novel recognizes for women.

Marya and Nikolai form a household marked by affection and disagreements over the treatment of people under their authority. Marya's moral discomfort can challenge Nikolai, but it does not turn the estate into a community of equals. Sonya's position makes another absence visible. She has loved Nikolai and helped raise the Rostov children, yet the marriage that might have given her a secure future does not happen. Her devotion is interpreted by others in ways that keep the family comfortable. A reader should listen for whose sacrifice becomes a background condition of somebody else's happiness.

Tolstoy's women are not reducible to symbols of virtue or temptation. Natasha acts unwisely and generously; Marya feels anger and love; Sonya is constrained by gratitude and dependence; Helene deploys social power in ways the novel often condemns. The novel's achievement lies in making many of these experiences vivid. Its limitation lies in the narrowness of some ideals through which the narrator judges them. Serious reading requires holding achievement and limitation together, rather than treating an exquisite scene of care as evidence that all questions of gender have been settled.

## War and Peace — Serfs, Soldiers, and the Social Order Beneath the Families

The principal families in War and Peace are landowning nobles. Their salons, music, estates, military commissions, and marriage prospects depend upon a social order in which many other people labor without comparable freedom. Russia's serfdom had not yet been abolished during the years the novel depicts. A reading focused only on the moral education of Pierre or the romance of Natasha can forget the people whose work gives those central characters their rooms, horses, meals, and leisure.

Pierre's attempt to improve his estates makes the system visible precisely because he wants to do good. He expects his orders to mean that the peasants' lives will improve. Estate managers can comply in appearance while protecting revenue or privilege. Grateful peasants can perform what the visiting owner expects, whether through politeness, fear, strategic calculation, or genuine benefit. The scene is not proof that all reform from above is worthless. It shows that a reformer's power to issue an order is not the same as knowledge of its consequences, and that people subject to the order may have little freedom to correct him.

At Bald Hills, the old Prince Bolkonsky exercises control over both his daughter and the peasants. During the invasion, the peasants hesitate to permit Marya's departure. Their actions arise from fear and uncertainty as much as from a political program. Nikolai Rostov intervenes as a noble officer and suppresses resistance. In the conventional heroic rhythm, a distressed woman is rescued. In the social rhythm, aristocratic command is restored. Tolstoy lets us observe both. We should not pretend that the second disappears because Marya and Nikolai will become sympathetic characters.

Count Rostov's endless hospitality is another form of inequality. His openness is loving; his accounts are disastrous. When debts accumulate, the costs do not fall only on the man who invited too many guests. They pressure his children into advantageous marriage and endanger the lives of dependents. Nikolai's later labor as estate manager can look like earned maturity, but the objects of management are not simply fields and animals. They include households whose members cannot negotiate with him as equals.

The novel's soldiers complicate social hierarchy in a different way. Men of different rank march, eat, and die within one army while remaining subject to distinct obligations and risks. Tolstoy repeatedly moves attention toward the labor of ordinary soldiers: carrying supplies, holding positions, mending equipment, enduring cold. This attention challenges histories that make campaigns entirely about generals. Yet visibility in a literary scene is not identical to political equality. Being admired for endurance is not the same as being able to refuse service or control resources.

Platon Karataev becomes central to Pierre's reorientation because he offers a form of equanimity Pierre could not purchase through wealth. But Karataev is not a simple mouthpiece for an entire peasant class. His sayings and habits arrive through Pierre's desperate gratitude. He dies in forced retreat, another reminder that a person's moral influence need not be rewarded by survival. We can learn from what Pierre learns while refusing to make a sentimental portrait stand in for the history of serfdom, conscription, and rural poverty.

The French-speaking nobility also inhabits a multilingual empire. Language can signal education, fashion, class, and distance from other Russians. The invasion heightens political suspicion of French conversation, but it does not make the aristocracy suddenly separate from its own habits. Tolstoy's movement between French and Russian invites the reader to hear how identities are performed. An English translation that leaves some French passages visible will create a different experience from one that translates everything into a single language.

A productive question is not simply whether Tolstoy approves of aristocracy. He depicts generous nobles, cruel nobles, wasteful nobles, and nobles capable of learning. The sharper question is whether the moral vocabulary of the novel reaches all who sustain the households. Watch for moments when the narration allows a servant or soldier a vivid existence, and moments when such a person is absorbed into a noble character's development. The tension between sympathetic observation and unequal narrative attention is part of what a modern reader must confront.

## War and Peace — How to Read the Battle Scenes

One way to read a battle in War and Peace is to ask who can see what. The question is more useful than beginning with a map and expecting the novel to illustrate it. Tolstoy portrays commands that arrive too late, men who mistake one unit for another, soldiers who continue because the people beside them continue, and officers who reinterpret the result after the fact. A battle is a collective action, but no participant lives the whole action. Tolstoy's distinctive literary achievement is to make this gap between participation and understanding part of the experience of reading.

Early in the novel, Nikolai Rostov discovers that fear does not obey the heroic narrative he brought to the army. He imagines how he ought to behave and confronts the actual movement of his body and horse. That disagreement matters more than whether a historian could eventually place him at an exact coordinate. War changes the grammar of self-knowledge. The officer who confidently says he is brave may discover bravery or panic in a moment too short for a considered decision.

At Austerlitz, Andrei's standard-bearing charge is shaped like a traditional heroic episode. He steps forward, becomes the center of attention, and seems to occupy the role he has imagined for himself. Then he falls and the narrative gives him the sky. This shift does not merely interrupt heroism. It transforms the scale at which heroism is judged. From the earth, the standards and military reputations are everything; from the perspective of a wounded man watching clouds, the whole competition seems smaller than before.

At Borodino the narrative chooses Pierre as a partial observer. He is not trained to decipher every unit or order. He is capable of astonishment, compassion, and error, and therefore brings us into a scene where sensation arrives faster than explanation. Men at the battery work with an efficiency produced by practice rather than philosophical agreement. Their familiarity with danger does not protect them. The specific details of their gestures make the catastrophe readable without allowing it to become tidy.

Tolstoy's treatment of Napoleon and Kutuzov adds a polemical layer. He does not simply offer competing strategies. He asks why historians credit commanders with an authority they cannot exercise at every point in space and time. Yet the novel's critique of generalship can exaggerate its case. Communication, resources, leadership, and choices are historically consequential, even if they cannot wholly determine the result. The responsible reader can appreciate the literary exposure of command's limits without assuming that every real-world historical claim has been established.

The retreat uses different materials: cold, distance, bread, weakness, and forced movement. Karataev's death means something different from a casualty listed in a military report because Pierre hears it within the exhausted rhythm of marching. Petya's death in a partisan action similarly breaks the pattern of youthful excitement. These are not merely illustrations of the author's thesis that war is cruel. They show how a large event enters the life of a particular person at a particular moment and permanently changes the people who survive.

To practice close reading, compare the verbs in three kinds of passage: a general gives orders, a soldier performs a physical task, and an observer later explains what happened. In a passage of orders, language suggests intention and control. In a physical scene, language must account for interruption, resistance, and sensation. In retrospective explanation, language smooths disorder into a story that can be repeated. Tolstoy is fascinated by the ease with which the third vocabulary erases the second.

The larger literary point is not that historical explanation must disappear. It is that explanation becomes dishonest when it forgets the position from which evidence could be obtained. War and Peace gives a reader both the desire for a panoramic view and the discomfort of being unable to possess one. Its battle scenes remain powerful because they refuse the fantasy that understanding an outcome means having lived the events that produced it.

## War and Peace — Composition, Translation, and an Unfinished Historical Argument

War and Peace entered print through a complicated process. Portions appeared in the magazine The Russian Messenger in the mid-1860s under the earlier title 1805; a substantially revised and expanded book took shape over the following years and appeared complete in 1868–1869. Tolstoy continued revising his work in later editions. The familiar English reading text is therefore not an untouched reproduction of one fixed manuscript. It is a particular textual arrangement transmitted through editorial choices and translation.

The novel is also formally hard to classify. A reader expecting one hero and an ascending sequence of obstacles discovers several families, historical figures, long conversations, battlefield scenes, and argumentative chapters that directly challenge conventional history. Tolstoy himself resisted easy genre descriptions. The essays are not an optional philosophical appendix pasted onto an otherwise uncomplicated romance. The narrative's changing scale has been preparing their questions from the opening party onward.

The linguistic experience of the book changes across editions. Russian nobles frequently speak French, and Tolstoy uses that fact to mark class, habit, aspiration, and estrangement. Some translators preserve French passages and supply translations in notes; others present more of the dialogue directly in English. The choice affects the social texture. The listener should know whether a character's French is part of the original performance or an effect lost in a seamless translated version. Pronouncing foreign names consistently is useful for listening, but a pronunciation guide is not a substitute for understanding their relationships.

In English the names also vary by transliteration: Andrei or Andrew, Nikolai or Nicholas, Marya or Maria, Karataev or Karatayev. The Maude translation, made by Louise and Aylmer Maude and available in public-domain digital form, is a reliable basis for a continuous English reading, but modern editions differ in vocabulary, structure, notes, and translation decisions. The aim of this lecture series is to clarify the work, not to imply that the Maudes' sentences are an unmediated rendering of Tolstoy's Russian.

One interpretive question concerns the two epilogues. The first returns us to a family world in 1820, where marriage, children, estate management, and political disagreement continue. The second asks how any account of human action can reconcile freedom and necessity. Readers sometimes wish the novel would stop with one and leave out the other. But the juxtaposition is precisely what makes the work difficult to domesticate. An intimate ending asks what lives can become after loss; the philosophical ending asks whether individuals can fully explain the causes of those lives.

Tolstoy's presentation of Napoleon, Kutuzov, and Russian national resistance is not historical evidence neutralized into objective narration. It is shaped by a distinctive argument and by retrospective Russian concerns. Tolstoy was writing in the 1860s about the 1800s and 1810s; the position of the writer is not that of the characters. When assessing a claim about 1812, one should compare the fictional scene with historical scholarship rather than citing Tolstoy alone. The historian's question and the novelist's question overlap but do not collapse into one another.

The novel's influence is best understood as an expansion of possibilities rather than a simple invention. Earlier fiction had narrated wars, love, money, and national histories. Tolstoy combined their registers with a degree of sustained psychological and social attention that became a reference point for later writers. In modern discussions of historical fiction, panoramic narration, and ordinary life under catastrophe, War and Peace remains difficult to ignore. A careful account of influence identifies specific writers and techniques rather than claiming that all later fiction descends from one Russian monument.

A final reading exercise is to place Pierre's rescue of a child in burning Moscow next to the philosophical argument against great-man history. In the scene, the act is not grand enough to change the campaign. He acts because a child would otherwise be in danger. In the essay, small actions appear as constituents of a historical movement no one commands. The two passages can illuminate each other without being identical. The novel's enduring question is whether one can act responsibly when one knows that neither good intention nor superior intelligence grants control over the consequences of history.
