# Journey to the West: The lotus of knives

## The wrong father

The six servants of Red Boy were not accustomed to being sent to their master's father. They knew the Bull Demon King's region, but the journey was long, and they were relieved when they discovered him apparently hunting in a hollow among the mountains. Hounds pulled at their leashes. A few attendants stood beside a lord with a bull's powerful face and broad shoulders.

They fell to their knees. Red Boy had sent them to invite the old king to Fire Cloud Cave for the eating of a rare prisoner.

The great lord considered their message. He would have to return home and change his garments before visiting, he said. The servants begged him not to delay. Their master would count the hours until they brought back his father. At last he consented to come as he was.

They had no way of knowing that the hounds and hunters were hairs from Wukong's body, or that their honored guest was the monkey himself.

The messengers led the false Bull Demon King through the ravine. Red Boy had his officers, soldiers, and musicians arrange a proper reception. The boy bowed four times before his supposed father. The guest accepted the honors and took the highest seat.

Red Boy eagerly reported his triumph. He had captured the Tang monk whose flesh could lengthen life. He had held off Wukong and Bajie with the True Fire. He had even caught Bajie in a leather bag by taking the form of Guanyin. The feast could begin as soon as the food was prepared.

Wukong had to hide his pleasure at hearing so much. He warned the boy not to meddle with Sun Wukong. That monkey, he said, had once broken the power of Heaven itself. If he learned that his master had been eaten, he might overturn the whole mountain and leave nothing for the demons to inhabit.

Red Boy did not like hearing his own victory dismissed. He repeated that Wukong had come to the cave twice and fled from the fire. Why should he fear a creature already defeated?

The false father found a new objection. He had begun to observe fasting days, he claimed, partly on the advice of Red Boy's mother. This happened to be one of those days. The holy monk could be washed and prepared tomorrow; there was no need to dine immediately.

Red Boy stared at him. His father had lived for more than a thousand years and never been known to refuse a meal of flesh on account of a fast. A few days of restraint could hardly balance such a lifetime. The father's face was familiar, his voice close enough, but the words were wrong.

The child left the room and quietly interrogated the six messengers. Had they been to the Bull Demon King's home? No. They had encountered him already hunting. That explained the speed of their return, and perhaps the stranger's suspicious manners.

Red Boy ordered his servants to have their weapons ready. He would ask a question known only to his father.

He came back smiling, bowed again, and said that he had once met a skilled astrologer who wanted the exact date and hour of Red Boy's birth. Would his father please repeat the eight characters of his birth record, so the astrologer could determine the boy's fortunes?

Wukong had spoken confidently about sworn brotherhood, but he had no knowledge of the child's birthday. He tried to laugh the question away. At his age he forgot details. Surely Red Boy's mother would know; tomorrow he would return and ask her.

That was enough. Red Boy shouted the signal. The chamber filled with spear points. Wukong sprang from the throne, recovered his true monkey shape, and swung his iron staff through the attacking ranks. He escaped in a streak of gold.

Red Boy slammed the cave doors behind him. His prisoner was still bound. Bajie was still in the bag. The demon was humiliated at having bowed to an enemy, but he resolved to lose no more time. If a monkey could come disguised as his father, tomorrow might bring another disguise. He would cook the monk soon.

For Wukong, the attempted family reunion was finished. He had neither persuaded nor frightened his nephew. He needed a power to which the child could not refuse obedience.

## At the South Sea

He flew to the southern ocean and approached Guanyin's home at Mount Luojia. Attendants received him at the outer hall. He bowed and reported the whole affair: the child suspended from the pine, Xuanzang taken in the storm, the blazing carts, the dragons whose rain had failed, the near drowning, and Bajie's journey south.

Guanyin asked why he had turned to the dragon kings first if he knew he faced the Samadhi True Fire. Wukong admitted that he had hoped water would be enough. He had then sent Bajie to ask for aid, but the child had changed into Guanyin's own likeness and captured the pig as well.

At that Guanyin became angry.

The theft of her likeness was no small matter. She took the pure vase from her hand and hurled it into the sea. Wukong watched the precious vessel disappear below the waves and wondered whether she had lost patience with him too. He had come for help, and now the bodhisattva seemed to be throwing away exactly what he needed.

The water swelled. A great turtle rose from the depths carrying the vase upon its shell. It made a sequence of bows before Guanyin and set the vessel near the rock. She told Wukong to pick it up.

He reached for it. It would not move.

This was an unpleasant discovery for a creature who could lift an iron staff weighing many thousands of pounds. Guanyin explained that the vase was not empty. It had passed through rivers, seas, springs, and waterways and borrowed a sea's worth of water. Wukong could not lift the ocean merely because its weight happened to be held in a small vessel.

She lifted it with one hand.

The monkey admired the power, then immediately began asking how the vase might be sent to Mount Hao. Guanyin said her water, unlike the dragons' ordinary rain, could overcome Red Boy's fire. She could send an attendant with it, but what guarantee did she have that Wukong would not trick the bearer out of such a treasure? He had a history of taking useful objects wherever he found them.

Wukong protested that his years as a thief of celestial property were behind him. What security could he possibly give? His robe was plain. His tiger-skin skirt was worth almost nothing. He needed the staff to defend his master. He did have a golden ring on his head, he observed, if Guanyin would kindly teach him how to remove it.

Guanyin was not amused by that offer. She asked instead for one of the three life-saving hairs she had once given him, a pledge of good faith. Wukong disliked the thought of surrendering even a hair. Those hairs had been reserved for desperate situations, and he did not know when he might need the last of them. He pleaded with Guanyin to come herself.

She chose to go. First, however, she made preparations that astonished the monkey.

Guanyin summoned her disciple Hui'an, also known as Muzha, the son of the heavenly general Li Jing. She sent him to his father's palace to borrow the full complement of heavenly swords: thirty-six blades of exceptional power. Muzha went to Heaven, delivered her request, and received the weapons through his family. He returned carrying the entire set.

Guanyin cast them into the air. They changed into a magnificent lotus throne. She seated herself upon it, with Wukong and Muzha nearby, and traveled toward Mount Hao.

Wukong looked at the new throne and privately wondered why Guanyin had borrowed someone else's blades when she possessed splendid lotus seats of her own. She told him to stop talking and follow.

## Preparing the mountain

Near Fire Cloud Cave, Guanyin halted upon a mountaintop and summoned the local spirits. The land gods who had once come trembling to Wukong now rushed to answer her. She ordered them to clear the surrounding country of living creatures. Nests, burrows, and hollows must be emptied. Small animals were to be carried to safe ground. No creature was to be left within the region where she intended to use her power.

This was not courtesy for the sake of display. Guanyin was about to make a great body of water appear where mountains had stood. When the gods reported that the ground was clear, she tilted the pure vase. Water rolled out like a sea rising in an instant. The valleys vanished beneath it. Only the arranged high ground and the place of the coming encounter remained.

Wukong looked down over the transformed landscape. He had fought the demon with fire and rain, and now a bodhisattva had remade the battlefield before anyone drew a weapon. Her concern had reached first to the small animals whose lives might have been lost beneath her miracle. The monkey, whose earlier attempt to destroy the demon had included smashing a child's body against a rock, could only watch.

Guanyin dipped her willow branch in the water, wrote a character upon Wukong's palm, and told him to close his fist around it. He was to challenge Red Boy, then retreat. He must not win the combat himself. He must lead the child to the place where Guanyin waited.

Wukong went to the cave and shouted for the gates to be opened. Red Boy refused. Wukong shouted again, this time reminding the boy that he had been calling the monkey his enemy when, by the Bull Demon King's old oath, he ought to call him uncle. The child would not come out.

Wukong broke a hole in the stone door with his staff.

Red Boy burst from the cave in fury. His flaming spear drove Wukong backward. The monkey parried a few blows, turned away, and ran. Red Boy pursued. Again the monkey paused and provoked him. Again he fled.

The child began to mock him. Was the conqueror of Heaven now afraid of a young boy's fire? Wukong replied that it was indeed the fire he feared, and kept retreating. Red Boy's anger overcame his caution. He chased the monkey down the mountain, across the newly prepared ground, until they came within sight of a figure seated in radiance.

'Who is that?' Red Boy demanded.

Wukong told him that Guanyin was watching. The child was too angry to listen. He drove his spear at the seated bodhisattva and demanded to know whether she had come as the monkey's ally. Guanyin said nothing. He threatened her again. Still she did not reply.

Then he thrust at her.

She rose as light into the air, leaving the splendid lotus throne behind.

Red Boy laughed. The monkey had brought a rescuer who ran away even faster than he did. The abandoned throne, he decided, would make an excellent trophy. He climbed onto it, crossed his legs, and sat as he had seen Guanyin sit.

High above him, Wukong looked at the empty sky and worried aloud that Guanyin had lost a beautiful seat. She replied that the child was exactly where she wanted him.

## The price of a seat

The petals vanished.

Red Boy was sitting upon the points of thirty-six heavenly blades. At Guanyin's command Muzha came down with a heavy implement and drove them into place. The boy cried out. He tried to wrench the swords free and could not. Guanyin changed them into barbed hooks that held him fast. He had come boasting of fire powerful enough to defeat the Monkey King. Now he found himself unable to rise from a seat he had stolen for amusement.

Guanyin forbade her attendant to kill him. She asked Red Boy whether he would accept discipline and enter her service. Bloodied and frightened, he agreed. She took a small razor and cut his hair into the style of a religious novice, leaving three tufts. She gave him a new name: the Good Wealth Boy, or Sudhana.

Red Boy made the required responses. Guanyin removed the blades; his body was restored. Muzha was sent to return the borrowed swords. For a moment it seemed that the danger had ended.

But Red Boy was not done. When the pain vanished, so did his submission. The child seized his spear and struck toward Guanyin, as though his promise had been only another way to escape.

Wukong lifted his staff, ready to finish the fight. Guanyin stopped him.

She possessed three golden restraints originally given for the scripture mission: one had already become Wukong's tightening headband, and another had been used for a different guardian. She had kept the third. Now she held it up, multiplied it into five rings, and cast them toward the child.

One settled upon his head, two around his wrists, and two around his ankles.

Guanyin began to recite the spell belonging to them.

Wukong startled at the first movement of her lips. He remembered too well what a spoken charm could do to a head encircled in gold. He begged her not to punish him. Guanyin assured him that this was a different spell, for a different prisoner.

Red Boy fell to the ground, clutching at the rings. He twisted and rolled, unable to pull them off. Each new word of the charm tightened the lesson. The boy who had called himself the Holy Infant King had been given a religious name, but he had not yet learned to live by it.

The child cried out. Guanyin continued the spell.

And at Fire Cloud Cave, beyond the water she had poured around the mountain, Xuanzang and Bajie remained bound, waiting for a rescue whose outcome they could not yet see.
