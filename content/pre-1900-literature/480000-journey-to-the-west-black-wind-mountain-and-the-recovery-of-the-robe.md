# Journey to the West: Black Wind Mountain and the recovery of the robe

## Journey to the West — The Banquet for a Buddhist Robe

The flight from the burned monastery to Black Wind Mountain took Wukong very little time. It was the country below that demanded attention. The mountain was beautiful in the new spring: gullies ran between wooded heights, cliffs rose over flowering slopes, and birds moved through quiet groves. It did not look like a hideout designed only for evil. A person could have spent a happy day walking among its trees.

Wukong was not in the mood for scenery, but a sound from below made him stop. Three figures were sitting together on a grassy slope, talking as though they had arranged a peaceful afternoon of learning. One was a dark-complexioned man, one a Daoist dressed as a religious adept, and the third a scholar in white. Their conversation concerned furnaces and elixirs, the practices by which seekers of long life tried to refine the body and escape ordinary death.

Wukong hid among the rocks to listen. He had once devoted himself to precisely such arts. He knew enough of them to judge a boast, and he was curious to hear what the strangers would say.

Soon the dark man announced that the anniversary of his birth was approaching. Would his two friends come to celebrate? The scholar in white said he would, as always. Then the host mentioned the particular pleasure he had prepared. The previous night he had acquired a rare embroidered Buddhist robe, a beautiful article worthy of a banquet. They could hold a celebration devoted to the garment itself, a festival of the Buddhist robe.

The phrase was enough for Wukong. The robe had belonged to Xuanzang. The night of its disappearance was also the night of the monastery fire. The black-clad speaker had supplied the connection himself.

The monkey sprang from concealment with his staff raised and demanded the stolen robe. The gathering dissolved in an instant. The black figure escaped as a gust of wind. The Daoist fled into the sky. The scholar in white was not quick enough. Wukong struck him down and discovered that the body was that of a white-flowered snake spirit. The monkey broke the body and left it behind, then hurried after the black figure into the mountain.

He came to a cave entrance with stone doors. Over them was a sign naming Black Wind Mountain and Black Wind Cave. Wukong knocked with his staff. A small attendant opened the door and asked what visitor presumed to strike an immortal's dwelling so violently.

Wukong wanted none of the ceremony. Tell your Black King, he said, that the owner of the robe has come. If the robe is returned promptly, the cave may be left standing. If not, its master will discover what happens to stone when the Great Sage is angry.

The attendant ran inside. The Black King, who had scarcely recovered from his interrupted conversation on the slope, put on armor, took up a black-tasseled spear, and stepped through the entrance. Wukong laughed at his imposing appearance and began with insults rather than introductions. The Black King returned them. Neither looked inclined to surrender the treasure.

When Wukong accused him directly, the creature made a revealing answer. He had indeed taken the robe. He had seen the fire, come to the monastery, and found a guest sitting upon a roof stirring the flames. The robe had been left where he could take it. What right did this roof-top arsonist have to demand it now?

Wukong explained his own claim, including the fact that he served the scripture-seeking monk from the Tang court. He then offered an elaborate account of his past exploits. He had found the path to immortality, taken a staff from the Dragon Kings, fought heavenly armies, survived execution, endured a furnace, and escaped it. The story ended with the Buddha pressing him beneath a mountain and Xuanzang releasing him after five centuries.

The speech was intended to terrify the opponent. Instead, the Black King recognized the famous rebel by the title Wukong most hated: the lowly keeper of Heaven's horses. The creature asked, with evident pleasure, whether this was that very stable officer.

Wukong's anger flared. The insult reached back through all the pride that had made the monkey rebel. He struck at once. The bear-king met the staff with his spear, and the two fought through the mountain air and across the rocks outside the cave.

Neither gained a decisive advantage. The sun climbed toward noon. At last the Black King proposed a pause so he could eat. Wukong was offended all over again. He had spent five hundred years under a mountain without a proper meal, he declared. Was this creature already asking to stop for lunch?

The black figure feinted with the spear, swept back into the cave, and closed the stone door. The attendants withdrew with him. He had no reason to risk his life for Wukong's convenience. Inside, he could eat, write invitations, and arrange the banquet he had promised his friends.

Wukong beat against the closed entrance, but the cave would not open. He could fight the Black King if the creature came out; he could not easily extract the robe from a sealed house whose owner had no intention of cooperating. For the first time on this errand, the problem demanded something other than another stroke of the staff.

He flew back to the monastery to tell Xuanzang that the thief was found. The monks, now sheltering in the few surviving rooms, greeted him with frightened reverence. Their master was alive, the robe's location was known, and that was enough to make them offer food and thanks.

Xuanzang asked the only question that mattered to him. Had Wukong brought the robe?

Not yet, the monkey said. But the robber had admitted taking it, and the monastery could no longer be blamed for hiding it. The Black King was strong, and his cave was locked. Wukong would return with another plan.

## Journey to the West — The Dead Messenger and the False Elder

Wukong ate a little of the food offered to him at Guanyin Monastery and went back toward Black Wind Mountain. A direct assault had failed: the Black King could match him in combat and then simply close his doors. Wukong had to find another way inside.

He had not traveled far when he spotted a small demon hurrying along the road with a decorated wooden box under one arm. There was no need to guess what such a courier was carrying. The Black King had talked of a birthday feast, and this creature was plainly going somewhere on its master's business.

Wukong struck before asking a question. The messenger fell dead. The box broke open, and inside lay an invitation, folded with all the formal care of a letter exchanged by polite religious friends.

Its address made Wukong laugh. The Black King was writing to Elder Jinchi of Guanyin Monastery, thanking him for previous kindness and inviting him to celebrate the splendid Buddhist robe recently acquired. The invitation expressed regret at having been unable to help with the monastery fire. A person reading only the letter might have imagined two refined practitioners arranging a tasteful visit.

Wukong had seen the charred monastery and the terrified surviving monks. He knew how the robe had been taken, and he knew Jinchi was dead. The invitation turned that disaster into a reason for further hospitality. But it also gave him a plan. If the Black King expected the old monk to visit, why not let him receive that very guest?

The monkey tucked away his staff, altered his face, stooped his back, and became a likeness of Elder Jinchi: old, bowed, and dressed for a visit between religious companions. He walked to the cave and asked the guard to admit him.

A servant announced that Jinchi had arrived. The Black King was surprised. His invitation had only just been sent. How could his guest already be at the door? He suspected a trick, and he ordered the precious robe hidden before admitting the caller.

Wukong entered a cave built with the care of an elegant retreat. There were cultivated spaces, decorated rooms, and screens separating one court from another. Even the inscription at the gate praised quiet seclusion and freedom from worldly concerns. He had expected a den. Instead he saw a place where a creature of the mountains could play at being a gentleman scholar.

The Black King received him politely. They exchanged greetings, offered compliments, and sat down to drink tea. The host asked why Jinchi had come so early. Wukong explained that he had encountered the messenger and hurried over when he learned of the robe festival. Might he see the new treasure? The monastery fire had thrown everything into confusion, he said, and he had not had time to admire the garment properly when it was in his own possession.

The Black King smiled. The robe, he observed, had belonged to the Tang monk who had been staying at Jinchi's monastery. Surely Jinchi himself had seen it. Why make such an elaborate visit to another man's cave merely to look at something the old monk had already held?

Wukong improvised. The robe had been lent for the night, but there had hardly been a moment to unfold it before the fire. Its disappearance had come with the destruction of many of Jinchi's possessions. What good fortune that such a worthy friend had carried the robe safely away. A single look at the recovered treasure would comfort the old man.

The conversation continued, smooth on the surface and dangerous underneath. Wukong was trying to move his host toward the hiding place. The Black King was trying not to reveal it. Neither believed the other's explanation.

Then another guard rushed in. He had found the dead messenger on the road. The invitation had been taken. Worse, there was news of a hairy monk who could change his shape. The supposed elder must be Sun Wukong himself.

The Black King snatched up his spear. The visitor abandoned the bent posture of old age and resumed his own form, pulling the iron staff from its hiding place. Their courteous visit ended in a battle that broke through the rooms of the cave, out into the courtyard, and up the mountain.

This time the contest lasted into evening. Wukong tried to force an advantage while there was still light. The Black King fought him to a standstill, then declared that he had no wish to continue after dark. He broke away as wind and locked the stone doors once more.

Wukong struck at the barrier, shouted accusations, and finally had to admit that the robe was still inside. He returned to Xuanzang, carrying only the stolen invitation and the story of a failed impersonation.

Xuanzang was not encouraged. Wukong had killed a messenger, assumed the form of a dead monk, fought the thief twice, and still brought back nothing. The monastery people, hearing that the Black King had really been Jinchi's friend, explained that the relationship was longstanding. Their elder had not himself been a demon; he had merely practiced religious arts with one who was. The Black King had taught him methods of breathing and bodily cultivation, which helped explain the extreme age for which the monks had revered him.

The invitation was signed with a name belonging to a bear. Wukong told Xuanzang that the adversary was almost certainly a black bear spirit. Xuanzang wondered how an animal could cultivate powers so great. The monkey had an obvious answer: he too was an animal, and he had once been the Great Sage Equal to Heaven. In a world where living beings could practice and transform themselves, why should a bear be denied that possibility?

Xuanzang returned to his first concern. If the bear could fight Wukong to a draw, how could his disciple get the robe back? Wukong had a remedy in mind, but for once he did not boast about it. He would leave at dawn and seek help from someone the creature could not so easily refuse.

## Journey to the West — Guanyin’s Rebuke and the Wolf Spirit

Xuanzang slept badly among the ruins of Guanyin Monastery. The whole catastrophe had begun with his disciple's determination to display a splendid robe. Now the elder was dead, the buildings burned, and the robe itself was hidden in a mountain cave. His disciple said he had a plan, but the promise was little comfort after two unsuccessful fights.

At first light Xuanzang was awake and urging him to go. Wukong told the monastery monks to take good care of the pilgrim, then announced that he was visiting Guanyin herself.

The master wanted to know why. The answer was characteristic. The monastery bore her name and had accepted offerings in her honor. A bear spirit lived nearby, cultivated religion with its monks, and had stolen a gift connected with her mission. Wukong was going to demand that Guanyin take responsibility and recover it.

Xuanzang asked when he would return. Wukong promised a short absence, perhaps no longer than a meal or the middle of the day. To the monk who could not fly, such a distance was beyond imagining; to Wukong, the Southern Sea was only a few somersaults away.

He rose into the sky and crossed to Guanyin's sacred mountain, where bright water surrounded wooded heights and religious attendants kept watch near her dwelling. They recognized the Great Sage and asked why he had left his charge. Wukong explained that the journey had encountered an urgent difficulty and requested an audience.

Guanyin received him. Wukong delivered his complaint without modesty. A demon next door to her monastery had taken Xuanzang's embroidered robe. The monkey had twice tried to recover it. Since the monastery had long used her name, why had she allowed such a creature to live beside it? She should help return what had been stolen.

Guanyin's reply gave him none of the satisfaction he expected. Who had taken the robe out before greedy strangers? Wukong. Who had increased the fire with a magical wind, burning the monastery that honored her? Wukong. He had shown off a treasure, helped turn a crime into catastrophe, and now come to blame the bodhisattva for the resulting theft.

The monkey bowed. There was no benefit in pretending she did not know what had happened. He admitted that her account was true and asked pardon. Then he explained the narrower reason for his visit. The bear spirit could fight him without losing. Xuanzang still had the tightening spell. If the robe did not come back, Wukong would be the one writhing on the ground with a terrible pain in his head. Could Guanyin not help him avoid that fate and get the pilgrimage moving again?

She agreed to accompany him, for Xuanzang's sake. The bear had genuine powers; he was not a little thief who would surrender at the sound of the monkey's name. They would go to Black Wind Mountain together.

On the road to the cave they met a Daoist carrying a glass-like dish. Two pills rested upon it, presented as an elegant gift. Wukong recognized the traveler as the religious man who had been speaking with the bear and the white-clothed scholar before the interrupted birthday invitation.

He did not greet the Daoist or ask a question. He raised his staff and killed him.

Guanyin stopped in horror and anger. What was the matter with Wukong? The man had neither attacked him nor stolen his robe. By what right did he kill a stranger on a road?

Wukong offered an explanation. This was one of the bear's companions. The monster had invited the pair to his birthday celebration, and this traveler had evidently come to bring a present. Wukong lifted the body. It was the body of a wolf spirit, hidden beneath the human appearance of a cultivated religious adept. The dish bore the name Lingxuzi, confirming to the monkey whom he had intercepted.

Guanyin's reproach did not disappear merely because the dead traveler was a wolf. But Wukong had found a use for the gift. A cave that would not open to a furious monkey might open willingly to a trusted guest carrying two splendid pills.

He proposed that Guanyin take the form of Lingxuzi and present the gift. Wukong himself would become a pill, slightly larger than the other, so that the bear would be encouraged to take that one. Once swallowed, the monkey could assume his proper size inside the bear and force the return of the robe. If the creature would not hand it over, Wukong could threaten to make a robe of his entrails.

The plan was brutal and ingenious, and Guanyin could hardly have missed either quality. Wukong spoke as though the choice were simple: accept the trick and retrieve the treasure, or abandon the pilgrimage and let him go home. She agreed.

Guanyin changed herself into the figure of the old Daoist. Wukong studied her appearance and could not resist one more joke. How was one supposed to tell a bodhisattva from a demon, he asked, when either could assume the other's form?

Guanyin answered that in the deepest sense the difference could turn upon a thought, and that the forms themselves had no fixed, independent essence. It was not merely a joke about disguise. Wukong, who had spent his life changing shapes, was hearing a rebuke about what those shapes were for.

Then he made himself into a pill. It settled upon the dish beside its innocent companion, a little larger, bright and tempting. Guanyin lifted the two pills and turned toward the cave entrance.

The Black King was waiting for friends to come to his celebration. He would receive one of them. What he did not know was that the more impressive of the two gifts was a living monkey with an iron staff and a grudge.

## Journey to the West — The Pill and the Bear’s New Office

The guards at Black Wind Cave saw a familiar visitor approaching with a gift on a dish. They knew Lingxuzi, the cultivated Daoist who had long been a friend of their king. They had no reason to question the robe or the figure beneath the robe. They bowed and sent word inside that the visitor had arrived.

The Black King came out to receive his friend. He was delighted. He had begun arranging the festivities for his birthday and the display of the splendid garment taken from the monastery. A gift of medicinal pills was entirely in keeping with the gathering. It suited a company that liked to speak of religious cultivation, long life, and the secrets of refining the body.

Guanyin, disguised as Lingxuzi, paid the proper compliments. She did not mention the dead wolf whose identity she had borrowed. She held out the shining dish and offered the host a pill as a token of good wishes. The bear-king admired them. There were two, one larger than the other. In the courtesies of gift giving he offered one back to his supposed guest, but the larger pill came to his own mouth.

He swallowed it.

The pill rolled down his throat and ceased to be a pill. Wukong unfolded himself within the bear's body, braced his hands and feet, and began to move. The Black King staggered. The gift that had promised long life had become a violent disturbance inside him, a living enemy in a place no spear could reach.

Guanyin let her disguise fall. She stood before him as the bodhisattva, no longer a visiting Daoist. She demanded the embroidered robe. Wukong, working his way out through the bear's nose, emerged and resumed his own shape, ready to fight again if needed.

The bear fell and struggled to rise. Guanyin moved faster than his spear. She cast a golden band over his head. The Black King snatched up his weapon and tried to attack, but the band had already fixed itself in place. From above, Guanyin pronounced the spell. The bear dropped the spear and rolled on the ground in agony.

Wukong laughed from the air. Here was a sight familiar to him from the most humiliating days of his new discipleship: a creature with great power reduced to helplessness by a few syllables and a ring he could not remove. He had suffered it at Xuanzang's hands, and he now delighted in seeing the same device work upon his adversary.

Guanyin asked the bear whether he would submit to the religious path. He pleaded for his life and promised to turn from his former conduct. Wukong preferred a quick ending with the iron staff. They had lost time already. Why not kill the thief and be done with it?

Guanyin stopped him. She had a use for the bear. Her own mountain by the Southern Sea needed a guardian for its rear precincts. If the creature genuinely submitted, he could perform that service. His power had made him a formidable thief; the same power could be put to another purpose.

Wukong was not persuaded by the mercy, though he could see its effectiveness. If he possessed the ability to make every obstinate monster writhe under such a spell, he declared, he would not have to spend his days fighting them. He could make an entire collection of troublesome bears obey.

Guanyin did not hand him that authority. Instead she took the Black King under discipline, received his submission, and had him stand with his spear as a newly appointed guardian. The robe was restored to its proper keeper. The monster who had wished to make it the center of a birthday banquet would leave his old cave and go to serve on Guanyin's mountain.

Wukong accepted the robe with gratitude. He offered to escort Guanyin back, but she dismissed the courtesy. His work lay with Xuanzang on the western road. He was to return, protect the pilgrim, and refrain from creating further disasters in the name of solving them.

He bowed and took his leave. Guanyin departed with the bear-guardian. The cave's great doors would no longer protect the thief, and the celebration of the stolen robe had been canceled before a single guest could sit down to the feast.

Wukong turned back toward Guanyin Monastery, carrying the very bundle he had insisted on displaying. He had gone there with his master looking for hospitality, boasted over a treasure, protected the monk during an arson plot, blown a fire into catastrophe, searched the surviving community, fought the thief twice, failed at a disguise, and finally recovered the robe by borrowing Guanyin's power. For all his ability, the task had not been his alone to finish.

Xuanzang was still among the ruins, unable to ride onward without the garment he had been charged to carry. The monastery's elder was dead; the people who had served him were living among ash and broken walls. The last of their precious robes could no longer pretend to be harmless signs of spiritual success. The most splendid garment had nearly killed its owner, destroyed its admirers, and made a monster into a prisoner of a new vow.

The pilgrimage would go on. Xuanzang would have his robe. Wukong would have the familiar iron staff and the tightening band around his head. Both would have more to learn about the difference between guarding a treasure and knowing what it was for.
