# Journey to the West: The red python and the foul road

## Hospitality on terms

The pilgrims left the ruins of Little Thunderclap behind them. For a time their road was neither magnificent nor mystical. The land opened into farms, and there was the ordinary concern of finding a place to sleep before darkness. Xuanzang was tired. After days of terror in the false monastery, the sight of cultivated ground and smoke rising from houses seemed a blessing.

They approached the home of an elderly man leaning on a staff. When Xuanzang asked for one night's shelter, the man began to explain why strangers could not be accepted. His village lived in fear. There was a danger nearby that sometimes came without warning, and he had no wish to bring trouble into his own courtyard.

Wukong disliked being lectured at a doorway after asking for a bed. He stepped forward. The old man studied the monkey's hollow cheeks, bright eyes, flat nose, and sharp mouth and took offense at the interruption. Whatever sort of foreign creature this was, he said, it was not fit to threaten an elder. Wukong laughed. A face was a poor way of judging what a person could do. If the man wished, he could hear the story of the stone monkey who had studied transformations, disturbed the sea, defeated powerful enemies, and traveled through Heaven and the underworld.

The old man listened. The longer the recital went on, the less he wanted to challenge the visitor. At its end he bowed, invited the travelers inside, and ordered a good vegetarian meal. The brothers entered a courtyard with thorn fences and simple rooms. Rice, vegetables, tofu, and warm soup appeared. Bajie, who had been thinking mainly of supper, was more interested in the table than the credentials that had secured it.

He whispered to Wukong that an old man who had refused them at the gate was now feeding them handsomely. There must be something he wanted. Wukong agreed. He had known the moment the man changed his mind. The meal was the beginning of a bargain, and the monkey was willing to hear it.

The host gave his surname as Li and explained that the place was Tuoluo Village, home to several hundred families. Three years earlier, a terrible wind had swept across the fields while people were harvesting grain and planting young rice. When the wind passed, livestock had disappeared. Then people began to vanish. The creature returned again and again. It took cattle, sheep, poultry, men, and women without distinguishing between them. Families stayed behind barred doors after sundown and listened for the noise that would announce another visit.

Xuanzang was troubled by the account. Wukong first appeared to hesitate. Catching a creature that had terrorized so many people might be difficult, he said. Bajie seized on the chance to agree: they were traveling monks, not village constables, and it was a long way to India. Li immediately accused them of boasting for a free meal and refusing to do what they had claimed they could do.

Wukong stopped smiling. He had been testing the old man's expectation. Why, he asked, had five hundred households endured such suffering for years without arranging a defense? Li answered with some heat. They had spent money. A visiting monk had come with scriptures, incense, and rituals, but when the monster arrived he had fought it and died. The villagers had paid for his coffin, his funeral, and compensation to his disciples. Even then the surviving disciple continued asking for more money. Money had not made them safe.

## The bargain that was not a bargain

Several of Li's neighbors came to meet the visitors. They saw Wukong and shook their heads. This creature, they said, was far too small to fight the monster. The beast was enormous. It came like a storm and left like smoke. Wukong replied that he had dealt with such things before. He did not need to be large to win.

The elders tried to agree on a price. Would the monks accept silver? Wukong would not. Gold? No. They proposed setting aside enough farmland to support a monastery if the travelers wished to remain. Wukong found that even less attractive. Fields came with rents, labor, taxes, and obligations to people who wanted something done every season. He had not agreed to carry a scripture-seeking master across half the world so he could retire to a farm in a village he had visited for one evening.

What reward, then, did he want? Supper, he said, and perhaps breakfast. That would be enough. The elders, relieved and baffled by an offer so unlike the claims of professional monster-catchers, promised to feed the travelers and let Wukong do the work. Xuanzang asked his disciple not to make promises lightly. Wukong answered that an obligation was better than endless talk if people were being eaten.

They were still discussing the matter when wind struck the courtyard. Roof tiles trembled. The lamps wavered. The old men ran indoors and barred the doors. Bajie considered going after them. Sha crouched to shield his face from the dust. Wukong caught both brothers by the clothing and pulled them back into the yard. They had eaten the villagers' food, he said, and should at least look at the problem before deciding that it was too large.

Across the dark sky came two bright points. Bajie, relieved, announced that the monster seemed to carry lanterns. A creature that took the trouble to light its road might be respectable after all. Sha looked harder. They were not lanterns. They were its eyes. Bajie's smile vanished. If its eyes were that large, he wondered aloud, what size was its mouth?

Wukong told the brothers to protect their master and leapt upward with his staff. The monster did not answer his challenge. It wielded what appeared to be a long spear, moving the point so rapidly that the shaft was difficult to see. Wukong pressed the fight from one side to the other, trying to force it to speak. The creature fought without a word. Hour after hour the weapon moved and the dark shape retreated or turned, but neither fighter gained a decisive advantage.

Bajie came up to help. He admired the monster's spear work and argued with Wukong about what school could have taught it such a supple style. Wukong wondered whether the creature had not yet acquired a human voice at all. A demon who remained close to its animal form might be strongest at night and flee when dawn weakened its powers. They should watch for that change.

## The red body in the morning light

The east paled. The monster withdrew from the two disciples and fled toward a narrow defile. The pursuers ran after it, and the air filled with a stink so strong that Bajie thought they had come upon a thousand overflowing privies. The defile was called the Rotten Persimmon Alley of Seven-Cut Mountain. Fallen fruit had accumulated in it over generations, fermenting until the old road was almost buried beneath a foul mass.

As the creature escaped through the foul air, its proper shape appeared. It was a giant red-scaled python. Its shining eyes had been the supposed lanterns. What Bajie called a spear was the movement of its forked tongue. The body ran across the ground like a river of red scales; the mouth opened to show long, close-set teeth. It had swallowed the villagers' animals and people because feeding was its only visible interest.

The serpent slipped headfirst into a hole. Bajie caught its long tail and pulled, but the animal's weight and strength defeated him. Wukong advised him to let go. A body that size could not turn inside a narrow tunnel. The hole must lead out somewhere else. If Bajie guarded the second opening and Wukong struck the first, the monster would be forced into their hands.

Bajie hurried to the other side of the ridge. Wukong thrust his staff into the tunnel. The snake shot out of the far mouth and struck Bajie with its tail before he could brace himself. He fell heavily and lay stunned. Wukong came running; Bajie scrambled to his feet and began swinging his rake at empty ground as if the battle had been his all along. His elder brother told him to stop fighting the dirt and follow the serpent.

They found it coiled beside a ravine. The python lifted its head and opened its mouth to swallow Bajie. The pig disciple stepped back, but Wukong went straight toward the open jaws. The serpent swallowed the monkey in a single rush. Bajie cried out that his elder brother had been eaten.

A familiar voice answered from inside the creature. Wukong ordered Bajie to watch. He braced the iron staff across the stomach and forced the serpent's body up into a high arch. There, he announced, was a bridge. Bajie said it was shaped like one, but no traveler would dare to cross it. Wukong pressed from another angle, flattening the belly and lifting the head like the bow of a boat. Bajie complained that this boat had no mast.

The monkey supplied one. He drove his staff upward through the creature's back. The serpent, maddened by pain, lunged across the countryside with the iron staff projecting from its body. It traveled many miles before it collapsed and died. Wukong emerged through the opening he had made. Bajie arrived and began hitting the corpse again, explaining that he had always enjoyed finishing off a dead snake. His brother asked whether he had no better work to do.

## The other monster: the road

The disciples dragged the huge body back to Tuoluo Village. Li and the other villagers came outside, stared at the red-scaled corpse, and recognized the creature that had killed their neighbors and livestock. Gratitude turned into a festival. Families offered meals, gifts, lodging, and public thanks. The pilgrims remained several days longer than they intended because every house wanted the honor of entertaining them.

When the travelers finally prepared to leave, the villagers escorted them with banners and food. Some rode mules; others walked. Before long the company reached the Rotten Persimmon Alley itself. Now the problem was clear. The old track through Seven-Cut Mountain had been filled by centuries of fruit dropped from trees above. Rotting layers had made a stinking blockage through which the horse and baggage could not pass. Killing the python had made the road safer, but it had not made it open.

Li promised to build a new passage in gratitude. Wukong pointed out that the mountains were too large for villagers to open a new way overnight. They would have to use the old defile. There was one disciple, he said, whose talents were fitted to the job—provided he received enough food for the labor.

Bajie stared at him. It was well enough to kill a snake, but opening a road through ancient rotten fruit meant getting filthy. Wukong reminded him of Thorn Ridge, where the rake had been such a splendid instrument. Xuanzang promised to count the task as a great service to the pilgrimage. The villagers brought rice, flatbread, and fruit, then watched Bajie remove his robe and prepare a transformation.

He grew into a vast boar, taller than ordinary trees, thick-skinned and broad-snouted. He ate a mountain of provisions and put his head into the mass of rotten persimmons. He dug forward with his snout and shoulders. The remains of old fruit moved before him like mud. The stench overwhelmed everyone behind. Wukong and Sha followed with the horse and packs, keeping the passage clear as the great pig worked.

For two days Bajie pushed on. The villagers traveled part of the way behind them and then turned back to collect more provisions. They caught up on the next day with another great meal, and Bajie ate it gladly before resuming the grim work. He had been mocked often for his appetite. This time the appetite was precisely what gave him the strength to clear the road.

When the last blockage yielded, the villagers said farewell. Their demon was dead, their road was open, and fields that had become too dangerous to visit could be tended again. Xuanzang thanked them for their generosity. The pilgrims went west, with Bajie still recovering from the foul labor and Wukong delighted that his younger brother had earned praise for something other than eating.
