# Journey to the West: beyond the frontier, the freed disciple, and the binding fillet

## Journey to the West - The Farewell, the Temple, and the Tiger's Den

The road west began with ceremony. Xuanzang had accepted the emperor's charge, promised to return with the true scriptures, and received the respect due to a brother of the Tang sovereign. But roads do not remain ceremonial for long. Beyond the gates of Chang'an, ranks of officials and formal farewells thinned into ordinary travel. The monk rode with two attendants and his baggage. His destination was the Buddha's monastery in the far west, but the nearest shelter, the nearest water, and the hour of the next sunrise were now as important to him as any distant shrine.

After a day or two the travelers reached Famen Temple. Its abbot and many monks welcomed the royal pilgrim, offered tea and a meal, and gave him a place to sleep. Night fell on a compound filled with prayers and lamps. There, in conversation, the resident monks began to speak about the journey in the way people often speak about a danger that someone else will face. They had heard of high cliffs, wild beasts, deep rivers, savage mountains, and monsters. They wondered how a monk with nothing more than his learning and vows could possibly survive.

Xuanzang did not offer a plan for conquering the mountains. For a time he simply pointed toward his heart. When they asked what he meant, he replied that the disturbances of the mind gave rise to its many demons. He had made his vow openly before the Buddha. His task now was to carry it through, so that the scriptures might return east. The temple monks praised his resolve. Yet their questions had named physical dangers which resolve alone could not remove.

Before dawn, Xuanzang awoke and called his attendants. It was deep autumn. The first cockcrow had sounded while much of the night still remained. The temple provided food and drink for the departing travelers, and then the three men and their horse went out beneath the moon, making an earlier start than prudence required. Frost lay upon the ground. The road gradually became less like a road. The travelers pushed through grasses and searched the hills for a passable path, afraid that they had gone astray.

After many miles they came to a slope where the ground gave way beneath them. Men and horse tumbled into a concealed hollow. Before Xuanzang could collect his senses, he heard a roar and an order to seize the intruders. Shapes came running from every side. Fifty or sixty monstrous servants hauled the three men up and dragged them into the presence of their lord.

The creature who ruled the place looked like a man only insofar as he could sit upon a seat and receive guests. His teeth were jagged, his claws hooked, his body marked with the terrible patterns of a tiger. His servants bound the travelers, and the monk understood that the tale was moving toward an ending no amount of imperial rank could prevent.

Then visitors arrived. The tiger lord, called General Yin, hurried out to greet two companions as though receiving respected gentlemen at a dinner party. One was a dark, powerful figure called Lord Bear of the Mountain. The other, a heavy, horned fellow called the Recluse, or Scholar, of the Wild Ox. They exchanged compliments, asked after one another's health, and settled themselves to converse. The prisoners lay before them, tied so tightly that they cried out in pain.

One guest asked where the humans had come from. They had delivered themselves, General Yin answered. Perhaps, suggested the guest, their host should put them to use as refreshments. The gentlemen considered the quantity of meat available. Better to eat two and leave one, said Lord Bear, so that something might remain. General Yin agreed.

The servants took Xuanzang's two attendants. The scene was not a threat offered to test a pilgrim's courage, nor a speech about the evils of the wilderness. The two men were killed. Their bodies were cut apart and served to the visitors while the demons consumed the rest. Xuanzang remained bound, forced to witness the destruction of the companions with whom he had left the capital. He had scarcely crossed the borderlands before discovering how little protection his high commission conferred.

It is easy, later, to remember the pilgrimage as the journey of a monk and his marvelous disciples. At this moment those disciples were not beside him. The future monkey guardian remained under a mountain, and the others had not yet joined the quest. The companions of the first departure were ordinary men, and they did not survive to become part of the famous company.

The feast lasted through the darkness. At dawn the guests rose, thanked General Yin for his hospitality, and went away with the promise that they would return the courtesy. The monster household, having finished its meal, left the surviving monk in his bonds. Xuanzang was cold, bewildered, and almost overcome with fear. The imperial farewell, the warm welcome at Famen Temple, and the respectful talk of vows now belonged to a world that felt very far away.

As light spread into the hollow, an old man approached with a staff. He did not fight the tiger lord or call an army to avenge the attendants. He simply came near, brushed the ropes with his hand, and they fell apart. He blew upon the half-conscious monk, restoring his senses, then asked whether anything had been lost. Xuanzang looked around. His companions were gone, but the horse and packs still stood nearby. The old man pointed them out and told him to follow.

Xuanzang had been rescued from the feast, but the road he had promised to follow was no less dangerous. The first night outside safety had cost him two lives. He was now a traveler without attendants, trying to bring his horse and his vow out of a place ruled by creatures who discussed human slaughter as the business of dinner.

## Journey to the West - The Gold Star and the Hunter of the Mountain

The old man led Xuanzang up out of the hollow where the tiger demons had held him. The monk followed on foot, leading his recovered horse, still shaken by what had happened to the two attendants. The stranger knew the way through the rocks and the rough ground. When they reached a passable path, Xuanzang tied the horse for a moment and turned to thank him.

The old man was already changing. A breath of wind moved through the trees; a white crane with a red crown rose into the air, bearing the rescuer away. Where he had stood lay a written message. It identified him as the Gold Star, Taibai, who had come from the western heaven to save the pilgrim. He told Xuanzang not to complain of the difficulties of obtaining the scriptures: help would come farther along the road.

Xuanzang bowed to the empty sky. The message offered something more precise than a general promise that virtue would be rewarded. There would be helpers. The old man had intervened at the exact point where the monk could not free himself. Now he had gone, and Xuanzang had to walk on alone.

The slope above Double-Fork Ridge was full of sound. Water poured through channels cut into the stones. Birds stirred in the undergrowth, and wild animals called from the woods. Nothing in the country was still. Yet amid all this life the pilgrim could see neither a village nor a person to whom he might appeal. His horse stumbled, the path rose sharply, and he grew hungry. He had escaped the monsters only to discover another kind of peril: a living wilderness without shelter or direction.

Then the larger animals began to show themselves. Tigers blocked one stretch of the path, snakes another. Creatures appeared on either side. Xuanzang could neither frighten them off nor slip past. The horse dropped to its knees. No amount of pulling would raise it. The monk had never been trained for this. He knew how to interpret scriptures, lead ceremonies, and keep his vows. He knew nothing that would persuade a tiger to make room for a frightened man and a horse.

Suddenly the beasts fled. The snakes vanished into the growth, and the tigers turned back. Xuanzang looked up and saw a man descending the slope with a steel hunting fork in his hand and a bow and arrows at his side. He was dressed in clothing suited to the mountains, with the assured bearing of someone who had spent many years among dangerous animals.

The monk fell to his knees and called upon the stranger to save him. The man put down his fork and raised him to his feet. There was no need to call him a king, he said; he was a hunter named Liu Boqin. People in the region called him the Mountain-Quelling Guardian. He had come out that day in search of game. It was his work to hunt wolves and tigers, so the beasts knew him and ran when he appeared.

Xuanzang explained that he was a Buddhist monk sent from the Tang court to seek scriptures in the west. Boqin looked upon him with greater sympathy at once. This country still lay within Tang territory. The two men lived under the same sovereign, even if one had come from the far-off capital and the other from a mountain household. Boqin asked him to come home for the night. In the morning he would escort the monk as far as he could.

They set out together. Where Xuanzang stepped cautiously, testing every stone, the hunter moved as if the hillside were a familiar courtyard. A wind rose before them. Boqin stopped and told the monk to wait: a large mountain cat was approaching, and it might provide dinner. Xuanzang hardly knew how to respond. He had just been close to being eaten by a tiger spirit; now his rescuer proposed to kill a tiger for supper.

The animal appeared, a great striped creature, and turned when it saw the hunter. Boqin shouted and gave chase. The tiger wheeled and sprang at him. He raised his three-pronged fork. They fought on the slope, the beast striking with claws and weight, the hunter shifting his footing to keep its jaws from his throat. Xuanzang sank to the ground and watched, unable to take part. The fight went on until the tiger slowed. Boqin found his moment and thrust the fork into its chest.

When the animal lay dead, the hunter drew out his weapon, took hold of the tiger, and dragged it toward the path. He was not out of breath. To Xuanzang's astonished praise, Boqin answered that there was nothing extraordinary in the act. The monk was fortunate; the tiger would supply a generous meal. They should go home and have it skinned and cooked.

Xuanzang praised the hunter as though he were a mountain god. Boqin, still carrying his fork and dragging the tiger, accepted the compliment with the easy modesty of a person whose daily work had just seemed miraculous to someone else. The monk followed with the horse. He had been saved first by a heavenly being and now by a mortal who knew the forest. Both rescues mattered. The Gold Star had freed him by a touch. Liu Boqin had rescued him by the practiced knowledge of a hunting life.

A dwelling came into view among trees and rough slopes, with bamboo, fencing, a stone bridge, and the noise of household dogs. The house belonged to Boqin and his family. Xuanzang had reached his first human refuge after the loss of his attendants. As the hunter led him inside, a new problem awaited them: the meal of which Boqin was so proud was one that his guest could not eat.

## Journey to the West - The Hunter's Household and the Boundary Mountain

Boqin's house seemed almost as far from Xuanzang's monastery as the den of General Yin, though here the difference between their lives was comic rather than murderous. Hunting gear hung from the walls. There were bows, crossbows, spears, skins, and the tools of a family that lived by trapping and killing animals. Food was prepared from deer, tiger, and other game. Boqin expected his honored guest to share the rich meal, and Xuanzang, who had been hungry on the mountain all day, could only put his hands together and refuse.

He had been a monk since childhood, he explained. He could not eat flesh. Boqin considered the matter and found it nearly impossible to understand. In this household even the vegetables were ordinarily cooked with animal fat. The pans and stoves had been used for meat for so many years that there was scarcely an implement fit to prepare a meal for such a guest. Was the monk really prepared to go hungry after being saved from the tigers?

Xuanzang was grateful, but he would not break his rule. A man who had escaped being eaten by tigers, he suggested, had already received much more than he could ask. If he had to endure an empty stomach for another day, he would do so.

Boqin's mother brought the dispute to an end. She and the young woman of the house could find a way to make clean food. They scrubbed a small pot repeatedly, boiled fresh water, prepared grains and vegetables without meat or grease, and set aside a separate meal. It was not the hunter's idea of a feast, but it was food Xuanzang could accept. He bowed and thanked the women whose careful work had made his rules compatible with their hospitality.

At the table, Boqin sat with plates of game before him and his guest with simple vegetables. Xuanzang began a short recitation before eating. Boqin was startled. Was there a prayer even for the beginning of a meal? The hunter had spent all his life acquiring food by strength and skill; the monk had spent his learning in a discipline where every ordinary act could be accompanied by a remembered obligation. They were both courteous. Neither quite understood the habits of the other.

After supper Boqin showed his visitor around. One room displayed more weapons and pelts. In the garden, animals were kept for future use. The hunter explained that people in a rich city stored money or grain. In the mountains he stored living game. By dusk the difference had become familiar enough that the guest could listen without fear. He slept in the household that had taken him in.

The family had a sorrow. Boqin's father had died, and his mother wished the monk to perform rites on the dead man's behalf. Xuanzang did so. He read and chanted, giving the family the service they had not known how to offer for themselves. That night the dead father appeared in dreams to Boqin's mother, wife, and son, announcing that the prayers had helped him and that he could now move toward a better condition. In the morning the family compared the dreams and thanked their guest with new feeling.

They offered silver. Xuanzang refused it. The monk had received shelter, protection, and a meal prepared at trouble and expense. He asked only that Boqin escort him farther along the road. The family made him rough travel bread. Boqin gathered some servants and hunting weapons, and together they accompanied Xuanzang into the hills.

The road climbed toward a towering mountain. To Boqin the ascent seemed easy; to Xuanzang it was another long struggle with stones, slopes, and the weary horse. The party advanced until the hunter stopped. This was the mountain that divided the lands, called Two-Worlds Mountain. To the east lay the Tang territory where Boqin hunted. Beyond its western side lay another country, outside his accustomed range and his authority. Even the wild animals over there, he said, did not know to fear his weapons. His escort could go no farther.

Xuanzang got down from the horse and begged him for a little more company. Boqin had saved his life, fed him, and guided him. The prospect of being alone again, with the memory of the demon feast still fresh, was almost unbearable. But the hunter would not cross the boundary. He was neither a court escort nor a supernatural companion. He belonged to a place and could protect someone only as far as his own world extended.

They exchanged farewells on the slope. Xuanzang clung for a moment to the man's sleeve, reluctant to turn away from the last person who understood the road. Then a voice boomed from the foot of the mountain, loud enough to make hunter and monk start in fright.

It was calling for a master.

The call sounded again. Whoever shouted was not asking to be fed, nor threatening a passerby, nor demanding tribute. The voice claimed that its master had arrived. Xuanzang and Boqin looked toward the rocks below them. Beneath that mountain, unknown to the monk who had just crossed into the west, a prisoner had been waiting for hundreds of years.

## Journey to the West - Freeing the Stone Prisoner

The voice came from beneath Two-Worlds Mountain. It shook the air around Xuanzang and the hunter Liu Boqin, demanding that its master come near. Boqin remembered an old story. Long ago, when the Wang Mang troubles were still remembered as a landmark of history, a mountain had fallen out of heaven and trapped a divine monkey beneath it. The creature could not die of hunger or exposure. A local spirit watched him, and his dreadful ration was said to be iron pellets and molten copper. The old people called him the monkey under the stones.

Boqin led the monk down the slope. There, between great masses of rock, lay a creature whose head and outstretched hands were visible while the mountain held the rest of his body. Grasses had grown around his face. Moss and dirt lay upon him. His eyes still moved with extraordinary brightness; his voice was sharp and eager. At the sight of the monk he began to call out all the louder.

At last, he cried. The one for whom he had waited had come. If Xuanzang would release him, he would guard him all the way to the western heaven.

The monk kept his distance while Boqin pulled away grass from the prisoner's face. He asked who the creature was and how he knew what business Xuanzang had on this road. The monkey answered without shame. He was Sun Wukong, once the Great Sage Equal to Heaven, the rebel who had made an uproar in the palaces above. The Buddha had pinned him beneath the mountain because he refused the order of Heaven. More recently Guanyin had come by, looking for someone who would seek the scriptures. She told him that if he gave up his violence and protected the pilgrim, he might earn a different future. He had promised to become a disciple and had waited ever since.

Xuanzang knew of Guanyin's mission because she had revealed it before the Tang court. Now an unexpected piece of that mission appeared before him, speaking from under a mountain. He was moved by the monkey's promise, but he had a practical question. There were no picks or hammers here large enough to shift the smallest portion of the weight above him. How could a traveling monk possibly free the prisoner?

The monkey did not ask him to lift the rocks. On the summit was a paper seal placed by the Buddha, bearing the sacred words that held the mountain in place. Remove it, he said, and I can come out on my own.

Boqin did not entirely trust the claim. Nevertheless, he took Xuanzang up the mountain. At the summit they found the inscription and the golden-lettered seal. The monk bowed before it and prayed that, if the prisoner had truly repented and would serve the journey, the power holding him might be released. Then he reached out to remove the paper.

A wind rose. The seal lifted and disappeared toward the heavens, to be received by the guardians who had kept watch over it. Xuanzang returned in haste to the rocks below. The monkey was delighted. The restraint had been taken away, and now, he said, they had only to give him space. The monk and Boqin retreated up the path. Wukong called for them to go farther still.

Then the mountain began to split and shake. There was a great sound, as if the roots of the earth had broken. Dust and stones rose; the ridge trembled beneath the travelers' feet. In the confusion a figure burst from the ruined prison and came leaping after them. When the dust cleared, the monkey was kneeling before Xuanzang, his hands together. He had broken free after five centuries and was bowing to the person who had lifted the seal.

Wukong thanked Boqin for bringing his master and for clearing the grass from his face. Then, without being ordered, he began to gather the packs and see to the horse. The animal trembled at the sight of him. Wukong had once served in the heavenly stables; a mortal horse sensed in him something that it did not understand. Xuanzang watched the former rebel handling baggage with an energy that seemed impossible after so long beneath stone.

The monk asked his new disciple's name. Wukong answered that he was of the Sun family and already had a religious name, Wukong, given by his first teacher, Subodhi. Xuanzang was pleased; it fitted the path he had chosen. He also gave the monkey a familiar description by which others might address him: Sun the Pilgrim, or Sun Xingzhe, because he looked like a little wandering mendicant.

Boqin could see that his service was finished. He bowed and turned toward his home, carrying Xuanzang's thanks to his mother and wife. The monk was no longer unattended. In place of the two lost attendants and the hunter who could not pass the border stood the creature who had defeated armies of Heaven. Yet no one looking at the newly formed pair could have mistaken them for easy companions. One followed rules of compassion, self-restraint, and formal respect. The other had learned to answer every obstacle with speed, cunning, and, when he chose, overwhelming force.

Their first work together was to go west. They had hardly set out before a tiger appeared on the road. Wukong did not wait for his master to be frightened twice. He stepped forward with the confidence of someone to whom a tiger was an inconvenience. The beast froze before him. The monkey drew the tiny needle from his ear, changed it into the iron staff that had once fought the celestial armies, and struck. The tiger fell. Wukong took its skin, intending to put it to use.

Xuanzang had acquired a protector of astonishing strength. He had not yet learned what kind of protection that strength would produce.

## Journey to the West - The Old Man, the Tiger Skin, and the Six Robbers

Wukong's explanation of the iron staff delighted him more than it did his master. Xuanzang had seen the monkey turn a needle into a weapon and strike down a tiger in an instant. He asked how the staff could vanish so completely after use. The monkey grinned and explained that he had obtained the treasure long ago from a dragon's palace beneath the sea. It had helped him overthrow the orderly life of Heaven, and it could grow larger or smaller whenever he wished. Between battles he kept it as a needle tucked inside his ear.

The monk listened with mingled relief and alarm. There was no doubt that this companion could defend him from beasts. He could also kill them without pausing to ask whether there was any other way.

The pair reached a farmhouse in the evening. Wukong went ahead and knocked, alarming an old man who came to the door. At the sight of the bright-eyed creature with the tiger skin around his waist, the host cried out that a ghost had come. Xuanzang hastened to assure him that the fierce-looking visitor was his disciple. The monkey was indignant. Did the old fellow really not remember him? In his youth the man had gathered firewood near the mountain, Wukong said, and had carried food or herbs past the very place where he lay trapped. The old man looked more carefully and began to recognize the prisoner's face beneath its new freedom. He admitted that the creature must be the one who had lain beneath the stones all those years.

This recognition changed the evening. The frightened household became a place of shelter. Its owner asked how the prisoner had escaped. Wukong explained the arrival of Guanyin, the monk's mission, and the removal of the Buddha's seal. He spoke as though the release had happened just as it ought to have done, forgetting for the moment the centuries of confinement and the cost of his earlier defiance.

He asked for water to wash. Five hundred years under a mountain, he said, was more than enough time to go without a bath. Once clean, he wanted needle and thread. He took the tiger skin, sewed it into a garment for his lower body, and tied it around himself. He also put on a cast-off white tunic from Xuanzang's belongings. Now, he said, did he not look more like a proper pilgrim? The monk approved. They had taken the first small step toward turning the Great Sage Equal to Heaven into Sun the Pilgrim, a mendicant with a bundle to carry and a teacher to serve.

At dawn the old man gave them a meal and sent them on their way. Their walking days took them across frosty tracks and among bare trees. The sun lowered on another afternoon when a whistle sounded ahead. Six men appeared from the brush with blades, bows, and spears. They blocked the path and ordered the travelers to leave the horse and all their possessions if they wished to keep their lives.

Xuanzang nearly fell from the saddle. He had lost two attendants to monsters, and now six ordinary robbers threatened him. Wukong caught him and told him not to worry. These men, he said cheerfully, had come to provide the travelers with new clothes and spending money. Xuanzang thought his disciple had misunderstood. The robbers had demanded money, not offered it.

The monkey told the monk to watch the horse and baggage while he negotiated. He stepped forward, bowed with a courtesy that made his appearance even more disconcerting, and asked the highwaymen who they might be. They declared themselves famous kings of the road. Wukong replied that he, too, had spent many years as a king, yet had never heard of them.

Their names were extraordinary. One was called Eye-Seeing Joy, another Ear-Hearing Anger, another Nose-Smelling Desire. There were also Tongue-Tasting Thought, Mind-Forming Want, and Body-Borne Sorrow. The six senses and the desires attached to them had taken the form of bandits with weapons. Wukong laughed at their grand titles. If they were great robbers, they could begin by sharing their spoils with him.

They were not amused. They rushed him together and struck at his head and shoulders. Swords and spears battered him, but he stood quite still, letting the weapons ring against his body until the men tired. Then he told them it was his turn to show them a needle. The bandits thought he must be some kind of physician. Why would they need an acupuncture needle when none was ill?

Wukong drew the needle from his ear, enlarged it into the staff, and struck. The six men were killed. He took what he wanted from their effects and returned to the monk with the pleased expression of someone who had settled an irritating piece of business. His master had watched the confrontation in horror.

They had been thieves, Xuanzang said, but they were human beings. Had there been no way to subdue them or drive them away? A Buddhist disciple should not take pleasure in slaughter. Wukong argued that men who attacked travelers with weapons had brought their fate on themselves. If he had fought such people when he ruled Flower-Fruit Mountain, he would have killed them without a second thought.

The reply did not reassure the monk. He had crossed the wilderness to seek teaching that might release suffering. He needed Wukong's defense, but he could not call everything done in his protection righteous. Wukong had never before been expected to listen quietly while a weaker companion judged his use of power. The old habit of having the last word, backed by his staff, returned at once.

Xuanzang continued to scold. Wukong's expression darkened. If this was how his master regarded him, he said, perhaps he was not suited to the religious life at all. Then there was no reason to remain. Before the monk could argue, the monkey leapt into the air. The wind tore past as Wukong flew east, leaving Xuanzang alone beside the bodies of the bandits and the horse that had frightened him.

Xuanzang stood on the road and called after him. There was no answer. He had released the strongest companion in the world from a mountain, and within days the two had quarreled so fiercely that the monkey had abandoned him. The monk would now have to decide whether to press on by himself or wait for a guardian who might never come back.

## Journey to the West - The Dragon King's Lesson and the Old Woman's Gift

Wukong's temper carried him a great distance. With a single leap he had left Xuanzang, the frightened horse, and the six dead robbers far behind. He flew east toward a place where a former rebel could be sure of recognition: the Dragon King's palace beneath the sea. He had been there in the days before his rebellion, when he had demanded weapons, armor, and the miraculous iron staff that now lay concealed in his ear.

The Dragon King received him with the respect one gives to a dangerous old acquaintance. Wukong explained that he had finally been freed from the Mountain of Five Elements. The monk who released him was on the way to obtain scriptures, and Wukong had become his disciple. That did not mean the arrangement was going smoothly. His master had objected when Wukong killed some men who threatened to rob them. The monkey had no taste for being lectured and had left.

The palace offered tea, and in the room Wukong noticed a picture on the wall. It showed an old man seated by a bridge and a younger man offering him a shoe. Wukong asked what the scene meant. The Dragon King told him the story of Zhang Liang and the mysterious elder known as Yellow Stone. The elder had dropped his shoe and ordered the young man to fetch it. Zhang Liang had done so and endured the repeated testing of his humility. Through patience and respect he had earned instruction, and he later became a great adviser and strategist.

Wukong had once thought himself beyond all instruction. The picture gave the Dragon King a way to speak without simply reprimanding him. If Sun the Pilgrim could not accept direction, the Dragon King said, he would remain a powerful monster who happened to know religious words. Protecting the Tang monk was the path by which his powers might be turned toward something lasting. If he abandoned the task whenever he was criticized, he would lose the chance that Guanyin had offered him.

For a time Wukong said nothing. He had endured five hundred years under a mountain, and the dragon's story made that price seem even harder to dismiss. At last he said he would go back. The Dragon King urged him not to delay. A master left alone on a mountain road could be in danger before the disciple finished his tea.

On the way back Wukong met Guanyin in the clouds. She saw through him at once. Why had he left the man he was supposed to protect? Wukong explained that Xuanzang called him cruel, and he had been angry enough to go away. He insisted that he meant to return. Guanyin told him not to waste the favorable change in his mind. He bowed and continued toward the west.

Meanwhile Xuanzang had resumed the road by himself. He had collected the packs and led the horse rather than ride it, as if keeping hold of something solid could make the lonely journey less frightening. Ahead of him appeared an elderly woman carrying a cotton garment and a flowered cap. The monk stepped aside to let her pass.

She asked where he was going alone. Xuanzang answered that he had come from the Tang realm and was traveling to the west for the scriptures. The woman was astonished that he intended to cover so much distance with a single horse and no escort. He told her that he had acquired a disciple but that the creature's violent nature had provoked a quarrel. Now the disciple had gone.

The woman said she had recently lost a son who had briefly become a monk. She had recovered his clothing and cap and was carrying them away as keepsakes. If Xuanzang's disciple returned, she would give the garments to him. The monk hesitated. How could he accept a gift intended for a man who was no longer with him?

The woman said that his disciple had traveled east, and her home lay that way. Perhaps she could persuade him to come back. There was something else. She knew a short sacred formula, a mantra for fixing the mind, sometimes called the tightening-fillet spell. If Xuanzang learned it silently, kept it secret, and persuaded his companion to wear the cap, he could use the words whenever that companion refused instruction or threatened violence. The gift would not merely clothe the unruly monk. It would give his master a means to restrain him.

Xuanzang listened intently as she taught him. The woman moved away and then vanished in a flash of golden light. He realized that Guanyin herself had come in disguise. He bowed toward the direction in which she had gone, then set the garments carefully among the packs. Sitting by the roadside, he repeated the words until he knew them by heart.

The two companions were being turned back toward each other by separate interventions. The Dragon King had appealed to Wukong's ambition and memory. Guanyin had made the monk a present that he did not yet fully understand how he would use. Xuanzang could not survive the journey without a protector, but persuasion alone had failed to hold that protector. Wukong could not gain release from his old punishment simply by traveling beside a monk; he would have to submit to a new kind of authority. Neither of them could see a path around the argument. A cap and a spell now waited by the road.

## Journey to the West - The Fillet That Would Not Come Off

Xuanzang sat beside the road with his horse and packs while he rehearsed Guanyin's words. The formula was not a prayer he had studied at his monastery. It had been delivered by a bodhisattva who knew precisely how violent his new companion could be. Every repetition carried the promise of restraint, and also the possibility of pain.

Soon Wukong returned. He had been gone only a short time by the measure of someone who could cross the sea in a leap, but for the monk the abandonment had been bitter. The monkey greeted him with cheerful familiarity. He had been to visit the Dragon King, he said, and he had come back to serve. Xuanzang was wary, but he could not conceal his relief.

The two prepared to eat. Wukong opened the bundles, searching for the rough bread given them earlier. Among the ordinary provisions he found a bright cotton robe and a cap embroidered with golden flowers. Such handsome garments seemed out of place beside the plain clothes of a mendicant. He asked where they had come from.

Xuanzang answered with an invention. They had belonged to him when he was young, he said. The cap had a special virtue: a person who wore it would learn the scriptures with remarkable ease. The robe would help its wearer perform religious observances. Wukong, who had spent years acquiring powers but had little patience for humble religious practice, was tempted. He asked to try them on.

The garment fitted. The cap fitted too. The monk looked at his disciple, then began reciting the formula he had learned by the roadside.

Wukong cried out. The pain was sudden and fierce, as though something were squeezing his head from every side. He pulled the cap apart in his struggle. Beneath the flowers he felt a narrow golden band against his skin, tighter than any ordinary circlet. The band would not move. He tugged and twisted. It seemed to have become part of him.

Xuanzang was alarmed by what he had caused, yet when Wukong reached for the staff in his ear and tried to prise the band loose, the monk recited the formula again. The pain forced the monkey to the ground. He rolled and twisted, begging the monk to stop. As soon as the words ceased the agony vanished, but the fillet remained.

Wukong asked whether his master was doing this to him. Xuanzang denied casting a curse; he was reciting a sacred text. If the text did nothing, Wukong replied, perhaps he should recite it again. Xuanzang obliged. The monkey instantly cried for mercy. He could now be made to suffer by words spoken softly from the back of a horse.

The master demanded obedience. Wukong said he would listen. Xuanzang asked whether he would again behave with such violence toward his teacher. Wukong promised he would not. Yet even while promising, the old anger returned. He guessed that the old woman had been Guanyin and declared that he would fly to the southern sea and demand to know why she had done such a thing to him. Xuanzang pointed out that the bodhisattva who supplied the formula would hardly be helpless against it. Wukong thought better of going.

For one dangerous moment he turned his fury toward the monk himself. His staff grew in his hand. Xuanzang began the words, and the monkey dropped it. He could not attack the person holding the spell without first enduring a pain that drove every other thought from him. Finally he knelt and asked Xuanzang not to use it constantly. He would remain with him and guard the journey; he would not abandon him again.

Xuanzang told him to help with the horse and the packs. Wukong got up, straightened the robe, adjusted the tiger skin around his waist, and returned to the work of a disciple. They moved west.

There was no simple reconciliation in this new arrangement. The monk had refused to accept the killing of the highwaymen as righteous merely because they were robbers. The monkey had refused to accept criticism from a weaker person. Guanyin's device settled the immediate question of who could compel whom. It did not settle whether violence could be transformed into protection, or whether restraint through suffering would teach wisdom rather than resentment. Those questions would accompany the pair far beyond this mountain road.

The once-unruly Great Sage now walked beside the horse with a ring fixed around his head. His master carried no sword and commanded no army, yet he held words that could stop Wukong where heavenly soldiers had failed. The relationship that would define much of the pilgrimage had begun with rescue, continued through terror, and become a bargain of service enforced by a spell.

They had to make the bargain work. The west lay ahead, and the road had scarcely begun.
