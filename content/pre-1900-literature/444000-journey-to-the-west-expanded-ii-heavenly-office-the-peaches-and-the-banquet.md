# Journey to the West expanded II: heavenly office, the peaches, and the banquet

## Journey to the West — The Southern Gate and the Heavenly Horses

The visitor who came to Flower-Fruit Mountain was not carrying chains. He was an elderly star-god with a court appointment and a persuasive manner. Taibai Jinxing, the planet Venus in his role as Heaven's envoy, had been sent to offer Sun Wukong a place among the immortals. The Dragon Kings and the rulers of the underworld had both complained about the monkey's behavior. The Jade Emperor's advisers had proposed dealing with him by bringing him into the system he had offended. If he could be persuaded to accept a post in Heaven, perhaps he would stop causing trouble below.

Wukong had already beaten death's clerks at their own ledgers and extracted a remarkable iron staff from the sea. He was not likely to be frightened by the prospect of meeting another set of officials. But an invitation was different from a threat. He received the envoy, heard the summons, and agreed to go. It was an opportunity to see the celestial world that had so often been spoken of as beyond his reach. There was also the prospect of a rank appropriate to his powers. He had been a king on Earth for a long time. Here, perhaps, even Heaven would recognize his importance.

They set out on clouds. That is to say, the star-god set out on a cloud, while Wukong used his somersault cloud, which crossed enormous distances in a single leap. He forgot the practical matter of travelling with a companion. By the time Taibai Jinxing came in sight of the Southern Gate, the monkey was already there, arguing with armed guards.

The gate itself offered a warning about the world he had entered. A splendid threshold of jewel-like materials opened toward halls, bridges, palaces, and towers. Beyond it stood innumerable officials of varying rank. But at the threshold there were sentries, and they did not recognize the little figure trying to push past them. They had no entry for Sun Wukong in their records. Their weapons came across the entrance. If someone was not on the list, how were they to know that the Jade Emperor had invited him?

Wukong considered this an outrage. He had not asked for admission. A messenger had come to his mountain and summoned him. What sort of invitation ended at a locked gate? He suspected the old man of deceit. Taibai arrived in time to explain that nobody was trying to revoke the offer. A creature who had not yet been registered in Heaven would naturally be unknown to the gate officers. Once his name was entered and his office granted, he could come and go through the proper channels.

The distinction was important to the messenger, though much less interesting to the monkey. Still, the guards lowered their weapons when the envoy spoke. Wukong went with him into a region where architecture seemed to have outgrown the ordinary purposes of building. There were halls for ceremonies, chambers for work, towers hung with rich colors, bridges under which no ordinary river ran, courtyards of flowers that did not wither. The story delights in cataloguing all of it, one structure after another, as though the imagination were discovering how to give an imperial city the dimensions of the sky.

For Wukong the grandest sight was the imperial audience. He entered the court with Taibai Jinxing and stopped beside him. The officials made their bows; the monkey watched. When the emperor asked who the newly arrived immortal was, Wukong answered on his own behalf. He was here, he said. The courtiers were horrified. A subordinate was supposed to prostrate himself, wait to be recognized, speak when instructed, and use the proper forms of address. The newcomer simply declared his presence as though he and the emperor were two acquaintances meeting in a courtyard.

The Jade Emperor forgave the insult on the ground that Wukong had only recently acquired a human-like form and did not know the rules. This was an indulgent ruling, but it did not teach Wukong anything about the rules. The officials thanked their sovereign on the monkey's behalf. Wukong gave an approximation of a greeting. Then the emperor asked whether there was an office available for him.

Heaven was full. Every great palace and department had its appointed officials. A military officer finally suggested the imperial stables, where a vacancy existed in the office of the keeper of heavenly horses. The title was Bimawen, a kind of superintendent at the horse establishment. The emperor approved. Wukong was delighted. A title! An assignment! People in uniforms ready to receive him! He had received exactly what a new court officer might expect to receive. There was no reason, at that moment, for him to look any further.

He went to the stables and learned their business. They were not small earthly stables, with a few worn horses pulling carts. Here were celestial horses of different kinds and colors, animals famous for speed, strength, and wondrous ancestry. Their hooves seemed made for mountains and clouds. Officers kept the records, laborers supplied food and water, attendants washed and tended the animals, and the new superintendent was expected to see that the work was done.

To the surprise of anyone who might have imagined the monkey would immediately shirk his duties, he took them seriously. He checked the lists of horses. He watched how their fodder was prepared and where they slept. He became restless when the animals rested and inattentive when they should have been fed; he hurried them to their troughs and got them moving when he thought they were becoming sluggish. By day he worked among them, and by night he still seemed unable to leave them alone. After a fortnight the horses were well cared for and the staff had a capable, if exhausting, master.

Perhaps this is why the following humiliation has such force. Wukong was not dismissed because the horses suffered. He was praised for making them thrive. A convivial meal was arranged to welcome him and celebrate his appointment, and amid drinking and conversation he began to ask the questions that nobody had thought to answer before. What was the place of a Bimawen in Heaven's ranks? Which grade of official was he? Did the title belong among the high offices, or above them?

The attendants explained that the office had no grade at all. It was beneath the regular ranks. One might describe it as the lowest work still dignified with an official name. A good keeper would hear that he had done well. If the horses grew thin, he could be reprimanded; if they were badly harmed, he could be punished. There was nothing in the office to suggest a sovereign being recognized by his peers.

Wukong had crossed oceans seeking immortality. He had studied transformations, survived battles, intimidated dragon courts, and erased names from death's books. Heaven had invited him with an official messenger and then set him to oversee fodder. His companions had been calling him king for centuries, while the people before him seemed to think he should be grateful for a chance to perform the least prestigious task in a well-run institution.

His mood changed so suddenly that the feast could not recover. He pushed aside the things before him, pulled the iron staff out of his ear, and let it grow into a weapon. He would not keep the post. He would not stand in these stables and wait to be told whether the emperor approved of the condition of his horses. He strode out through the establishment, passed the very guards who had once refused him admission, and left through the Southern Gate. This time the guards knew his name and status. Having received celestial registration, he was entitled to pass. They did not try to stop him.

When he returned to Flower-Fruit Mountain, the monkeys greeted him with great excitement. They believed he had been away for years. He insisted that he had only spent a little more than a fortnight above. The difference came from the speed at which time passed in Heaven: a single heavenly day corresponded to a year below. For the animals who had watched him depart, much of a lifetime had slipped away while he attended to stables and court procedure.

His followers asked what splendid position he had won. The answer was bitter. He told them that the appointment had been a sham, that the emperor had not recognized his abilities, and that he had returned rather than tolerate contempt. The monkeys welcomed him. From their point of view, a ruler whose home was Water-Curtain Cave had no business serving as someone else's groom. Wine was brought, the hall filled with noise, and Wukong sat again among creatures who acknowledged him without reference to a book of offices.

Yet he had seen Heaven. He knew how its gates operated. He knew that its ranks were elaborate, that its officers could be frightened, and that a title could be given without giving much of anything else. The journey had been an education, though not the kind Taibai Jinxing had hoped to provide. Wukong had returned to his mountain not reconciled to the celestial order, but with a sharper idea of what he wanted from it.

## Journey to the West — The Banner of the Great Sage

The new claimant arrived at Water-Curtain Cave with an offer of service and a gift. He was a one-horned demon king, a visitor from the surrounding mountains, and he had heard that Sun Wukong had returned from Heaven. He presented a robe of reddish-yellow, the color of sovereign display, and asked to join the monkey's followers. Wukong accepted the garment and the allegiance. He enjoyed both. When the demon asked what office Heaven had given him, the story of the stables came out again, but this listener was more inventive than the attendants who had consoled the monkey at the earlier feast.

If Heaven did not know how to measure Wukong, why should Heaven choose his title? He should name himself. The demon proposed the title Great Sage Equal to Heaven. To Wukong it had everything the horse office lacked. It was not a task. It was a declaration of importance. It did not define him by the service he performed for someone else. It placed him beside the highest authority in the universe—or, at any rate, it sounded as though it did.

The idea pleased him so much that he ordered a banner made immediately. Four great characters were written on it: Qi Tian Da Sheng, Great Sage Equal to Heaven. The flag was raised outside the cave so that every visitor and every rival would know how its ruler wished to be addressed. Wukong told the monkeys to use the new title rather than their old form of address. The one-horned demon was rewarded with a military post. This was a court of its own now, with its own officers, its own colors, and its own announcement to the world.

The celestial court had meanwhile discovered the empty stables. The attendants reported that the new official had thrown down his duties and fled. The guards at the Southern Gate confirmed his departure. The Jade Emperor ordered him arrested. This was no longer a matter of fitting a stranger into the bureaucracy; an office granted by the throne had been openly rejected. The emperor entrusted the expedition to Li Jing, the pagoda-bearing heavenly king, and his son Nezha, a young-looking warrior whose power made his age irrelevant. They gathered troops and sent the Giant Spirit God ahead to challenge the rebel.

At the mountain, the heavenly army established its camp. Flags stood among the clouds. Weapons flashed over the forest. The Giant Spirit God marched toward the entrance of Water-Curtain Cave with a great axe, expecting the monkey's followers to be intimidated by the names of Heaven and the force behind him. He demanded that Wukong surrender before the whole mountain suffered. The lesser creatures rushed inside to tell their master that a fearsome officer was calling from the gate.

Wukong dressed for battle. He put on his golden armor, fastened the crown he had obtained from the dragon palaces, pulled on his cloud-walking shoes, and took up the iron staff. He emerged to find an enormous warrior making threats. The Giant Spirit God announced his rank and the emperor's authority. Wukong asked what right an officer of that rank had to instruct him. If the emperor would recognize the name written on his banner, there need be no war. If he refused, the celestial court should not expect to keep its throne undisturbed.

The Giant Spirit God looked at the flag and mocked it. A mountain monkey calling himself equal to Heaven was an offense that deserved an axe. He swung, and the fight began. The axe was heavy and the spirit strong, but the staff was the weapon that once held down the eastern sea. The monkey was quicker, practiced in transformations, and remarkably hard to surprise. He met the blade, turned it aside, and struck so forcefully that the giant's axe handle snapped. The challenger retreated with his pride damaged and his weapon in pieces.

Li Jing was furious. A defeated general was a dangerous precedent when one intended to frighten a rebel into submission. The heavenly king threatened punishment, but Nezha asked to take the field before his father judged the failure. Nezha could fight in forms no ordinary soldier could imitate. He went to the cave wearing armor and carrying divine weapons, confident that the monkey would not stand against him.

For a time he had reason to be confident. Nezha changed into a form with three heads and six arms and wielded multiple weapons at once. Wukong answered with a matching expansion of his own power. His iron staff could become countless staffs. Nezha's weapons could multiply across the air. What had begun as a duel near a cave became a storm of blades and metal in which the combatants seemed too numerous to count. One fighter attacked from several directions; the other filled those directions with counterattacks. Neither gained the advantage.

But Wukong possessed a trick that was not the same as merely meeting strength with strength. In the confusion he pulled out a hair and transformed it into a second Wukong. The duplicate remained in front of Nezha, parrying and pressing the fight. The real monkey slipped around behind the prince. Nezha turned too late. The staff caught him on the shoulder and he broke away, wounded. The heavenly army had now lost its first challenger and its strongest young champion.

When Nezha returned to his father, he admitted that the monkey had substantial powers and told him about the demand for the Great Sage title. Li Jing made a practical decision. The first expedition had failed, and there was no reason to throw the rest of the army against an opponent whose abilities they had underestimated. They returned to Heaven to ask for further orders.

At Flower-Fruit Mountain the news had an immediate effect. The animal kings came to celebrate. Wukong's six sworn brothers joined the gathering, including the Bull Demon King. Wukong suggested that they, too, choose great titles. The Bull Demon King called himself the Great Sage Who Pacifies Heaven. Others selected sea, sky, mountain, wind, and spirit as the domains their new names would command. For a joyful interval the mountain was filled with beings acting as though the naming of a title settled the question of power. They drank together, applauded one another, and went their separate ways.

In Heaven, however, the emperor heard that an ape had defeated his general and wounded Nezha. Worse, the ape had raised a flag bearing a title that appeared to rival the emperor's authority. The Jade Emperor ordered sterner action. Taibai Jinxing intervened again. It would be costly to send more troops against a creature already able to resist the first expedition. Why not give him the title? There was no need to give him an army, lands, business to manage, or any salary. The title alone would satisfy his vanity. Bring him into Heaven, construct a residence for him, and keep him near enough to watch.

The emperor accepted the proposal. The old star-god went back down to the mountain. Wukong recognized him and greeted him warmly. Although he had been angry about the stables, he had not forgotten that this was the envoy who first took him to Heaven. The messenger explained that the court had now approved the title Great Sage Equal to Heaven. Wukong was delighted and readily agreed to return.

This time the guards welcomed him at the Southern Gate. In the imperial hall, the emperor confirmed the new title and told him not to misbehave. A splendid house was ordered built near the peach garden. Two offices were established for his comfort, with names suggesting Tranquility and Peace of Mind. He was given fine wine and ceremonial gifts. There were attendants and quarters suitable to a great personage, yet no actual sphere of responsibility and no income attached to the title. Taibai Jinxing's plan had been carried out exactly as proposed.

Wukong did not inquire about those missing details. The lesson of the horses had taught him to resent a low rank, not to examine the powers behind a grand one. He drank his wine, made himself at home, and enjoyed the fact that everyone now addressed him by the title he had chosen. For Heaven, this was a way to disarm a troublesome opponent without admitting him to real authority. For Wukong, it felt like recognition. Both parties believed they had won, and for the moment neither felt any need to ask what would happen when the Great Sage Equal to Heaven discovered that an empty title had left him with all the time in the world.

## Journey to the West — The Peach Garden and the Seven Maidens

Heaven had given the Great Sage a house and a title, but it had made no arrangement for his days. Sun Wukong was not a creature disposed to sit politely in a garden and enjoy the honor of belonging to an institution. He ranged through celestial palaces, greeted officers, made friends of star gods, and behaved toward high officials as though they were acquaintances of the same generation. His manner was familiar, cheerful, and entirely unconcerned with the distances that made heavenly society possible. Some of the officials humored him. Others feared that a powerful immortal with no duties would sooner or later create the kind of trouble that unoccupied people discover for themselves.

One adviser brought the matter to the emperor. The Great Sage had nothing to do. He wandered where he wished and addressed everybody without regard to rank. Would it not be better to give him a task? The Jade Emperor agreed and entrusted him with the Peach Garden of the Queen Mother of the West. It was an assignment designed to occupy him, to direct his energies toward something useful, and to put him somewhere they could know where to find him. In each of those respects it was reasonable. The one person not consulted was the monkey.

Wukong accepted the appointment enthusiastically. He had been pleased with the horses before he knew what the post meant, and now he was pleased again. In the new garden there were no troublesome horses to be fed on a schedule. There were trees extending in long rows under a sky with no ordinary weather. The flowers and leaves seemed to belong to all seasons at once. Immortals tended the roots, supplied water, shaped branches, removed decay, and kept the paths clear. The local earth spirit welcomed the Great Sage and presented the workers. They knew the garden as a sacred place and guarded its fruit with care.

The first thing Wukong wanted to know was how many trees he had been given. The garden spirit reported three thousand six hundred. There were three groups of twelve hundred trees, arranged according to the time each fruit required to ripen. The first bore peaches every three thousand years; eating them would bring the eater into the life of an immortal. The second needed six thousand years, and its fruit conferred still greater longevity and ascent. The third bore peaches only every nine thousand years. Its fruit was said to give a life lasting as long as Heaven and Earth themselves. The Great Sage listened to this inventory in a state of growing delight.

Recall what had started his long journey from Flower-Fruit Mountain. He had wept at a feast because he could imagine himself growing old and dying. He had crossed oceans and studied under a master in search of escape from the underworld. Now Heaven had entrusted him with thousands of trees bearing fruit that could strengthen and prolong life almost beyond measure. He had already learned immortality, but a creature who fears losing a pleasure may still desire the pleasure again. The garden offered the promise of abundance without limit, and it was only a short climb from the path to the branches.

At first he performed his new duties. He inspected the rows, asked questions, and returned to his house. Then he began visiting frequently, sometimes every few days, and sometimes simply to look. The old trees carried peaches at different stages, some pale and green, some flushed with color. The ripe fruit was fragrant and heavy. Each time he passed under the branches, he was aware that the workers were following him. They treated him respectfully and stayed close because he was their new superintendent.

Wukong devised a simple solution. He announced that he wished to rest and told the attendants to wait beyond the garden. When they were gone, he removed his official finery and climbed a tree like the monkey he had always been. He chose the largest ripe peaches, ate them among the branches, and returned to the path after satisfying himself. Then he put on his ceremonial headgear again and called for the attendants. To them he was the great official finishing his inspection. To himself he had simply discovered that the gardener was also the best-placed thief.

Once he had tasted the peaches, he came back for more. He did not carry away cartloads or arrange a secret market. He ate on the tree, for the immediate delight of eating. The distinction is characteristic of Wukong. He wants to possess what is beyond his reach, but once it is within reach he often wants to use it now, in great handfuls, without thought of an account kept by someone else. The orchard's extraordinary fruit becomes the object of a thoroughly familiar appetite.

Meanwhile the Queen Mother prepared her great Peach Banquet. Such an occasion did not merely provide food for hungry guests. It affirmed the ordering of Heaven. Distinguished beings from the different regions of the world would be invited. Divine teachers, celestial rulers, sages, guardians, and ancient immortals would gather according to the custom of the court. The peaches would mark long life, the banquet would honor its guests, and the guest list would say, without any need for an argument, who belonged in which circle.

Seven maidens, each dressed in a different color, were sent to harvest the fruit. They came with baskets and orders. At the garden entrance they encountered the earth spirit and the attendants. They were told that the garden now had a superintendent and that the Great Sage ought to be consulted before the trees were picked. The maidens were surprised by the delay but went to find him.

There was no official to be seen. His clothing had been set aside in a pavilion. The workers assumed he had wandered away on a visit, as he often did. The maidens could not return to the Queen Mother without peaches, so they began their work. In the first rows they found smaller fruit. Farther into the garden they gathered more. But the largest peaches, those from the ancient trees that ought to have supplied the most honored guests, were nearly gone. The harvesters searched the branches and wondered what had happened to the precious fruit.

Hidden above them was the reason. Wukong had eaten his fill and transformed himself into a tiny figure scarcely the length of a finger. Curled among the leaves, he had fallen asleep. He woke when the maidens disturbed the branches. At once he returned to his ordinary shape, drew out his staff, and demanded to know who had dared take peaches from the garden under his care.

The seven women were frightened. They explained that they were not thieves, but attendants sent by the Queen Mother for the banquet. They had entered with the knowledge of the earth spirit and the workers, and they had tried to obtain the superintendent's permission. Their request for pardon immediately changed Wukong's mood. He was not troubled by the fact that he had eaten what they had come to gather. What interested him was the banquet.

Who, he asked, was being invited? The maidens began to name the distinguished guests. There were great Buddhist and Daoist figures, rulers of celestial regions, immortals of the islands, heavenly officers, divinities connected with the mountains, seas, stars, and underworld. The list was so large that its recitation was itself a kind of ceremony. But one name did not occur in it. Wukong asked whether the Great Sage Equal to Heaven was among the guests.

The maidens could not say that he was. They had received no order to invite him. Perhaps the present banquet would differ from earlier ones, they suggested, but they knew nothing of it. The answer exposed the trick in his new rank. He had been given a title that seemed to put him beside the highest powers, yet the people preparing the highest ceremony in Heaven did not know to offer him a seat.

For the first time since receiving his grand house, Wukong had a concrete way of testing what the honor meant. He did not send a petition or wait for an explanation. He told the maidens to remain where they were while he went to make inquiries, cast a spell that fixed them in place, and flew toward the banquet halls. Behind him the seven women stood motionless among the trees, their baskets partly filled, while the garden whose fruit he had consumed prepared to become the scene of a much greater offense.

## Journey to the West — The Uninvited Banquet and the Army of Heaven

The seven peach-gathering maidens remained still beneath the trees. Sun Wukong was already on his way to the Queen Mother's feast. He had not been invited, and he had not settled what he meant to do about it. Anger carried him rapidly enough. The great houses of Heaven stood about him, splendid, orderly, and apparently secure. Somewhere among them tables were being laid, jars of wine were waiting, and the guests for whom all this effort had been made were still on their way.

As he approached the banquet, he met the Barefoot Immortal, who was going to the celebration by the usual route. Wukong decided to make use of the meeting. He presented himself as an official with urgent instructions and claimed that a new rule had been issued. Before going to the feast, guests were to visit another hall and perform a preliminary ceremony. The Barefoot Immortal was puzzled. The arrangement was not what he remembered. But the Great Sage spoke as though he knew the emperor's wishes, and an honorable guest who had received an invitation was not eager to disobey a last-minute command. He turned away to do as he was told.

Wukong took the immortal's appearance and went on in his place. He reached the hall before the guests. The elaborate tables and vessels were already arranged. Fine foods waited in the open, and the smells of the feast rose through the rooms. The Monkey King gazed at them with an appreciation entirely absent from the solemn arrangements that had placed them there. He might be uninvited, but that did not prevent him from thinking the preparations excellent.

He found the workers finishing the wine. They stood beside jars, bottles, and vessels containing drinks made for gods. Simply ordering them away would invite questions. So he took hairs from his body, used his magic to turn them into tiny creatures that brought sleep, and sent those creatures among the attendants. Eyes closed, hands dropped from work, and the staff sank into stupor. Wukong looked about the hall. No one was likely to disturb him now.

He began with the food. He passed among dishes meant for the great guests, sampled them, and returned to what he preferred. He drank from the celestial wine jars, drank again, and kept drinking until he could scarcely remember that his original purpose had been to investigate the guest list. He did not seize a throne or proclaim a new calendar. He ate and drank until the feast that was meant to display Heaven's harmony had become the disorderly remains of a private meal.

At last the thought of discovery alarmed him. What if the guests arrived and found him here? They could report everything, and the emperor would be furious. Better to return to his own house and sleep until he had recovered. He left the banquet hall and went in what he believed was the correct direction. But the heavenly wine had confused his sense of the roads. Instead of reaching his residence he wandered into the palace of Laozi, the great Daoist sage, high in the celestial order.

He recognized the place and decided that, since he was here, he might as well pay a visit. The rooms were deserted. Laozi was elsewhere discussing religious matters, and his attendants had accompanied him. Wukong entered the chamber where the medicines and elixirs were made. A furnace stood among the instruments of cultivation. Nearby were five gourds containing pills whose preparation represented an immense labor of skill and time. They were not to be eaten like ordinary sweets. The Great Sage looked at them, understood that they were treasures, and decided to taste them.

He opened the gourds and poured the elixir pills into his mouth as though they were beans. By the time he finished, there were none left. The excitement of eating and the effects of the miraculous medicine cleared his drunkenness. The result was an unwelcome moment of understanding. He had eaten the Queen Mother's precious peaches, ruined a feast attended by the greatest beings in Heaven, deceived an immortal with a false instruction, and now swallowed Laozi's carefully prepared pills. This was not the kind of offense that could be smoothed over by an apology or a new title.

There were, as far as Wukong could see, two choices. He could remain in Heaven and be found, or he could leave. He chose the course most familiar to him. He slipped away by another gate, used magic to conceal himself, and returned to Flower-Fruit Mountain.

His followers came forward with the joy and complaints of creatures who had been left behind. They believed he had spent many years away. For him, the time had seemed shorter. He told them that the emperor had accepted his chosen title and given him a splendid house. But he also told them of the Peach Garden and of the banquet to which he had not been asked. The monkeys listened eagerly as he described the wine, dishes, and elixirs he had found in the celestial halls.

They offered him their own drink. It was coconut wine, familiar to the mountain and ordinarily welcome at the king's feasts. He tasted it and declared that it was not nearly as good as what he had drunk above. His officers reminded him that home had its own value, but Wukong's response was to make that value material. If the celestial wine was so excellent, why should his people never drink it? He leaped back toward Heaven, made himself invisible, stole jars from the banquet, and brought them down to the cave. There, the Great Sage and his monkeys held the feast from which he had been excluded, with the best drink Heaven had to offer.

In the celestial garden, the spell on the seven maidens finally wore off. They returned to the Queen Mother and reported both their delay and the strange state of the peach harvest. Other reports came quickly. The workers responsible for the feast had awakened to find food and wine missing. Laozi discovered that the medicines he had prepared for a great occasion were gone. The Barefoot Immortal described the false instructions that had sent him on an unnecessary errand. The officials assigned to attend the Great Sage could not find him. Piece by piece, the court assembled a complete account of the monkey's movements.

There was no longer any plausible way to treat the affair as a misunderstanding. The emperor ordered the heavenly army to bring Wukong in by force. Li Jing and Nezha were again in command, joined by the Four Heavenly Kings, the celestial constellations, star gods, and many other officers. The army was said to number a hundred thousand divine soldiers. Around Flower-Fruit Mountain they spread eighteen layers of celestial nets, surrounding the place by land and sky so that an ordinary fugitive would have found no path out.

At first Wukong continued drinking. Reports of officers demanding his surrender came into the cave, and he shrugged them off. He had good wine, his companions were merry, and he preferred to enjoy the present hour. But the officers would not stand at the entrance indefinitely. The nine fierce star gods pressed forward. The lesser mountain creatures rushed back in alarm, and Wukong at last took up his staff. He sent his one-horned commander and the rulers of the surrounding caves into the field, then followed with the four marshals of his own monkey troop.

The battle was immense. The nine star gods could not hold against the Monkey King and withdrew. Then the Four Heavenly Kings and their allies joined the fighting. Divine banners and mountain standards filled the air. Spears, axes, swords, and the iron staff crossed in quick succession. Through the day the armies struggled, and by evening the mountain was scarred by battle. Wukong's allied animal kings and the one-horned commander had been captured; the four monkey marshals and their troop had retreated to the cave. The losses were real, even though the king himself remained unharmed.

As the light failed, Wukong plucked a handful of hairs, changed them into a host of fighting copies of himself, and sent them across the field. A hundred thousand soldiers could surround one monkey, but the sight of countless monkeys, each appearing to wield the same tremendous staff, was another matter. The divine forces fell back. Wukong gathered his hairs again and returned to the mountain, a victor in the immediate contest.

The four marshals were waiting at the bridge. They wept and laughed as they greeted him. They wept because the mountain's allies had been taken, and laughed because their own king was alive and had driven back the army. Wukong dismissed the losses of the other animal rulers more lightly than his followers did. None of his own monkeys had been captured, he said, and the enemy had been beaten off. The army remained below the mountain, but everyone was tired. They should eat, rest, and fight again in the morning.

For Heaven, the campaign had revealed a serious problem: a powerful army could seize many lesser creatures without subduing the individual who had provoked the war. For Wukong, the evening could still end with wine, confident threats, and sleep. Around him the celestial nets remained in place. At dawn the soldiers would be there, and the monkey's next opponent would not be another ordinary commander of the court.
