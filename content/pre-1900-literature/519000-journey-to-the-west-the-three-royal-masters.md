# Journey to the West: The three royal masters

## A court that has already seen rain

The rain had fallen, and the four Dragon Kings were standing in the sky where everyone in the palace could see them. All the ceremonies of Chechi Kingdom had led to this moment. The king had believed that three Daoist wonder-workers alone possessed the power to call down water. The monks he had imprisoned and worked nearly to death had been declared useless. Then a pilgrim from the east had prayed, the clouds had opened, and Sun Wukong had ordered the dragon kings to show themselves in public.

The king could not deny what he had seen. Neither could his three royal masters. Tiger Strength Immortal, Deer Strength Immortal, and Goat Strength Immortal had spent years enjoying his favor. Their palaces, servants, and standing before the court rested upon a claim to command powers ordinary people could not approach. They were not prepared to surrender that position because four dragons had appeared on a rainy afternoon.

There would be other trials, they said. A rainfall might be arranged by trickery. A genuine religious master ought to display many kinds of supernatural attainment.

Wukong welcomed the challenge. He had already watched the three masters use prayers as an excuse to enslave five hundred men. Winning an argument would not be enough to end such a system. The king must see something that the three could not explain away, and his own disciples would have to survive whatever their opponents proposed.

The first new contest was not a battle. It was a feat of stillness.

The courtiers brought out tables and stacked them into two tall pillars, fifty tables to a pillar. Their tops swayed high above the palace pavement. A great religious adept, the Daoists said, could sit cross-legged on such a summit without fear or motion, his mind settled beyond the disturbances of ordinary flesh.

Wukong glanced toward Xuanzang. This, he admitted, was not a thing he did particularly well. He could leap over mountains, cross the sky, shrink to an insect, and scatter a hundred versions of himself from the hairs on his body. He could not remain in meditation for long without growing restless. If the contest required sitting motionless on a high platform, his master was better qualified.

It was a rare moment in which the monk's training was plainly more useful than the monkey's powers. Xuanzang had spent years disciplining his body to remain still while his thoughts moved through scripture and prayer. He was frightened by the height, but Wukong promised to get him safely to the top and back again. With a lift of wind and a careful hand, the monk was seated above the palace court, robe gathered around his knees.

Tiger Strength climbed his own tower. The two figures sat in full view of the king. Officials watched their backs for a wobble. The people looked up until their necks ached. Hours of practiced stillness can be impressive, but a motionless figure does not make much of a spectacle. The crowd began to wait for something to happen.

Tiger Strength decided to arrange it.

He shook a hair from his body and sent it across the space between the towers. It became a small biting insect that crept into Xuanzang's clothing. The monk felt an intolerable prickling. He wanted to scratch, but any sudden movement could make him fall. His face tightened. He shifted on the narrow platform and struggled to maintain his posture.

Wukong noticed at once. He knew the difference between a disciple distracted by fear and one being attacked by magic. He caught the insect, disposed of it, and pulled a hair of his own. The creature he sent toward Tiger Strength was no delicate gnat. It became a centipede with a cruel bite.

A moment later Tiger Strength cried out, sprang from his meditative posture, and tumbled from the tables. The king's servants hurried to help him. Xuanzang remained seated above them, breathing carefully, his body still.

The court awarded the contest to the monk.

## A treasure hidden in a box

A second test was proposed. The palace would conceal an object inside a covered cabinet or box. The two sides must identify its contents without looking. A man who could see through ordinary walls and coverings ought to succeed without difficulty.

Xuanzang did not claim such sight. Wukong whispered that the matter could be left to him. The king's people selected a fine garment from the court, folded and enclosed it, then presented the challenge. The royal masters prepared their answers. Wukong left his visible body standing quietly, slipped his spirit into the hiding place, and altered what was inside. When the box was opened, the magnificent garment was no longer what the officials had put away. A coarse, ragged thing had taken its place.

The court argued about what the object had been. There was no argument about what it was now. Wukong's side appeared to have seen correctly; the royal masters looked as if they had relied on a description from someone who had already left the room.

The next object was a great fresh peach. The king's attendants placed it in the same manner, certain that no one had disturbed it. Wukong entered, ate the fruit, and left the stone behind. What had been a tempting peach became an embarrassing pit. Again the answer he supplied matched the thing that appeared when the cover was taken off.

The third concealed object was no garment or fruit. It was a living Daoist boy placed out of sight. Perhaps the royal masters expected that no pilgrim could guess a child had been hidden behind the panels. But this offered Wukong another opportunity. While the boy waited, Wukong shaved his head, changed his clothing, and gave him the appearance of a young Buddhist novice. He spoke to the boy in a commanding voice, pretending to be an elder of his own order. The child did as he was told. When the hiding place opened, a startled little monk emerged where the royal masters had expected one of their own attendants.

The boy tried to account for himself and ended up uttering a Buddhist invocation. People who had believed the three masters' every word could not understand how all three surprises had been arranged. To the audience it seemed as though the rival strangers had a sight deeper than the walls themselves.

Wukong had not solved the problem by spiritual discernment. He had secretly altered the evidence. His side won because the thing being tested did not remain what the other side believed it to be. The trick suited the monkey who had once turned Heaven's own ceremonies into a private joke. It made the royal masters furious. It also made it difficult for them to complain. How could they admit that their claimed powers of hidden sight depended on the objects staying politely where a servant had left them?

They demanded a different contest, one in which no hidden cabinet and no child could be tampered with. The king listened. Each challenge was becoming more dangerous, but he had given the three immortals authority for so long that he still hoped for a result which would justify his faith in them.

## A head removed and returned

Tiger Strength now proposed beheading.

The thought brought the attendants to silence. This was no longer a prayer for rain or an impressive sitting posture. A person would have his head cut from his body. If he were truly an immortal, he would recover it and live. If not, the court would be performing an execution under the name of a religious examination.

Wukong stepped forward cheerfully. He could survive much worse than a blade. The king ordered the instruments prepared. The monkey knelt. The sword fell, and his head was severed. The crowd gasped. For a moment there seemed to be nothing but a headless body and the terrible mistake of a king who had permitted the deed.

Then Wukong called for his head. It returned to him. He set it back upon his shoulders, shook himself, and grinned at the officials who had been convinced he was dead.

Tiger Strength could not withdraw. He had proposed the challenge, and the monkey had passed it. He stepped forward, offered his neck, and endured the blade. For a moment his decapitated body did not fall. He too called for the head that had been taken from him.

Wukong understood how the trick worked. Not every marvel displayed by his enemies was empty boasting. These three were beings with real powers, real training, and real ways to make the world obey strange rules. Yet a power that depends on returning a severed head can be ruined if someone prevents the head from coming back.

He transformed a hair into a yellow dog and sent it racing through the crowd. The animal seized the fallen head and ran. Tiger Strength called again, but nothing answered. His body staggered and collapsed. The appearance of the defeated master revealed a great tiger where the court had expected a man.

The king stared at the fallen creature. He had called the tiger a teacher, awarded him honors, and trusted him to guide the realm. For years that same teacher had ordered monks beaten and condemned. Now he lay on the palace ground in his animal shape, and the monkey who had deprived him of his head stood smiling beside him.

It would have been possible for the contest to end there. One royal master had died and two remained. But Deer Strength and Goat Strength would not submit. Neither felt that the king had been given a fair demonstration of their own mastery. Their comrade's death looked to them like a theft accomplished during a ritual. They proposed trials they believed could not be stolen.

## The open body

Deer Strength offered to have his belly cut open. A true adept, he said, could remove his own inner organs, wash them, and put them back without harm. It was a dreadful test to imagine, much less perform. No one wanted to see the pavilion turned into a butcher's stall. Wukong, however, was not troubled. With his staff near at hand and the whole court watching, he opened his belly and showed that he could manage his own body as though the ordinary ties of flesh did not bind it. He restored himself and stood unharmed.

Deer Strength took his place. His entrails came out and lay before him. The court saw the power at work. Then Wukong sent another creature into the scene: a hungry hawk. It swooped from above and carried the exposed organs away. The adept had nothing to replace inside himself. His strength failed. His human form slipped away, and a white-horned deer lay dead before the throne.

Now the remaining master was Goat Strength. His two companions had perished in public, one deprived of a head, the other of the organs needed to close his wounds. Goat Strength was frightened, but pride and anger held him where he stood. He knew a feat that he believed his enemies could never copy. They would enter boiling oil. There would be no head for an animal to carry away, no organs exposed to a bird. The substance itself would do the killing.

The king's attendants placed a vast cauldron over the fire. Oil shimmered and roared as it heated. The smell filled the courtyard. If the contest had appeared grotesque before, it now seemed nearly impossible that anyone would leave it alive.

Wukong asked to go first. He entered the cauldron and sank below the surface. He had survived heavenly furnaces and had traveled through fire before, though he had learned from Red Boy that there were kinds of flame even he ought not underestimate. This oil did not destroy him. He remained below it until the watchers could scarcely bear the suspense.

Then, for his own amusement, he changed into a tiny hard object at the bottom of the cauldron and stayed out of sight.

Bajie looked into the boiling oil and saw no monkey. For once his brother's silence seemed final. Instead of offering a solemn prayer, he began arguing about the monkey's fate in a voice loud enough for everyone to hear. Xuanzang was distressed. Sha Wujing looked carefully at the boiling surface, unwilling to believe that the companion who had rescued them so often had vanished in a single kettle.

Wukong heard them. He could not resist making his absence into a joke. At last he emerged unharmed, and the onlookers recoiled as though a ghost had climbed out of the oil. He pretended to take offense at the suggestion that he was dead. When an official behaved as if an apparition had appeared, Wukong gave him a sharp lesson in the difference between seeing a living traveler and shouting about a ghost.

It was Goat Strength's turn.

The surviving master lowered himself into the oil. He did not cry out. The oil boiled around him, yet he remained alive. The king's face brightened. Had his last teacher finally discovered a test the pilgrims could not defeat? Perhaps the earlier deaths would now be answered by a victory.

Wukong felt the concealment of another force. Goat Strength had not simply made his flesh invulnerable. A cooling power was protecting him within the cauldron. Wukong summoned the Dragon King of the Northern Sea and demanded an explanation. The dragon knew what had been done: a cold dragon, brought to assist the adept, was drawing away the heat. Remove that protection and the oil would behave as ordinary oil does.

At Wukong's command, the hidden protection was taken away.

Goat Strength died in the cauldron. When he was lifted out, the last of the king's three great masters had lost the form in which he had deceived the court. He was a goat, just as his companions had been a tiger and a deer. Whatever their genuine magical skills, they had been three animal spirits wearing the authority of immortal teachers.

## Victory before a mourning king

The officials could not deny the outcome. The four Dragon Kings had testified to the rain contest. Xuanzang had remained steady on the high tower. The royal masters had lost the tests of secret sight, severed head, exposed organs, and boiling oil. The king now knew the creatures he had elevated to sacred office. In the courtyard lay the evidence, and there was no one left to announce another contest.

Yet the king did not leap up to thank Wukong.

He mourned. These had been his teachers for years. He had believed they had brought the rain that saved his people, and he had built his government around their counsel. Their death was the collapse of a part of his world. Now he faced not only three corpses but also the five hundred monks whom he had allowed to be forced into labor. The question of what to do with them remained.

Wukong had defeated the teachers by using tricks as ruthless as theirs. He had held back weather, changed the contents of hidden boxes, taken a severed head, and stolen organs from a body. His extraordinary strength had never been the only force at work. More important was his ability to see that every spectacle relied upon some arrangement that could be interrupted.

The king wept for his dead masters while the pilgrims waited for the consequences. One kind of contest had ended. The harder task—persuading a ruler to undo the cruelty he had licensed—would begin when the court had finished mourning.
