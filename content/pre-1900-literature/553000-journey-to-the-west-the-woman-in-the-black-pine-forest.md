# Journey to the West: The woman in the Black Pine Forest

## A forest that hid the sky

The people of Bhikshu Kingdom followed the pilgrims far beyond their city walls. They had seen the return of the cages and the boys who came out alive, and gratitude was not easily ended by a wave of the hand. At last Xuanzang left the royal conveyance, mounted his white horse, and persuaded the crowd to let the four travelers return to the road. Seasons changed as they walked. Winter drew to its end; flowers opened on the hillsides. Xuanzang counted neither the months nor the kingdoms as signs that the journey must soon finish. He looked toward the next mountain and wondered how many more dangers lay beyond it.

Wukong laughed at his master's question about whether a road could cross such a mountain. Roads, he said, did not stop being roads because hills rose in their way. Bajie assured everyone that they must be getting near the Buddha's country and could expect peaceful travel. Sha Wujing told them to continue walking rather than spend the whole morning debating how far they had left to go.

They crossed a stony ascent into a forest of dark pines. Tall trunks stood close together, their branches shutting out much of the light. Roots, thorns, and vines tangled across the path. Xuanzang remembered his departure from the Tang court and spoke aloud of home. He had left under the emperor's patronage with a promise to return bearing scriptures. He had met strange companions and passed through trials enough for many lives. Yet the western destination remained somewhere beyond the next trees.

Wukong made a path with his staff. After hours under the pines, Xuanzang found a place where the shade was pleasant and asked to rest. He was hungry. Wukong took the begging bowl and rose by cloud to seek food. From the air he could see a gentle radiance hovering near the place where his master sat—a mark, to his eyes, of the monk's long spiritual discipline. Wukong thought of the years when he had ruled Flower-Fruit Mountain with tens of thousands of followers and had needed to obey no one. Now he was protecting a mortal monk, learning to be patient, and perhaps nearing a reward greater than the noisy title of Great Sage.

As he watched, a black vapor began to curl upward from another part of the forest. It thickened and drifted across the brightness near Xuanzang. Wukong's delight vanished. The forest held something that did not belong with a resting party. He turned back before collecting food.

## A voice tied to a tree

Xuanzang was sitting alone reciting the Heart Sutra when he heard a woman calling for help. He rose and followed the sound past a tangle of roots. Behind a broad tree he found a young woman whose upper body was bound to the trunk with vines. Her lower body had been buried in earth, as if the forest itself were holding her prisoner. Her face was wet with tears.

She said her family lived in a country some distance away. During a visit to their ancestors' graves, armed robbers had attacked. Her relatives escaped with their horses and litters, but she had fallen and been taken into the forest. The robbers quarreled over who would claim her. At last, unable to agree, they bound her there and left. She had waited many days, she said, growing weaker and expecting to die. Xuanzang could see that she was beautiful and frightened. The story supplied him with every reason he needed to offer help.

He called to Bajie and Sha, who had wandered away to gather fruit and look around. Bajie came running. He was already pulling at the ropes when Wukong dropped from the air and seized his ear. The pig fell backward with a shout. Why had his elder brother knocked him over for trying to save someone? Wukong said the woman was a demon and the ropes were part of its snare.

Xuanzang was furious. He could see a bound person. How could Wukong look at her and see only an enemy? Bajie made matters worse by teasing that the monkey wanted to drive the others off so he could return alone to such a beautiful woman. Wukong answered that the pig's appetite for romance had never been a reliable guide to good sense. Then he insisted that his master leave the stranger alone.

Xuanzang hesitated. The Monkey King had recognized demons before, sometimes correctly when nobody believed him. For a moment his trust in Wukong's experience prevailed. The pilgrims continued west, leaving the woman tied to the tree.

The woman waited until they were out of sight. Then she sent her voice along the wind straight into Xuanzang's ears. How could a monk who sought the scriptures pass a living person and refuse to save her? Was not the preservation of one life better than grand displays of piety? The words were aimed at the part of Xuanzang that had rescued starving strangers, challenged cruel kings, and refused to treat compassion as optional.

He stopped the horse. None of the other disciples had heard the appeal. Wukong pointed this out. Xuanzang answered that the words were just, whether or not the others had heard them. He would not put the value of the scriptures above the immediate life of someone calling for help. He went back, ordered Bajie to dig and untie her, and brought her out of the forest.

## How to rescue a stranger

Wukong greeted the rescued woman with a smile that made Xuanzang angrier. Why was he laughing at a life saved? Wukong answered with a series of practical objections. If monks traveled with a young stranger, a magistrate might accuse them of having abducted her, regardless of their good intention. More directly, the party had a swift horse and a difficult road. The woman had delicate feet and could not keep pace. To rescue her from one tree only to abandon her to wolves at night would hardly count as saving her.

Xuanzang accepted that last point and asked what should be done. Wukong proposed, with deliberate provocation, that the woman ride on the horse with Xuanzang. The monk was scandalized. Then perhaps Bajie should carry her, Wukong suggested. Bajie protested that his elder brother would never stop teasing him about that journey. At last Xuanzang decided that he would walk beside the woman while Bajie led the empty horse. They would find an inhabited place or a monastery and leave her safely with other people.

Wukong could argue no further without openly disobeying. He knew what the dark vapor meant, but his master had chosen a course that fit the heart of his vows. The monkey walked at the edge of the party with the staff in his hand, watching the woman rather than the road. She said little. Her chance had arrived. The ropes were gone, and she was within the protection of the person she intended to deceive.

Toward evening they came to a monastery. Its outer walls were damaged, its gates hung crooked, and weeds grew between broken stones. Xuanzang went ahead to ask for lodging. Inside the first courts he found a place so ruined that he could scarcely bear it. Statues had fallen, rain had washed their colors away, and a great bronze bell lay partly sunk into the earth. He touched the silent bell and lamented that it no longer called anyone to prayer.

As he spoke, the bell rang sharply. Xuanzang stumbled backward, convinced that the neglected vessel had become a spirit. A dark-faced caretaker hurried over and confessed that he had struck it with a brick. He had been uncertain who was walking among the ruins and wanted to frighten away intruders. The monk's fear dissolved into a shaky apology, though he still looked with sadness at the ruined halls.

The caretaker led him farther in. The inner monastery was entirely different. There were sound buildings, clean courts, painted images, tended lamps, and working rooms. Xuanzang asked why the front was abandoned while the back flourished. Bandits, the caretaker said, had used the old halls for shelter and firewood. The monks could neither resist them nor repeatedly restore what would be destroyed, so they had built new quarters behind the ruins. One institution had become two landscapes: a wasted public entrance and a protected living sanctuary.

## A place behind the Heavenly King's image

The monastery was called Zhenhai Chanlin Temple, the Ocean-Guarding Monastery. Its resident monks included men whose clothing and speech were unfamiliar to Xuanzang. Some were astonished when his three disciples arrived. The novices ran inside reporting that the courteous holy man had been accompanied by monsters: one with a thunder-god face, one with a pig's snout, and one blue-faced and formidable. Xuanzang laughed and assured them that these were his disciples, rough in appearance but precious in their service. He asked the hosts not to judge them by their bodies.

The woman came in with the travelers. Her presence excited curiosity as well as concern. To the residents she was a young stranger among four male pilgrims, and the circumstances required care. Xuanzang explained that he had found her bound in the Black Pine Forest and would be grateful if she could rest somewhere safe before being sent onward. The monks offered food, prepared lodging, and considered where a woman might sleep without embarrassment or suspicion. They chose a place behind the image of a Heavenly King in the temple hall.

Xuanzang thanked them. Wukong remained watchful. He had crossed the Black Pine Forest under a cloud of dark vapor, and he had watched his master's compassion lead the demon into an inhabited religious house. He did not yet know what disguise she would use next. The monks believed they were offering shelter to a rescued traveler. The woman had entered with all the safety that a good host could provide, and the day closed without anybody in the temple hearing her true intentions.
