# Wuthering Heights: the two generations and the evidence of narrators

## Wuthering Heights — Story: Lockwood and the Haunted House

The first person to speak in Wuthering Heights is not Catherine Earnshaw and not Heathcliff. It is a tenant called Mr. Lockwood, newly arrived in the Yorkshire countryside in 1801. He has rented Thrushcross Grange from a landlord who lives at a harsher house nearby, Wuthering Heights. Lockwood thinks of himself as a man who prefers solitude. Almost immediately, the novel lets us see that he is not especially good at understanding the people around him.

He visits Heathcliff at the Heights and takes his reserve for a kind of kinship between two solitary men. The welcome is unfriendly. Dogs threaten him, the servants are brusque, and the house's occupants seem divided by hostility as well as by unfamiliar relationships. There is an elderly religious servant, Joseph, who is constantly scolding; a young man called Hareton Earnshaw; and a young woman whom Lockwood first misunderstands as Heathcliff's wife. She was born Catherine Linton, though marriage has given her the surname Heathcliff. She is not the Catherine whose childhood will soon occupy the story. The recurrence of names is one of the novel's traps. A listener needs to keep the generations separate even when the book itself makes them seem to echo each other.

On a later visit, bad weather prevents Lockwood from going home. He is put to sleep in a room where an old bed, a window, and scratched names connect the present to a life he knows nothing about. He sees variations on the name Catherine: Earnshaw, Heathcliff, Linton. The discovery is more revealing than the explanations he has been given downstairs. During the night he has a terrifying experience at the window, where a voice and a small hand seem to belong to a wandering child who wants to be let in. Lockwood responds with fear and violence. Heathcliff, hearing the disturbance, is overwhelmed. He calls for Catherine to come back.

The scene is often treated as proof that Wuthering Heights is a ghost story. But notice how little the outsider can verify. Lockwood has had a nightmare or a supernatural encounter; the text refuses an easy separation. He has learned that Heathcliff is tormented by an absence, not what the absence means. A name appears in several forms because a woman has lived through changes in family status and marriage. The window represents a threshold between the dead and the living, but it is also part of a room whose history has been concealed from the visitor.

Lockwood returns to Thrushcross Grange ill and fascinated. He asks its housekeeper, Ellen Dean, usually called Nelly, to explain the people at the Heights. Nelly knew the Earnshaw and Linton households for many years. Most of the long novel will consist of her recollections, set inside Lockwood's account. So we will hear the histories of Catherine, Heathcliff, Edgar, Isabella, Hareton, and the younger Catherine through a witness who has been a servant, caretaker, confidante, and sometimes participant in their decisions.

Nelly begins with the older Earnshaw family. Mr. Earnshaw once returned from a journey to Liverpool carrying a foundling boy whom he had found there. The boy was given the name Heathcliff, after a dead child in the family. He did not arrive with an established surname, known parentage, or secure place in local society. Catherine and her brother Hindley reacted differently to him, and the household never fully settled what it meant to bring an outsider into a family organized around inheritance.

We are now entering a past decades before Lockwood's visit. The house will contain deaths, marriages, debts, revenge, and children whose names resemble those of their parents. The narrator who claims to prefer solitude is about to listen to an intricate history of intimacy he can barely comprehend. Emily Brontë could have started with the children on the moor. Instead she begins with a stranger who reads almost every signal badly. Before we know the central tragedy, we have learned to question how a story is received, and who has the right to explain a household's history.

## Wuthering Heights — Story: Catherine, Heathcliff, and a Choice

The foundling Heathcliff becomes inseparable from Catherine Earnshaw. They run across the moors, defy household rules, and share a fierce intimacy that seems to belong to a world outside ordinary family arrangements. Yet their freedom is never complete. Catherine is the daughter of the house. Heathcliff has been taken in by her father, but he has no independent claim to the family's name or property. Her brother Hindley resents the newcomer and increasingly treats him as a rival for affection and status. Childhood friendship grows inside a hierarchy that the children do not control.

When Mr. Earnshaw dies, Hindley inherits control of Wuthering Heights. He has married Frances, and he uses his new authority to strip Heathcliff of education and reduce him to labor. The cruelty is partly personal revenge, but it is enabled by the power of a male heir over an unprotected dependent. Heathcliff can endure hardship and answer contempt with contempt. He cannot simply leave the social system that has placed him beneath the young men whose rank he resents. Catherine continues to love his company, but their shared life grows more difficult to imagine as a settled adult future.

One night Catherine and Heathcliff spy on the neighboring household at Thrushcross Grange. The Lintons' domestic world seems elegant, sheltered, and ridiculous to them until a dog bites Catherine. She is taken into the house to recover, and she remains there for several weeks. When she returns to Wuthering Heights, she has been dressed and instructed in the manners of a young lady. Edgar and Isabella Linton have become familiar companions. Heathcliff experiences the transformation as a judgment on his rough clothes, manners, and prospects. Catherine can cross between the moor and the drawing room. Heathcliff increasingly cannot.

Frances gives birth to a son, Hareton, and dies soon afterward. Hindley sinks into drinking and bitterness. His treatment of Heathcliff grows worse, and he neglects his own child. Nelly, who cares for Hareton, becomes more than an observer of the household. She has reason to judge Hindley's failures and Heathcliff's resentment, though her account cannot be mistaken for an impartial transcript of what everyone felt. The family is destroying the conditions in which its children might have grown without inheriting a feud.

Edgar Linton courts Catherine. He offers wealth, social position, and a kind of cultivated affection that her early life with Heathcliff never offered. Catherine wants what marriage to Edgar makes possible, yet she has not ceased to regard Heathcliff as essential to herself. In a conversation with Nelly she tries to explain the conflict. She says it would degrade her to marry Heathcliff in his present position. She also declares that her bond with him is deeper than ordinary romantic preference: 'I am Heathcliff.' The two statements belong together. Her desire for social security and her sense of shared being do not cancel each other, and she has no practical account of how they might coexist.

Heathcliff overhears the part about degradation and leaves before hearing the rest. He disappears for years. Catherine searches and grieves, but the decision to marry Edgar proceeds. Her choice has a logic; it also betrays the person with whom she thought she shared a life. Emily Brontë makes it difficult to classify Catherine as either a selfish social climber or a victim of circumstance. She has felt the force of status, made an advantageous choice, and perhaps believed she could preserve the older intimacy without reckoning with what that would ask of Edgar or Heathcliff.

At the close of this early phase, the novel has created the possibility of a romance and dismantled its simple form. The problem is not merely that two lovers are kept apart by a cruel world. Catherine wants incompatible arrangements, Hindley governs through resentment, Edgar belongs to a class that assumes its own authority, and Heathcliff has been trained by mistreatment to look for revenge. The moors can hold the memory of freedom, but they cannot supply a lawful household or undo the decisions made indoors. The story turns when Heathcliff comes back.

## Wuthering Heights — Story: The Return and Catherine's Death

Three years after his disappearance, Heathcliff returns. He is well dressed, apparently wealthy, and able to move among the people who once despised him. How he acquired his money remains obscure. The blank is important: we cannot treat an imagined biography as evidence of his growth. We can observe what he chooses to do with his new resources. He returns to Wuthering Heights, begins exploiting Hindley's gambling and debts, and renews his connection with Catherine, now married to Edgar Linton.

Catherine rejoices in Heathcliff's return and expects Edgar to accommodate their intimacy. Edgar cannot understand why he should welcome a man who openly despises him. Heathcliff sees that Catherine's marriage gives her status but also makes her available to be used in a struggle with her husband. He begins courting Edgar's sister Isabella, not because he has developed a tender regard for her, but because the relationship will wound the Lintons and offer another path into their family's power. Catherine warns Isabella about Heathcliff's character, even as she fails to grasp how far he is willing to go in anger.

The encounter between these three adults repeatedly turns private feeling into a contest for control. Edgar orders Heathcliff out of his house. Catherine refuses to allow their claims to be separated into a calm choice. She locks herself away and will not eat, and illness follows. Her suffering is not a simple method for securing victory; she is exhausted, frightened, and increasingly unable to live within the life she has chosen. Nelly recounts the illness with a mix of concern and judgment. Our strongest descriptions of Catherine's condition are still carried by somebody who must interpret the events she witnessed.

Isabella elopes with Heathcliff. The marriage is disastrous. He treats her with contempt and cruelty, and her love is rapidly exposed as a fantasy about a man she does not know. It would be a grave mistake to describe their life as a dark but admirable variation on romantic passion. Heathcliff's conduct harms Isabella directly and is part of a larger revenge against her brother. Brontë places the marriage beside Catherine's illness so that the costs of desire cannot be confined to the central couple.

As Catherine's health declines, she and Heathcliff meet in a scene of almost unbearable accusation. Each charges the other with betrayal and suffering. They cling to each other, but their reunion cannot recreate the childhood world of the moors. Catherine is gravely ill and pregnant; she gives birth to a daughter, also named Catherine, and dies. The girl will be known as young Cathy. This is the second generation, and her place in the novel must not be confused with her mother's. The mother's story has become a legacy the daughter did not choose.

Heathcliff's response is terrifying. He does not wish simply to preserve Catherine's memory. He begs her spirit to remain near him, even to haunt him rather than leave him in a world without her. His grief is real and immense, and it does not render his earlier or later acts innocent. He curses the separation he cannot reverse. In the opening chapters, Lockwood encountered a man who seemed to be begging a dead woman to return at a window. Now the old grief becomes intelligible, although the question of a literal haunting remains open.

Catherine's death could have ended a conventional tragic romance. Emily Brontë has not reached even the middle of her novel. The damage must continue through other people, property, and years of ordinary life. Heathcliff's wealth and will give him power to make the next generation inhabit the consequences of a bond that those children never shared. The story's central horror is not that love has been defeated by death. It is that a survivor chooses to use living people as the material of his mourning and revenge.

## Wuthering Heights — Story: Isabella, Hindley, and the Children

Catherine Earnshaw Linton is dead. Her husband Edgar is left with their infant daughter. Heathcliff has not relinquished his wish to punish the families he holds responsible for his suffering. To understand the second half of Wuthering Heights, we have to follow not only his grief but the changing ownership of houses, the lives of his dependents, and the children who will inherit the conflict.

Isabella, who married Heathcliff against her brother's wishes, sends Nelly a bitter account of her experience at Wuthering Heights. She has discovered a household governed by hatred and disorder. Hindley is drunk, Heathcliff is ruthless, and she has become the object of her husband's contempt. He makes no secret of wishing to hurt Edgar through her. The narrative gives Isabella moments of anger and resistance as well as suffering. She is neither a romantic martyr who proves her love by enduring abuse nor the simple fool that some characters dismiss. The husband she imagined and the husband who confines her in fear are not the same person.

After a violent struggle at the Heights, Isabella escapes. She eventually settles away from Yorkshire and bears a son, Linton Heathcliff. The shared surname makes a claim about identity and belonging before the characters have explained it. The child belongs by birth to both the Heathcliff and Linton lines, and his future will be treated as an instrument of property and revenge. His mother is able to leave Heathcliff, but her marriage and his legal claim to their son cannot be wished away. Later, after Isabella's death, those claims will return to the center of the story.

Hindley Earnshaw also dies. His drinking and gambling have left Wuthering Heights financially exposed. Heathcliff has lent money against the property and succeeds in taking control of the house. Hindley's little boy, Hareton, is now dependent on the man his father degraded. Heathcliff seizes the opportunity to repeat the injury in reverse. He denies Hareton the education and social prospects that would suit his family position, allowing him to grow up with the manners of a rough servant. This is not a necessary way of protecting Hareton. It is a deliberate humiliation of Hindley's heir.

Here the novel makes revenge visible across generations. Hindley used his power to push Heathcliff into labor and ignorance. Heathcliff now controls the house and can do the same to Hindley's child. He is not merely recovering what was stolen from him. He is recreating the structure of an injury with himself in the position of master. The logic of retaliation promises symmetry; the reality is that a boy who did not commit the original cruelty suffers for it.

At Thrushcross Grange, meanwhile, Edgar raises his daughter Catherine in relative seclusion and affection. Young Cathy grows spirited, curious, and more sheltered than her mother. She wants to explore the moors beyond the borders she knows. In time she meets Hareton, without fully understanding who he is or why his manners seem so different from her own. He belongs to her family network through the Earnshaws, but social degradation hides that fact. The second generation has inherited family names without access to a truthful account of what the names mean.

Isabella becomes fatally ill in the south. Edgar goes to see his sister and brings her son Linton back to the Grange after her death. The boy is fragile, fretful, and dependent. Heathcliff demands that his son be sent to him, and Edgar has little power to resist. Linton is taken to Wuthering Heights, where a father who shows him little tenderness recognizes that a son connected to the Linton estate may be useful. Young Cathy and Linton are cousins, and Heathcliff sees a marriage between them as one more means of securing control over the family's future.

The surviving children are strikingly different. Hareton is physically strong but deliberately denied cultivation. Linton is delicate and selfish, shaped by fear and his father's manipulation. Cathy has been loved and educated, but she knows little about the history connecting their households. None was present when Heathcliff and the elder Catherine made the choices that still govern them. The revenge plot has moved from passionate adults to vulnerable inheritors. We are being asked whether history must repeat itself simply because those with power intend it to.

## Wuthering Heights — Story: The Forced Marriage and the Estates

Young Cathy loves her father Edgar Linton, and he is becoming seriously ill. Her world is increasingly occupied by care, anxiety, and the wish to know the wider countryside. Heathcliff sees in her affection and in Edgar's declining health an opportunity. He promotes a relationship between Cathy and his son Linton, who is also Cathy's cousin. Linton appears delicate enough to arouse sympathy, and Heathcliff knows how to use his son's weakness to bring Cathy where he wants her.

Nelly Dean attempts to warn Cathy and to regulate the correspondence. Letters pass between the two young people, and secrecy grows. The situation is not a simple forbidden romance. Cathy is alternately charmed, worried, and frustrated by Linton, who has little of the courage or generosity she hopes to find. He is ill, fearful of his father, and often petulant or manipulative in his own right. He is also being used by a man whose control over him is severe. The novel makes it possible to recognize a child's vulnerability without pretending all of his actions are harmless.

As Edgar grows weaker, Cathy becomes desperate to balance her duty to him with the demands made by Linton's supposed illness. Heathcliff encourages meetings while concealing his own designs. Nelly's interventions are important and sometimes mistaken. She is a caretaker trying to protect her charge, but her warnings, concealments, and judgments also shape what Cathy is allowed to know. The novel's narrators are never wholly separate from the events they recount.

At last Heathcliff gets Cathy and Nelly inside Wuthering Heights and prevents them from leaving. He intends to force a marriage between Cathy and Linton before Edgar dies. Nelly is confined, and Cathy is denied the freedom to return to her dying father until Heathcliff's scheme has been carried through. This is coercion, not the triumph of a romantic attachment. The children are being made into legal instruments in a dispute that began before either of them was born.

Cathy marries Linton under the pressure of confinement. After severe distress she is able to return to Edgar, but he soon dies. His death leaves her exposed to Heathcliff's control. Linton himself is gravely ill and dies not long after the marriage. Through the marriage and the property's legal arrangements, Heathcliff succeeds in taking possession of the Grange as well as the Heights. The exact machinery involves inheritance rules and the wills and claims of the people involved, not a magical transfer of property by personal hatred. Brontë makes law and family structure carry the revenge.

Cathy is brought to Wuthering Heights to live under Heathcliff's authority. She has lost her father, her home, and even the frail husband whose illness was used to lure her. She is angry and grief-stricken. Hareton offers gestures of concern but is met with contempt for his ignorance. Cathy has been raised with books and schooling, while Hareton has been kept from them deliberately. The difference is not evidence of natural superiority. It is the result of choices made by adults who controlled their education.

At this point Lockwood's first visit finally becomes comprehensible. The sullen young woman is not Heathcliff's wife but his widowed daughter-in-law. The rough young man is Hareton Earnshaw, heir to the family Heathcliff has dispossessed. Joseph's scolding and the household's coldness are the residue of decades of injury. Lockwood encountered the consequences long before he knew the causes. His habit of assigning obvious roles to the people he met was never enough to explain them.

Heathcliff has achieved much of what he wanted in the practical world. He governs both houses and has made descendants of his enemies dependent upon him. But the victory has brought no restoration of Catherine. It has also created a question the novel has not yet answered. Can the younger people learn to see one another differently, or will they continue acting out injuries they did not commit? The answer will be found less in a grand confrontation than in small acts of attention, literacy, and companionship.

## Wuthering Heights — Story: Cathy, Hareton, and the Last Visit

When Lockwood first visited Wuthering Heights, young Cathy and Hareton appeared to despise one another. By the time he returns after an absence, their relationship has changed. The change begins not in a revelation about ancestral crimes, but in ordinary conduct. Cathy starts to recognize that Hareton's rough manners and limited reading are consequences of a deliberately restricted upbringing. Hareton has tried to learn because he wants her respect. His attempts have sometimes drawn ridicule. She begins to offer him the dignity and practical help he needs instead of treating ignorance as a fixed mark of inferiority.

They read together. Cathy teaches, Hareton persists, and the shared activity gives each a way to be seen outside the roles that Heathcliff's revenge assigned to them. Their intimacy is not identical to the wild childhood companionship of the elder Catherine and Heathcliff. It is more deliberate and reciprocal. It includes the patient work of changing a habit and apologizing for injury. The novel has already shown how schooling and its denial can be used to establish rank. Here learning becomes a form of friendship rather than an instrument of contempt.

Heathcliff notices their closeness. Hareton resembles the young man Heathcliff once was in his humiliation, while Cathy recalls the woman Heathcliff has never ceased to mourn. The resemblance disturbs him, but it no longer gives revenge the energy it once did. Heathcliff could continue tormenting them. He still holds power over the households. Yet he begins to lose interest in using it. Their living faces draw his attention toward the past more strongly than toward the next act of punishment. His success has proved empty, and his obsession with Catherine has overtaken his will to control the present.

Heathcliff withdraws from eating, sleeping, and ordinary company. He wanders outside and behaves as though something beyond the visible world is compelling him. Nelly sees a man both exhausted and strangely elated. He speaks of Catherine as though the barrier between them were about to dissolve. We have no need to decide whether her ghost appears in any literal sense to understand his condition. The haunting may be supernatural, psychological, or a form of storytelling that leaves the two open to one another. The relevant fact is that he can no longer live fully among the people he spent years dominating.

Heathcliff is found dead in his room near an open window. Nelly arranges his burial near Catherine, whose grave lies close to Edgar Linton's. The story has ended without the conventional vindication of a passionate lover. He has died after using property, intimidation, and marriage to damage many lives, and no final speech absolves him. Nor is the depth of his grief revealed to be false. Brontë allows contradictory judgments to persist: immense attachment, immense cruelty, a longing that did not make him safe for others.

Cathy and Hareton plan to marry and move into Thrushcross Grange. They intend to leave the Heights behind as a home, though its history will remain part of theirs. Their future is not a repetition of the earlier lovers' tragedy. Both have reason to mistrust others; both have taken steps toward reconciliation. The possibility of building a different household comes from their ability to learn and change, not from a prophecy that the old wound must finally heal by itself.

Lockwood visits the graves of Catherine, Edgar, and Heathcliff, surrounded by the quiet countryside. Stories circulate about figures walking on the moors. Lockwood's closing reflections find it difficult to imagine that the dead can be disturbed in such calm earth. His peace is a visitor's interpretation, just as his fear at the bedroom window was a visitor's interpretation. The novel has never given us a narrator authorized to pronounce the ultimate state of these souls.

Wuthering Heights begins with an outsider who mistakes the people in a house and ends with that outsider contemplating three graves. Between those moments, Nelly has told a history of love, injury, inheritance, and repeated names. The final living possibility belongs to the younger generation. Cathy and Hareton do not have to undo what happened to their parents in order to refuse its repetition. They need to be allowed to live as themselves.

## Wuthering Heights — Lockwood, Nelly, and Narrative Evidence

Imagine being asked to decide what happened in a household twenty years ago. You cannot enter that household in the past. You have an outsider's notes, a servant's long recollection, some letters, and fragments of speech that have survived because somebody chose to repeat them. That is the position of a reader of Wuthering Heights. Emily Brontë does not simply tell us that a passionate man and woman suffered. She constructs a system through which their story reaches us, and makes the system itself a subject of interpretation.

Lockwood is the outer narrator. His visit to Wuthering Heights gives the novel its present setting, beginning in 1801. He sees a grim landlord and a quarrelsome household, assumes relationships that are not true, and repeatedly mistakes his own discomfort for profound insight. His first judgments are comic as well as consequential. We soon learn more about the characters than he knew when he wrote his notes. A narrator can describe an experience accurately while misunderstanding its meaning. Lockwood teaches the reader that narration and knowledge are not synonyms.

Nelly Dean supplies much of the interior history. She has lived with the Earnshaws and the Lintons, cared for children, witnessed quarrels, carried messages, and offered advice. Her position gives her access no distant observer could possess. But access is not neutrality. Nelly has loyalties, a sense of propriety, an understanding of what children ought to do, and judgments about who deserves sympathy. She sometimes withholds information or makes an intervention that changes an outcome. She is a participant giving an account of events in which she played a role.

Consider Catherine's confession that marriage to Heathcliff would degrade her, while her bond with him feels deeper than marriage. We hear it because Nelly reports a conversation made in her presence. Heathcliff overhears only part of it. Nelly knows how much he missed, and what she does or does not tell him matters. The tragedy grows from different degrees of access to the same speech. A reader should not treat the reported quotation as an independent recording made by an objective narrator. Its literary force comes from its place in a chain of remembering, hearing, and failing to hear.

Letters create smaller documents within the account. Isabella's letter describes the aftermath of her marriage with Heathcliff, providing a view from someone subjected to his cruelty. It is still a person's statement, full of anger and fear, but it disrupts the possibility of hearing only Heathcliff's grievances or Nelly's judgment. Young Cathy's correspondence with Linton similarly complicates what a caretaker thinks she knows. The novel includes testimony not to make a legal dossier, but to show the painful difference between what is happening and what people are allowed to understand about it.

Time is another source of difficulty. Lockwood begins after many of the central characters have died. Nelly moves back across decades. We are introduced to young Cathy before her mother has become a fully living presence in the narrated past. Names repeat, houses change owners, and a man who appears to be simply a brutal landlord turns out to have once been a vulnerable child. The order in which we learn facts makes character judgment provisional. A later explanation can alter an earlier scene without making the earlier injury vanish.

Does this mean the novel gives us no facts? No. Catherine dies; children are coerced; property changes hands; Heathcliff's treatment of Isabella is described in several ways that converge on cruelty. Skepticism should not become a lazy claim that everything is unknowable. Rather, ask what can be established through converging accounts, what depends on a narrator's interpretation, and whose voice is absent. The servants' testimony is not disposable gossip, but neither is it an all-seeing account.

For practice, take Lockwood's opening encounter, Nelly's account of Catherine's childhood confession, and Isabella's letter. For each scene, draw a simple chain: person who experienced it, person who recounts it, person who writes it down, and reader. Where could knowledge be lost? Where could a motive be supplied by the teller rather than the actor? The novel's emotional power remains, but it becomes more precise. Wuthering Heights is not only a story of haunting. It is a story about the impossibility of entering another person's past without passing through the words of the living.

## Wuthering Heights — Property, Inheritance, and Revenge

Heathcliff is often presented as a lover who cannot bear separation from Catherine. That is true as far as it goes. But the plot of Wuthering Heights would not work if desire had no connection to property. Hindley Earnshaw inherits the Heights; Edgar Linton has the Grange; Heathcliff begins as a foundling without a secure family claim; the children are expected to receive what their parents own. Revenge becomes effective when Heathcliff finds ways to manipulate those arrangements. One way to understand the novel is to follow each person's capacity to stay in a home, inherit it, or be expelled from it.

The first injury takes place under a father's roof. Mr. Earnshaw brings Heathcliff home, protects him, and gives him affection. But protection is not the same as secure standing. After the father dies, Hindley takes control and reduces Heathcliff to the status of a laborer. He can do this because he possesses household authority, and Heathcliff lacks a clear independent claim. The humiliation affects Catherine's calculation when she decides that marriage to him would lower her position. Class is not a decorative obstacle introduced from outside the love story. It shapes what the characters imagine to be possible.

Heathcliff later returns with money from an unknown source. He uses it not to escape the old hierarchy but to invert it. Hindley's gambling and debts give Heathcliff leverage over Wuthering Heights. After Hindley's death, Heathcliff controls the estate and Hareton, who should have inherited his father's position. Heathcliff denies the boy education and lets him live in degraded circumstances. The revenge is deliberately historical: he makes Hindley's son experience the dependence and contempt Hindley once imposed on him. The punishment falls upon someone who had no responsibility for the original acts.

The Grange requires a different scheme. Edgar has a daughter, young Cathy; Heathcliff has a son, Linton, by Isabella. If Cathy and Linton marry, legal and family relationships may give Heathcliff a route to the Linton property. Heathcliff exerts pressure on his weak and frightened son, lures Cathy to the Heights, and confines her until a marriage takes place. The mechanism is legal in its eventual property consequences but coercive in how the agreement is obtained. An apparently regular document or marriage ceremony cannot tell the whole truth about how a young person came to sign or consent.

The novel's property transitions involve particular nineteenth-century inheritance arrangements, debts, and attempted legal protections. Edgar tries to protect Cathy's interests; Heathcliff's strategy exploits the obstacles and delays around him. A reader should not reduce the result to a universal rule that husbands automatically owned all property in all places and times. The specific plot needs careful attention to its wills, creditors, guardians, and marriages. Yet its broad point is clear: people who control legal and economic arrangements can use them to turn other people into means.

The houses themselves are therefore actors in the plot. Wuthering Heights carries memories of the Earnshaws' rough domestic power, while Thrushcross Grange is associated with the Lintons' comfort and gentility. Their opposition is unstable. Catherine crosses it as a child and changes her manners; Heathcliff acquires both houses and makes them serve the same coercive design. The appearance of gentility does not guarantee ethical conduct, and the roughness of a home does not make every act there inherently honest.

At the end, young Cathy and Hareton begin to build a life together. Their reconciliation does not magically cancel the complex history of ownership. It does, however, create a future in which a shared household need not be organized by the humiliation of its inhabitants. Their proposed move to the Grange offers a practical contrast with Heathcliff's drive to possess both estates. He wanted houses in order to dominate people; they want a home in which two people may learn to live together.

Reading property carefully prevents us from sentimentalizing the revenge. The anguish of separation is not the same thing as a right to take someone else's freedom, literacy, marriage, or inheritance. Emily Brontë makes a passionate obsession historically powerful by giving it access to money and law. That is why its consequences endure for so long after the first lovers can no longer speak to each other.

## Wuthering Heights — Love, Violence, and Moral Judgment

The words Catherine and Heathcliff use about their bond make it sound stronger than ordinary affection. Catherine believes that whatever makes up their deepest selves is somehow shared. Heathcliff experiences her loss as the destruction of his own life. The novel gives those feelings extraordinary imaginative force. It does not follow that every action performed in their name deserves admiration. Wuthering Heights is difficult partly because it asks us to recognize intensity without confusing intensity with goodness.

When Catherine says that marrying Heathcliff would degrade her, she is speaking within a class system that has stripped him of education and status. Yet she also wants to marry Edgar and retain Heathcliff as an indispensable companion. That desire may be psychologically intelligible without being fair to either man. Edgar has a reasonable expectation that marriage creates obligations of trust and household life. Heathcliff has reason to feel rejected, but that reason does not entitle him to govern Catherine's marriage. The conflict is made of more than one person's pure love opposed by other people's wickedness.

Heathcliff's childhood mistreatment is severe. Hindley uses authority to humiliate him, and Catherine's move toward polite society sharpens the injury. Brontë lets us understand the formation of a bitter person. Yet understanding the formation of harm is different from excusing its repetition. Heathcliff later exploits Hindley's debts, degrades Hareton, uses Isabella's affection as a weapon, intimidates Linton, and forces young Cathy into marriage. The people who endure these acts are not responsible for the way Heathcliff was treated as a child. His revenge extends the old violence into new lives.

Isabella offers the clearest test of romantic glamour. She imagines that beneath Heathcliff's hard exterior there may be a love she can reach. Her experience of marriage dismantles the fantasy. He does not merely refuse tenderness; he acts cruelly and uses the relationship to injure Edgar. Reading Isabella's letter closely forces the listener to take the perspective of someone whose vulnerability might be obscured if we treated Heathcliff only as a monumental figure of passion. A novel can represent a magnetic character without asking us to admire his conduct.

Catherine's illness and death likewise resist a simple accounting. Her marriage has constrained her, her expectations are contradictory, and she confronts Heathcliff with the force of a person who feels she has lost a part of herself. Their last encounter is both moving and destructive. The language of accusation suggests two people who cannot separate love from the wish to make the other feel their own pain. Heathcliff's grief at her death is sincere. Its sincerity cannot undo its consequences for the living.

The novel's second generation is therefore vital to its moral design. Young Cathy has suffered coercion and bereavement; Hareton has been denied education and dignity. When they begin to care for one another, affection does not require total possession or a shared identity that erases difference. It requires correction, learning, forgiveness, and willingness to see the person beside you. Their reconciliation does not prove that everything has been healed. It shows a kind of love that need not feed on injury.

One interpretive temptation is to make the older lovers the authentic pair and the younger lovers merely a mild domestic afterthought. But Emily Brontë gives the book enough length after Catherine's death to force us to reckon with the consequences of authenticity claimed without limits. The younger relationship has its own moral drama: whether a person can interrupt the habits of contempt passed down by a family. The quiet action of helping someone read can matter as much as a cry across the moors.

There is no need to turn the book into a fable where desire is bad and ordinary marriage is good. Edgar can be possessive and limited; Cathy can be proud; Hareton can answer shame with anger. The characters remain imperfect. What the novel makes available is a distinction between feeling deeply and acting responsibly. A love that absorbs a person's whole identity may be powerful material for art. Whether it gives that person a claim over another life is a different question, and one the novel refuses to let us forget.

## Wuthering Heights — Generations, Names, and Education

It is possible to lose track of people in Wuthering Heights even while listening carefully. There is an elder Catherine and a younger Cathy. There are two different Lintons who matter by name: Edgar Linton, Catherine's husband, and the sickly Linton Heathcliff, the son of Isabella and Heathcliff. Hareton Earnshaw bears his father's family name but does not inherit his father's position. The same houses exchange occupants and owners. The confusion is not evidence of careless construction. It is part of a novel concerned with how family identities repeat and change under pressure.

The scratched names Lockwood finds early in the story suggest the problem before we hear its history. Catherine Earnshaw, Catherine Heathcliff, Catherine Linton: the names join childhood, desire, and marriage in combinations that cannot all be realized in one life. Later another Catherine passes from the Linton family into a coerced marriage with Linton Heathcliff. What looks at first like a simple list of relations becomes a record of changes imposed by kinship and property. A surname can record a legal bond while saying little about whether the bond was desired or humane.

One of the novel's strongest repetitions concerns education. Hindley strips the foundling Heathcliff of the instruction and gentility once available to him. Years later Heathcliff takes control of Hareton and raises him with deliberately restricted schooling. The object of revenge is no longer Hindley himself; Hindley is dead. By damaging his son, Heathcliff converts the past into the conditions of another person's present. Hareton can appear uncultivated to a visitor who does not know the history. Nelly's account changes the explanation: the young man's manners have been produced by deprivation rather than by a permanent natural difference.

Young Cathy has grown up in a gentler and better educated household. Her early contempt for Hareton exposes the limits of her education. She knows how to read, but she does not yet know how to see the dignity of someone who has been denied the same opportunity. The reversal begins when she recognizes his effort and helps him learn. Their shared reading is a repair not only of a personal quarrel but of a historical injury. It gives Hareton a tool the revenge scheme tried to keep from him, while requiring Cathy to abandon a habit of superiority.

The contrast with the first generation should not be oversimplified. Catherine and Heathcliff's childhood freedom on the moor was not free of class difference. Catherine could eventually adopt the manners of the Lintons; Heathcliff was made into a servant. In the second generation, Cathy and Hareton face a difference also manufactured by adults, but they respond by changing their relation instead of accepting inherited contempt as destiny. The resemblance of people and situations gives the reader grounds for comparison. It does not make them identical characters living through the same fate.

The repetition also reaches the servants. Joseph continues to invoke religious condemnation in the household's day-to-day life. Nelly moves between houses and generations, carrying memory, judgment, and habits of caretaking. They are part of the social continuity that makes the two houses more than a collection of owners. When a family changes, not everything changes at once. Language, routine, and relations of authority survive in the people who remain.

Why does the novel give the younger pair an ending that seems to break the pattern? We cannot say that inherited violence automatically burns itself out. Heathcliff's power diminishes, but the younger characters have to do something with the opening. They must revise judgments, risk kindness, and learn new ways to inhabit a shared room. The final promise of marriage is significant because the novel has spent so long showing marriage used to secure advantage or impose control. It becomes possible to imagine a bond whose future is not already written by the people who came before.

To map the novel, make two family columns and then add the people who move between them. Track not just marriages and births but who has access to schooling, who owns a house, and who can choose where to live. The structure makes clear that repetition is a fact of history but not necessarily a sentence. Cathy and Hareton cannot choose their ancestors. They can choose not to reproduce every decision those ancestors made.

## Wuthering Heights — Moors, Ghosts, and Language

The title Wuthering Heights names a house exposed to rough weather. Lockwood hears the local term explained through the force of the wind around it. The setting is memorable enough that readers may imagine the moors as a free, wild world opposed to the confining rooms of social life. Emily Brontë uses that opposition and repeatedly complicates it. The weather can exhilarate, but it can also trap a visitor in a dangerous house. The home can provide shelter, but it can become a place where people are imprisoned and intimidated. Landscape and domestic architecture act upon one another.

Look first at the contrast between the Heights and Thrushcross Grange. The Heights is rough, noisy, and associated with the Earnshaws' fierce habits. The Grange is comfortable, orderly, and associated with the Lintons' refinement. Catherine's childhood visit to the Grange changes her clothing and manners. Yet the houses are not allegories in which one represents authenticity and the other falsehood. Hindley's cruelty grows at the Heights; Edgar's narrowness and the pressures of status develop at the Grange. Later Heathcliff controls both houses, showing that property and authority can make their contrasts less stable than they initially appear.

The moors are the country of Catherine and Heathcliff's early intimacy. They can run there beyond the immediate surveillance of a parent or teacher. That freedom matters because the pair's later lives are regulated by rank and inheritance. But the idea of freedom is inseparable from exposure. Lockwood's winter journey nearly traps him. Jane Eyre, in another Brontë novel, nearly starves while wandering a moorland district; here the country does not become a sentimental place where nature automatically solves society's harms. People require shelter, care, and arrangements through which they can live with one another.

The novel's Gothic devices similarly operate on more than one level. Lockwood dreams of a small hand at the window, pleading for admission. Heathcliff cries out to the absent Catherine. Later he seeks signs of her nearness and behaves as though death may restore their connection. Other characters report ghosts or rumors of figures on the moors. Do we have to decide whether such figures are real? The text permits multiple readings. The cry at a window can be a supernatural visitation, an image of grief, or a story shaped by the terrified consciousness of a bad sleeper. Its force does not depend on a verdict from a modern paranormal investigation.

At the same time, the novel contains remarkable physical detail: furniture, doors, paths, illness, food, household labor, letters, and the struggle over an estate. These details do not cancel the Gothic atmosphere. They make it harder to dismiss horror as something safely separate from ordinary life. The most consequential acts of violence are not performed by ghosts. They are performed by living people who lock doors, withhold education, arrange marriages, or neglect children.

Language deepens the setting. Joseph speaks in regional Yorkshire dialect, while Lockwood's polished observations can sound absurdly confident. The contrast has been altered by some later editors and translators, who have to decide how much dialect to preserve or explain. Readers who struggle with Joseph should remember that unfamiliar speech is not a direct measure of intellectual worth. The novel makes speech part of class perception, just as it makes clothing and schooling parts of status. Nelly's language has a different role: she moves among classes and retells the story, carrying local knowledge through a voice that is both vivid and judgmental.

A useful reading exercise is to compare three thresholds: the window at which Lockwood seems to hear Catherine, the gates and doors that confine young Cathy, and the room where Cathy later helps Hareton read. At one threshold a voice cannot enter; at another a person cannot leave; in the third a relationship changes because one person makes room for another's mind. The Gothic house has become a map of human exclusion and possible hospitality.

The final quiet graves should not be taken as a simple proof that the landscape has reconciled everyone. Lockwood sees the peace of the grass and asks how anyone could imagine the dead restless beneath it. We already know how frequently his interpretations have gone astray. Emily Brontë closes with a scene in which the earth is calm and the past remains powerful enough to generate stories. She does not need to solve the haunting in order to end the novel.

## Wuthering Heights — Publication, Editions, and Close Reading

Wuthering Heights appeared in December 1847 under the name Ellis Bell. Emily Brontë's novel occupied two volumes of a three-volume publication issued with Anne Brontë's Agnes Grey. Charlotte Brontë's Jane Eyre had appeared earlier that year under the name Currer Bell. The resemblance of the pseudonyms led to confusion about authorship, while the younger sisters' publisher, Thomas Cautley Newby, benefited from the excitement around Jane Eyre. These facts belong to the history of a literary marketplace, not to the inner chronology of the fictional Earnshaws and Lintons.

The original reception was mixed and often hostile. Readers and reviewers confronted violence, cruel domestic relationships, difficult dialect, and characters who would not fit expectations of a morally reassuring novel. A hostile review can tell us what some readers found objectionable; it does not settle the value of the book. The Brontë Parsonage Museum preserves some of this early response. The novel's later reputation as an essential work of English fiction should not be projected backward as if it were universally acclaimed at publication.

Emily died in 1848. In 1850 Charlotte prepared a new edition of Wuthering Heights, along with biographical and editorial material. The later edition contains many changes from the 1847 text, not all of which can be assumed to express Emily's own final wishes. The first edition had printing problems, and editorial decisions about correcting errors, retaining dialect, and distinguishing authorial revisions from a sister's interventions remain important. Modern scholarly editions state which witness they follow and what they emend. A freely accessible Project Gutenberg transcription is valuable for continuous reading, but without a documented collation one should not treat its every comma or spelling as the unmediated 1847 text.

For a first close reading, return to Lockwood's opening description of Heathcliff. He thinks he recognizes another person who loves solitude. Mark the sentences in which he reports visible behavior and those in which he supplies an explanation of the behavior. How quickly does the book show that his confidence is misplaced? This is a lesson in narration before the central love story has even begun.

Second, read the sequence around Catherine's conversation with Nelly about Edgar and Heathcliff. Identify the part Heathcliff overhears, the part he does not, and what Nelly knows about both. Then compare Catherine's desire for the status Edgar offers with her declaration of shared identity with Heathcliff. A short quotation such as 'I am Heathcliff' can mislead when separated from the competing calculation in the same conversation. Listen for conflict, not for a single slogan that cancels it.

Third, examine Isabella's letter after her marriage. What changes when a woman harmed by Heathcliff describes the household from inside it? Does the letter simply repeat Nelly's judgment, or does it offer perceptions that would be missing from a story centered entirely on the older lovers' suffering? The narrative's method matters because a magnificent speech of grief from Heathcliff can otherwise dominate a reader's memory of the people he hurts.

Fourth, study the scene in which young Cathy changes her treatment of Hareton. The work of teaching him to read is quiet beside the elder lovers' quarrels and desperate cries. Yet it reverses a deliberate deprivation through which Heathcliff had prolonged his revenge. Compare how the two pairs imagine being together. Does one require sameness and possession, while the other becomes possible through patience with difference? Do not turn either pair into an abstraction; test your answer against what they do.

Fifth, read the final pages beside the bedroom-window scene. Lockwood has heard a possible ghost and later contemplates three apparently peaceful graves. He is the same imperfect interpreter in both scenes. Ask what we know, what we imagine, and what the novel declines to certify. The last image does not erase the violence that preceded it, but it leaves the future of Cathy and Hareton open beyond the dead who shaped their world.

Wuthering Heights continues to be read because its form resists the shortest version of its plot. It is a novel of intense attachment, but also of property and kinship; of Gothic haunting, but also of letters and disputed testimony; of generations repeating injuries, but also of a fragile decision to stop. Reading it well means neither idealizing Heathcliff nor stripping the characters of the emotional complexity that gives their choices force. It means hearing the whole household, not only the loudest cry from the moor.
