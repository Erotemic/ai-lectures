# Journey to the West: Three faces of the White Bone Demon

## The hunger on the mountain

The travelers left Five Villages Abbey at last. Behind them stood the restored ginseng fruit tree, an immortal's anger turned into friendship, and an argument about fruit that Bajie would probably never hear the end of. Ahead of them rose a different range of mountains. There were no welcoming roofs under the trees, no attendants waiting with a tray, and no promise that their next meal would arrive at all.

Xuanzang rode slowly. Sha Wujing led or steadied the white horse where the path was difficult. Bajie carried the luggage and watched the shadows, sometimes with a soldier's caution and sometimes with the expression of a man wondering how much farther one could walk on an empty stomach. Wukong ranged ahead, his golden eyes searching the slopes.

The monk became hungry.

He called to his eldest disciple. They had been walking a long time. Did Wukong know of a house where they could ask for a little vegetarian food?

Wukong looked around. Mountain ridges layered one behind another. A grove of trees might hide a village, but he saw no smoke from cooking fires, no path worn by farmers, no fields close enough to explain a dwelling. He told his master to wait while he searched.

Xuanzang was dismayed. The monkey could travel so quickly that a distant town meant nothing to him; the monk could not climb hills and cross ravines for the sake of a bowl of rice. Wukong said he was not asking him to. Let the horse rest. Let Sha keep watch. Let Bajie stop complaining for a few moments. He would fetch whatever could be found.

He chose a high place, climbed the rocks, and looked toward the south. There, in a patch of green among the ridges, he found trees bearing ripe peaches. They were not a village meal, but they would keep his master from going hungry. Wukong leaped away to gather them.

There was something else in those mountains. It had been watching the travelers while Wukong searched for fruit.

The creature was a demon whose true form was a heap of white bones. It had long cultivated its power and learned to clothe itself in appearances. From its hiding place it looked at the Buddhist pilgrim on the horse and recognized a prize far greater than peaches or rice. This was the monk from the eastern lands, the one whose flesh was said to grant longevity to whoever ate it.

The demon coveted that flesh. Yet it did not rush down upon the travelers. It saw the supernatural force of the disciples guarding him. The monkey in particular was dangerous. His eyes penetrated disguises that deceived ordinary people. His staff had already brought many monsters to an abrupt end.

Now the monkey had gone. The demon did not need to defeat four travelers in battle. It only needed the monk to believe that an innocent person had come along the road.

## A woman with a meal

A young woman appeared on the mountain path carrying food vessels. She was neatly dressed, with the manner of someone accustomed to walking between a household and its fields. She held a container of rice and a second vessel of vegetarian food. Her face was pleasant, her movements modest, and her presence seemed to answer Xuanzang's hunger with remarkable speed.

Bajie noticed her first. His interest sharpened immediately. It was not merely the food. A pretty woman in the mountains was worth inspecting as far as he was concerned, and a pretty woman carrying dinner was better still.

He hurried to meet her and asked where she had come from.

The woman told a straightforward story. Her family worked fields on the hillside. She had been carrying a meal to those who were laboring there. Seeing a monk on pilgrimage, she would be happy to make an offering. Buddhism taught generosity. Why should a traveler bound for holy scriptures be left to go hungry when food was at hand?

Bajie had no difficulty understanding the last part of this reasoning. He invited her to come forward.

Xuanzang was less ready to accept. The path was remote. She was alone. He asked about her family, and when she answered he was still uneasy. To take a meal intended for field workers might leave them with nothing. To accept offerings without understanding their circumstances might be another kind of harm. The monk did not want his hunger to become an excuse for carelessness.

The woman answered every concern with greater courtesy. The meal was freely offered. The merit of giving was hers to choose. The monk's disciples could eat, too. She had enough.

Bajie listened to all this with mounting impatience. Here stood a woman willing to feed them, and his master was examining the gift as if it were a court document. He wished Xuanzang would simply take the bowl.

At that moment Sun Wukong returned with peaches.

He saw a woman leaning toward his master, the smell of food in the air, and Bajie almost celebrating. But the sight that mattered was not visible to the others. Wukong's eyes looked through the woman's shape. The figure was not a charitable villager. It was a mountain demon trying to get close enough to seize Xuanzang.

The monkey cried out. He accused the visitor of masquerading as a human being. He was already lifting his iron staff.

Xuanzang ordered him to stop. The woman had offered food. Even if there were cause to mistrust her, a disciple of the Buddha could not strike her to death on suspicion. Bajie cried out too, but for a different reason. The monkey was about to destroy their dinner.

Wukong knew there was very little time. A spell in a demon's hands could be completed while a kind monk was still deciding which questions to ask. He struck.

The woman's body fell on the ground.

Xuanzang stared in horror. The food vessels lay beside her. Bajie turned on Wukong. Had the monkey gone mad? A young woman had brought them rice, and he had killed her without allowing her to speak for herself.

Wukong insisted that there had been no young woman. The body was an abandoned appearance, a thing the demon had made to occupy their eyes while it slipped away. He turned their attention to the food. Its seeming rice and vegetarian dishes had changed into crawling, unclean things: wriggling creatures where nourishment had seemed to be. The gift had not been a gift.

Bajie would not concede the point. The monkey could turn himself into insects, animals, and men. If he could do that, could he not also change food into crawling things to justify an act he had already committed? Bajie had seen a person carrying dinner; now he saw a corpse. He found the corpse more persuasive.

The monk found it worse than persuasive. He was frightened by the possibility that his own disciple had murdered someone while supposedly protecting him. He remembered the six robbers killed earlier in the journey and the violence that had forced him to reckon with Wukong's nature. A Buddhist pilgrim ought not to bring death to innocent people merely because he could command a creature strong enough to inflict it.

Xuanzang recited the spell that tightened the golden fillet on the monkey's head.

Pain seized Wukong. His staff dropped toward the earth. He clutched his temples and sank down. The tightening band could not be pushed away. A monkey capable of wrecking palaces in Heaven could be brought to the ground by a few words spoken by one human monk.

He begged his master to stop. He had not killed a person. If his master would look, he would understand. But the monk saw the ruined body and heard Bajie's accusations, and he continued the recitation.

When the pain subsided, Xuanzang told Wukong that he could no longer tolerate such killing. The monkey should leave.

Wukong answered not with his old boast about fighting the armies of Heaven, but with the debt he owed. For five hundred years he had lain trapped beneath the Mountain of Five Elements. Xuanzang had released him and offered him a path toward redemption. Wukong had undertaken to escort him to the scriptures. How could he abandon the pilgrimage because of a demon's trick? Where else could he repay the kindness of liberation?

The monk relented, unwilling to break that bond entirely. He warned his disciple that there must be no further violence against people. Wukong agreed that he would obey, and they went on.

The demon, meanwhile, had not died. It had escaped through a method by which a false body was left behind while the spirit fled. The dead woman on the path had never been a woman at all. The creature watched them continue and considered what it had learned.

The monkey could see through its face, but the monk did not trust the monkey's seeing. That weakness was worth more than any weapon.

## The mother looking for her daughter

The pilgrims had not traveled far before they met an old woman walking alone on the mountain path. Her back was bent. Her face bore the marks of age. She moved as though the climb cost her strength, and she called anxiously into the trees.

She was looking for her daughter.

Had the travelers seen a young woman carrying a meal? The girl had left home with food for the workers and had not returned. Her mother feared that something dreadful had happened.

Xuanzang's heart sank. The new arrival seemed to explain the first. A young woman had come with food; a worried mother had followed her. The monk remembered the body on the ground and found himself standing before the human consequence of what he believed Wukong had done.

Bajie was quick to connect the stories. There, he said in effect, was the proof the monkey could not explain away. The young woman had a family. Someone would search for her. They could not kill a stranger on the road and expect that no one would ask where she had gone.

Wukong saw what his master could not. This old woman was the same demon in another shape. It had chosen not merely to resemble a harmless person but to become the supposed relative of the first victim. Its new lie made the earlier death more terrible.

He warned Xuanzang. This was another disguise. The mountain was full of deception. If they allowed the creature to approach, it would seize their master.

Xuanzang would not accept the explanation. The monkey had said that about the young woman, too. Did every visitor in the mountains become a demon as soon as Wukong disliked her? How many old women would he strike before admitting a mistake?

The answer could not be settled by argument. The old woman approached. Wukong raised his staff again.

He struck the figure. It fell, and the demon again escaped by casting off a false body.

For the second time Xuanzang saw what appeared to be a murdered human being. The first could perhaps have been explained away as an accident or a misunderstanding. The second looked to him like a deliberate refusal to obey. He began the fillet spell with redoubled fury.

Wukong collapsed. The pain was not imaginary, and his protests were not a performance. His head was gripped as though it would split. He begged his master to stop, but Xuanzang's fear for the lives of ordinary people was stronger than the reassurance offered by the disciple who had struck them. The monk repeated the spell again and again until Wukong could scarcely stand.

When it was over, Xuanzang said he must send the monkey away.

Wukong tried a different argument. Could his master remove the golden fillet first? If he were no longer a disciple, why must he carry the painful emblem of discipleship? The monk could recite the tightening words but did not know how to take the band off. That secret belonged to Guanyin.

Wukong saw the unfairness and the opportunity at once. If his master sent him away wearing the fillet, anyone who knew the spell might still torment him. If he was expected to remain under that restraint, he should at least be allowed to fulfill the purpose for which it had been given: protecting the pilgrim. He could not be dismissed as a danger and kept as a bound servant in the same breath.

Xuanzang was shaken. The tie between them was neither simple friendship nor ordinary employment. It had been placed upon a criminal immortal for his reformation and upon a vulnerable monk for his protection. Their duties were intertwined, even when they did not understand one another.

The monk gave him a last warning and permitted him to remain. Wukong resumed his place, angry, hurt, and watchful. Bajie had not withdrawn his accusations. Sha kept the burdens moving and tried not to worsen the quarrel. The mountain path took them forward, though the party's trust was already coming apart.

The demon was delighted. It had now made its second escape. Its third form would take better advantage of the story it had begun.

## The father reading a scripture

An old man appeared in the distance. He carried a staff and moved slowly, murmuring the words of a scripture as he walked. His white hair, deliberate pace, and devotional manner would have made him seem a harmless elderly householder even without the book of verses on his lips.

He was searching, he explained, for a wife and daughter who had gone into the hills.

Now the invented family was complete. The daughter with the meal, the mother who followed, and the father who could not find either of them belonged to one false history. Xuanzang, who believed he had already witnessed two killings, was being offered the last person who might testify against his party.

Bajie understood the danger in terms he knew. This was no longer just a matter of conscience. If relatives were being killed on the road, local authorities could demand an answer. A pilgrim bearing letters from the Tang emperor might find himself accused of murder in a foreign jurisdiction. The monks could be bound, tried, and punished. What was the point of fetching scriptures if they were arrested before reaching the western land?

Xuanzang was horrified. He could not find words to justify the deeds he thought his disciple had committed. He feared the old man, not as a demon but as a father whose family had been destroyed by their journey.

Wukong saw the same demon again. This time he understood that killing a false body would not be enough. Twice it had left a lifelike corpse and escaped, and twice the corpses had turned his master against him. He needed to prevent the creature's spirit from slipping away.

He called upon the local mountain spirits and guardians. He ordered them to surround the creature and block its flight. The demon had used its shifting form to exploit the pilgrims; Wukong would use the authority of his own supernatural office to make its next escape impossible.

Then he advanced on the old man.

Xuanzang shouted for him to stop. Bajie cried that the monkey was going to finish murdering an entire family. The old man seemed to shrink from the staff.

Wukong struck.

The human appearance broke. What lay upon the ground was not a fallen old man. It was a pile of white bones, the true remains at the center of the demon's craft. On the backbone appeared its name: White Bone Lady. At last there was something the others could see besides the face the creature had chosen to show them.

Wukong pointed to the bones. There was the monster he had warned them of. Three appearances had been one enemy. The girl, the old woman, the old man: none was a murdered human being. If Wukong had waited for Xuanzang to believe him, the demon might already have taken the monk and eaten him.

For a moment Xuanzang wavered. The evidence was there. He looked at the bones, and his heart was disturbed by the possibility that the monkey had been protecting him all along.

Bajie spoke before that possibility could settle. The monkey knew spells, he said. He had changed his own body countless times. Why could he not change a dead human body into bones? Was this not exactly how he had changed the rice into loathsome creatures? A demon's name written on a backbone might be the last trick of a disciple desperate to excuse himself.

It was a powerful accusation because it drew upon something true. Wukong was capable of extraordinary transformations. But the inference was false. The fact that he could deceive the senses did not prove that he had done so. His abilities, which should have made him a more credible witness to supernatural danger, made him in Xuanzang's mind a less reliable one.

The monk's anger returned. He saw three bodies where Wukong saw three discarded disguises. He had warned his disciple twice. A third blow, delivered with the same staff, appeared to him proof of a settled disposition toward killing. He could not preach compassion and continue willingly under the protection of someone he believed to be a murderer.

The mountain was quiet. The three members of the false family were gone, but the danger they had left behind remained.

## A letter of dismissal

Xuanzang decided that his word alone was insufficient. He would write a formal dismissal. A statement in writing would release Wukong from the obligation to follow him and establish that the monk no longer accepted responsibility for his actions.

He took out paper and brush and composed the document. Its purpose was plain: Sun Wukong was no longer a disciple in the pilgrimage party. He was to return to his former dwelling and not claim the monk as master. The words were more severe than a fit of temper shouted on the roadside. They made the break deliberate.

Wukong asked him to reconsider. He had fought monsters, freed his master from caves, and crossed dangerous country for him. He had restrained his own pride more than anyone who knew the Great Sage in his former days would have believed possible. Did one disagreement—though repeated three times—erase all that service? He was not asking to be praised. He was asking his master to live long enough to reach the West.

Xuanzang did not withdraw the letter.

The monkey's face changed. His anger had kept him upright through the quarrel, but the dismissal touched the part of him that had accepted the pilgrimage as something more than a sentence. He had once been king of Flower-Fruit Mountain. He had once dared to confront Heaven itself. Those glories had left him imprisoned under a mountain. On the road with Xuanzang he had been learning a different kind of greatness: the hard, repetitive business of keeping another person alive.

Now that person had told him he was no longer wanted.

He approached to make a farewell bow.

Xuanzang refused to receive it. A master who had dismissed a disciple should not accept the gestures that confirmed the discipleship. Wukong insisted that whatever his master thought of him, he would not leave without showing respect for the man who had released him from the mountain.

The monk turned away.

Wukong plucked three hairs from his body and blew upon them. Three monkeys appeared beside him, copies of the Great Sage himself. They placed themselves on all sides of Xuanzang, and the four Wukongs bowed together. The monk could avert his face from one, but not from all four directions. The gesture was comic in its mechanics and painful in its meaning: the disciple used a conjuring trick not to win a fight, but to give a last respectful farewell that his master would not allow.

Then Wukong took the dismissal and turned to his brothers.

He spoke especially to Sha Wujing, the most reliable of the two. Keep close to the master. Lead the horse carefully. Guard the luggage, watch the hills, and do not let Xuanzang wander into danger because his heart is soft. The road was full of demons, and the monk still had far to go.

He warned Bajie as well. A careless word could cost a life. The pig must not flatter himself that a monster would announce its nature before asking to be invited to dinner. Wukong knew that Bajie did not welcome lectures from him. He gave the warning anyway.

The party could not agree on whether its best defender had killed three people. It could agree on one fact: he was leaving.

Wukong mounted a cloud and rose above the mountain. He did not return to the road. He turned east toward the country where the stone monkey had first been king, toward the waterfalls and trees of Flower-Fruit Mountain.

As he traveled, he passed over waters that reminded him of the long exile from his home. He looked back toward the western road and began to weep. The novel lets the Great Sage cry. For all his boasting, spells, victories, and transformations, he was still capable of grief at being sent away by the master he had sworn to protect.

Below him, Xuanzang had the white horse, a hungry Bajie, a steady Sha Wujing, the emperor's charge, and the hope of reaching the scriptures. He no longer had Sun Wukong.

The White Bone Demon had failed to eat the monk. It had nevertheless accomplished something serious. By appearing as the people Xuanzang most wished not to harm, it had turned the master's compassion against his ability to recognize danger. By leaving corpses that seemed to demand justice, it had made Wukong's protective violence look like murder. By giving Bajie evidence that could be misread, it had made one disciple's resentment into a weapon against another.

That was how the pilgrims lost their strongest protector: not because he was defeated in battle, but because the companions could no longer agree about what they had seen.

## Non-spoken source and reading companion

**Scope:** Selected transmitted Chinese chapter 27; the three deceptions of the White Bone Demon and Wukong's formal dismissal. The subsequent events are not anticipated here. The dialogue and transitions are independently composed English retelling prose, not quotations from a copyrighted English translation.

**Primary witness:** [*Journey to the West*, chapter 27](https://zh.wikisource.org/wiki/%E8%A5%BF%E9%81%8A%E8%A8%98/%E7%AC%AC027%E5%9B%9E) (`cl-jtw-ch27`). Three appearances in order: girl with rice and vegetarian food, old mother, old father reciting scripture. The demon's first two false deaths leave human-looking bodies. For the third encounter, Wukong summons mountain guardians to prevent escape; the remaining white bones carry the name White Bone Lady. Bajie argues that Wukong may have created the physical evidence by transformation. The monk gives Wukong a written dismissal; the monkey bows with three hair-created duplicates and departs toward Flower-Fruit Mountain. The emotional and ethical conflict is a reading of the narrative, not a claim that demons or longevity food exist.
