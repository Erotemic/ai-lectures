# Journey to the West: The tower without light

## Men in wooden collars

The fire was out at last. Wukong had returned the true plantain fan to Princess Iron Fan after using it to cool the mountain and remove the source of its flames. Xuanzang and his three disciples crossed the eight hundred li of the Flaming Mountains with water underfoot where burning stones had been. Autumn deepened into winter. Leaves thinned along the roads, the fields were being harvested, and the mornings turned cold. The next great city rose ahead of them beneath pale skies.

Wukong saw towers, city walls, and broad approaches to a royal residence. Bajie questioned his certainty that this was a king's city; surely towns also had gates and impressive roofs. Sha asked what country it might be. Wukong could not know without entering, but he saw the scale of the place and guessed they had reached a capital. The pilgrims crossed a bridge and joined the markets and traffic, expecting the ordinary business of presenting their travel pass and receiving another stamp.

Then Xuanzang saw the monks.

They walked from door to door wearing heavy wooden collars and shackles, begging for food. Their robes were worn and their faces drawn. The sight hurt Xuanzang before he knew what crime they were accused of. He told Wukong to ask who they were and why their lives had been reduced to this.

The men knelt and said they belonged to the Golden Light Monastery, built under royal protection. They had been punished for something they had not done. They begged the visitors to come to the monastery so they could explain without the street listening.

The entrance bore a fine inscription announcing the King's protection, but inside the buildings had lost their splendor. Weeds grew around the halls. The prayer drums were silent; unused lamps sat dusty on their shelves; cobwebs covered the painted figures. In a rear court several younger monks were chained to pillars. Xuanzang looked at them, then at the elders with their wooden collars, and wept.

The monks had a reason for believing help would come. On the previous night, they said, they had shared a dream. A holy monk would arrive from the Tang empire, recognizable by the strange appearance of his disciples. He would establish their innocence. They had been praying and crying for aid for years, and at last a company matching the dream stood in the empty hall.

Xuanzang asked for the story from the beginning.

This was the kingdom of Jisai, the monks said, once a powerful country among its neighbors. Foreign rulers sent tribute to it, not because the king had continually conquered them, but because a marvelous tower at the monastery shone over the land. Its summit held a sacred relic, and its radiance could be seen from far away. The king had treated the tower's light as a sign of his realm's greatness. Neighboring rulers paid him respect, or at least valued the brilliant sign that seemed to bless his city.

Three years earlier, on a night at the beginning of autumn, a rain as red as blood had fallen. The tower was stained. Its radiance vanished. The kingdom's neighbors ceased bringing tribute. The king and his advisers searched for a reason. A few ministers suggested that the monks must have stolen the treasure from the summit. The king accepted the charge without evidence. The accused were beaten and interrogated. Two earlier groups of monks had died under the torture, and now the surviving group remained in chains, still unable to confess to a theft they had never committed.

Xuanzang listened with grief and anger. The government's failure was as clear as the monks' suffering. But without knowing where the treasure had gone, he could not simply demand that the king believe him. He said he had made a vow before leaving the Tang realm: whenever he came to a temple he would worship, and whenever he came upon a tower he would sweep it. He would begin there. Perhaps the work itself would reveal what had happened.

## A broom in the night

The monks hurried to prepare a meal and warm water. First they needed help with the younger captives chained to the rear pillars. Bajie asked whether someone had a knife to cut the locks. Then he laughed and pointed toward Wukong. Their eldest brother, he said, had been a specialist in opening confinement long before he became a monk. Wukong touched the locks and they fell away. The novices ran to the kitchen, scrubbed the pots, and began making food. Other monks went into the city to obtain fresh brooms.

After the evening meal Xuanzang bathed, changed into clean clothes, and took one of the brooms. He would ascend the tower at night rather than delay until morning. Wukong asked to accompany him. Something had fouled the tower, and if a demon was still nearby, his master should not stand alone among narrow stairs and dark balconies. Xuanzang agreed.

They lit lamps and offered incense before beginning. Xuanzang bowed and spoke a prayer for the falsely accused monks. He did not pray for restored tribute, royal pride, or rewards. He asked that the cause of the desecration be revealed and that innocent people cease to suffer.

Then master and disciple started sweeping.

The tower had thirteen levels. Its stairways climbed through narrow turns and dark landings. The windows were thick with grime. Dust covered the images, and old mouse droppings filled the neglected corners. Xuanzang brushed a stair, climbed, brushed another. They worked one floor after another until the night was well advanced. At the seventh level he was tired. By the tenth his back and legs ached. He sat and admitted that he could not finish the three levels remaining.

Wukong told him to rest and went on alone. He swept the eleventh floor, then the twelfth. From above came the sound of voices, cups, and drunken laughter. No monk should have been drinking in the highest chamber at that hour, and no prisoner from the monastery would have climbed the sealed stairs to celebrate there.

Wukong set down his broom and slipped up to the thirteenth level. Two creatures were playing drinking games beside the sacred chamber. Their table held food and wine. The light of the missing relic was not there; its thieves had stationed watchmen in the place it once blessed. Wukong blocked the exit with his staff and shouted that he had found the thieves.

The creatures flung cups and bowls in terror and tried to escape. Wukong could have crushed them with a stroke. Instead he held them against the wall and demanded a statement. Without living witnesses the court would have only his accusation against its former accusation, and the innocent monks might remain in danger. He took both creatures down to the tenth floor, where Xuanzang was dozing.

The prisoners were servants, not the leaders, they said. Their names were a pair of tongue-twisting variations on one another: Benbo'erba and Babo'erben. One was a catfish spirit, the other a blackfish spirit. They had come from the Dragon King of All Saints, whose residence lay beneath the Emerald Waves Pool among the Broken-Stone Mountains. The dragon king's daughter, Princess All Saints, had married a powerful Nine-Headed Son-in-Law. Together the family had made a plan to steal the relic from the tower.

The blood-colored rain had been their work. It had stained the tower and hidden the theft. The sacred relic now shone inside the dragon palace instead of above Jisai's city. The princess had committed a second theft as well: she had taken the Queen Mother's nine-leaf spirit fungus from Heaven, and it was being used to preserve and nourish the stolen treasure beneath the water. The fish spirits had been sent back to the tower to keep watch in case some powerful traveler came seeking the missing object.

Wukong remembered the Bull Demon King's banquet. He had ridden the Bull's mount into that very pool and watched him drink with a dragon court. He had entered as a crab and left with a borrowed face. Now he knew more about the family that had entertained his old brother. The lavish feast he had glimpsed had been held in the household that kept Jisai's stolen sacred light.

Bajie arrived at the tower with lamps and younger monks. When he heard the prisoners' confession, he raised his rake. The creatures deserved to die, he declared, and their bodies might make soup for the people whose suffering they had helped cause. Wukong stopped him. These two were needed alive as witnesses before the king and as guides to the dragon palace. They were bound and carried down, while the first joyful news spread among the monks that their innocence could be proved.

## A king hears another account

The next morning Xuanzang dressed in his embroidered robe and ceremonial hat and went to the royal court with Wukong and the travel document. The king welcomed a monk sent from the Tang empire, whose emperor had undertaken so distant a religious mission. But when Xuanzang asked about the Golden Light Monastery, the ruler made a bitter accusation. His own monks, he said, had stolen the country's light and ruined its prestige.

Xuanzang answered that the theft was real but the accused were wrong. He and Wukong had swept the tower. They had discovered the fish spirits still watching it and had obtained their account of the dragon king's crime. The prisoners were at the monastery. The king ordered his guards to bring them to court.

Wukong went with the guards, this time seated beneath a ceremonial umbrella in a magnificent chair. Bajie, seeing him carried in state, laughed that the monkey had resumed his old occupation of being a king. Wukong told him to stop joking and help bring the prisoners. The fish creatures were led through the streets while the townspeople followed, eager to see the beasts that had kept the holy men in chains.

Before the throne the prisoners repeated their confession. Their names and their forms drew the court's attention, but the details mattered more: the rain, the stolen relic, the princess's spirit fungus, the dragon palace, and the Nine-Headed Son-in-Law. The king had blamed the wrong people. He could no longer say there was no evidence of another culprit.

He ordered the collars and chains removed from the Golden Light monks. The freed men would not receive back the lives of those who had died under torture, but the court could at least stop punishing them. Xuanzang did not celebrate as if the matter were finished. The relic remained stolen, and the tower remained dark.

The king gave a banquet for the pilgrims and asked how many troops would be needed to recover the sacred treasure. Bajie was eating enthusiastically and volunteered at once. He and Wukong needed no army, he said. Their own rake and staff would do. Xuanzang approved, though he knew from long experience that confidence at a table did not always make a battle simple.

Wukong asked that the two prisoners be brought along to identify the place. Sha would stay with Xuanzang and the horse. Bajie had finished enough bowls to satisfy even his own appetite. He took one captive while Wukong took the other, and the two brothers lifted them into the air toward the Emerald Waves Pool.

The palace Wukong had once visited as an uninvited crab now waited as the stronghold of thieves. The monks' pardon had been won; the return of their light would require a fight.
