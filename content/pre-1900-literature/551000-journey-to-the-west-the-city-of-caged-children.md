# Journey to the West: The city of caged children

## The cages before the doors

The pilgrims traveled away from Lion-Camel City through the cold months. The ridge and its monstrous rulers fell behind them, but the road did not become easier merely because one army had been removed. Rain and snow followed them, and the leaves thinned along the roadside. When they saw another walled city in the distance, Xuanzang asked its name. Wukong said they would learn at the gate. In a kingdom, they must renew their travel papers. In a district town, they might pass without appearing before a monarch. This was the ordinary work of traveling, and after Lion-Camel City ordinary business felt almost welcome.

A watchman was dozing against a sun-warmed wall near the entrance. Wukong woke him to ask the name of the place. The man opened his eyes, saw the monkey's face, and dropped to his knees as though the thunder god himself had come to question him. Wukong told him that he was no thunder god, only a disciple accompanying a monk from the eastern Tang lands. The watchman answered that the kingdom was called Bhikshu, or Biqiu, but that people had lately begun calling it the City of Children.

Bajie guessed that perhaps an old ruler had died and a boy had succeeded him. Xuanzang was not convinced. As they entered, they saw streets full of markets, merchants, embroidered cloth, and prosperous houses. But outside the doors, one after another, stood small cages decorated with colored fabric. Xuanzang wondered whether this was a festival. Bajie suggested marriages and celebrations. Wukong turned himself into a bee and flew through the covering of one cage.

Inside sat a child. He went to another cage. Another child. Then another, and another. Some were playing with whatever they had been given. Some cried. Some slept. The children he inspected were young boys, none older than about seven. The houses looked peaceful; the cages did not. Wukong returned to his master, who could no longer pretend that the decoration meant celebration. These were children being held for something, and the city was trying to make their captivity appear orderly.

They found the official hostel and asked to rest there. The host welcomed the foreign monk, accepted the purpose of his journey, and arranged food and rooms. At first he spoke readily about the kingdom, but when Xuanzang asked why every household kept a child in a cage, his manner changed. He lowered his voice and warned the monk not to speak of it. The visitor, he said, would be wiser to finish his business in the morning and leave.

Xuanzang would not let the question go. He had crossed deserts and kingdoms to seek scriptures for the relief of suffering; how could he sleep in comfort without knowing what was being done to children a few steps beyond the hostel door? At last the official cleared the room and told the four travelers what the city had been hiding.

## The king's promise of long life

Three years earlier, an elderly Daoist-looking stranger had arrived at court bringing a beautiful girl of sixteen. He presented her to the king, who made her his favored consort. Soon the other queens and women of the palace had been pushed aside. The king devoted himself to pleasure, neglected ordinary duties, and became sick and wasted. His physicians exhausted their remedies. The court's hope then passed to the old stranger, whom the king had raised to the rank of royal father-in-law, or state father.

The state father promised a medicine made from rare ingredients gathered far away. It would extend life beyond the ordinary span. All the ingredients were supposedly ready except the final substance needed to activate the prescription. For that, he required the hearts and livers of one thousand one hundred and eleven young boys.

The king had ordered the children selected. Their families could not protest openly because the decree bore royal authority. The cages before the houses were waiting places for a sacrifice that had not yet begun. Parents could feed and watch their sons but could not release them. The host had seen the foreign monk's reaction and feared that the same reckless compassion would bring punishment upon his house.

Xuanzang wept. He could understand a sick man's fear of death, but not the notion that another thousand lives could be spent to purchase his recovery. He spoke of children who had not chosen any part in the illness, the prescription, or the king's appetite. Their parents had been made powerless before a crime whose instruments were banners, orders, and official seals. The monk asked why a kingdom rich enough to fill its markets with goods had allowed its wealth to become a screen for such suffering.

Bajie told him to stop carrying everybody else's sorrows. A ruler's orders were a matter between a ruler and his subjects; the pilgrims already had enough dangers of their own. Sha Wujing answered more carefully. The old state father might be a person practicing a bad art, or he might be a demon disguising murder as medicine. They could learn more by meeting the king. Wukong saw that the question had already become urgent. The children might die before morning. First save them, he proposed, and then investigate the prescription.

His master, who could hardly speak for grief, bowed to his disciple and asked him to act. Wukong told the others to stay inside and pray. If a cold wind rose in the night, that would be the sign that the children had been removed from danger.

## A night of hidden rescuers

Wukong rose above the roofs and summoned the city's guardian spirits, its earth gods, temple protectors, and the attendants who had watched over the pilgrimage. They gathered in the night air to hear him. He told them that a king had been persuaded to kill more than a thousand children for a false medicine. They must take each cage out of the city, carry its occupant to a safe place among groves or sheltered hollows, and keep watch until the danger passed. They must also feed the children, calm them, and protect them from fright. These were not objects to be stored until convenient. They were frightened children whose lives required active care.

The gods went out over the streets. A wind rose. It carried dust and damp mist past the colored cages. Across the city, parents woke to find their sons and the cages vanished together. They cried out in terror, not knowing who had taken them. The night was not gentle, because a rescue hidden from the ruler could not yet be explained to the people he ruled. Wukong trusted that the children would return alive once the deception was exposed.

At the hostel, Xuanzang, Sha, and Bajie heard the wind and repeated prayers for the protection of life. When Wukong returned, he reported that the children had all been moved and were under guard. Xuanzang thanked him again. The danger had not ended, but the king's officers would find no victims waiting in cages when they came with their knives.

In the morning Xuanzang dressed in the brocade robe reserved for an appearance at court. He carried the royal passage document and his ringed pilgrim's staff. Wukong said he would accompany him unseen. Xuanzang worried that his disciple's manner might offend the ruler, so Wukong changed himself into a small insect and settled upon his master's hat. Bajie and Sha remained behind with the horse and baggage.

The king received Xuanzang. He looked older than his years, his movements uncertain, his face drawn by exhaustion. Even his attention wandered across the pages of the document he was asked to stamp. He gave the necessary seal and appeared willing to dismiss the foreign visitor. Then the state father arrived. The king struggled to stand in welcome. The old man entered as one accustomed to being obeyed without explanation. He scarcely bothered to return Xuanzang's greeting.

## Two accounts of immortality

The king asked whether taking Buddhist vows might confer long life. Xuanzang answered that the way he had learned was not a purchase of immortality with other bodies. A person could practice restraint, cleanse the mind of greed, and give help to others. A body with form would change and perish; the point of practice could not be to cling to it at any cost. He spoke of the discipline of not injuring life as part of the substance of a holy path.

The state father laughed at him. The monk, he said, knew how to sit still and talk of emptiness. What did he know of roots, minerals, celestial essences, the movements of heat and cold, and the ways in which a trained adept could preserve vitality? He delivered an ornate defense of his supposed Daoist methods, and the courtiers applauded a learned voice they were eager to please. Xuanzang did not win the debate. He bowed, received leave to depart, and went back toward the hostel.

Wukong flew off the hat and hid where he could hear what followed. Ministers had come to report a disaster: every cage in the city had vanished. The king was horrified. Without the children, where would the medicine come from? The state father did not lament the missing boys. He announced that Heaven had granted the king something better than a thousand childish hearts.

He had noticed the foreign monk. That monk's body had been disciplined through many lives of religious practice. Its heart and liver would be worth more, he said, than all the children in the city. The king could live far longer if he took this new ingredient. The ruler, already persuaded that killing could be medical treatment, allowed himself to be persuaded again. He ordered the gates closed and armed men sent to surround the hostel. They would invite Xuanzang to court, and if he refused to surrender his heart, they would take it by force.

Wukong hurried to his master with the news. Xuanzang collapsed at the thought that his saving of the children might bring the knife upon himself. Bajie observed, with the poor timing that was often his gift, that the wind had indeed saved the children but had brought trouble to the monk. Wukong refused to let his master be taken. They would exchange appearances, he said. He shaped clay with magic, pressed a monkey face upon Xuanzang, dressed the master like a disciple, and took Xuanzang's robes and likeness for himself.

When the armed men came, the monk they seized looked serene. The true Xuanzang, disguised and trembling in the room behind him, had been rescued from the blade but not yet from the king's rule. Wukong stepped toward the palace in the monk's clothing, ready to test whether a royal physician could tell a heart of compassion from a heart hungry for power.
