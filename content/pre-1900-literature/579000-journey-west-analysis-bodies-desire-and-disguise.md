# Journey to the West — Bodies, Desire, and Disguise

## The stranger who is not what she seems

A young woman approaches with food. She seems vulnerable. The monk wants to help. Wukong raises his staff, and the reader learns that the woman is a demon. Later another woman approaches, or a man claims to be searching for someone he has lost. The same pattern returns with sharper consequences. In *Journey to the West*, appearances are not only what people see. They are tools that make others decide how to act.

The novel is full of transformed bodies: animals who become humans, humans who seem to become animals, ordinary objects that are actually magical devices, celestials concealed in domestic roles, and monsters borrowing the signs of holiness. The changes are funny, frightening, and often related to desire. Who wants to eat, marry, govern, survive, possess a powerful body, or be recognized as something other than they are? Which desires does the narrative mock, and which does it take seriously?

It is especially important not to let the large number of female monsters collapse into a statement about women in general. The novel includes human queens, captive princesses, goddesses, disguised examiners, and dangerous demons. They do not perform identical functions. They do, however, invite questions about the cultural assumptions under which a male monk's chastity is threatened, women's claims are judged, and shape-shifting turns the female body into a site of suspicion.

## A body that will not stay in its category

The monkey's own body begins the problem. He is born of stone. He calls himself a king, studies techniques of transformation, and performs himself before Heavenly authorities. His hair can generate bodies, tools, and disguises. To defeat an enemy he may become a fly, an insect, a familiar relative, or something small enough to enter another being. His body is not a stable sign of his nature.

Yet Wukong often acts as the novel's expert in exposing false bodies. In the White Bone Demon episode, Xuanzang sees a succession of human figures; Wukong sees the same predator behind them. In the two-Monkey-Kings episode, this privilege of perception fails. Another being has Wukong's face and powers so effectively that celestial authorities struggle to identify the original. If identity were always visible to the right eyes, the story would have no need for that terrifying double.

The point is not that the book denies the existence of true and false. It repeatedly distinguishes them, sometimes with enormous moral urgency. The point is that the process of knowing which is which is fraught. A person can be wrong because the illusion is convincing, because desire makes the illusion attractive, or because a superior voice announces an answer one is expected to accept.

Bodies are therefore the place where the novel's metaphysical and social questions meet. A monster's copied face can trigger hospitality. A counterfeit master can command respect. A splendid religious image can convince pilgrims they have reached their goal. And a supposedly ordinary horse may be a dragon punished into a life of service.

## Chastity as both commitment and peril

Xuanzang's renunciation of sexual and domestic life is essential to his mission. When marriage is offered, the issue is not simply whether a particular woman is attractive. The monk has promised a form of life incompatible with becoming a husband, king, or household head. In several episodes, apparent generosity becomes a threat because it requires him to exchange that commitment for another identity.

The trial of the four divine visitors appearing as a widow and daughters exposes Bajie's lingering interest in domestic comfort. Its comedy depends on a pilgrim whose desire outruns his declared vocation. The fact that the women are disguised sacred testers prevents us from classifying the episode simply as proof that women are temptresses. In the plot, the figures performing temptation are the agents of a moral test. The test operates by placing a genuine human longing—comfort, intimacy, a settled house—against a religious vow.

Xuanzang's encounter with the queen of Western Liang is different. The queen is a ruler with a kingdom and political capacity, not merely an animal monster in an attractive guise. She proposes a marriage that would make Xuanzang part of her court. The offer can be read as an exercise of royal agency, a sincere desire, a temptation to abandon vows, or several of these at once. The story makes Xuanzang's commitment decisive, but his refusal does not make the queen a demon. The analysis must keep the human negotiation separate from the later violent abduction by the scorpion spirit.

The scorpion's cave sharpens that contrast. There the monk is captured and subjected to coercive desire. The danger does not arise because he has calmly considered a legitimate proposal and refused it. It arises because another being is willing to use power to secure access to his body. The novel's moral categories are clearer when we distinguish desire, proposal, and coercion rather than calling all three 'temptation.'

## The country where men can become mothers

The Mother-and-Child River offers a different kind of bodily disruption. When Xuanzang and Bajie drink its water, they become pregnant. The spectacle puts pregnancy into bodies that a patriarchal social order would not normally imagine in that role. Bajie's bodily panic and the difficulty of finding the Water of Release generate comedy. But the episode also makes physiological vulnerability part of the pilgrims' immediate reality.

One reading emphasizes that the novel has turned pregnancy into a joke at men's expense. Another notices that a subject often experienced by women is made briefly unavoidable to male protagonists. A third asks whether the story's desperate search for a remedy trivializes pregnancy by presenting it primarily as a grotesque inconvenience. These readings are not equivalent. Each needs attention to how the narration treats the men's suffering, the local society's rules, and the value of control over one's body.

The same region contains the women's kingdom, but the two episodes should not be confused. The pregnancy ordeal creates a bodily problem that must be remedied for travel to continue. The queen's proposal creates a social and moral problem about vows, marriage, and sovereignty. One is a magical disruption of reproductive categories; the other is a negotiation between differently situated adults. Their proximity makes each more interesting, not interchangeable.

## The bath, the web, and the watching eye

The spider sisters episode offers another important test for readers. Xuanzang enters a seemingly hospitable setting and is caught by female demons who can control space with their webs. Later, at the Cleansing Spring, the plot brings together bathing bodies, disguise, and observation. Bajie's actions are comic in the narrator's manner, but they also invite criticism because his impulses can become intrusive and degrading.

A responsible reading does not place all the danger on the women while treating the male pilgrim's behavior as morally invisible. If desire in this novel means attachment or loss of self-command, that concept must be applied to male protagonists as well as to monsters. Bajie's earlier conduct at Gao Village already makes the point. The story's use of comedy may make misconduct entertaining; it does not stop us from asking who has control over the encounter and who has been made a spectacle.

The companion episode at Yellow Flower Temple adds a dangerous male figure: the Hundred-Eyed Demon, whose apparent hospitality conceals poison and murder. The contrast is instructive. Beautiful bodies are not the only vehicles of deception. A place of religious seriousness and polite hosting can be equally lethal. The demon's gender and appearance are less important than the method of turning another person's trust into an opportunity for harm.

## A false princess and the cost of substitution

The Tianzhu marriage sequence near the end of the novel repeats the problem of false identity within a royal household. A woman is confined away from court while a counterfeit occupies the role of princess. An embroidered ball selects Xuanzang as a prospective husband, and the ceremonial machinery of marriage begins to turn. The public signs of legitimate rank, femininity, and royal choice have been taken over by an impostor.

When Wukong exposes the Jade Rabbit and the real princess returns, the episode reveals how dangerous that substitution has been for the woman whose place was stolen. She is not just a deceptive costume in a demon's adventure. She is a displaced person whose claim must be heard and restored. The novel resolves the matter through supernatural detection and intervention, but the human cost is an important part of its plot.

Compare this with the White Bone Demon. In both, a being uses an innocent-looking form to gain access to a victim. But in Tianzhu the disguise also occupies a social position: the right to be recognized as the king's daughter, to act in her name, and to direct a royal marriage. The transformation is political as well as bodily. A familiar face can authorize decisions that its real owner never made.

The golden-nosed white rat in the Bottomless Cave episodes adds another complication. Her presentation as a victim initially recruits Xuanzang's compassion. She later kills novices and abducts him. Her supernatural family links to Li Jing and Nezha provide a route to her capture. As elsewhere, recognizing the real identity does not happen by simply discovering that attractive women are dangerous. It requires tracing the social relationships, injuries, and acts concealed by a performance of need.

## The difference between narrative evidence and a universal rule

It would be too convenient to say that all the novel's disguised women are metaphors for lust, all disguised men are metaphors for ambition, and every hero's desire is the same. The queen has governmental agency; the real princess needs restoration; the white bone creature is a predator; the scorpion is coercive; the spider sisters form a community of monsters; Guanyin is a powerful female bodhisattva figure who repeatedly rescues the pilgrims. The distinctions are part of the plot.

At the same time, a gender-aware reading should not ignore recurring asymmetries. The male pilgrim's celibacy is repeatedly threatened by women who desire him, while his own desires are largely represented through food, fear, and religious ambition rather than sexual pursuit. Bajie's more conspicuous desire becomes an object of mockery. Female characters often receive less freedom to explain themselves than the central male companions. Some are recovered by male or divine proprietors when their story has ended. Those are features worth examining as literary constructions, not a verdict on the moral worth of women or men.

The novel's transformations challenge the assumption that bodies disclose identities, but the story must still rely on social markers to build its plots. Beauty, clothing, rank, piety, family relation, and ritual speech can all become costumes. Once we realize that these markers are performable, the question of desire becomes inseparable from the question of knowledge: when we want to believe a person or institution, what do we take as proof?

Wukong's disguises complicate the answer further. He, too, deceives. He may borrow a face to rescue someone, torment a monster, or steal an object necessary for the mission. It is not possible to separate heroic transformation from villainous transformation by technique alone. Both sides can imitate and enter another body. Their difference must be argued from purpose, consent, proportionality, and consequences—standards on which the novel does not always pass an easy test.

## The human face at the end

When Wukong touches his head in the final chapter, he encounters a last bodily transformation of a different order: something that had hurt him for much of the journey is simply absent. The fillet has gone without another creature putting on its shape. For once, bodily change does not disguise an identity or make a victim vulnerable to deception. It registers an altered relation to authority.

That ending makes the novel's long fascination with shape-changing more coherent without explaining it away. A body can be an instrument of control, a surface on which social rank is performed, a site of hunger and pregnancy, a disguise for predation, or a sign of release. None of these meanings cancels the others.

The strongest way to read desire in *Journey to the West* is not as a catalogue of forbidden temptations but as a problem of how embodied beings act toward one another. The road tests the capacity to distinguish invitation from capture, perception from suspicion, and necessary disguise from self-serving manipulation. The characters fail these tests often enough to make their final deliverance something more than a procession of correct answers.
