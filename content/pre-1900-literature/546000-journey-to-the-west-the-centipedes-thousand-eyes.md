# Journey to the West: The centipede's thousand eyes

## A temple after the spider cave

The pilgrims had rescued Xuanzang from Spider-Silk Cave, but they had not entirely escaped its consequences. Wukong had burned the web-bound dwelling, and the seven sisters had fled the flames. The sisters did not forget the loss of their home. Nor had they forgotten Bajie's behavior at the hot spring, or the death of the insect children who had guarded their cave. They now had somewhere else to go, and someone they trusted to make the pilgrims pay.

A short way along the western road, Xuanzang saw a beautiful establishment among green slopes and trees. It had spacious galleries and an imposing hall, with flowers and water arranged as carefully as at a nobleman's country residence. Above its entrance hung a tablet identifying it as Yellow Flower Temple. It was a Daoist house, not a Buddhist monastery. That did not trouble Xuanzang. A traveler should be courteous to people who followed another discipline, and he could hardly complain of the hospitality offered by strangers before they had offered any.

Bajie remarked that Daoists and Buddhists wore different clothes but both cultivated religious practice. Sha Wujing agreed. They could inquire about food for the master and rest the horse for a little while. Wukong, whose mind worked faster than his manners, made a joke about furnaces and alchemical medicines when he saw the inscriptions. Xuanzang pinched him to keep quiet. The host had done nothing to earn mockery, he said. They were merely paying a brief visit.

In the eastern corridor they found a Daoist seated at his work, preparing pills. His elaborate headgear and fine robes marked him as no humble village priest. He rose to receive the visitors, adjusted his clothing, and invited them inside. Xuanzang saw images of the Three Pure Ones and burned incense before them. A Buddhist monk paying respect in a Daoist sanctuary was a peaceful sight; to the master it was simply good conduct on a difficult road. They sat down together, and the Daoist called to his attendants to prepare tea.

But the movements of the attendants disturbed seven women who had arrived by another entrance. The spider sisters recognized the pilgrims before their host learned who had come to call. They ran to him, complained of the destruction of their cave and the injuries they had suffered, and begged him to take revenge. They were his younger companions, and they knew precisely which appeal would move him. The Daoist listened. A hospitable host became an avenger without changing the expression he intended to show his guests.

## Tea served with great respect

The Daoist owned a poison he regarded as a treasure. He took a little locked case from a hidden place in his rooms, opened it with a key kept close to him, and brought out the deadly substance. Its origin was filthy rather than noble; the Chinese story makes a grim joke of the elaborate care with which this supposed immortal's medicine had been produced. Nothing about its reputation suggested a cure. It was intended to destroy even beings stronger than ordinary people.

He prepared tea for the travelers with the help of the spider sisters, taking pains that the poisonous cups should be served to the correct guests. One cup, apparently inferior because it contained two dark dates rather than the red dates in the others, was reserved for himself. The other cups waited for the disciples. At that point he returned to the hall smiling, apologizing for his absence. He said he had been arranging vegetables and a proper meal; surely one traveler of the Way should entertain another.

He asked where Xuanzang came from. When the monk explained that he had been sent from the Tang empire to the Western Heaven for scriptures, the host pretended to be overwhelmed with respect. So distinguished a traveler deserved better tea, he said, and he called for the prepared tray to be brought out. He offered a cup to Xuanzang, then Bajie, then Sha, and finally Wukong, whom he took for the smallest and least senior disciple.

Wukong noticed that his host's cup was different. He proposed they exchange drinks. The Daoist laughed and offered a plausible account of a poor household that had saved its best fruit for guests. Xuanzang intervened: their host was trying to be kind, and Wukong ought not to make a quarrel out of hospitality. Wukong accepted the cup, covered it with his hand, and watched the others.

Bajie was hungry. He swallowed his drink and dates almost at once. Xuanzang and Sha Wujing also drank. For a moment nothing happened. Then Bajie's face changed color, Sha's eyes streamed with tears, and foam rose at Xuanzang's mouth. One after another they collapsed where they sat. The beautiful room became an emergency from which Wukong had no orderly exit: his master's life was failing, the two men who could help him were helpless, and their smiling host stood on the other side of the table.

Wukong flung his untouched cup at the Daoist, shattering it against the floor, and demanded an explanation. What injury had they done him? They had entered his gate, greeted him, and taken their places. The Daoist answered with two questions. Had the pilgrims begged food at Spider-Silk Cave? Had they disturbed the women at the Cleansing Spring? Wukong understood at once. The sisters had reached this sanctuary first. What appeared to be a polite religious visit had been turned into a trap by a complaint from people who meant to kill his master.

## Seven sisters seek revenge

Wukong pulled the iron staff from his ear and brought it down at the Daoist's head. The host seized a sword and fled into the courtyard. They fought among pillars and trees, steel striking iron, until the noise drew the spider sisters from their hiding place. The women joined the battle by releasing their strands of silk. In an instant the space above the court filled with cords. The threads crossed in every direction and threatened to enclose Wukong as thoroughly as they had enclosed Xuanzang the day before.

At the spring, Wukong had refused to strike the sisters while they bathed, preferring to take away their clothing. He had not spared their cave. Now they were attacking him while his master lay poisoned. He broke away from the sticky mass and used hairs from his own body to raise a sudden whirlwind of copies, each of them tearing at the web. The silk spread and fluttered and collapsed. Wukong reached the seven sisters. In the violence of the fight he struck them all down. They did not escape a second time.

The Daoist cried out. The women whom he had been defending lay dead, and his poison had not stopped the one disciple he most needed to kill. He fought on, pressing his sword against the staff, but Wukong drove him backward. At last the Daoist withdrew and revealed the weapon that made him more dangerous than the spider sisters together.

He stripped away his upper clothing. Beneath his ribs, on both sides, were not two eyes but hundreds upon hundreds, an impossible host of shining eyes. As they opened, light poured out in thick golden sheets. The sun itself seemed to have split apart and descended upon the courtyard. Wukong was enclosed by it. He could not advance to strike the Daoist or retreat toward the hall. He tried the sides. He tried the air. Even with the body that had survived axes, blades, and furnace-fire, the golden brilliance bruised and stunned him when he leaped against its boundary.

Wukong grew furious with himself. He had rescued his master from one cave only to lead him into a temple where he might die of poison, while the rescuer himself stood trapped in a column of light. Then the Monkey King reconsidered the directions available to him. If he could not go forward, back, left, right, or upward, the earth remained. He changed into a hard-clawed pangolin, drove his pointed snout into the soil, and dug. The golden light reached far beyond the walls, but not through the whole depth of the mountain. He tunneled until he could emerge beyond its edge.

He changed back, exhausted and aching, and for a time could only weep. He had fought kings, dragons, and demons; yet here, at a place with a quiet temple name and courteous tea, he might lose the man he had promised to protect.

## The mourning woman on the mountain

From behind a ridge came the sound of someone else weeping. A woman dressed in mourning clothes approached carrying food for the dead and paper offerings. Wukong greeted her and asked whose death she mourned. Her husband, she said, had quarreled with the master of Yellow Flower Temple over a purchase of bamboo. The priest had poisoned him. She intended to burn these offerings for his spirit.

Wukong's own eyes filled again. The woman reproached him: why should a stranger stand weeping at her grief as though making a joke of it? So Wukong told her who he was. He recounted the seven spiders, the revenge at the temple, the poisoned tea, the thousand eyes, and his flight underground. His master and brothers were dying inside while he had found only a way to save himself.

The woman changed her manner. She knew the priest's true nature. He was the Hundred-Eyed Demon Lord, a many-eyed monster who possessed a power Wukong could not break by ordinary force. Yet there was a holy being who might help. On Purple Cloud Mountain, in Thousand Flowers Cave, lived the bodhisattva Pilampo. Her intervention could overcome the golden light. The woman pointed the way south, warning Wukong that delay might mean the poisoned pilgrims were beyond saving.

Wukong turned to thank her. She had vanished. He searched the sky and called for the name of the benefactor who had come to him in such plain clothing. From above, a voice answered. It was the Old Mother of Mount Li, who had assumed the form of a bereaved wife so that Wukong would stop and ask a question. She told him to go quickly, and not to name her as the one who had sent him. Pilampo preferred her seclusion.

Wukong bowed to the place where the Old Mother had been and set off at once. Mountains and distance shrank beneath his cloud. He reached a place of purple haze, flowers, and quiet water, far from the noise of the poisoned temple. There he found the hermitage and called for the bodhisattva. She appeared and listened, reluctant at first to leave her retreat. Then he explained that the creature was not simply a powerful opponent. Three companions lay drugged, their lives measured now in hours, and the light of a thousand eyes had defeated him.

Pilampo possessed a tiny needle with a radiance that could overcome the demon's brilliance. The needle had been prepared through the power of her son, the rooster-star official, who belonged to the heavenly order. Against ordinary enemies Wukong had carried a mountain-shattering staff. Against this enemy the necessary weapon would fit between two fingers. He accompanied her back to Yellow Flower Temple, intent on learning whether the monk and his brothers were still alive.

## A light put out by a smaller light

At the temple the Hundred-Eyed Demon Lord stood secure behind his shining defense. He had reason to believe Wukong was gone for good. When the Monkey King reappeared he could still boast of the golden rays shining from his body. Pilampo waited for the right instant. She took out the small needle and directed its power toward the blaze. The great field of light dissolved. What had filled the landscape like a second sun went out before a point of concentrated brilliance.

The Daoist could no longer hide inside his rays. Deprived of that advantage, he was exposed as a monster whose true form was a long, many-legged centipede. Wukong raised his staff, ready to avenge the poisoned travelers and the treacherous meal. Pilampo stopped him. The creature would be subdued rather than killed. She would take it away and assign it service under her authority. That was not the verdict Wukong would have chosen, but she had come when no one else could save his master.

The urgent matter was now the hall. Pilampo provided the medicine needed to counter the poison. Wukong hurried inside and gave it to Xuanzang, Bajie, and Sha Wujing. They recovered their breath, their senses, and finally the ability to sit upright. Bajie complained as a man might complain after a disastrous dinner; only moments earlier there had been little prospect he would ever eat another. Xuanzang learned what his disciple had done to save them. He had not seen the golden prison, the mourning woman, or the needle. He had experienced a courteous welcome followed by darkness, and now another rescue.

There was little affection left for Yellow Flower Temple. The Daoist host had offered death in the shape of a meal, and Wukong had seen enough of the establishment. He set fire to it. The flames took the halls in which the poison had been served. The beautiful refuge that had tempted the pilgrims from the road became smoke, while the surviving travelers gathered their belongings and returned to the westward path.

Xuanzang rode beneath the open sky again. Behind him lay two houses in which ordinary hospitality had concealed a scheme to kill him. Ahead lay another stretch of mountains, still without any guarantee that the next warning or invitation would be honest. Wukong walked close by the horse. He had discovered that force could free his master from silk, but not from every enemy, and that even the Great Sage sometimes survived because he had listened to a stranger who knew where to find help.
