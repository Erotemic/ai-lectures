# The Stone Monkey, the Waterfall, and the Fear of Death

The mountain stood in the sea east of the land of Aolai. Waves struck its rocks, clouds crossed its ridges, and the wooded slopes were full of creatures who had never seen a human king. High above the shore, on the summit of Flower-Fruit Mountain, a stone received the warmth of the sun, the light of the moon, and the changing weather of an age so long that a person could not have counted its years. The stone did not remain stone forever.

One day it opened. From it came a round stone egg. Wind touched the egg and it became a living monkey, with a head, limbs, a face, and a pair of extraordinary eyes. He stood up and bowed toward the four directions. Light flashed from his eyes into the heights of Heaven.

Far away, the Jade Emperor sat amid the elaborate order of his celestial court. A report of strange light had reached him. He sent the officials whose sight and hearing extended beyond ordinary limits to learn what had happened below. They returned with a description of a stone-born creature who could walk and feed himself. The light from the newborn's eyes was fading now that he had begun eating and drinking. The emperor decided that the event required no further attention.

It is a wonderfully modest bureaucratic beginning for the creature who will eventually overturn offices, break celestial formations, frighten great commanders, and resist execution. Heaven has noticed the monkey. Heaven has also filed the matter away.

The monkey knew nothing of that report. His world was the mountain, its clear streams and rich trees, its dangerous slopes and sheltering caves. He lived among other monkeys. They swung through branches, gathered fruit, watched seasons change, and played together beside the water. No one instructed the newcomer to rule. He had no parents to give him a household, no teacher to name him, and no inherited rank. His companions accepted him because he moved and played as they did, though he was capable of more than they guessed.

On a hot day the troop followed the course of a mountain stream. The current ran hard and cold. The monkeys wanted to discover where so much water came from. They scrambled upstream, calling to one another and clambering over wet stone until they reached a waterfall. Water fell in a great white sheet from the cliff. It was beautiful, but it also concealed whatever lay behind it. The monkeys were delighted with their discovery and curious about the secret of the water's source.

Someone proposed a contest: whoever could pass through the falls, find what was on the other side, and come back without harm should be their king. The proposal was repeated, and for all the noise of the troop, nobody jumped. They could see the curtain of water but not the landing. The fall might have hidden a pit. A river could have swept a swimmer into the rock. A promise of kingship was a poor comfort to a drowned monkey.

Then the stone-born newcomer stepped forward. He offered to go. He gathered himself, closed his eyes against the spray, and launched his body into the falling water.

He did not strike a wall. Behind the waterfall he found space, light, and a metal bridge. The water ran beneath that bridge through an opening in the mountain, while the falling stream outside made a curtain over its entrance. Beyond the bridge was a cavern fitted like a dwelling. There were stone seats, stone tables, basins and bowls, places for resting, and things that suggested the home of an absent immortal. Plants grew near the openings. The cave was wide enough for a great company. In its midst an inscription identified the place as Water-Curtain Cave, a blessed place of Flower-Fruit Mountain.

He took his time. If he had merely slipped through the waterfall and returned, the other monkeys might have heard an extraordinary boast. Instead he had discovered a home. Here the troop could sleep beyond reach of storms, cold winds, and rain. The stream would guard the entrance; the bridge would provide a passage. The mountain had hidden a palace in plain sight.

Out he came through the water again, dripping and triumphant. The monkeys crowded him, wanting to know whether the water was deep, whether he had found the source, and how he had survived. He told them about the bridge and the rooms behind it. He assured them that the water itself did not fill the cavern. If they would follow, he could show them a place better than any they had known.

The boldest leaped in after him. The timid remained on the rocks, craning their necks and clinging to one another until the noise of the others' excitement persuaded them. When the whole troop was inside, quiet did not reign for long. They seized seats, claimed beds, shifted vessels, chased one another through the rooms, argued over the best places, and behaved exactly like a crowd of monkeys discovering that a palace belonged to them. Eventually the uproar exhausted them.

The stone monkey found a raised seat. He reminded the troop of its promise. He had crossed the waterfall unharmed, returned, and guided them into a shelter they could all enjoy. They had proposed to make such a monkey their king. Would they keep their word?

They did. One after another, the monkeys bowed and acknowledged him. He put away the word stone from his title and became the Handsome Monkey King. It was not a title granted by Heaven or an official seal. It was recognition by companions who had watched him take a risk on their behalf. In a later chapter he will demand a title so extravagant that the celestial court can hardly say it aloud without embarrassment. For now he has acquired a title by finding his people a home.

There followed years of freedom. The monkeys made their life in the cave. They wandered out for fruit and roots, played in the forest, returned to their protected rooms, and lived without answering to rulers of beasts or kingdoms of men. The king enjoyed their affection. He could lead an expedition, preside at a feast, or lie at leisure while others amused themselves. From outside the mountain, an ordinary observer would have seen little cause for dissatisfaction.

One day, during a celebration, tears came into the king's eyes. The other monkeys were alarmed. They had food, a home, and freedom from most dangers. Was he ill? Had they displeased him? Why should he weep in the middle of a feast?

He explained that a thought had disturbed him. Their present happiness would not last. Bodies grew weak. Old age came. Death would eventually claim them. They were free from a human ruler's laws, but they had not escaped the authority of the underworld. Some day the officials who kept accounts of living creatures would summon even the Handsome Monkey King. All his victories would end in a visit he had not chosen.

His words changed the feast. The other monkeys, who had been thinking only of their enjoyment, began to mourn their own mortality. Not all of them knew what advice to give. At last one of the older and wiser monkeys spoke of beings who did not die as ordinary animals did. There were sages, immortals, and Buddhas who had learned a way beyond the usual limits of life. If the king could find such a teacher, he might learn how to escape the sentence that terrified him.

This advice turned the direction of the monkey's life. He had leaped through a waterfall because he wanted to see what lay behind it; now he would cross an ocean because he wanted to see what lay beyond death. The first journey had won a cave for the troop. The second might take him away from the troop for years. He was not told where a wise teacher lived. He would have to search.

His companions provided food and a raft. They held a farewell gathering beside the mountain. The king took leave of his followers and pushed out over the sea. The current and the wind carried him away from the island and toward lands where monkeys did not rule and strangers did not know his name.

On the great southern continent he found human settlements. People wore clothes, lived in streets and houses, bought and sold food, and behaved according to customs different from those of his mountain. A stone-born monkey attracted attention. He learned to imitate humans, to dress himself, and to travel among them. He sought an answer to death from people who seemed skilled or respectable. But much of what he observed was the pursuit of money, pleasure, and reputation. Whatever else those pursuits provided, they did not supply the knowledge he needed.

He searched for years. He passed through towns and countryside. People could show him a road to another market or village, but few could direct him to anyone who knew the way to lasting life. The monkey's journey was full of practical discoveries that did not satisfy its central purpose. He could cross water, find food, and follow human speech. He still could not explain why he had to die.

Eventually he crossed another sea and reached the western continent. In a wooded region he heard a woodcutter singing a song about a life detached from worldly struggles. The words were enough to make him stop. Perhaps, at last, a person who sang about freedom and the Way knew how to achieve them.

He approached the woodcutter eagerly and asked whether he was an immortal. The man laughed at the mistake. He was a working person who cut and sold firewood to support his family. The song was one he had learned from a sage who lived nearby. He could sing it, but he had not mastered the mysterious discipline behind it. When the monkey begged him to lead the way, the woodcutter explained why he could not simply abandon his own obligations. Yet he gave precise directions to the cave where the teacher lived.

The monkey followed the path up the mountain until he reached a place called the Cave of the Slanting Moon and Three Stars, on the Mountain of Mind and Heart. The names sound like riddles, and the novel does not strip them of their suggestiveness. The monkey has crossed seas looking for someone able to teach him what a life is. At the end of that enormous search he stands before a door named for the mind.

A disciple came out to meet him. The master inside had already expected someone to arrive. The monkey entered and found an accomplished teacher known as Patriarch Subodhi. The master asked where the visitor had come from. The answer seemed unbelievable: a creature from an eastern mountain, born of stone and carried across seas, now asking to become a student. Subodhi asked whether he had a family name. He did not.

The teacher gave him one. He would be Sun. And because the teacher wished him to awaken to emptiness, he would be called Wukong. From this point the Handsome Monkey King is also Sun Wukong, a name he will carry into Heaven, into prison, and onto a long pilgrimage not yet imagined by anyone in Water-Curtain Cave.

The naming is not the attainment of wisdom. It is an invitation to learn. The monkey who feared death now has a teacher, and this new form of belonging is different from kingship. On the mountain he had been acclaimed for a single daring leap. Here he must listen, obey, and begin at the beginning. For a creature already accustomed to being king, that may prove a harder test than the waterfall.
