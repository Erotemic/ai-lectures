# Journey to the West: The three princes of Jade Flower

## After the rain

The roads beyond Fengxian had changed with the season. The rain that had broken the drought was behind the pilgrims now. They traveled through the last of autumn, under trees whose leaves had begun to thin, past fields where people were gathering what they could before the cold. Xuanzang was still thinking about the thousands who had received water. Sha Wujing said that saving a whole prefecture must count as a greater act than rescuing the children of Bhikshu Kingdom. Bajie agreed that Wukong had done a splendid thing, then complained that the Great Sage had refused the only reward he really wanted: several days of undisturbed eating.

"There is always a good deed," Bajie grumbled, "and then there is always the road. You rescue people by the thousand and not one of them is allowed to feed me for a week."

Xuanzang told him to stop counting meals and walk. They had not crossed mountains and rivers for all these years merely to become comfortable guests in one prosperous city after another.

Ahead another wall rose above the trees. Xuanzang asked what country it belonged to. Wukong said that a wall did not announce its name from a distance. When they reached an old traveler leaning on a bamboo staff, they dismounted to ask. He called the place Jade Flower, Yuhua, a district governed by a prince of the royal family of Tianzhu. The prince was known for his good treatment of monks and his concern for ordinary people. It was a place where the travelers could expect a civil reception.

That reassurance pleased Xuanzang. He straightened his robes, checked that the imperial travel document was safely packed, and led his improbable company toward the gate. For years they had entered courts with monsters hidden beneath royal titles. Here, so the old man said, the ruler was human, courteous, and devout.

They were admitted, but courtesy was immediately tested by the appearance of the disciples. The prince welcomed the monk from the Great Tang and looked with growing astonishment at a monkey-faced attendant, a pig-headed man, and a broad, dark-faced warrior. The strange company was assigned food and lodging at a pavilion, where the court could entertain them without alarming every servant in the palace. The prince's three sons, however, had seen enough to become curious rather than frightened.

## Three young warriors

The princes were fond of martial training. One practiced with a staff, another with a rake-like weapon, and the third with a heavy pole. They had spent their youth practicing movements before instructors who praised them. When they saw the pilgrims, they did not know that the travelers carried weapons for which earthly smiths could make no equal. The princes thought the strange disciples might be fighters of some regional school, and there was a trace of youthful vanity in their invitation to compare skills.

Bajie took one look at the second prince's rake and laughed. "A handsome little tool," he said. "If you set it beside mine, it might pass for its grandson."

The prince was offended. Bajie pulled the nine-toothed rake from where he carried it and let its supernatural radiance spread across the courtyard. The young man stopped waving his own weapon. The rake he had treated as an ordinary iron implement suddenly seemed like a relic from a different order of existence.

The eldest prince stepped forward with his staff, but Wukong took the gold-banded cudgel from his ear. At his wish it grew thick and tall. He thrust it into the ground and offered it to the prince as a gift. The young man grasped it with both hands. He pulled, planted his feet, strained again, and could not move it an inch. Wukong drew it out as lightly as someone picking up a walking stick. Sha Wujing then showed the youngest prince the demon-subduing staff, which shone in his hands with an authority that the court weapon could not imitate.

The princes had expected a contest. Instead they realized that the men whom they had been about to challenge were masters of arts they could scarcely describe. They bowed and begged to see a genuine demonstration. Wukong looked around the pavilion and judged that the roof was too low for serious practice.

"We will show you in the sky," he said.

With a whistle he leaped upward and stood on a cloud high above the palace. The cudgel wheeled around him. One instant the crowd saw a monkey and his staff; the next they saw circles of light and iron moving so quickly that the wielder disappeared into his art. Bajie could not bear to have the elder brother monopolize the admiration. He rose into the air on a gust and put his rake through its heavy turns, making a din like wind across roof tiles. Sha followed. The third disciple's movements were quieter but no less formidable, controlled changes of grip and direction through which his staff seemed to pass without effort.

People poured from houses. Officials came out on palace steps; merchants stared from market streets; monks and nuns lit incense. The old prince saw colored cloud and moving metal above his residence and bowed with his household, thinking that immortals had appeared over his city. His sons told him afterward that these wonders had come from the very travelers whom he had just entertained.

## The request to become students

The three princes visited their father with a proposal. Each wished to become a disciple of one of the visitors. Learning martial technique from Wukong, Bajie, and Sha would protect their country, they said, and give their own training a purpose greater than courtly display. The old prince approved. He and his sons walked to the pavilion without a grand procession, knelt before the teachers, and offered rewards if they would take the young men as students.

Wukong laughed at the mention of money. He had not crossed half the earth to sell lessons by the hour. If the princes sincerely meant to learn, he would teach. The others agreed. Bajie was delighted by the prospect of having someone officially call him master. Sha accepted his own student with a gentler seriousness.

First they asked Xuanzang's permission. Wukong bowed and explained that men who became his pupils would also become spiritual grandsons to the monk who had freed and guided him. Bajie and Sha followed suit. Xuanzang gave his consent gladly, though he reminded them all that the company was still traveling west. Whatever they taught must be fitted into the little time they could remain.

There was a banquet, followed by more instruction. The princes had courage and willingness, but courage did not make an ordinary body capable of using a gold-banded cudgel. Wukong arranged for the three students to purify themselves, offer incense, and concentrate in a quiet room. He spoke charms, breathed immortal force into their bodies, and taught them the exercises by which it could be gathered and circulated. When they rose they were transformed in strength. The eldest could lift Wukong's staff; the second could turn Bajie's rake; the third could raise Sha's weapon.

The first time each prince moved his borrowed implement, his teachers were pleased. Then the boys tried to complete a series of blows and blocks. Soon they were panting. Wukong's weapon could grow and shrink to suit its owner; theirs was only borrowed strength, and that strength still belonged to flesh. Having the capacity to pick up a weapon was not the same thing as having practiced with it until judgment and body moved together.

The princes understood. They needed weapons made to the same pattern but light enough to practice with daily. A court smith could copy the design of the staff, the nine-toothed rake, and the demon-subduing pole in ordinary iron. Would the masters allow their originals to be kept as models while the copies were fashioned?

"Certainly," said Bajie. "We shall need ours for fighting demons, and you will need yours for learning not to hit one another. Have the smiths make proper training weapons."

## Light over the forge

The prince summoned smiths and ordered iron brought in. A forge was built in the palace compound. There was hammering by day and the glow of charcoal after dark. The three precious weapons were laid where their shapes could be measured. What the smiths saw was only the surface of a greater thing. Wukong's staff had once served as a measure for seas and rivers. Bajie's rake had its own heavenly history. Sha's staff belonged to a life before the monk of the flowing sands had joined the pilgrimage. No craftsman could reproduce those histories by copying iron.

Nevertheless the smiths worked carefully. The princes watched their new weapons taking shape and dreamed of the exercises they might perform before their teachers departed. The travelers stayed nearby, fed and honored, while the work continued.

They had made a mistake. Their divine weapons were not usually left where strangers could look at them. Wukong kept his staff as a needle in his ear. Bajie and Sha carried their own instruments close. In the forge the three treasures blazed outward day and night, sending columns of light through the courtyard and above the walls. Anyone sensitive to magical power for miles around could see that something precious had been set out in the open.

Seventy or so miles away, on Leopard Head Mountain, a spirit called the Yellow Lion was sitting in his cave. He saw the glow and flew to investigate. From above the palace he recognized the light of three treasures. He did not pause to ask who owned them or whether the smiths might be guarding them for sacred travelers. He felt desire, then the foolish certainty that desire itself was a kind of permission.

"What splendid things," he murmured. "Left here with no one attending them. They must have come into my hands by good fortune."

He descended under cover of darkness and gathered up all three weapons. The smiths, exhausted after long labor, slept through the theft. By the time the morning workers came to light the furnaces, the divine staff, the rake, and the demon-subduing pole were far away in Tiger Mouth Cave.

The three apprentices had gained strength and instruction, and now their three teachers had lost the instruments by which they protected their own master. Beyond the palace roofs, the night gave no sign of the creature who had carried the weapons away.
