# Journey to the West: The rope around the lion's heart

## The voice behind the teeth

The blue-maned lion lay on the floor of his cave, doubled over in pain. His brothers had watched him swallow Sun Wukong. They had heard the horrible certainty of jaws closing on their enemy. Yet the swallowed monkey was not dead. He was inside the lion, drunk on the wine intended to poison him, turning somersaults, kicking at the monster's organs, and offering cheerful remarks about the quality of his lodgings.

When the lion could manage to speak, he begged. The Great Sage had won. Would the Great Sage not show mercy? Wukong, who had repeatedly been told by this same monster that he would be eaten, enjoyed the change of titles. Why waste breath calling him a great and merciful bodhisattva? Why not simply call him Grandfather Sun? The lion, needing to live, was ready to call him anything at all.

He offered a bargain. No fighting, no more swallowing, no further threats against the Tang monk. The three demon kings would carry Xuanzang in a fragrant rattan sedan chair, tend him like an honored guest, and escort the pilgrimage safely beyond the mountain. Wukong admitted that this would be more useful than a heap of treasure. Their master needed passage, not ornaments. He told the lion to open his mouth.

The roc bent close to the lion's ear. The moment the monkey emerged, he whispered, the lion should bite down and finish him. It was a simple plan, if one forgot that Wukong could hear it from within. The monkey sent his iron staff ahead of him to test the welcome. The lion snapped his jaws. There was a crack like stone splitting. The demon's front teeth shattered against the staff.

Wukong drew it back and announced that he would never leave. He had offered mercy, and the lion had tried to chew him. He would remain in his new house until the house fell down around him. The lion turned on his youngest brother. It was all very well for a bird outside the body to suggest biting; he was not the one whose teeth had just broken.

The roc changed his argument. The Monkey King, whose name had terrified Heaven itself, was making himself look cowardly. What sort of hero fought from somebody else's stomach? Let him come outside to face the three kings properly, with weapons in an open field. That stung Wukong's pride. He knew he could destroy the creature by tearing out its insides, but he also wanted to be seen defeating his enemies as a warrior. He agreed to come out—once they had gone somewhere wide enough to fight.

## A very long leash

The demons assembled a vast army beyond the cave. Their ranks filled the slopes. The blue lion opened his jaws again, and Wukong could hear voices massing outside. He suspected a trap, which was sensible: the last offer had been a trap too. But this time he prepared something the demons had not considered.

He plucked a hair from his tail and changed it into a long, narrow cord. He tied one end around the lion's heart, with a sliding knot that grew tighter when pulled. Then he drew the other end carefully through the throat. He considered leaving by the mouth, but saw the ranks of teeth and remembered what the lion would do if the cord were in reach. He climbed upward through the nose instead. A terrible tickling seized the monster. The lion sneezed, and out shot Sun Wukong, like a seed hurled from a fruit.

In the open air the monkey grew to fighting size. The lion swung a sword. The elephant came at him with his weapon, and the roc thrust with his great halberd. Wukong met them for a moment, then sprang clear of the encircling soldiers. From a bare ridge above their heads he began to pull the cord.

The effect was immediate. The lion rose into the air, then jerked downward as Wukong hauled on the other end. Every pull tightened the knot about the heart. Below, the lesser demons watched their king flail above the ground. They decided the monkey was flying a kite, though the season for kites had not come. Wukong pulled harder. The lion struck the earth so violently that he made a hollow in the hard soil.

His brothers ran up the slope. They did not threaten the monkey now. They fell on their knees and begged for the lion's life. Wukong named the problem in terms even a demon could understand: if three kings gathered tens of thousands of armed servants to attack one traveler, they could hardly complain when that traveler found a different way to fight. He offered to remove the cord if they would truly let Xuanzang pass.

The lion was desperate to have it taken off. Would Wukong crawl down his throat a second time? Not necessarily. Wukong told him to swear to keep his promise. When the lion swore, Wukong shook his body and reclaimed the enchanted hair. The cord vanished; the pain vanished with it. The three demons thanked him and went back to prepare their promised escort.

Wukong returned to camp to find a small but painful ceremony in progress. Xuanzang was lying on the ground in tears. Sha Wujing was trying to manage the baggage. Zhu Bajie had opened the packages and was dividing the journey's possessions, since he considered the eldest disciple dead. The sound of Wukong's voice made Sha turn in relief. Bajie, who had seen the lion swallow him, insisted it must be his ghost.

Wukong slapped his cheek to persuade him otherwise. Bajie demanded to know how a swallowed monkey could return alive. Wukong described the pulling of the lion's heart. Xuanzang rose and bowed to him, ashamed of his despair but grateful that his disciple lived. Wukong said the demons were coming with a sedan chair. Bajie, who had just been arranging his departure for home, had to pack the bags again.

## An invitation through the mountain

The three kings came as promised, bringing bearers, attendants, and the chair. Xuanzang greeted them with ceremonial gratitude. The monk had not stood inside their cave among the bones; he saw fearsome-looking beings who were behaving with elaborate courtesy because his disciple had compelled them. He believed that victory and repentance might have made the road safe.

Wukong believed something similar. His victories had forced the lion to plead, broken the strongest prison of the kings, and sent their soldiers running. He could tell himself that the monsters were finally conquered. He walked ahead with the iron staff. Bajie and Sha followed the chair, tending the horse and load. The rattan bearers worked in relays. Food came at the proper hours, places to rest appeared before nightfall, and a difficult mountain passage became, for a little while, a strange procession of hospitality.

The novel allows this deceptive calm to last. They traveled hundreds of miles under the demons' care. Xuanzang received meals and lodging. The masters of the procession seemed to have exchanged appetite for service. Yet the lion, elephant, and roc were not surrendering their kingdom. They were transporting its intended meal.

At last Wukong came within sight of Lion-Camel City. He could see a dark vapor rising from it, and then forms within the streets and towers. Wolves occupied the gates. Tigers held offices. Foxes, boars, leopards, and serpents went about the business of a whole city. This was not merely a stronghold at the top of a ridge; it was a human kingdom turned into the machinery of demons. The sight brought Wukong up short. The arrangement of life there told him what the escort had concealed.

As he looked, the trap closed. The roc sprang upon him with his halberd. The lion attacked Bajie, the elephant drove at Sha. Every fighter had been separated from the others by the order of the march. The bearers seized Xuanzang and hurried toward the city gates, all at once shouting that their rulers had captured the Tang monk.

The defenders understood their orders. They opened the gates quickly, then muffled the drums and suppressed the noise. The monk, they had been told, would be frightened into poor eating if they alarmed him. He must be handled gently until they had decided how to prepare him. They carried him into the palace and seated him on the royal dais with an elaborate show of welcome. Tea and food appeared, but he could see that his protectors were outside fighting for their lives.

He looked from one servant to another, searching for a human face, or even for a single person who might help. Every attendant belonged to the rulers who wished to consume him. The courtesy that had seemed to promise safety on the mountain had brought him into the center of danger.

Outside, Wukong's staff clashed with the roc's halberd; Bajie met the lion's sword, and Sha fought the elephant's trunk and weapons. The three disciples were still standing. Xuanzang was already in the city. Neither triumph nor catastrophe had yet reached its end. The kings had used an honored guest's escort as a weapon, and the pilgrimage was divided once again.
