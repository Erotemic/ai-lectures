# Anna Karenina I: the two households and their endings

## Anna Karenina — Story: A Family Crisis, a Visit, and a Ball

The novel begins not with Anna but with a household in distress. Stepan Arkadyevich Oblonsky, commonly called Stiva, has been discovered in an affair with a former governess. His wife, Dolly, is wounded and angry. Their children and servants inhabit a home whose ordinary routines have been unsettled by the husband's betrayal. Stiva regrets the discomfort, but his charm and appetite remain largely intact. Tolstoy does not need to announce a moral conclusion. The different costs of the affair are already visible: the wife must manage the damage while the husband imagines reconciliation without a fundamental change in himself.

Stiva's sister Anna Arkadyevna Karenina is coming from Saint Petersburg to help. She is married to Alexei Alexandrovich Karenin, a senior government official, and has a young son, Seryozha. Her purpose in Moscow is familial and specific: she hopes Dolly and Stiva can speak again. Anna is intelligent, warm, and socially assured. Dolly is inclined to trust her, even though the two women live under different circumstances. Anna knows how to console without pretending that the original injury did not happen. The opening therefore puts her inside a network of women trying to keep households intact before her own marriage becomes the center of public scandal.

Another thread begins with Konstantin Dmitrich Levin, a landowner who prefers the countryside to fashionable Moscow. He has come to propose marriage to Kitty Shcherbatskaya, Dolly's younger sister. Levin is earnest, awkward in company, and unsure how his life should be organized. He wants work, affection, and a family that does not depend upon empty social performance. Kitty likes him, but the handsome officer Count Alexei Vronsky has been courting her in public. She hopes Vronsky will propose, so she rejects Levin. The scene hurts both of them. Levin withdraws to his estate; Kitty prepares for a future that has not actually been promised to her.

At a Moscow railway station, Anna encounters Vronsky. He has come to meet his mother, who traveled with Anna and enjoyed her company. Their first meeting has an immediate force that neither can fully explain. At the station a worker is killed by a train, and the accident troubles Anna. Vronsky gives money to the bereaved family. The scene joins generosity, class privilege, machinery, and ominous chance. It would be a mistake to treat the accident as proof that the future is already fixed. Tolstoy places an image of danger in front of us; the characters continue making choices.

Anna succeeds in bringing a degree of peace to Dolly's household, though the deeper imbalance between Dolly and Stiva remains. She prepares to return home. Before she leaves, she attends a ball. Kitty expects to shine, partly because she believes Vronsky's attention has made her future secure. Instead his attention turns to Anna. There is no formal declaration. A dance, a glance, and a withdrawal tell Kitty that she has misread her situation. She is humiliated, and Anna is frightened by the attraction she feels. The event gathers the novel's emotional geometry: Levin's disappointed hope, Kitty's disappointed confidence, Vronsky's pursuit, and Anna's married life.

Anna returns to Saint Petersburg by train. Vronsky follows, and their encounter on the journey confirms that what began in a public room has not ended there. In the noisy wind and machinery of travel, they speak with the intensity of people who know that an ordinary friendship is not what is at stake. Anna still returns to her husband and son. Their home, however, now feels altered to her. Karenin's habitual mannerisms irritate her, while she remains devoted to Seryozha. No one has yet made the final choices that will separate them.

The opening has already established two parallel stories, not a primary tragedy and a minor romance. Anna is a woman capable of affection and social insight whose life will be tested by desire and law. Levin is a man whose uncertainty extends from courtship to the meaning of work and faith. The two do not need to meet in the first part for their stories to illuminate each other. Both want a life that feels true, but the resources and freedoms available to them are very different.

The part ends with a series of mistaken expectations. Kitty expected Vronsky to propose; Levin expected that a sincere offer might be enough; Dolly hoped that apologies could restore a family; Anna hoped her visit could remain an episode in someone else's marriage. Tolstoy will return again and again to the difference between what people think their lives are becoming and what they discover only after they have acted.

## Anna Karenina — Story: The Affair, the Race, and Kitty's Recovery

After the Moscow ball, Anna returns to a marriage whose habits are suddenly difficult to bear. Her husband Karenin occupies himself with state affairs and expects a regulated household. He does not respond to emotional danger in the same register as Anna. Vronsky pursues her, and the attraction becomes an affair. Tolstoy does not ask us to see the relationship as mere wickedness or as a purely liberating escape. It brings genuine feeling into a life Anna experiences as restricted, while imposing risks upon a wife and mother whose social and legal position differs from that of her lover.

Petersburg society knows how to talk about affairs while pretending to enforce strict propriety. Men may be forgiven, and women may be tolerated when they obey the necessary conventions of concealment. Anna's visible attachment to Vronsky challenges the code that allows society to enjoy impropriety provided the forms are respected. Countess Lydia Ivanovna and others will later treat Anna's public disgrace as a moral certainty. The power of their judgment is not confined to gossip: it bears on access to a child, invitations, and the possibility of sustaining a social life.

Karenin begins to notice the whispers and the changed manner of his wife. He warns her about appearances and duty, with language so administrative that it can fail to reach her. Yet he is not merely a machine. His later conduct will include moments of unexpected generosity. At this stage he believes that maintaining the household's recognized form is the most important defense against scandal. Anna finds the expectation intolerable, and Vronsky imagines that a decisive passion can overcome the rules he understands only imperfectly.

The horse race concentrates their conflict. Vronsky is an experienced rider on a mare called Frou-Frou. He wants to win, and Anna watches with fear she can barely hide. During the race he makes a damaging movement, the horse falls badly, and she is fatally injured. Anna's distress at the accident reveals to Karenin that her emotional allegiance has shifted. In the aftermath she tells her husband that she loves Vronsky and is his mistress. The confession is a rupture, not a resolution. Karenin must now decide how to preserve position, marriage, and fatherhood; Anna must live with the consequences of declaring what had been hidden.

At the same time, Kitty's health suffers after Vronsky's failure to propose. Her family arranges a stay at a spa abroad. There she admires a young woman, Varenka, whose apparently selfless conduct seems to offer a different model of life. Kitty tries to imitate a charitable ideal before discovering that sincere care cannot simply be adopted as a performance. Her education is not a punishment for having misjudged Vronsky. She begins to recognize the difference between being admired, being useful, and choosing an adult relationship.

Levin has returned to his estate after Kitty's rejection. Agricultural work fills his days, but work does not erase his desire for her. He follows the labor of mowing, discusses arrangements with peasants, and worries about how to make farming both productive and meaningful. The land he owns gives him authority, and his dissatisfaction with the habits of other landowners does not place him outside the hierarchy. His interest in the practical intelligence of workers is real, but a listener should not mistake his admiration for an equal distribution of property or political power.

Stiva remains a connective figure among households. His charm opens doors, earns invitations, and helps him borrow money or avoid unpleasant conversations. Dolly bears the more persistent burden of managing children and domestic expenses. Their marriage keeps the novel's comparison alive. Anna's affair provokes public punishment that Stiva does not face in the same form, though his conduct has directly harmed his family. Tolstoy makes the distinction visible through the daily consequences rather than by interrupting the story with a lecture on hypocrisy.

By the end of this movement Anna and Karenin have admitted a breach that their society might have preferred not to name. Vronsky has experienced the cost of ambition in the fallen horse without learning to calculate fully the cost of his desire for Anna. Kitty has begun to grow beyond the fantasy of a dazzling match. Levin has returned to labor that can steady him but cannot answer every question. What began as a ball in Moscow now reaches into law, work, parenthood, and the bodies of animals and people caught in one person's pursuit of victory.

## Anna Karenina — Story: Levin's Work and Anna's Illness

Two kinds of work occupy the center of Anna Karenina. Levin attempts to manage his estate and understand the labor of the peasants who work its land. Karenin attempts to preserve a marriage through the formal procedures available to a senior official. Both are highly educated men, and both discover that the methods that serve them in ordinary life do not necessarily explain the people closest to them.

Levin's agricultural efforts are practical and often frustrating. He joins workers in mowing, feels the pleasure of coordinated physical labor, and tries to improve how farming is organized. His experience in the fields does not make him a peasant, and the workers' views are not simply extensions of his own. They have families, customs, judgments, and incentives that his abstract plans often fail to include. His wish to create a more just and productive arrangement runs up against his dependence on owning the land. Tolstoy makes the problem of moral improvement concrete rather than allowing Levin a painless escape into good intentions.

Levin remains hurt by Kitty's rejection. At one moment he glimpses her in a passing carriage and realizes that the affection he thought he had buried is still alive. The encounter is brief; it does not transform their lives immediately. His brother Nikolai, ill and estranged, also draws him away from the self-contained routines of estate life. Nikolai's poverty, illness, and quarrels disturb Levin's wish to make problems soluble by work. No scheme of agricultural improvement can settle what a family owes a suffering brother.

Anna's position grows more desperate. Karenin intends to control scandal through separation without openly dissolving the marriage. Anna, however, is pregnant with Vronsky's child and refuses to treat the affair as a secret that can be filed away. She is frightened for her son, Seryozha, and increasingly trapped by the impossibility of keeping both relationships in the form she wants. Vronsky's prospects in military and fashionable society are altered by the affair, but he retains freedoms unavailable to Anna. The mismatch becomes a recurring source of strain.

As Anna prepares to give birth, her health collapses. She becomes seriously ill and seems close to death. Karenin arrives expecting betrayal and accusation, but he encounters suffering that disrupts his usual moral categories. In an extraordinary scene he forgives Anna and Vronsky. Vronsky, overwhelmed by humiliation and despair, attempts to shoot himself; he survives. Anna also survives her illness and gives birth to a daughter, usually called Annie. What briefly appeared to be a final reconciliation cannot easily become a lasting household. Forgiveness in an emergency has not changed the laws governing marriage, divorce, and children.

Kitty and Levin meet again under changed conditions. She has experienced disappointment and recovered some independence; he has learned that wounded pride cannot determine the rest of his life. Their renewed closeness grows through small gestures rather than an elaborate public display. In one scene they communicate through the initial letters of words, discovering that they understand each other's wishes before the full sentences have been supplied. Their engagement does not mean that their future will be free of doubt. It means they are willing to begin a shared life while still imperfect.

Stiva's role in arranging meetings and smoothing social discomfort contrasts with Dolly's daily work of keeping her family together. He can assist Levin and Kitty while remaining unable or unwilling to correct the pattern of neglect in his own marriage. This is one of Tolstoy's sharpest social observations: charm may be genuinely helpful in one setting and profoundly irresponsible in another. The same person can be liked and trusted in public while leaving private debts, financial and emotional, for others to pay.

Anna and Vronsky eventually leave Russia together with their daughter. Karenin remains with Seryozha. The departure gives Anna a form of freedom but also separates her from the child she loves and leaves her uncertain status unresolved. Vronsky can imagine a life abroad as a new beginning. Anna carries a loss that foreign travel cannot repair. The difference in their burdens will become more visible when they return.

By now the two main paths of the novel have crossed a threshold. Levin and Kitty are moving toward marriage after learning something about the limits of romantic display. Anna and Vronsky are moving into a life outside recognized marriage after discovering that powerful feeling cannot abolish family bonds or social institutions. Tolstoy refuses to make either path simple. The task of living truthfully has become, for both pairs, a problem of other people's needs rather than a private feeling alone.

## Anna Karenina — Story: Marriage, Exile, and Illness

Levin and Kitty marry, and their wedding is both a public ceremony and a private test. Levin worries about his religious uncertainty and about whether he deserves the trust Kitty places in him. Ordinary preparations become occasions for anxiety; the couple must discover how to live with each other after the drama of courtship ends. Their first days of marriage are not an idealized continuation of romance. They quarrel over small matters, discover differences in habit, and learn that affection does not remove the labor of understanding another person.

Kitty enters Levin's rural world, bringing skills and judgments he has underestimated. She is not simply the charming young woman who rejected him in Moscow. When the two must respond to illness, her practical steadiness becomes unmistakable. Levin's brother Nikolai is gravely ill. The newlyweds go to him, and Kitty helps provide care even as Levin struggles with fear, disgust, love, and helplessness. Tolstoy describes the difficulty of a death watched at close range. Nikolai does not become an illustration of a neat philosophy. The body is failing, the room requires work, and the people around the bed cannot solve the approaching loss. Kitty's pregnancy makes the scene an encounter between the anticipation of new life and the irreversible end of another.

Anna and Vronsky, meanwhile, are abroad. Their life in Italy has beauty and freedom, but it does not settle the problem of belonging. Vronsky tries art and moves through fashionable circles; Anna is at once his partner and a woman cut off from the social structure that once gave her identity and access to her son. The lovers are not interchangeable. For Vronsky, travel and a new activity can look like experiments. For Anna, happiness with him must bear the weight of everything she has lost. Their return to Russia will reveal how little their public positions have changed.

Anna longs to see Seryozha. She risks a visit to her former home on his birthday, where mother and child share a moment of painful affection. The visit does not become a reconciliation with Karenin or a restoration of Anna's place as the boy's mother. She must leave again. The scene is crucial because it resists any account that treats the affair as simply a question of whether Anna has chosen the right man. She has a relationship with a child who cannot choose the legal arrangements governing his life.

When Anna tries to appear in public at the theatre, the response is openly hostile. Society that once welcomed her now makes a spectacle of exclusion. Vronsky is furious at the insult, and Anna is wounded. But he also cannot fully experience the risk of being a woman publicly marked as outside respectable marriage. He retains forms of masculine mobility and professional possibility she does not. Their quarrels increasingly emerge from this asymmetry, even when the immediate subject seems to be a particular outing or conversation.

Karenin has his own difficulties. His household has become a place where public reputation, religious counsel, administrative pressure, and private hurt interact. Countess Lydia Ivanovna takes an active role in interpreting what has happened, and Karenin becomes more susceptible to the guidance of people who claim moral certainty. We should not flatten him into a villain or a saint. His forgiveness during Anna's illness was genuine, and his subsequent decisions can still be harsh. Tolstoy's characterization insists that a moral action in one moment does not make all later actions morally correct.

Back with Levin, Kitty's pregnancy draws the couple toward a future he cannot fully picture. Levin is often better at abstract thinking than at living peacefully with uncertainty. His anxieties about work, death, and faith remain even as his marriage brings real joy. Kitty's presence offers neither a magical cure nor a minor decorative part in his education. She makes decisions, directs care, and protects the household in practical ways he does not always understand.

At the end of this movement the parallel plots have not become a simple moral equation in which a legitimate marriage is always happy and an illicit relationship always miserable. Levin and Kitty quarrel despite their public approval; Anna and Vronsky feel affection despite society's condemnation. The difference lies in the resources available to them and in how their relationships change under pressure. The novel continues to ask what happens when private desire, legal status, bodily dependence, and the opinions of others compete for authority over a person's life.

## Anna Karenina — Story: Estates, Jealousy, and Two Households

Life in the countryside might seem far from Anna's exposed position in fashionable society, but Tolstoy makes the two worlds part of one system. Levin and Kitty are establishing a household while Anna and Vronsky have settled at his country estate. Both homes contain affection, work, guests, servants, money, and the small frictions of intimacy. The differences become clearer when we ask who belongs to the household in law, who can make decisions, and who can imagine a future without asking permission from an estranged spouse.

Levin's estate is full of concrete problems. There are fields to supervise, books to read, hunting trips to organize, and relationships with workers that do not conform to his theories. His attempts to improve agriculture remain vulnerable to the opinions and interests of people who do not see him as a philosopher. Kitty's presence, along with visiting relatives and friends, changes the atmosphere. Levin can grow jealous or irritated even in the marriage he so long desired. An encounter with an excessively attentive guest unsettles him; his response reveals that moral seriousness does not free him from possessiveness.

Kitty's marriage is also affected by her pregnant body and by the demands of the extended family. Tolstoy describes households as systems of work, not isolated pairs exchanging declarations of love. Women manage illness, meals, visits, children, and the atmosphere of rooms where men may discuss history or agriculture. The value of this labor is visible even when the people benefiting from it do not fully recognize its cost. Levin's ideal of a true family life must become more practical if it is to deserve that name.

Dolly visits Anna and Vronsky. She is weary of Stiva's infidelity and the burdens of a crowded home. Anna's household initially appears attractive: beautifully appointed rooms, leisure, attentive servants, and a confident lover who appears willing to supply material comfort. Yet Dolly soon observes strain beneath the display. Anna lacks the security that marriage would confer, and her dependence on Vronsky is growing. She cannot simply compare the advantages of two domestic arrangements as though they were freely available consumer choices.

The problem of divorce remains unresolved. Anna would like a legally recognized future, but the process is controlled by Karenin and by religious and social constraints that do not operate equally upon both spouses. Her daughter is with her, while her son remains in Karenin's household. Vronsky has ambitions for his estate, local political affairs, and his public standing. Anna is not incapable of social thought; she simply confronts a shrinking range of actions that society will tolerate. In the same rooms they can disagree over what it means to be free.

Vronsky's estate provides a stage for comparison with Levin. Vronsky brings money, technical order, and managerial assurance to his property. Levin tends to be more troubled by the labor system's inner contradictions. Neither man's estate is outside class power. Their land and income depend on social arrangements that permit owners to make decisions and workers to bear their consequences. Tolstoy presents both men sympathetically in places without requiring readers to accept their economic position as natural or just.

When Dolly returns to her own life, she has not found a simple answer to the problems of marriage. She can feel solidarity with Anna, judge parts of her conduct, and recognize the burdens of her own household. This complex perspective is valuable because the novel can otherwise seem divided into a condemned woman and a morally approved landowner. Dolly sees dimensions of both lives that their protagonists overlook. Her exhaustion may make a luxurious house look desirable, but her observation allows her to notice how little control Anna actually possesses.

Toward the end of this phase Anna becomes more suspicious of Vronsky's absences and interests, while he grows impatient with constant examination of his loyalty. Neither can make the other feel secure by one more explanation. The problem is not simply a defect of temperament. Anna's precarious legal and social position has made the relationship carry an impossible weight. Vronsky's continued freedom becomes, for her, a reminder that he could imagine a life after the affair more easily than she could.

The two households therefore offer neither a perfect ideal nor a perfect cautionary tale. Levin and Kitty must learn how to sustain everyday commitments amid irritability and work. Anna and Vronsky must confront a society that recognizes his status more readily than hers. The central question is how people can create a stable life when their desires are intense but their responsibilities and freedoms are so unevenly distributed.

## Anna Karenina — Story: The Railway and Anna's Death

In the seventh part of Anna Karenina, the two main plots approach events that neither household can fully control. Kitty gives birth to a son, Mitya. Levin, who has spent years thinking about what human life ought to mean, finds that the immediate fear and astonishment of childbirth do not fit his theories. He has difficulty recognizing the change in his own feelings. The child is vulnerable, and affection becomes inseparable from responsibility for a person who cannot speak or protect himself.

Levin also confronts the attractions and irritations of Moscow society. His encounter with Anna is important because the parallel plots briefly touch directly. He finds her intelligent, engaging, and deeply human, not the abstraction constructed by gossip. He is moved by her presence. Kitty is troubled by his response, and their marriage experiences another moment of jealousy. The scene refuses to let Anna exist solely as a scandal in someone else's moral imagination. It also shows that sympathy and attraction can themselves cause tension in a household committed to honesty.

Anna's life with Vronsky has become more fragile. She feels abandoned when he is away and reads ordinary arrangements as signs that love is departing. He is frustrated by the intensity of her demands and resents feeling watched. Their quarrels repeat, each one carrying the memory of earlier wounds. Anna has fewer routes toward an independent social position, a secure marriage, or reunion with Seryozha. Vronsky's relatives and acquaintances appear to her as potential agents of separation. Her suspicions are part of her experience, but the novel does not establish every feared betrayal as an actual event.

Tolstoy gives sustained attention to Anna's movements of thought. Her interpretations can change within a moment; fear turns a neutral remark into an insult, and an insult becomes evidence of an entire future. We are drawn close enough to understand her anguish without being required to accept all her conclusions. Her emotional life cannot be reduced to a medical diagnosis supplied from outside the text. Nor is the disaster adequately explained by calling her selfish. The narration shows an increasingly constricted life in which fear, social exclusion, and relationships damaged by conflict amplify one another.

She and Vronsky separate after a quarrel. Anna travels through the city, watching strangers and connecting passing sights with her own suffering. The railway, present at her first meeting with Vronsky, returns. She remembers the worker killed at the station and experiences thoughts of revenge, escape, and the wish to make the pain stop. At the station she dies by suicide beneath a train. The novel presents a devastating event, not a heroic liberation or a moral verdict proving that she deserved punishment. Her final perceptions move rapidly, and the narrative refuses to make her death a simple answer to the contradictions that preceded it.

The consequences reach people who survive her. Vronsky loses the woman he loved and with whom he had built a life, however damaged that life had become. Karenin remains involved in the future of the children, including the daughter born during the affair. Stiva and Dolly's world continues, but the scandal that fashionable society tried to contain has become a loss no social rule can undo. A family system cannot be judged only by whether its members preserve appearances. Its hidden harms appear in the people who must continue living afterward.

The contrast with Levin's experience is powerful but not a simple reward for conventional behavior. He has been given a child and a chance to build a family, yet he still faces anxiety about death, faith, and the proper use of his life. Anna has died, but the novel's question about how to live has not been answered. The eighth part will continue after her death and examine what people do with pain, public conviction, ordinary commitments, and the need for meaning.

If you are listening to this story for the first time, keep three distinctions in mind. Anna's fear that Vronsky is abandoning her is not identical to a proven fact about every action he takes. The inequality of the social rules surrounding her does not make every choice she makes harmless. And the horror of her ending does not provide a license to treat suffering as a punishment imposed by the novel's author. Tolstoy places us inside a consciousness that is losing room to imagine alternatives, while showing that life and moral inquiry continue beyond the event.

## Anna Karenina — Story: Part Eight and Life After Loss

Anna Karenina does not end at the railway station. Its eighth part is not an optional appendage to the title character's fate. Tolstoy returns to those who live after her death and to questions about public duty, grief, faith, and daily responsibility. The movement from intimate tragedy to political argument can be startling. It is also one of the book's central acts of composition: the need to live meaningfully cannot be settled by one person's death.

Vronsky is devastated. The confidence and ambition that once made him a commanding figure now appear emptied of purpose. He joins a group of Russian volunteers traveling to support the Serbian cause in the conflict with the Ottoman Empire. There is personal despair in the decision and a political movement larger than him. Tolstoy does not give us simple access to Vronsky's future or present enlistment as moral redemption. The choice may contain courage, but it also arises from a life the character no longer knows how to inhabit.

Stiva continues to move through the society of connections and positions, while Dolly continues her life with the children and all its demands. Their household does not become a happy reward for remaining together. Anna's death has exposed a system in which men and women are judged and protected differently, and the system persists. Karenin is still responsible for the children bound to him by marriage and by the affair's aftermath. The narrative's return to ordinary arrangements is not a declaration that the harm has been repaired.

Levin, meanwhile, has a wife and young son, a working estate, and a mind still troubled by the possibility that life lacks a lasting reason. His marriage has not extinguished the fear of death. He has thought about suicide, and the force of those thoughts is serious even as he continues managing the household and responding to his family. The novel does not portray philosophical uncertainty as a mark of superior intelligence that exempts one from ordinary care. Levin's intellect is both an instrument of inquiry and a source of distress when it attempts to solve the whole problem of existence at once.

A conversation about a peasant's goodness gives Levin a different way to understand moral life. He hears an ordinary distinction between living for oneself and living for God, or for what is right. The insight does not arrive as a philosophical proof of every religious doctrine. It is a recognition that the obligations he already feels toward others need not wait for an absolute theoretical foundation. He can care for people, acknowledge weakness, and act without first possessing an exhaustive account of the universe.

The novel tests the insight immediately. Levin still grows irritated; family life still contains disagreements. A frightening summer storm threatens Kitty and the child, and his love becomes urgent in the face of possible loss. Later his public opinions about the Serbian volunteers do not automatically agree with those expressed by people around him. He questions the confident claim that an entire people has one voice and one political will. Tolstoy's argument about Russian public feeling belongs to a specific historical context and remains an argument, not a neutral measurement of popular opinion.

Levin's final recognition is therefore modest in one sense and enormous in another. He does not become permanently calm or perfectly selfless. He realizes that each day still allows him to make moral choices, and that the meaning of a life may reside in those choices rather than in a theory that eliminates doubt. The closing movement returns us to ordinary people, familiar rooms, and repeated acts of kindness or failure. The novel offers not an escape from imperfection but a reason to continue acting in its presence.

The contrast with Anna's death is not a formula that says marriage produces truth and passion produces destruction. Anna faced severe inequalities and made consequential decisions; Levin has privileges and makes mistakes of his own. Their stories share a concern with what other people are owed. One character's life becomes tragically constricted; another finds a way to continue without solving every question. The novel asks us to examine both under the same demand for careful moral attention.

Part Eight was not printed in the same journal sequence as the first seven parts. Tolstoy published it separately after disagreement with the journal's editor over its political discussion; the full novel appeared in book form in 1878. That publication history matters because it reminds us that the ending's argument was not an incidental afterthought. It was significant enough to become the site of disagreement about what a novel should say in public.

A listener who has followed all eight parts can now see why the title does not confine the book to a single life. Anna's story gives it tragic force. Levin's ending refuses to let tragedy become the only possible meaning of human existence. Between them are children, workers, spouses, parents, and strangers whose lives are shaped by the same economic and social arrangements. Tolstoy's final claim is not that one answer explains them all. It is that we must keep attending to them even when our explanations remain incomplete.
