# Diagnose deterioration and choose compatible repairs

## Crack Patterns and Repair Diagnosis

A line appears in a basement wall. You can fill it, photograph it, ignore it, or investigate it. Only one of those choices tells you much about why it exists. Concrete and masonry crack for different reasons: shrinkage during curing or drying, thermal movement, settlement, load-induced bending, corrosion of embedded steel, freeze-thaw damage, and restrained movement between unlike materials. The same visible width can represent different risks in different structures.

Cracking does not mean all concrete has failed. Cementitious materials can be strong in compression and relatively weak in tension. Reinforced concrete is designed with that distinction in mind, and some cracks in tension zones may be expected under service loads. But a crack can also provide a route for water and chlorides to reach reinforcement. Structural adequacy and long-term durability are separate questions. A noncritical crack can still accelerate damage if it repeatedly admits water into a freeze-prone surface.

### Start with geometry

Before naming a cause, map the crack. Is it vertical, horizontal, diagonal, stepped through mortar joints, or branching? Is it widest at one end? Does it pass through bricks or follow the mortar? Is there displacement across the crack, not merely separation? Does it continue around a corner or through several stories? Are there matching symptoms in doors, windows, floors, and adjacent surfaces?

A stair-step crack in masonry can reflect differential movement, but it cannot identify the cause on its own. A diagonal crack near an opening may follow stress concentrations or movement in the supported wall. A horizontal crack in a retaining basement wall can be associated with lateral pressure and bending, which is a more serious hypothesis than harmless surface shrinkage. Those patterns help direct attention; they are not stand-alone structural diagnoses.

Cracks also have an age. Drying shrinkage may create early cracks that later remain relatively stable. Seasonal temperature and moisture changes can make others open and close. Progressive displacement is different from a stable cosmetic line. A sound investigation asks whether the defect is active and over what interval, but avoids pretending that a few days of no visible change proves permanent stability.

### Watch where water goes

Concrete's surface is a record of exposure. Efflorescence, rust-colored staining, damp edges, spalling, and freeze-damaged faces offer clues about water and dissolved salts. Efflorescence is material deposited when moisture moves through porous masonry and evaporates at a surface. It does not prove which opening admitted the water. Corrosion around embedded reinforcement can create expansive products, cracking or detaching surrounding concrete. A cosmetic patch over active corrosion may conceal accelerating damage.

A crack that receives water under pressure poses a different repair problem from a narrow, dry crack in a nonstructural slab. Some repairs attempt to restore structural continuity; others manage water; still others accommodate ongoing movement with flexible materials. Using a rigid filler where a joint is meant to move can simply cause the adjacent material to crack again. Injection into a crack caused by foundation movement does not, by itself, stabilize the foundation.

### Compare structural and material mechanisms

Consider two patios. One has a fine network of shallow surface cracks appearing shortly after placement in hot, dry weather. The other has a slab edge displaced vertically after several winters. The first suggests early-age drying or plastic shrinkage as candidates; the second requires investigation of base support, drainage, frost, and settlement. Both are 'cracked concrete,' but their mechanisms, consequences, and repair choices differ profoundly.

Now consider two basement walls. The first has a fine vertical line without measurable displacement and no water entry. The second bows inward, has a horizontal crack, and follows a period of saturated soil or altered drainage. The second requires prompt structural assessment, irrespective of whether the crack happens to look narrow in a photograph. Visible shape and loading conditions carry information that a single crack-width number does not.

Mortar and units complicate masonry diagnosis. A weak or eroded joint may shed material without a large crack. Hard repointing mortar in older, softer masonry can alter moisture transport or transfer stresses to the units. Repair materials must be compatible with the original assembly. A strong-looking patch is not necessarily a durable or appropriate one.

### A method for observation

Make an annotated drawing and take photographs with scale, location, orientation, and date. Record weather, drainage alterations, freeze-thaw exposure, adjacent excavation, load changes, and any water transmission. If measuring crack width, repeat at the same marked locations with a stated method. Note displacement and wall shape separately. Distinguish a surface finish crack from one passing through structural material. Where signs indicate active structural movement, do not wait for months of data before seeking qualified evaluation.

The American Concrete Institute explicitly treats the significance of cracks as dependent on the structure, environment, and cause. That is more useful than universal sayings like 'a crack under a certain width is fine.' A crack in a water-retaining tank or a salt-exposed reinforced slab has different consequences from one in an interior non-load-bearing finish.

The lesson is to read a crack as a trace of stress, shrinkage, chemical or moisture action, or movement. First determine what sort of failure process may be occurring. Only then can a repair be selected that addresses the process rather than painting over its record.

## Repointing, Patching, and Material Compatibility

A crack pattern is evidence for choosing a repair mechanism, not simply a shape to fill.

A brick wall develops eroded mortar joints. Someone removes loose material and packs the joints with a strong modern mortar. At first the wall looks renewed. Years later, the mortar is intact but the brick faces begin to break away. The repair did not make the wall stronger in the practical sense. It changed the mechanical and moisture behavior of an assembly whose materials had previously worked together.

Masonry is composite construction. Brick, stone, or concrete masonry units transfer forces across joints and resist exposure with their own particular porosity, strength, and stiffness. Mortar fills irregularities, bonds units, and provides a path for transferring compression and some shear. Depending on the masonry type, it may also be the more replaceable material in a weathering system. When repairs introduce a material with very different stiffness, permeability, or thermal behavior, the joint between old and new becomes a problem.

### What damage is actually telling us

Repointing means removing deteriorated mortar from an existing joint to an appropriate depth and installing compatible replacement mortar. It is different from replacing damaged units, restoring a failed lintel, stabilizing a moving wall, or repairing the source of water penetration. If a downspout discharges directly onto the wall, new mortar is unlikely to compensate for the continued wetting. If the wall is moving, filling a widening crack does not stop movement.

Start by reading the damage pattern. Are mortar joints recessed and sandy while bricks remain sound? Are brick faces spalling near a leaking sill? Is staining limited to a location below failed flashing? Is the crack diagonal through multiple units, or largely confined to a mortar line? Salt deposits, movement, heat, and repeated freeze-thaw cycles can produce different appearances and require different responses. A crack filled without a diagnosis can become a very neat marker of continuing failure.

Water transport is central. Porous brick and mortar can absorb moisture by capillarity. Drying may occur outward or inward depending on weather and assembly. In traditional soft-brick masonry, a hard, dense replacement mortar can concentrate stress in the softer unit and change where moisture exits. That is one reason preservation guidance emphasizes matching mortar to the **properties** of original masonry, not merely selecting the strongest mix available at a supply store.

### Why compatibility has several dimensions

Compressive strength matters, but so do elastic stiffness, bond behavior, permeability, shrinkage, thermal expansion, and workability. Mortar that is too weak for a modern structural application can be problematic; mortar that is too strong for a historic soft unit can be damaging. Neither 'always use lime' nor 'always use Portland cement' is a universal rule. The existing masonry, exposure, structural role, and original materials govern the choice.

Color is also not enough. Two mortar mixes can dry to similar colors while having different sands, binders, pore structures, and stiffnesses. A repair specification may require examining sound original mortar and masonry units, testing representative samples, and making a trial panel. The trial panel reveals joint profile, color, shrinkage, and surface appearance before the entire facade is changed.

### The joint has geometry

Preparing a joint requires removing unsound material without chipping the edges of the adjacent brick or stone. Aggressive grinding can widen joints and scar historic units. Joint depth, cleaning, moisture conditioning, placement pressure, and curing depend on the material system. A replacement mortar must be packed into a sound joint, not spread like cosmetic putty over loose material. Thin smears may detach because they lack mechanical engagement and cure poorly.

Fresh mortar needs conditions in which its binder can develop the intended structure. Rapid drying, freezing, and incompatible additives can interfere. Portland-cement-rich systems and lime-based systems have different setting and curing processes. The plan must be appropriate for the actual material, season, and exposure. Protective coverings and moisture management during curing are functional work, not optional decoration.

### Patches are interfaces

A chipped concrete edge, a detached stone face, and a decayed mortar joint all look like places to add material. But a patch makes a new interface between materials of different ages, stiffnesses, moisture histories, and surface conditions. For structural concrete, bond, corrosion of embedded reinforcement, movement joints, and crack causes matter. A patch that traps water against steel or conceals an active corrosion cell may have a short life even if it appears to adhere.

Compare two repair proposals. The first says, 'Remove the visible loose mortar and apply a high-strength patch.' The second documents moisture sources, unit condition, joint depth, proposed mortar compatibility, sample work, curing requirements, and acceptance checks. The second offers testable reasons that its methods should preserve the wall's function. That is what a durable repair plan should do.

Repointing is a useful example of a broader conservation principle: an intervention must be compatible with what remains. The goal is not to win a strength contest against old masonry. It is to restore a working assembly while preserving the conditions that allow it to bear loads, manage moisture, and age without sacrificing its weaker components.

## Concrete Repairs, Spalling, and Corrosion

A slab has lost a patch of concrete at its edge. A reinforcing bar is visible beneath. Someone proposes filling the hole with a bag of fresh patch material. That might restore the outline of the slab, but it might not restore its durability. The missing piece is the visible consequence of a process that began inside the concrete long before it broke away. Good repair asks why the concrete separated, whether the cause remains active, and what the new material must do after installation.

### Concrete is not simply a stone that does not move

Concrete is a composite of cement paste, aggregates, pores, and often reinforcement. It is strong in compression relative to its tensile strength. Reinforcing steel adds tensile and ductile capacity when designed and detailed correctly. Concrete also shrinks as it dries and changes with temperature; restrained deformation can produce cracks. Water moves through connected pores and cracks. Salts and gases can change the chemistry around the reinforcement over time.

A spall is a fragment or area of concrete that has broken away. Delamination describes a separation inside concrete that may remain under a seemingly intact surface. A hollow sound on suitable inspection can suggest delamination, but sounding alone does not identify the chemical cause or establish load-carrying capacity. Surface scaling, a thin flake from freeze-thaw damage, is not the same as a deep spall exposing reinforcement.

### Why reinforcing steel can turn from an ally into a source of stress

Steel in sound, sufficiently alkaline concrete is ordinarily protected by a passive surface film. That protection can be compromised, particularly by chloride ingress or loss of alkalinity through carbonation under suitable exposure conditions. When steel corrodes, its products occupy more volume than the original metal. Pressure develops around the bar, cracking and separating the surrounding concrete cover. The new cracks allow additional water and salts to enter, so deterioration can accelerate.

Rust visible on a bar does not tell us exactly how much load capacity remains. What matters includes actual loss of steel section, bond, anchorage, crack extent, concrete strength, and the member's structural function. A rust-stained spot on an exterior step is a different risk from longitudinal cracking and delamination along reinforcement in a suspended balcony. Both need diagnosis; the second may require immediate structural evaluation.

A patch placed over chloride-contaminated concrete does not necessarily stop corrosion in adjacent material. Different electrochemical conditions between the repaired area and the surrounding concrete can concentrate corrosion at the boundary. This is sometimes described as an incipient-anode or ring-anode effect. Repair planning may require evaluating the surrounding substrate and reinforcement rather than only replacing what already fell away.

### Water and freeze-thaw create another path to damage

When concrete becomes sufficiently saturated and repeatedly freezes and thaws, water expansion and hydraulic pressures can damage its pore structure. Proper air entrainment in freeze-thaw-exposed concrete creates small voids that help relieve pressure. Exposure to deicing chemicals can add chemical deterioration, not only mechanical freeze damage. Poor drainage and standing water often amplify the problem.

Imagine a walkway at the end of a downspout. Each winter it becomes soaked and receives deicing salt. Thin surface scaling appears across much of the concrete. Patching isolated flakes without redirecting the water does not remove the exposure. On a reinforced exterior stair landing, a different pattern may appear: a linear crack following steel, followed by larger spalls. The two surfaces may need different material investigation and repair design even though both are called damaged concrete.

### Good repair begins at the boundary of sound material

A durable repair requires a suitable substrate. Loose concrete, contamination, and weak interface material reduce the ability of a patch to bond or transfer stress. Proper preparation can involve removing unsound material, cleaning or replacing reinforcement as justified by design, establishing a geometry that can hold the repair material, and controlling moisture and curing conditions. Manufacturer instructions matter because cementitious mortars, polymer-modified materials, epoxy systems, and other repairs require different surfaces, thicknesses, and temperature ranges.

The repair must also be compatible with the existing member. A very stiff patch against moving or cracked substrate can transfer stress and crack again. A thin patch at a feather edge may fail if the product is not formulated for it. Shrinkage, thermal expansion, vapor and water exposure, bond strength, and finish slip resistance may all matter. A beautiful smooth patch is not proof that reinforcing corrosion or the original water path has been addressed.

Sometimes repair is the wrong first step. A slab carrying a vehicle, supporting a balcony, or acting as a retaining element may have structural deterioration that cannot be judged from a photograph. Significant section loss, expanding cracks, displaced supports, or uncertain reinforcement require qualified assessment. A cosmetic patch can conceal a worsening structural condition, making future diagnosis harder.

### Build an investigation, not a shopping list

Start with the location and pattern. Is the damage isolated at a salt-exposed joint? Is it close to an exposed steel bar? Is the concrete scaling across a broad surface, cracked in one direction, or separating from an edge? Is water accumulating? Are temperatures and deicing practices relevant? Does the deterioration progress from year to year? Photographs with scale and dated notes establish whether a process is active.

Next ask what the repaired surface must do. Support people or vehicles? Remain watertight? Protect reinforcement? Tolerate freeze-thaw cycles? Resist abrasion? The objective determines material and preparation requirements. A patch that survives a dry indoor floor might fail quickly on a saturated exterior ledge.

Concrete repair is often treated as replacing a missing volume. A more useful view is that the original concrete was a protective and structural system. Something broke that system. The repair must restore the necessary functions while changing the exposure or chemistry that caused the damage. Filling the cavity is only one part of that work.

## Historic Stone Masonry, Mortar, and Repair Compatibility

A nineteenth-century stone wall has recessed, powdery mortar joints. Someone fills them with a very hard modern cement mortar because stronger sounds better. Several seasons later, the mortar remains hard, but edges of the softer stone begin to break away. The repair may have shifted the damage from a replaceable joint to the masonry units themselves.

Historic masonry asks us to think about assemblies rather than individual materials. A stone or brick wall may have soft units, multiple wythes, voids, rubble infill, and mortar that differs significantly from current structural mortars. Its drainage, load path, and drying behavior may have evolved over decades. A compatible repair should address why deterioration occurred and how the original assembly distributes stresses and moisture.

### Mortar is more than glue

Mortar fills irregularities, bonds units, and helps transmit compressive forces. Its strength, stiffness, permeability, moisture retention, shrinkage, and bonding behavior influence how the wall responds to movement and weather. In many historic assemblies, the mortar was comparatively compliant and repairable. A joint that weathers and can be repointed may help preserve the adjacent stone. Replacing it with a stiffer, less permeable material can concentrate stresses or modify where moisture evaporates. The hard repair can outlast the softer units while accelerating their loss.

This does not mean all old mortar is lime-only or that a particular historic recipe fits every building. Natural cement, early Portland cement, lime, sand and local aggregates have all been used in different periods and circumstances. Color alone is not a reliable analysis. Mortar selection should consider the actual masonry units, surviving joints, exposure, and structural conditions. The National Park Service's Preservation Brief on repointing emphasizes material and workmanship compatibility rather than prescribing maximum compressive strength.

### Find the water before replacing the joint

Stone can be durable while joints deteriorate from repeated wetting, soluble salts, freeze-thaw cycling, vegetation, or movement. A wall might become wet because gutters discharge onto it, soil bridges an intended damp barrier, or a later hard coating traps moisture. Look for concentration of damage near grade, roof edges, window sills, and runoff paths. Salt deposits can mark evaporation locations but do not prove a single source or chemical mechanism.

A useful investigation distinguishes three kinds of evidence. Surface loss might indicate weathering or salt action. A crack that continues through multiple units may indicate movement or structural distress. A joint gap may simply indicate loss of mortar while units remain sound. These observations require different responses. Repointing a moving wall without investigating its support and loading is a cosmetic intervention, not a structural solution.

### Why preparation controls the repair

Removing old mortar is not a contest to create the deepest or widest groove. Aggressive power tools can damage the edges of historic units, especially soft brick and irregular stone. Existing sound mortar should not be destroyed merely to achieve uniform appearance. Where repointing is appropriate, the joint is prepared to the specified geometry without breaking units, cleaned appropriately, and packed with compatible fresh mortar using methods suited to its composition. Curing and protection against rapid drying, rain, and freezing matter to the resulting bond and durability.

The color and aggregate texture also matter for historic appearance, but they are not only aesthetic. Different sands alter handling and can influence water demand and hardened properties. Changing one ingredient to match color while ignoring mechanical and moisture behavior can produce a visually convincing but harmful joint.

### Not all cracks are mortar problems

Consider a stone foundation where one diagonal crack widens year by year and a nearby opening becomes harder to close. The pattern warrants assessment of movement: soil, loads, water, settlement, or lateral pressure may be involved. Filling the crack does not remove those forces. By contrast, a shallow, stable loss of mortar in a sheltered area may need compatible repointing after the source of deterioration is addressed.

Historic buildings often contain hidden construction details and have cultural value beyond their structural function. Before grinding, dismantling, coating, or injecting unfamiliar materials, document the existing condition. Photographs, measured crack maps, representative sound joints, and records of earlier repairs help establish what should be preserved. A conservation specialist may be needed to specify compatible mortars or assess structural movement.

### Evaluate proposed repairs by their failure mode

Ask a contractor what the proposed mortar is intended to do. Will it remain sacrificial relative to the masonry? How will it manage wetting and evaporation? What are the preparation tools and trial areas? How will the finished joint be cured? What previous repairs, if any, will be removed, and why? A good answer connects material selection to observed wall behavior rather than promising “the hardest joint possible.”

The guiding principle is restraint informed by mechanics. Make the smallest intervention that addresses the demonstrated problem, use materials that work with the original assembly, and preserve evidence and historic fabric where possible. Durability is not measured by how long the new mortar survives alone. It is measured by whether the wall as a whole remains sound.

## Salt Damage, Efflorescence, and Masonry Maintenance Plans

White powder appears on an old brick wall every spring. A person washes it off, and a few weeks later the powder returns. The wall is not necessarily producing new material. Water is transporting dissolved salts through pores, and evaporation is concentrating them at or near the exposed surface. The recurring white mark is a clue about moisture movement.

Masonry is a porous system. Brick, mortar, stone, and concrete vary greatly in pore structure, capillary behavior, strength, and the way they tolerate cycles of wetting and drying. Soluble salts can originate in raw materials, groundwater, deicing products, incompatible repairs, or adjacent construction. As water moves, it carries some dissolved material. When the water evaporates, that material can crystallize.

### Surface deposits versus internal damage

Efflorescence is visible crystalline salt deposited on a surface. It can be mostly an appearance issue, but it tells us that both water and soluble salts are present. Subflorescence describes crystallization within pores. Crystals growing in constrained spaces can exert pressure that damages masonry or mortar. Repeated cycles may produce powdering, flaking, and loss of surface material. It is therefore important to distinguish a removable deposit from a wall whose faces are gradually crumbling.

Imagine two walls exposed to the same splash from a sidewalk. One shows white deposits but remains mechanically sound. The other develops flaking near the bottom course. A cleaning contractor may make the first wall look better, but cleaning alone will not stop the moisture. On the second wall, aggressive cleaning could remove already weakened faces and accelerate deterioration. The source of wetting and the condition of the masonry should determine the repair plan.

### Trace how water enters and leaves

Start at the top of the assembly. Are roof runoff and gutter discharge directed away? Are parapet caps, window sills, and coping stones shedding water? Does pavement slope toward the building? Is a wet retaining wall pressed against saturated soil without adequate drainage? A salt band at a consistent elevation may reflect capillary rise from the ground, splash, or a changing evaporation zone. It does not prove which source is dominant.

Season adds evidence. Deicing salts may arrive during winter and become visible after drying. Groundwater or rising damp can sustain deposits through seasons with little rain. A defective downspout may produce localized staining after storms. Document location and timing, preferably with photographs taken from the same vantage point. Compare unaffected sections made of the same material. The pattern matters more than a single white patch.

Moisture can enter brick masonry through cracks, porous units, missing mortar, and failed flashings. Older solid walls often rely on different drying behavior than modern cavity walls. A modern brick veneer should have a designed drainage cavity and paths for water to exit. If an incompatible impermeable coating or dense repair mortar is added, evaporation may shift into softer brick faces rather than out through mortar joints. The visible damage can move even when the total water entry stays the same.

### Why cleaning and coating sometimes fail

Dry brushing or a carefully selected cleaning method may be appropriate for some deposits, after the underlying moisture issue is addressed. Strong acid or high-pressure water can damage some brick and mortar, alter colors, introduce additional moisture, or leave residues. More aggressive chemistry is not automatically more effective. A patch test on a representative area, manufacturer's guidance, and consideration of historic material are essential before cleaning.

Similarly, sealing a damp wall can trap moisture or redirect it toward another evaporation surface. Some water-repellent treatments reduce rain absorption while allowing vapor movement, but their compatibility and service life vary. A wall that is already wet from the ground cannot be fixed merely by changing the appearance of its outward face. Identify the supply of water before choosing any surface treatment.

Repairs to historic masonry often require mortar that is compatible in hardness, permeability, and movement. Repointing with a much harder mortar can make the surrounding brick become the sacrificial part of the system. That is not a universal prohibition on high-strength materials: it is a reminder that different wall types and periods require different performance relationships.

### Build a maintenance plan from observations

The plan should identify sources, not just symptoms. First inspect drainage, flashing, grade, downspouts, joints, and cracks. Note which observations coincide with rainfall, freezing, or deicing. Second, document the material and extent of deterioration, including any structural movement. Third, prioritize preventing liquid-water entry and providing credible drying paths. Finally, choose compatible cleaning or repair methods and establish a schedule to observe recurrence.

The outcome of a good maintenance program is not a wall that has been aggressively washed once. It is a wall whose moisture mechanisms are understood, whose damage is not progressing, and whose interventions can be evaluated after the next wet season. A white deposit has told us that water traveled somewhere. The durable repair starts by finding the route.

## Moisture, Freeze-Thaw, and Surface Protection

Moisture exposure and freezing can continue to act after a surface has been repaired, so durability remains part of the diagnosis.

A concrete step looks sound in autumn. By spring, thin flakes have come off its top surface, and the edge has broken into small pieces. It is tempting to blame the cold. Cold matters, but cold alone is not enough to explain the damage. The more informative question is how much water was in the material, where it could move, and what happened as temperatures crossed freezing.

### Water in pores is part of the material's behavior

Concrete, mortar, brick, and natural stone are not solid blocks without internal space. They contain pores and connected microcracks of different sizes. Water can enter from rain, soil, standing snowmelt, capillary uptake, or a leak behind the assembly. Some of it can evaporate outward; some may stay because drainage is poor or a coating restricts drying. Different masonry units have very different pore structures and absorption behavior.

Water expands when it freezes, but 'ice expands nine percent' is not a complete explanation of masonry failure. The interaction of pore size, degree of saturation, ice formation, hydraulic pressure, and water migration determines the stresses. The material's tensile resistance is limited even though it may carry compression well. Repeated freezing and thawing can grow cracks, scale surfaces, and eventually break corners. A dry or well-drained piece often survives exposure that damages a saturated neighbor.

Concrete intended for freezing exposure may use an air-entraining admixture. Tiny, deliberately distributed air voids help accommodate movement of water and ice-related pressures. Their volume and spacing matter; an arbitrary large bubble from poor placement is not the same as an engineered air-void system. Mixture proportions, curing, finishing, aggregate quality, and exposure conditions also influence durability. A surface that was overworked or finished while bleed water remained can have a weak near-surface layer even when the deeper slab is sound.

### Salt changes the exposure

Deicing salts create another set of effects. Salt solutions can alter freezing behavior, keep surfaces wet under some conditions, and contribute to scaling of vulnerable concrete. Salts also travel into porous masonry with moisture and can crystallize as the water evaporates, producing pressure or surface deposits. On steel-reinforced concrete, chloride exposure can help initiate or accelerate corrosion when protective conditions are lost. Corroding steel occupies more volume than the original metal, which can crack and spall its surrounding concrete.

It is important to distinguish these processes. A white deposit on brick might be efflorescence, evidence that water moved through the masonry and left salts behind. It does not automatically prove the brick is structurally failing. Rust staining and delamination near reinforcing steel suggest a different problem. A shallow flaking concrete surface may reflect finishing and freeze exposure. Each pattern directs the investigation toward a different source and repair.

### Protection begins by controlling the water path

Before selecting a water repellent, ask where water enters and how it leaves. A step with a flat, ponding top and poorly drained soil beside it has a persistent wetting problem. Coating the surface may change which path water takes rather than removing the source. A wall with leaking coping or missing flashing may receive water from above; sealing the visible face while the top remains wet can trap moisture behind the coating.

A vapor-permeable water-repellent treatment may reduce liquid water absorption in some masonry while leaving a drying route. But compatibility varies by substrate, prior coatings, exposure, and historic preservation requirements. Some film-forming coatings create a surface that looks dry while moisture accumulates behind it. The result can be peeling, salt crystallization, or freeze-thaw deterioration displaced to another location. A treatment should be chosen after the cause of wetting is understood and the masonry's condition assessed.

What about patching? A replacement mortar or concrete repair material must be compatible in stiffness, permeability, bond, thermal movement, and exposure. A very hard impermeable patch on soft historic brick can concentrate stress in the original units. A repair that seals a crack without addressing settlement or continuing movement may fracture again. In reinforced concrete, covering rust stains with a cement coating is not a substitute for evaluating the embedded steel and source of corrosion.

### Read patterns, not just damage labels

Imagine three steps on the same entrance. One has shallow flaking only on its upper face. A second has a crack through the body and tilts toward the house. The third has loose surface mortar where a handrail anchor has corroded. All can be described as 'damaged by winter', but their mechanisms may be different. The first invites investigation of saturation, finish and salts. The second raises bearing, foundation movement, and trip hazards. The third raises an anchorage and corrosion concern. The same sack of patching compound is not an appropriate answer to all three.

Useful observations include the time of year when damage progresses, where snowmelt sits, whether water drains away or collects at joints, which surfaces face prevailing rain, whether salts are used, and whether cracks are active. Photograph the same reference locations in dry and wet conditions. Tap or probe only where this can be done without destabilizing loose masonry. If a guard, step, retaining wall, or load-bearing element is compromised, secure the hazard and have the structure evaluated before cosmetic work.

Masonry durability is a combined problem of material, moisture and temperature. The goal of surface protection is not to make every exposed face impermeable. It is to keep bulk water from entering where it should not, provide drainage and drying where necessary, and choose materials that can live together through repeated weather cycles.

##### Crack survey fields

Location; structural role; material and joints; pattern; width and measurement method; displacement; visible bowing; staining or water entry; temperature/weather; installation history; neighboring symptoms; change between dated inspections.

##### Example comparison

A 0.3 mm crack with active chloride-bearing water ingress in reinforced concrete and a 1.0 mm stable crack in a nonstructural interior finish should not be ranked solely by width. Exposure, movement, reinforcement, and function decide the risk assessment.

**Review boundary:** These observations do not establish structural capacity. Active bowing, settlement, displacement or deteriorated load-bearing members require prompt competent structural evaluation; generic crack-filling advice is insufficient.

**Track:** Masonry and Concrete. **Background:** mortar, moisture, cracking, freeze-thaw, and foundations. **Purpose:** understand why the strongest patch is not always the most durable repair.

#### Technical companion (non-spoken)

##### Reading and attribution

Robert C. Mack and John P. Speweik, *Preservation Brief 2: Repointing Mortar Joints in Historic Masonry Buildings*, National Park Service (1998), provides the historic-masonry framework used here. It emphasizes correcting underlying causes before repointing and selecting mortar for compatibility with original units.

##### Repair-review matrix

| Evidence | Decision it informs |
|---|---|
| Spalling brick but hard intact mortar | Investigate incompatibility, freezing, or moisture concentration |
| Joint erosion without displaced units | Assess mortar deterioration and local moisture source |
| Bowing or displaced wall | Structural evaluation, not cosmetic repointing |
| Persistent efflorescence | Trace liquid path and evaporation before surface coating |

**Review boundary:** Mortar analysis, structural assessment, historic-preservation requirements, and respirable-silica controls are project-specific. Avoid damaging original masonry during joint preparation. Do not translate preservation recommendations blindly to reinforced modern concrete or engineered block walls.

#### Technical companion (non-spoken)

##### Distinguish deterioration mechanisms

- Corrosion-driven spalling: chloride or carbonation exposure, passive-film loss, expanding corrosion products, delamination, possible steel section loss.
- Freeze-thaw/scaling: saturation, freezing cycles, pore structure, air entrainment, deicing exposure.
- Other hypotheses: impact, shrinkage, poorly compacted original concrete, chemical attack, structural overload.
- ACI RAP-7 concerns horizontal surface spall repair; the decision to repair structural components and amount of removal require the applicable engineering evaluation.

##### Field evidence table

| Observable clue | What it suggests, but does not prove |
|---|---|
| Linear crack along rebar | Reinforcement corrosion or restraint effects |
| Broad shallow scaling | Freeze-thaw/deicing or surface paste weakness |
| Rust stain | Corrosion products or exterior metal runoff |
| Hollow-sounding surface | Possible delamination requiring evaluation |

##### Critical limitation

Do not infer structural capacity from surface appearance. Repair standards, mix design, cover requirements, chloride assessment, curing, and structural detailing are project-specific. Recorded source excerpts are a map for engineering review, not a substitute for it.

### Historic Stone Masonry, Mortar, and Repair Compatibility

### 012000 — Historic Stone Masonry, Mortar, and Repair Compatibility

#### Technical companion (non-spoken)

##### Condition record

Map joint recession, spalled faces, through-unit cracks, settlement patterns, salts, vegetation, water sources, previous hard patches and coatings. Photograph entire elevations and details with scale; monitor movement when indicated. Record stone type only when established rather than guessed.

##### Compatibility checklist

| Property | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| Compressive strength and stiffness | Load transmission and damage concentration at softer units |
| Vapor/water transport | Where absorbed moisture can evaporate |
| Sand grading and joint profile | Workability, visual match and local durability |
| Cure and protection | Proper hardening, avoidance of washout or freezing |
| Removal technique | Risk of irreversible damage to stone edges |

**Editorial boundary:** Historic mortars should not be specified from a generic mix ratio alone. Site sampling, local conservation requirements and structural review may be necessary. A visually stable wall is not necessarily structurally verified.

### Moisture, Freeze-Thaw, and Surface Protection

### 015000 — Moisture, Freeze-Thaw, and Surface Protection

#### Technical companion (non-spoken)

##### Diagnostic observations

- **Scaling on horizontal surface:** pooled water, deicer history, surface finishing, air-void durability, freeze exposure.
- **Localized corner spall at reinforced element:** cover thickness, crack paths, rust staining, chloride/water supply.
- **Efflorescence:** moisture entry/evaporation paths and soluble salt sources; not proof of structural failure by itself.
- **Coating blistering:** trapped moisture, substrate contamination, incompatible coating permeability.
- **Settlement crack or step rotation:** soil, drainage, foundations, load and movement rather than simple surface weathering.

##### Planning distinction

Assess water shedding, flashing, drainage, substrate type, moisture, movement, and material compatibility *before* specifying a sealer or repair mortar. Historic masonry and reinforced structures need separate specialized review.

**Technical boundary:** No generic sealant or patch detail is appropriate for every masonry assembly; hazard evaluation and structural repair design require field-specific assessment.
