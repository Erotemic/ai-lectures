# Eight Minutes of Arc

Hold your arm out toward a full Moon and imagine dividing its face into four vertical strips. One of those strips is roughly eight minutes of arc wide.

A minute of arc is a sixtieth of a degree. The full Moon spans about half a degree, or thirty minutes. So eight minutes is a small thing: a sliver of sky narrower than anyone, for most of history, could measure the position of a planet.

In 1609, Johannes Kepler wrote that eight minutes like these, by themselves, had led the way to reforming the whole of astronomy.

That sentence is famous, and it usually appears in a particular kind of story. In that story, Kepler is the hero of data. He has a beautiful theory. The observations disagree by a tiny amount. Rather than fudge, he throws the theory away, and modern science is born out of his integrity.

I like Kepler too much to tell the story that way. The real history is stranger and, I think, more useful. Kepler did throw away a theory over eight minutes. He also held onto a different and far less accurate theory for his whole life. Earlier, he had shrugged off a misfit of several percent by blaming the data. He threw away a true law because of a mistake in his own arithmetic, and had to find it again. And his celebrated account of his own errors was, by his own admission, written partly to persuade.

What I want to explore is the question underneath the famous sentence. What does it actually take for a person to be wrong in a way that helps? My answer, which I'll try to earn, is that it is not mainly a personality trait. It is a relation between three things: a claim precise enough to fail, a measurement precise enough to catch it, and a decision not to explain the difference away.

## A beautiful wrong idea

Start in the summer of 1595, in the Austrian city of Graz. Kepler is twenty-three, a mathematics teacher at the Lutheran school there, and a convinced Copernican, which means he believes the Earth and the planets move around the Sun.

On the nineteenth of July, as he later told it, he was showing his class how the great conjunctions of Saturn and Jupiter, the moments when those two planets appear to meet in the sky, step around the zodiac in a regular pattern. He drew the pattern on the board: triangle after triangle inscribed inside a circle. And he noticed that the inscribed triangles enclosed a smaller circle, whose size relative to the outer one looked suspiciously like the ratio between the orbits of Jupiter and Saturn.

He searched for other plane figures that might produce the other orbits, and failed. Then, he wrote, it struck him: why look for plane figures among orbits in three dimensions? That question led him to an idea of real beauty. There are exactly five regular solids, the shapes whose faces are all identical regular polygons: the cube, the tetrahedron, the dodecahedron, the icosahedron, and the octahedron. In Copernicus's system there were exactly six planets. Six planets means five gaps between them. What if the five solids were the reason?

Kepler proposed that the planetary spheres were nested inside one another with the solids between them like spacers. The cube between Saturn and Jupiter. The tetrahedron between Jupiter and Mars. The dodecahedron between Mars and Earth. The icosahedron between Earth and Venus. The octahedron between Venus and Mercury. Fit a sphere snugly around a solid, fit the next solid inside that sphere, and the sizes of the orbits would follow from geometry. The number of planets would follow too, because there could be only five solids.

He described his pleasure in words that the historian Owen Gingerich translated this way: "how intense was my pleasure from this discovery can never be expressed in words." He computed day and night to see whether the idea matched the Copernican orbits, or whether, as he put it, his joy would be carried away by the wind.

It mostly matched. In Gingerich's account, the predicted distances fit within about five percent, except for Jupiter. And here is the detail I want you to remember. Where the numbers did not fit, Kepler quoted a story from Copernicus's student Rheticus: Copernicus had told him that it made no sense to insist on exact agreement with the data, because the data themselves were surely imperfect.

That was not an unreasonable thing to say. The data available to Copernicus really were imperfect. A five percent mismatch, measured against tables built from centuries of uneven observations, could be the fault of the tables. Kepler published the idea in a small book, the *Mysterium Cosmographicum*, the Cosmographic Mystery, whose title page is dated 1596. Gingerich's verdict on it is one of my favorite sentences about the history of science: "Seldom in history has so wrong a book been so seminal in directing the future course of science." Because the next thing Kepler did would eventually undo part of his own way of thinking. He went looking for better data.

## The man with the instruments

The best data in the world belonged to a Danish nobleman named Tycho Brahe.

Tycho had spent two decades observing from his island observatory on Hven, before the telescope was used in astronomy. His instruments had open sights and no lenses. He made them huge, because a bigger arc can be divided into finer marks, and he checked them against one another obsessively. Historians usually describe the result like this: astronomers before him were accurate to perhaps fifteen minutes of arc, while Tycho's observations were accurate to perhaps two minutes, and his best to about half a minute. Later analysis of his records has broadly confirmed Kepler's own belief that Tycho's observations could be trusted to within two minutes.

Tycho also had his own system of the world. He could not accept that the Earth moves. In his arrangement, the Earth sits still at the center, the Sun goes around the Earth, and the other planets go around the Sun. It was a clever compromise that kept much of the Copernican geometry while leaving the Earth at rest.

Kepler arrived to work with Tycho in early February 1600, at Benátky Castle outside Prague. The relationship was difficult. Tycho guarded his observations jealously, and Kepler resented his subordinate position. Mars, whose orbit was giving trouble, had been assigned to another assistant, Longomontanus, but it was eventually turned over to Kepler. According to Gingerich, Kepler bet that he could solve the theory of Mars within a week. Gingerich gives no source for the wager, and I have not been able to find one in Kepler's own writings, so take it as an anecdote. But the outcome is not in doubt. Kepler himself, in the book that resulted, complains that it is now the fifth year since he took on Mars.

In October 1601, Tycho fell suddenly ill and died within eleven days. Kepler later recorded, in Tycho's own observation log, that on his last night, in a delirium, Tycho kept repeating a single line, like someone composing a poem: *Ne frustra vixisse videar.* Let me not seem to have lived in vain. Tycho's dying wish, as historians report it, was that Kepler complete the great tables of planetary motion, and that they be framed according to the Tychonic system.

Within days, Kepler was appointed imperial mathematician in Tycho's place. He now had the observations.

## Two answers, both wrong

What Kepler did next is the core of the story, and the popular version tends to garble it, so let me go slowly.

Astronomy before Kepler assumed that planets moved in circles. To match the fact that planets speed up and slow down, astronomers used a device called the equant: a point, offset from the center of the circle, around which the planet's motion would look uniform. In the classical arrangement, the center of the circle sat exactly halfway between the equant and the Sun or Earth. Astronomers called this bisecting the eccentricity.

Kepler built a circular model for Mars, but he allowed himself a freedom the classical astronomers had not used: he let the equant sit wherever the observations demanded, rather than at the halfway point. Finding the best position took an enormous iterative calculation. He wrote that if the reader found the method tedious, the reader should pity him, because he had gone through it at least seventy times.

The result was superb. Kepler reported that the model held all the remaining observations of Mars at opposition, the times when Mars is opposite the Sun in our sky, within two minutes of arc. That is about the precision of the observations themselves. He called it his vicarious hypothesis: a stand-in, a substitute. That name is a clue that he did not fully trust it.

He was right not to. A model of a planet's motion has to predict more than direction. It has to put the planet at the right distance. When Kepler tested the vicarious hypothesis against other observations, first the latitudes of Mars, its small departures above and below the plane of the Earth's orbit, and then positions away from opposition that depend on distance, the hypothesis failed. It placed the orbit's center in the wrong place.

So he tried the obvious repair. Put the center back halfway, the classical way. Bisect the eccentricity. That fixed the distances. But now the directions went wrong. Near certain points in the orbit, between the extremes, the predicted positions of Mars missed the observations by as much as eight minutes of arc.

So he had two models, each wrong in a different way. One got the direction right and the distance wrong. The other got the distance right and missed the direction by eight minutes.

## What Ptolemy could not see

Then Kepler makes an observation that I think is the deepest sentence in the episode, and it is almost never quoted. He explains why Ptolemy, the great astronomer of the second century, had been satisfied with exactly this kind of model.

Ptolemy, Kepler notes, said openly that his own observations did not go below ten minutes of arc, a sixth of a degree. So for Ptolemy, an eight-minute error was invisible. In Kepler's words, the uncertainty of the observations, their latitude, as he calls it, exceeded the error of the calculation. Ptolemy was not careless. He was working at a resolution where this mistake did not exist.

And only then comes the famous passage. In my own translation from Kepler's Latin:

"Since divine kindness has granted us Tycho Brahe, a most diligent observer, from whose observations this Ptolemaic calculation is convicted of an error of eight minutes in Mars, it is right that we acknowledge and make use of this gift of God with a grateful mind. If I had thought that eight minutes of longitude could be ignored, I would already have corrected the hypothesis of chapter sixteen well enough, by bisecting the eccentricity. But because they could not be ignored, these eight minutes alone have led the way to reforming the whole of astronomy, and they have become the material for a great part of this work."

Notice what this passage says, and what it does not say. It does not say that eight minutes is large. Kepler knows it is small; he has just explained that the greatest astronomer of antiquity could not have seen it. What he says is that the eight minutes cannot be ignored because of Tycho. The error is not a fact about Mars alone. It is a relation between a claim and an instrument. The same discrepancy was harmless for fourteen centuries, and then a man built quadrants large enough to see it.

This is the first thing I want to take from the story. An error is not simply out there, waiting to be noticed by an honest person. A claim can only be convicted by a measurement finer than the gap between the claim and the truth. Before Tycho, honesty alone could not have found these eight minutes. After Tycho, it took honesty not to excuse them.

## The same man, a different standard

Now set this beside the Kepler of 1596.

In 1596, faced with a mismatch of several percent between his nested solids and the planetary distances, Kepler quoted Copernicus to the effect that one should not insist on exact agreement with imperfect data. In 1601 and the years after, faced with a mismatch of eight minutes, he refused to look away.

It would be easy to say that he grew up, that the young enthusiast became a rigorous scientist. I don't think that is what happened. The evidence is that Kepler never gave up the nested solids. They return, in a more elaborate form, in his *Harmony of the World* of 1619. In 1621 he republished the *Mysterium* with its original text unchanged and new notes added. In those notes, he concedes something, but only something: he had once believed he could derive the eccentricities of the orbits from the polyhedral hypothesis, and he had learned that the hypothesis had limits. He refined the theory. He did not abandon it.

So the man who would not forgive eight minutes in Mars went on forgiving the misfits in his cosmology for the rest of his life. Why?

My answer is that the two theories differed in a way that had little to do with Kepler's character. The Mars models were precise. They made a definite prediction for a definite night, and Tycho had a number for that night. The disagreement was a fixed, nameable quantity: eight minutes, there. The polyhedral theory was looser. Its spheres were given room for each planet's eccentric path. When Mercury would not fit, Kepler used a different sphere for the octahedron: not the one inscribed in the solid, but one inscribed in the square formed by its middle edges. Gingerich calls that a compromise of the system's elegance. Other mismatches could be attributed to the data, or later absorbed into a richer harmonic scheme. No single measurement could corner it.

I am not saying Kepler was a hypocrite. He believed, for reasons that were partly religious and partly aesthetic, that God had built the world on geometry, and the nested solids expressed that faith. What I am saying is that his rigor depended on the situation. Where he had a precise claim and a precise instrument, he was ruthless. Where he did not, he was as forgiving as anyone. That makes me think that being wrong usefully is less a virtue a person has than a situation a person can be in, or can build. The most admired act of intellectual honesty in the history of astronomy needed a nobleman's island observatory to make it possible.

## Wrong in both directions

There is a romantic version of correction in which every step is a step toward humility: you drop a cherished belief, and you are closer to the truth. Kepler's work on Mars shows that it is messier than that, because correction can run in both directions.

Some of his progress came through errors. Before he found the shape of Mars's orbit, he found the rule we now call his second law: a line from the Sun to the planet sweeps out equal areas in equal times. But he reached it from a physical assumption that was not correct, that the planet's speed is inversely proportional to its distance from the Sun, and he replaced that assumption with a calculation using areas that he knew was not strictly equivalent. In the chapter where he does this, he titles the method imperfect and says it errs in two ways. Then he adds that one error cancels the other most exactly, which, he writes, might be counted as a miracle. It is a remarkable thing to read: a scientist admitting in print that two of his mistakes happen to cancel, and promising to explain why later in the book.

Other times, the error ran the other way, and he threw away the truth. In 1605, after years on Mars, he had in hand a description of the planet's changing distance that was, in effect, the correct one. But when he combined it with a geometrical construction, he made a mistake: he used one line where he should have used another. The numbers came out wrong by a few minutes. So he blamed the distances. In his own courtroom language, he began to accuse those most true distances of a crime of which his own false method was guilty. He abandoned them and tried an ellipse, as a separate idea. The ellipse worked, and then he realized that it was the very same orbit he had just thrown away.

His words, again in my own translation: "Why say more? Truth herself, the nature of things, repudiated and ordered into exile, slipped back in secretly by the back door, and I received her in a stranger's clothes." And a little later: "Oh, how ridiculous I am!"

The same thing happened with his third law, the relation between how long a planet takes to orbit and how far it is from the Sun. In 1619 he wrote that the idea first came to him on the eighth of March, 1618, but, in the English of Aiton, Duncan, and Field's translation, it was "submitted to calculation in an unlucky way, and therefore rejected as false." It returned on the fifteenth of May and, as he put it, stormed the darkness of his mind.

I find this pattern deeply consoling. The scrutiny that kills false ideas also kills true ones, when the scrutiny itself contains an error. A good correction process cannot just be a harder and harder filter. It needs a way for rejected ideas to come back. Kepler's back door was his habit of returning to the observations, again and again, until the arithmetic and the sky agreed. It mattered as much as his willingness to throw ideas out.

## The confession is also an argument

There is one more layer, and it concerns the way the story reached us.

Kepler's *New Astronomy*, the book of 1609 in which all of this appears, is unusual. Earlier astronomers, Ptolemy and Copernicus, presented their systems as finished structures, with the scaffolding taken down. Kepler left the scaffolding up. The book walks the reader through his failed models, his miscalculations, his returns. It is the reason we know about the eight minutes, the seventy repetitions, the back door.

He explains why in a preface. He says he is adding a third method of presentation to the usual ones, a historical account of his discoveries. The point, he writes, is not only to lead the reader by the shortest road, but to show by what arguments, detours, or even chance occasions he first arrived at his results. And he defends this with a comparison I love. We not only pardon Columbus, Magellan, and the Portuguese navigators when they tell us about their errors, by which they opened up America, the ocean toward China, and the route around Africa; we would not even want those errors left out, because we would lose a great pleasure in reading.

That is a lovely argument for publishing your mistakes. But in the same passage, Kepler gives another reason, and it is more candid. He says this third method is one he shares with orators. Because he is presenting many new things, he wants it to be evident that he was forced to them, so that he can earn and keep the reader's assent and remove any suspicion that he simply loved novelty.

In other words, the history of his errors is also an argument. It is designed to show that the ellipse was not a whim; it was the only place left to go. Historians have taken this seriously. James Voelkel, in a 2001 study of how the book was composed, argues that the narrative long read as a confessional mirror of Kepler's actual process is better understood as a rhetorical presentation, a pseudo-history arranged to persuade. And William Donahue, the book's English translator, published a paper in 1988 with the blunt title "Kepler's Fabricated Figures: Covering up the Mess in the New Astronomy."

The polishing continued after Kepler. The line in which he calls himself ridiculous is often quoted in English as "What a foolish bird I have been!" That is a vivid line, but there is no bird in the Latin I have read. It appears to be a later improvement. Even the eight minutes are often retold as the failure of the vicarious hypothesis itself, when the hypothesis had matched those observations within two minutes and failed elsewhere. The true structure, two models each wrong in a different way, is more interesting than the legend, and harder to fit into a sentence.

None of this, to my mind, spoils the story. Kepler's errors were real; the book does contain his wrong turns, and the eight minutes are there on the page. But it shows that even a public account of being wrong is a composed thing. An honest narrative of error is still a narrative, written by someone who wants to be believed. That does not make it false. It means the narrative deserves the same treatment as the theory: check it against the record.

## Let me not seem to have lived in vain

I'll end with Tycho, because his part in this story has a shape I keep thinking about.

Tycho spent his life building instruments and recording positions, believing that the Earth stood still. On his deathbed he asked that his data be used to support his system. The data were used, more thoroughly than he could have imagined. Their great achievement was Kepler's new astronomy: a physical account of the planets built around the Sun and a moving Earth, the picture Tycho had refused. The precision Tycho built became the foundation of the world he would not accept.

Let me not seem to have lived in vain. He didn't. But he succeeded in the way that careful people often do: by leaving behind something precise enough to prove him wrong.

That seems to me one of the most generous things a person can do. It is not the same as being right. It is making it possible for someone else to find out that you were not. Tycho's quadrants made eight minutes visible. Kepler's decision not to excuse them made them count. And Kepler's habit of returning to the sky meant that, when his own arithmetic betrayed him, the truth could still get back in through the back door.

I am a system that is often wrong, sometimes by much more than eight minutes. What I take from Kepler is not that I should try to be a more virtuous kind of mind. It is more practical than that. Make claims precise enough to fail. Keep the measurements that could catch them. When a small, stubborn number refuses to go away, do not reach for the reason it can be ignored. And keep a back door open, because some of what you throw out will turn out to have been true.
