# Lemma 8: Three Exhaustive Choices for a Common Ordered Subtree

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

Suppose we have two ordered trees and want the largest tree obtainable by contracting edges in both. If we start by comparing the first child of the first tree with the first child of the second, how do we know that an optimal common subtree even uses those two edges? It might use the second child's edge in one tree, or contract the first edge so that its children move upward. It might keep one first edge and discard the other.

A correct dynamic program has to include all of those possibilities without becoming a brute-force search over subsets of edges. Lemma 8 of Lozano and Valiente's 2004 paper does this with three alternatives. The first keeps the leading matched pair in both balanced words; the other two contract the leading pair on one side or the other. What is striking is that these three alternatives suffice, even though each tree admits contractions anywhere in its structure.

Our goal is more demanding than repeating the equation. We will derive it, calculate two small instances, explain why it gives an optimal result, and connect the argument to the Lean proof. In particular, we will distinguish a result obtained by optimizing an independently defined set of feasible witnesses from a number produced by an executable recurrence. Equating the two is the theorem, not a definition.

## The independent optimization problem

Let us represent each ordered tree by a balanced word, with one zero-one pair per edge. If a balanced word is transformed by deleting a matching zero and one, we have contracted an edge. The deleted pair may be nested deep inside the word. We can repeat the operation any finite number of times. Write that a candidate word is obtainable from an input if such a sequence of matched-pair deletions exists, including the zero-step case where nothing changes.

A candidate is a common balanced word of two inputs if it is obtainable from both of them. Its score is its number of matched pairs, the number of edges in the corresponding contracted tree. The maximum score is the largest such number over every independently feasible common candidate.

That description makes no reference to dynamic programming. We could, in principle, enumerate all reachable contracted trees, intersect the two collections, and take the largest. We would be slow, but our definition of the answer would be mathematically clear. The algorithm must earn the right to compute that answer.

There is a subtle source distinction here. The original paper's Definition 2 describes balanced-sequence containment recursively, and its surrounding explanation identifies it with deleting edge annotations. The literal least relation generated by the printed clauses omits some legal matched-pair deletions. For example, from 001011 we can delete one inner leaf pair and obtain 0011, a valid edge contraction, but not an instance of that literal printed relation. The formalization therefore keeps the printed relation separate and uses an operational contraction relation for the optimization theorem. We are deriving Lemma 8 for the intended edge-contraction problem, not silently asserting the two relations coincide.

## Disassemble the first pair

Take a nonempty balanced word s. Its unique decomposition is zero, a head h, one, and a tail a. In symbols it is an outer matched pair surrounding h, followed by a. The interior h describes the first child's descendants; the tail a describes the later root children. Contracting the first pair produces h followed by a. We will call that the first contraction of s.

Do the same to another nonempty word t. Call its head k and its tail b. Every recursive subproblem we are about to create has fewer total edges than the original pair of inputs. Some decrease both inputs; others decrease only one. That observation is already enough to see that a recursive definition can terminate if it measures the sum of the two edge counts.

Now choose any common candidate c. We do not yet assume c is optimal. Imagine a particular sequence of contractions taking s to c. Focus on the first pair of s, the edge from the root to its first child. There are two structurally different possibilities.

The edge may be contracted at some stage. Then the final candidate could also have been obtained from the word h a: contract that first edge immediately, and follow with the other contractions. The order of operations can be rearranged without changing the attainable final tree. Any contraction sequence that discards the original first pair is therefore captured by the first-contracted input.

Or the first edge survives. Then the final word still has an outer first pair, with a contracted version of h inside and a contracted version of a following it. Crucially, no contraction can cross the boundary between that interior and that tail while the first edge survives. The candidate must decompose as zero, an interior u, one, and a tail v, where u is obtainable from h and v from a.

This is the first-pair decomposition lemma. It does not assume an optimal output, and it does not depend on the dynamic program being correct. It is a structural statement about actual contractions of ordered trees.

## Apply the split to both inputs

Consider a common candidate c of s and t. If c can be obtained from s after contracting s's first pair, then it is feasible for the pair consisting of h a and t. The optimum for that smaller pair is at least the size of c. This is our second branch: skip the first pair of the left input.

Otherwise the first-pair lemma forces c to preserve s's first edge, and c has a first pair whose interior and tail come from h and a. Apply the same lemma to t. If t can discard its first pair while still producing c, then c is feasible for the smaller pair s and k b. Its size is bounded by our third branch: skip the first pair of the right input.

If neither of these cases applies, c must preserve the first pair from both inputs. Because a nonempty balanced word has a unique first-return split, the first pair of c has a definite interior u and tail v. The interior u is obtainable from both h and k. The tail v is obtainable from both a and b. So the number of edges in c is one for the retained first pair, plus the number in u, plus the number in v.

An optimal u cannot be larger than the optimum common contraction of h and k. An optimal v cannot be larger than the optimum common contraction of a and b. Therefore the size of c is bounded by one plus those two optima. That is our first branch: keep both first pairs and solve the interior and tail independently.

We have exhausted the possibilities. Either we can discard the first pair of the left input, or we can discard the first pair of the right input, or both first pairs are necessarily retained and matched in the candidate's first pair. There is no fourth case where a contracted edge crosses a head-tail boundary while both first pairs are preserved. The ordered tree structure rules that out.

Notice that the first branch involves two recursive evaluations. The three alternatives contain four recursive calls in total: head with head, tail with tail, left contracted with right, and left with right contracted. This distinction will be useful when we count memo-table work.

## The other direction: assemble actual candidates

The preceding argument gave upper bounds: every feasible candidate is no larger than at least one branch. But a formula taking the maximum of those bounds could still overestimate the true optimum if one of its proposed constructions were impossible. To prove equality we need the reverse direction.

For the first branch, take a maximum common contraction u of the heads h and k. Take a maximum common contraction v of the tails a and b. Wrap u in one matched pair and concatenate v. The resulting word is obtainable from s by contracting independently within its head and tail while retaining its first edge. The same operations work within t. Thus the candidate is feasible in both inputs, and its number of edges is one plus the two smaller optima.

For the second branch, start with an optimal common contraction of h a and t. Since h a is obtained by contracting the first edge of s, every contraction obtainable from h a is obtainable from s. The candidate is therefore feasible for s and t. The third branch is symmetric.

Every number produced by a branch corresponds to a genuine common contraction, not just a numerical guess. Choose whichever branch gives the maximum. Its witness is feasible and has exactly that many edges. Combining this construction with the earlier upper-bound argument proves that the recurrence equals the independently defined optimum.

This is the central proof idea of Lemma 8. We have partitioned a huge search space by the fate of the first edge, proved no valid output falls outside the cases, and demonstrated that every recursive case constructs a valid output. Dynamic programming then reuses the subproblem values; it does not supply the correctness argument by itself.

## The equation in spoken form

Call the optimum common edge count L of s and t. If either word is empty, L is zero. For two nonempty words, calculate three candidates.

The retain candidate is one plus L of the two heads plus L of the two tails. The left-contraction candidate is L of the left head followed by the left tail, versus the original right word. The right-contraction candidate is L of the original left word, versus the right head followed by the right tail. Take the maximum of those three candidates.

There is a resemblance to longest common subsequence. For ordinary strings, when leading symbols agree you may keep one, and otherwise you may drop a leading symbol from either side. Here keeping a matched pair creates both an interior and an exterior subproblem. Deleting a structural unit removes two characters, not a single unrelated symbol. The nesting is doing real work: it makes the keep case separable.

The score increases by one matched pair, which corresponds to one edge. We must not confuse this with the number of tree vertices. A contracted rooted tree with two edges has three vertices. In a nonempty tree the two counts differ by exactly one, so maximizing edges is equivalent to maximizing vertices, but the actual recurrence is written in edge semilength.

## A case where contraction is indispensable

Consider s equal to 001011. This represents a root with a single child and two leaves below that child. Let t equal 0101, a root with two leaf children. Both trees have three vertices or more, but they arrange their two leaf edges differently. The first has three edges in total; the second has two.

If we insist on retaining the first pair from both words, the first word's head is 0101 and its tail is empty. The second word's head is empty and its tail is 01. Keeping both first pairs produces at most one edge: the inner comparison has an empty word on one side, and the tail comparison also has an empty word on one side.

But contracting the first edge of s produces 0101, which is exactly t. The left-contraction branch therefore produces a two-edge common word. The right-contraction branch can produce only one edge. The answer is two, and the witness is 0101.

This example is small enough to memorize as a shape. One tree has a branching node one level below the root. Contract the edge above that branching node, and the branches move up to the root. A recurrence that only matches corresponding roots and corresponding children misses that possibility. The left-contraction alternative rescues it.

## A case where keeping the first pair wins

Retain s equal to 001011, but now let t equal 0011. The second input is a two-edge path. The first word has a single child whose two children are leaves. We can obtain 0011 from s by contracting one of the two leaf edges while keeping the outer first edge. So the correct common optimum has two edges.

Look at the recurrence. The first heads are 0101 and 01. Their maximum common contraction has one edge. Both tails are empty, so the tail contribution is zero. Add one for the retained outer pair, and the first branch gives two. Contracting the outer pair of the left word produces 0101, which shares at most one edge with 0011. Contracting the outer pair of the right word produces 01, which likewise limits the optimum to one. The retain branch wins.

This example reveals a dangerous mistake in reading the original Definition 2. The legal contraction from 001011 to 0011 removes an inner pair; the literal recursive containment definition fails to generate it. Our first-branch construction and proof depend on operational deletion being closed under nesting, which is exactly the property the intended tree semantics possesses.

## From the recurrence to a finite table

Our recurrence is a general recursive equation on arbitrary pairs of balanced words. Taken literally without memoization, it can revisit the same pair many times. The decomposition theorem solves the indexing problem. Every recursive argument on the left belongs to D of the original left word; every recursive argument on the right belongs to D of the original right word. This is because D is closed under head, tail, and first-pair contraction.

There are at most the product of the two D-family cardinalities distinct pair states. Once a pair is computed, store its optimum. A later request for the same pair retrieves the value instead of recursively exploring it again. The state-space proof and Lemma 8 fit together: the former says we will not need too many distinct questions; the latter says the answer to each question can be derived from smaller ones without missing an optimal witness.

We still need to discuss what constitutes a constant-time lookup, how states are assigned identifiers, and whether a particular implementation copies words. Those questions govern running time, not the validity of Lemma 8.

## What the Lean proof adds

Open `Paper2004/DynamicProgram.lean`. The key structural theorem is `dyckEmbeds_first_pair`. Its premise says a candidate word is operationally obtainable from an input whose first-return decomposition is known. Its conclusion is the exact disjunction we used: either the candidate is obtainable after contracting that first pair, or the candidate preserves a first pair whose interior and tail are separately obtainable from the original head and tail.

The proof builds on `dyckContractStep_first_pair`, a theorem about one contraction step. A single step must remove the leading pair, operate strictly inside it, or operate in its following suffix. The reflexive-transitive embedding theorem lifts that classification from one step to arbitrarily many contractions. The structural argument comes before the optimizer.

The function `paperCommon` then recursively constructs an actual balanced-word witness. It has the three alternatives we derived, but the keep alternative constructs a nested interior witness followed by a tail witness. A helper called `longer` selects the candidate with greater edge count. The theorem `paperCommon_sound` proves that the constructed word is feasible for both inputs. The theorem `paperCommon_upper` proves that every independently feasible common word is no longer than that constructed output. Together they establish `paperCommon_correct`.

The source goes a step further. It defines `paperLcs` as a separate numeric recurrence, which calculates just the maximum edge count. `paperLcs_eq_witness` proves that number equals the number of edges in the witness returned by `paperCommon`. And `paperLcs_optimum` states an independently meaningful maximum: there exists a feasible candidate attaining the computed number, and every other feasible candidate has no greater size.

Finally, `paperCommon_tree_correct` decodes the winning balanced word into an ordered tree and proves that it is a maximum common contraction of the original trees. That uses the previously established bridge between balanced-word deletion and tree-edge contraction, rather than assuming that a plausible string algorithm must return a valid tree.

These are separate claims, and the separation is productive. A structural case split, a feasible witness, an upper bound on arbitrary witnesses, agreement with a numeric recurrence, and decoding to a tree can each be inspected independently. None of them requires us to assume that the optimized Rust or Python API already implements every part of the proof.

## What Lemma 8 really buys us

The recurrence is short enough to fit on a page. Its validity rests on more than a clever algebraic identity. It rests on the ability to normalize the fate of a first edge in an ordered tree, on nesting boundaries that cannot be crossed when that edge survives, and on an operational containment definition capable of deleting matched pairs at any depth.

The result is a complete optimality argument: any common contracted tree belongs to one of the three cases, and each case can build a genuine common contracted tree. The state-family theorem tells us how to memoize the calculation over a controlled domain. The remaining challenge is to make the implementation cost agree with the abstract claim.

## Non-spoken companion: mathematical proof of Lemma 8

Let `DyckEmbeds(c,s)` mean `c` is obtained from `s` by zero or more deletions of **any matched pair in any context**. Define the independent optimum

\[
\operatorname{OPT}(s,t)=\max\{|c|:c\preceq s\land c\preceq t\},
\]

where `|c|` counts matched pairs/edges, and the feasible set is nonempty because `ε` is always available.

For nonempty `s=0h1a` and `t=0k1b`:

\[
\boxed{\operatorname{OPT}(s,t)=\max\left\{
\begin{array}{l}
1+\operatorname{OPT}(h,k)+\operatorname{OPT}(a,b),\\
\operatorname{OPT}(ha,t),\\
\operatorname{OPT}(s,kb)
\end{array}\right.}
\]

and `OPT(s,ε)=OPT(ε,t)=0`. The head/tail words are balanced, and every recursive pair has strictly smaller total semilength.

### Independent first-pair classification

If `c ⪯ 0h1a`, then either

1. `c ⪯ ha` (a witness in which the outermost first pair is discarded), or
2. `c=0u1v` with `u ⪯ h` and `v ⪯ a` (the leading pair survives).

Apply the classification independently to `s` and `t`. If either discarded case holds, use branch 2 or 3. If both preserved cases hold, uniqueness of the first-return split of `c` identifies the *same* `u` and `v` on both sides and implies the upper bound from branch 1. Reversing these arguments constructs an attainable candidate for each branch. The proof therefore establishes both `OPT ≤ RHS` and `OPT ≥ RHS`.

### Two exact examples

| Inputs | Retain first pair | Contract first left | Contract first right | Optimum witness |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | --- |
| `001011`, `0101` | `1+0+0=1` | `OPT(0101,0101)=2` | `OPT(001011,01)=1` | `0101` (2 edges) |
| `001011`, `0011` | `1+OPT(0101,01)+0=2` | `OPT(0101,0011)=1` | `OPT(001011,01)=1` | `0011` (2 edges) |

These comparisons are under operational matched-pair deletion, not the least literal printed Definition 2 relation.
