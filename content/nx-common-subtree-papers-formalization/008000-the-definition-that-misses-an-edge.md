# The Definition That Misses an Edge: A Formal Audit of Lozano–Valiente Containment

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

A paper can describe the right mathematical problem in words, give an algorithm that solves it, and still contain a formal definition that is slightly too restrictive. This is not a contradiction. Natural-language descriptions, recursive definitions, and executable algorithms are three different ways of specifying a problem. Their agreement is itself a mathematical claim. If nobody proves the agreement, an error can hide in a very small gap between them.

The 2004 paper by Antoni Lozano and Gabriel Valiente gives us an exceptionally concrete example. The intended problem is maximum common embedded subtree for rooted ordered trees. An embedded subtree is obtained by contracting edges. The authors encode trees as balanced sequences, one matching pair of symbols per edge, and propose a dynamic program for the largest common sequence. In Section 2, Definition 2, they also give a recursive description of sequence containment.

If we interpret that recursive description as the smallest relation generated by its two stated clauses, it is not equivalent to arbitrary edge contraction. The first counterexample needs only three edges. The accompanying Lean repository proves the disagreement for all possible uses of the printed clauses in that example, and proves a general equivalence between a corrected, operational sequence relation and tree contraction.

That is our subject: not the drama of finding a typo, but the mathematical technique of discovering which statement an algorithm actually proves.

## What is supposed to count as a contraction?

Start with a rooted ordered tree. Each vertex has a finite left-to-right list of children. Contracting the edge from a parent to one of its children removes that edge and merges the two endpoints. In terms of the ordered child list, replace the chosen child by its own children, in their existing order. The other siblings keep their positions. This operation is allowed at the root or inside any descendant subtree. Repeat as often as desired.

If the tree has three edges, one contraction leaves two. Contract another, and it leaves one. Eventually we can reach the one-vertex tree, with no edges. The root does not disappear from the formalization when the final edge is contracted: a tree is always a rooted nonempty tree, even though its balanced encoding may be the empty word.

Encode an edge by writing a zero when entering the child and a one when returning to the parent. The zero and one belong together. Write children in order, recursively. Thus a root with two leaf children is zero-one, zero-one: 0101. A root with one child, which itself has one child, is zero-zero-one-one: 0011.

Contracting an edge deletes the two symbols associated with that edge, keeping the complete interior of its pair and the surrounding symbols in their original order. Deleting a matched pair does not mean deleting two arbitrary zero and one symbols. Their nesting is what identifies the edge. And, crucially, a matched pair can lie inside another matched pair.

We can define this transformation directly. There is a one-step relation: from a balanced word, remove any single enclosing matched pair within a balanced context. Then there is its reflexive and transitive closure: zero or more such operations. We will call this operational containment. It is independent of a dynamic-programming recurrence, and it can be applied to any depth in the tree.

## The printed recursive clauses

Now examine the other description. Definition 2 says that a sequence is contained in itself. Otherwise, the larger word can be partitioned into a left balanced word, an enclosing zero and one around a middle balanced word, and a right balanced word. The smaller word is assembled from three corresponding balanced pieces, each recursively contained in the corresponding larger piece. In that second clause, the displayed enclosing pair is gone.

All six constituent pieces must themselves be balanced. This qualification is not cosmetic. A prefix that leaves a zero unmatched is not an allowed left piece. Balanced prefixes cut between complete top-level sibling subtrees, never halfway through a nested edge.

Suppose we turn those clauses into a least inductively defined relation. We permit precisely the finite derivations created by the identity clause and the removal clause, with no extra rules smuggled in. In Lean, the relation is called PrintedContains. Its constructors are named refl and remove. The latter says, in essence, that if each small piece is printed-contained in the matching large piece, then their concatenation is printed-contained in the larger word formed by inserting an enclosing matched pair around the middle large piece.

You might expect a recursive definition to allow removing a pair anywhere, because each constituent word can shrink recursively. The difficulty is that each nontrivial derivation must start by removing a pair at the top level of the larger word. Recursive shrinking can then occur in the three pieces, but only after a top-level pair has been removed. There is no constructor saying that containment inside an enclosing pair can be lifted to containment of the enclosing pair itself while preserving that outer edge.

A missing contextual-closure rule is enough to change the relation.

## Three edges are sufficient

Picture a root called R. R has one child, A. A has two leaf children, B and C, in that order. Its balanced word is 001011. The outer pair represents R to A; the inner two pairs represent A to B and A to C.

Now contract the edge from A to C. C has no children, so contracting that edge simply removes C and leaves R to A to B, a path of two edges. Its balanced word is 0011.

This is a valid tree contraction by any reasonable reading of the intended problem. Its witness is entirely explicit: select the edge A to C and contract it. In the balanced word, delete the matched zero and one for that edge, which are nested under the outer pair. The result is 0011.

Does the printed relation permit it?

The larger word has three matched pairs. The smaller has two. So any derivation of printed containment must remove exactly one pair in total. This observation saves us from having to enumerate every possible recursive proof tree. Every use of the remove constructor loses at least one pair. If the whole derivation loses only one, it can use the removal constructor only once; each of its three subderivations must preserve its size. But a printed-containment derivation of equal-sized words can only establish equality. Thus the only possible one-pair printed reductions delete a top-level matched pair and leave the rest unchanged.

Our larger word, 001011, has exactly one top-level pair: the outside pair around its entire interior. Deleting that pair produces 0101, a fork, not 0011, a path. It is impossible to delete the inner leaf pair in a one-step printed derivation while keeping the outer pair. So the printed relation does not admit 0011 as a contained word of 001011.

The entire disagreement fits into six characters. An operational contraction says yes. The literal least recursive relation says no.

## Why the first missing case is so small

The formalization proves a useful general lemma: if a printed-contained word has exactly one fewer matched pair than the larger word, it must arise by deleting a top-level pair. It also proves that a single nested word has a unique top-level pair. Put those together and the contradiction above is forced. There is no appeal to a bounded search or to the fact that no one has found another derivation.

For intuition, consider a two-edge tree. Its two possible shapes are a two-edge path and a root with two leaf children. Removing the root-to-first-child edge from a path and removing the first top-level edge of a fork already cover the available one-edge results. The missing ability to contract a descendant edge without contracting an ancestor becomes visible when a root has one child whose descendants branch. That first occurs at three edges.

The repository includes an independent finite checker. For every balanced word up through five edges, it enumerates outcomes under the literal recursive grammar and under arbitrary matched-pair deletion. The two relations agree on all smaller examples; at three edges, exactly one of the five possible input shapes exhibits a discrepancy. By four edges, six of the fourteen shapes do. By five, twenty-six of the forty-two do. These are checks on a finite domain, not the general proof. They help us find the example and test implementations. Lean supplies the general reasoning that rules out every printed derivation of this particular example.

The paper itself offers a second kind of evidence. Its larger Example 2 lists balanced sequences contained in one chosen input. The repository's independent enumeration finds thirty-four operational outcomes for that input, but only eleven under the literal grammar. The paper's list agrees with the operational family. That is strong contextual evidence that the recursive clause failed to express the full intended operation, rather than the algorithm having been designed for a smaller problem all along.

A fair reading keeps these levels of evidence separate. The authors explicitly describe deleting edge annotations, Definition 5 explicitly uses edge contraction of trees, and the examples are consistent with that goal. We should not infer that the authors consciously intended the narrower least relation. We should also not pretend that the printed clauses, taken literally, establish what they do not.

## How the optimum itself changes

A missing feasible output is interesting, but a missing *optimal* output is more consequential. Let us compare two trees: the two-edge path encoded by 0011, and the three-edge branching tree encoded by 001011.

Under operational containment, the entire two-edge path is feasible in both. It is feasible in itself by doing nothing; it is feasible in the branching tree by the leaf-edge contraction we just described. A common output cannot have more than two edges because one input only has two. Therefore the operational optimum is exactly two.

Under the printed relation, the path is not contained in the branching tree. Could some other two-edge shape be a common result? The printed relation cannot map an input to a *different* word without decreasing its number of pairs. Consequently the only two-edge word printed-contained in the two-edge path is the path itself. And that one is forbidden on the other side. Thus every common printed-contained word has at most one pair. A one-edge result is possible by deleting an outer pair where appropriate, so the printed optimum is one.

This is an observable numerical difference: the intended answer is two; the literal printed-relation answer is one. The recurrence from Lemma 8 returns two. That is not a defect in Lemma 8. It is evidence that Lemma 8 computes the edge-contraction objective described elsewhere in the paper, rather than the strictly smaller language generated by the printed clauses.

The repository verifies this with independent exhaustive computations as well. Its recurrence checker agrees with the operational optimum on all nineteen thousand five hundred and three unordered pairs of the 197 balanced words through six edges. It disagrees with the printed optimum on seven thousand five hundred and fifty-four of those pairs. Again, such exhaustive finite checks are excellent diagnostics, but not proofs of general correctness. The full Lean proof of Lemma 8 against operational containment is a separate theorem.

## What the repair actually is

We do not need to invent a new objective. We need to formalize the one the tree definition and the algorithm already use.

Take the one-step rule that removes an arbitrary matched pair inside any balanced context. Context means a structural expression with one hole. A hole can be surrounded by balanced siblings; it can also sit inside another pair, at any nesting depth. Fill the hole with an enclosing pair to obtain the before-word; fill it with the pair's interior to obtain the after-word. This is DyckContractStep in the Lean repository. Define DyckEmbeds to be zero or more such steps.

The difference between the printed relation and this operational relation is not that one is recursive and the other isn't. Both admit inductive reasoning. The difference is the set of permitted constructors or closure rules. The operational relation is closed under taking an elementary deletion inside an arbitrary balanced context. The printed one does not have the corresponding inside-a-preserved-pair clause.

The correct formalization should preserve both definitions. If we silently redefine PrintedContains to mean DyckEmbeds, we lose the ability to state the discrepancy and might accidentally claim that a theorem has validated the original wording. Keeping two names gives us a clean theorem: every printed containment is an operational containment. And it gives us a clean countertheorem: not every operational containment is a printed containment.

For the intended problem, we then prove a different equivalence. A tree can be obtained from another rooted ordered tree by edge contractions if and only if the corresponding balanced word can be obtained by operational matched-pair deletions. This result crosses representations. It is not a syntactic manipulation of the printed grammar.

## Why the two-way bridge is the right theorem

To prove the forward direction, take an actual tree edge contraction. Find the parent, its chosen child, the siblings to either side, and the ancestors above them. Contracting the edge splices the chosen child's children into the parent's child list. When we encode the tree, that exact change removes the pair corresponding to the selected edge, while preserving every other nested region and sibling order. Therefore one tree contraction induces one valid word deletion. Repeat along a sequence of contractions to obtain the implication for their reflexive and transitive closures.

For the reverse direction, begin with a matched-pair deletion inside a balanced context. Decode the word into an ordered forest, then attach the implicit root. The deleted pair identifies a real parent-child edge in that tree. Removing the pair splices the decoded interior forest into the surrounding sibling forest, exactly as edge contraction requires. Each word deletion gives one tree contraction, and finite sequences preserve the correspondence.

The Lean theorem embeds-iff-dyck-embeds packages both directions. It relies on the encoder and decoder being inverses, not merely on each preserving some examples. If a representation bridge only establishes one direction, it can justify soundness while still admitting missed feasible solutions. Our containment discrepancy is precisely the kind of problem that can survive when completeness is left implicit.

## Reading the three key Lean statements

The first source file to open is PaperContainment dot lean. The inductive declaration PrintedContains has the two constructors we discussed: reflexivity and removal of a top-level pair, with three recursive containment premises. The theorem PrintedContains dot toDyckEmbeds proves the forward implication to operational deletion. It does not assert an equivalence.

The second file is PrintedContainmentCounterexample dot lean. Find one-pair-drop-top-level and one-pair-drop-nested; they explain the structural obstruction. The theorem branching-example-not-printed denies the precise relation between the path and branching input. Finally, printed-containment-not-complete negates the universal converse. Read that final negation carefully. It says there is no theorem converting *every* operational deletion into an instance of the printed relation. The counterexample proves it.

The third file is ContractionCompleteness dot lean. The theorem dyckContractStep_decodeDyck turns a single actual pair deletion into a tree contraction. The theorem embeds_iff_dyckEmbeds states the full equivalence of tree containment and operational sequence containment. This is the one to rely on when transporting an optimality theorem across the tree and sequence representations.

Notice the different styles of results. The printed-grammar theorem is a one-way inclusion. The counterexample is a negative statement about that inclusion's converse. The corrected bridge is a genuine if-and-only-if theorem between two independently defined operational domains. Those are three logically distinct accomplishments. A source-level algorithm proof will ultimately depend on the third, not on replacing it with the first.

## What this teaches us about formalization

There is a tempting attitude toward formalizing an algorithm: write definitions that look like the code, state the desired theorem, and make Lean accept the proof. That can verify that the code and those definitions are internally consistent, but it does not automatically verify the paper's intended problem. A program can be perfectly correct against the wrong specification.

Here the project guards against that circularity in two ways. The tree-level contraction relation is defined independently of balanced words and the algorithm. And the balanced-word contraction relation is defined independently of the dynamic-programming recurrence. Only then are the semantic bridge and the recurrence's optimality proved. The printed definition is represented separately, with its own positive and negative results.

This distinction will recur in the Droschinsky papers. A weighted matching table may be proved correct against a rooted recurrence while the intended graph witness still needs an independent definition. A path-embedding implementation may reconstruct plausible edges while the witness specification still needs to rule out intersecting interiors. A fast runtime may follow the right recurrence while its arithmetic model, source translation, or caller boundary remains unverified.

The lesson from 001011 and 0011 is therefore not merely to inspect definitions for mistakes. It is to ask which implication has actually been proved. Is every algorithmic output feasible? Does every feasible output have a corresponding algorithmic case? Does decoding preserve the objective? Does reconstruction preserve the original input identities? A robust formalization turns those questions into separate propositions rather than one vague claim of correctness.

In the present case we can finish on a firm result. The literal printed least relation is too narrow. Operational matched-pair deletion correctly models ordered-tree edge contraction in both directions. Lemma 8 computes the maximum for that operational problem. The mathematics of the intended 2004 problem survives the specification audit, and the proof states exactly which meaning of containment makes it true.

## Non-spoken companion: exact objects and arguments

**Paper:** [Lozano and Valiente, 2004, §2 Definition 2 and Example 2; §3 Definition 5 and Theorem 2; §4 Lemma 8](https://www.cs.upc.edu/~antoni/subtree.pdf).

Let `DyckWord` be the balanced words, with `0` as the empty word in the Lean additive notation and `w.nest` corresponding to `0 w 1` in the paper's binary alphabet.

**Literal printed least relation** (`Paper2004/PaperContainment.lean`):

\[
\begin{aligned}
&\operatorname{PrintedContains}(w,w),\\
&\frac{\operatorname{PrintedContains}(s_i,t_i)\text{ for }i=1,2,3}
{\operatorname{PrintedContains}(s_1s_2s_3,t_1\,0t_2 1\,t_3)}.
\end{aligned}
\]

**Operational pair deletion** (`ContractionSoundness.lean`):

\[
\operatorname{DyckContractStep}(C[0u1],C[u]),\qquad
\operatorname{DyckEmbeds}(s,t)\iff C_1,C_2,\ldots\text{ gives a finite deletion sequence }t\leadsto^*s.
\]

Here `C` is a balanced *context with one hole*, allowing nesting and balanced sibling concatenation; the second informal display means reflexive/transitive closure, not an existential quantification over only two fixed contexts.

**Smallest counterexample:**

```text
T = node [node [singleton, singleton]]   encode(T) = 001011
S = node [node [singleton]]              encode(S) = 0011

S is an actual contraction of T:
  delete the pair for the second leaf edge inside the outer pair.

one-pair printed reduction of a singly nested word:
  001011 -> 0101 (outer pair only), never 0011.

printed common optimum of (0011, 001011): 1 pair
operational common optimum of (0011, 001011): 2 pairs
Lemma 8 recurrence for the same inputs:       2 pairs
```
