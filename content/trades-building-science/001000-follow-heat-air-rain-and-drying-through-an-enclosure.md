# Follow heat, air, rain, and drying through an enclosure

## A House Is a System of Flows: Heat, Air, Water, and Drying

Imagine waking on a cold morning to find water on the inside of a bedroom window. The instinctive diagnosis might be, "The window leaks." But what exactly leaked? Rain may never have touched the glass. The water may have originated in your own breath, your cooking, or a shower in a different part of the house. To explain that puddle, we need to trace the paths of heat, air, and water vapor. A house is not a static collection of objects. It is a system through which energy and matter are always trying to move.

A remedy for one kind of movement may aggravate another, so the two must be distinguished. A window replacement can change the temperature of the glass and the air leakage around its frame. Added insulation may keep an interior surface warmer, yet make another hidden surface colder. Sealing an attic hatch can reduce an important air path without changing the insulating value of the hatch itself. If we understand the flows separately, we can predict which intervention addresses which mechanism.

### Where heat goes

Heat flows from higher temperature toward lower temperature when there is a path for transfer. In buildings, three mechanisms matter. Conduction occurs through solids and stationary fluids: heat passes through drywall, wood, insulation, masonry, glass, and the thin layers of air adjacent to them. Convection carries thermal energy with moving air or water, including air leaking through gaps. Radiation exchanges energy between surfaces without requiring a solid connection. You can feel radiant heat from the sun or from a hot stove even when the surrounding air is cooler.

Insulation slows heat transfer; it does not manufacture warmth. Its thermal resistance, often called R-value, describes resistance to heat flow under specified conditions. A larger R-value usually means less heat flow through that material assembly under comparable conditions. But a wall is a collection of parallel paths. The timber studs in a framed wall interrupt the insulation layer, creating thermal bridges. A photograph of fluffy insulation between studs tells us little about the whole-wall thermal performance unless we include the framing, sheathing, finishes, and connections.

There is also a distinction between heat loss and the sensation of cold. A person beside a cold window can lose thermal energy by radiation toward that surface; drafts can create additional convective loss. Raising air temperature alone may not fully address discomfort if surrounding surfaces remain cold. That is one reason building enclosure improvements can change comfort even when the thermostat stays at the same number.

### Air does not wait for permission

Air moves when a pressure difference drives it through a connected opening. Wind creates pressure differences over the exterior. Exhaust fans can depressurize an interior zone. In a heated building during cold weather, differences in air density can cause stack effect: lower openings tend to admit air and upper openings tend to exhaust it, with the actual pattern depending on the house and weather. A house can be well insulated and still leak considerable air if the enclosure has gaps.

Now consider moisture. Warm indoor air usually contains water vapor. Suppose some of that air escapes through an opening around a ceiling light into a cold attic. As it cools, its capacity to hold vapor at saturation decreases. If the air encounters a cold enough surface, some vapor may condense into liquid water or deposit as frost. By carrying moisture directly into a cold cavity, air leakage can matter more than vapor diffusion through an intact sheet of material. That does not make vapor diffusion irrelevant; it means the two mechanisms need separate analysis.

An air barrier is a continuous system intended to restrict air movement through the enclosure. It may involve membranes, sheathing, sealants, and carefully detailed connections. A vapor retarder slows the diffusion of water vapor through material. A rain-control or water-resistive layer manages bulk water coming from the exterior. These are different jobs. Some materials perform more than one, but a product label cannot replace a drawing of how all the layers connect at windows, corners, roofs, and foundations.

### Water has more than one way in

There are at least four broad sources to investigate when a building is wet. Bulk water arrives from rain, snowmelt, groundwater, or plumbing. Capillary action draws liquid into pores or fine gaps, sometimes against gravity. Water vapor diffuses in response to differences in vapor pressure. Air movement transports vapor through openings. A fifth category is water already present during construction, which must dry out.

Try this thought experiment. A basement wall is damp near the floor after heavy rain. Painting its interior with a waterproof coating may conceal the visible symptom for a time. But if water pressure outside the foundation has not changed, the moisture must still go somewhere. It may find another crack, accumulate behind a finish, or continue through adjoining porous materials. A more useful investigation follows the rain from roof to gutter, downspout, grading, soil, drainage system, and foundation. We would ask whether the pattern correlates with rainfall, condensation, groundwater conditions, or a plumbing fault before selecting a treatment.

In the same way, rot is rarely explained by saying simply that wood is old. Decay fungi require favorable conditions, including sufficient moisture. A durable repair addresses why the wood remained wet, not merely the wood that finally lost strength. The location, magnitude, and persistence of wetting all matter.

### The dew point is a condition, not a location

At a given pressure, an air mixture has a dew point: the temperature at which its existing water vapor concentration would reach saturation if cooled without adding or removing vapor. When a surface falls below the relevant dew point, condensation may form there. The dew point is not a permanent line inside a wall; it changes with moisture conditions. Even where the temperature profile suggests condensation potential, actual moisture accumulation and drying depend on transport rates, materials, weather, and time.

Take indoor air at about twenty degrees Celsius and fifty percent relative humidity. Its dew point is roughly nine degrees Celsius. A pane of glass at five degrees may collect water, while a pane at twelve degrees may remain clear under the same indoor conditions. Lowering indoor humidity, warming the glass, or reducing air contact with the cold surface can alter the outcome. This small example gives us three different levers, not one universal repair.

Notice what relative humidity does not tell us. Fifty percent relative humidity in cold air represents a different amount of water vapor than fifty percent in warm air. Air can cool while containing essentially the same mass of vapor and become much closer to saturation. That is why apparent moisture problems can emerge in cold weather without any new leak.

### A wall must both resist wetting and permit recovery

A durable assembly is not necessarily perfectly sealed against every molecule of water. It needs control of significant wetting and a plausible drying path. Drying may proceed inward or outward depending on the climate, season, and assembly. A layer that is appropriate in one climate may restrict drying in another. The relative positions of insulation, vapor-control layers, water barriers, and materials that can store moisture matter. We should be skeptical of any advice that specifies a single plastic sheet as correct for every wall in every region.

The same principle applies to renovations. Before adding insulation to an existing attic, for example, ask about air sealing at the ceiling plane, roof leakage, ventilation and mechanical equipment, and how moisture enters and leaves. Insulation can improve energy performance while changing temperatures elsewhere in the assembly. The correct solution depends on the entire system.

### Learn to diagnose, not just to label

Return to that wet window. We can write several competing explanations: indoor humidity is high; the glass is cold because of weak thermal performance; air is leaking around the frame; rainwater is entering at an exterior joint; or some combination of these. Each predicts something different. Does the water appear during dry freezing nights or specifically during rain? Is it on the room-facing glass, between panes, or under the trim? Does a nearby bathroom exhaust outdoors? Are other windows wet? Which surfaces are cold? Observations can narrow the explanations before anyone buys a product.

Building science is the art of turning those observations into an account of flows. Heat needs a temperature difference. Air needs a pressure difference and a path. Liquid water needs a source and a route. Vapor moves through air or materials; condensation depends on the moisture state and temperature. Materials need opportunities to dry. The lasting question is not merely, "Where is the damage?" It is, "What process produced it, and how can we change that process without creating another problem?"

## Heat Flow, Thermal Bridges, and Whole-Assembly R-Values

The whole-enclosure model can now be separated into the particular routes through which heat moves.

On a winter morning, touch the painted wall between two studs and then the wall where a wooden stud passes behind the drywall. One spot may be cooler. The difference is small enough that your hand may not notice it, but a thermal camera sometimes will. What you are observing is not a hole. It is a path: two different materials connecting the same warm room to the same cold outdoors.

People ask, “How much insulation is in the wall?” A better question is, “How much heat passes through the complete wall, including its interruptions?” Today we will construct that answer. We do not need an elaborate simulation to see the central problem, only a few ideas from thermal circuits.

### The resistance of a path

Heat flows from higher temperature to lower temperature. In a steady approximation, the rate of heat flow through a layer grows with the temperature difference and shrinks as thermal resistance grows. An insulating layer has a high thermal resistance; a good conductor has a low one. The thermal resistance of a homogeneous slab increases with its thickness and decreases with the material's thermal conductivity.

In North American building practice we often quote R-value. Do not mix unit systems. An R-value stated in square-foot degrees Fahrenheit hours per British thermal unit is not numerically the same as a resistance stated in square-meter kelvins per watt. The corresponding U-factor is the reciprocal of assembly resistance in the appropriate units. Higher R means less heat transfer under comparable conditions; higher U means more.

Layers in sequence act approximately like resistances in series. A wall might contain interior surface resistance, drywall, a cavity layer, sheathing, continuous exterior insulation, siding, and an exterior surface resistance. For a one-dimensional steady calculation, we add the layer resistances to get the resistance of that path. Neither insulation labels nor a material's R-value by itself accounts for gaps, metal fasteners, or air moving through the assembly.

### Two paths through the same wall

Imagine that most of a framed wall is insulated cavity, while the rest is wood framing. Suppose, for a deliberately simplified example, that the cavity path has an effective resistance of twenty in consistent R-units, and the framing path has a resistance of six. Suppose the framing occupies twenty-five percent of the wall's projected area. What is the combined resistance?

It would be wrong to average the R-values arithmetically and say sixteen and a half. Heat does not take a democratic vote among materials. The two paths experience approximately the same indoor-to-outdoor temperature difference, and their heat flows add. We must average their conductances, the reciprocals of resistance. Seventy-five percent divided by twenty, plus twenty-five percent divided by six, gives about zero point zero seven nine units of conductance. The reciprocal is approximately twelve point six. Our whole-wall resistance is much closer to twelve point six than to twenty.

This arithmetic reveals why the cavity insulation rating is not the wall rating. It also reveals why framing fraction matters. Plates, headers, studs, corners, window surrounds, and blocking all provide heat paths. Thermal bridges do not have to be wood: steel studs, metal shelf angles, fasteners, concrete balconies, and lintels can produce larger local effects.

### Continuous insulation changes the topology

Now add an uninterrupted insulating layer outside the framing and the cavities. If it adds an R-value of five to both paths, the cavity path rises from twenty to twenty-five and the framing path rises from six to eleven. The combined result is approximately nineteen, using the same area fractions. That is a larger improvement than simply adding five to the weakest part or judging by the cavity rating alone.

Why does this work? Continuous insulation forces both parallel paths to pass through another resistance in series. Framing still affects the result, but its bypass advantage is reduced. This is an important architectural idea: sometimes it is better to change the network of paths than to improve the best path in the network.

Real details complicate the model. Exterior insulation may be interrupted at window openings or attachments. Studs conduct heat in two and three dimensions, not only across a neat slice of wall. Surface temperatures, wind, moisture, fastener geometry, and installation quality change performance. The simple parallel-path model is for reasoning, not a claim that we have calculated the rating of a particular wall.

### Insulation quality is not the same as air sealing

A section of fluffy insulation installed with a gap around an outlet can perform worse than the nominal material rating suggests. Compression can change its effective thickness and structure. Incomplete coverage can create low-resistance routes. Moving air can carry heat through leaks without following the conduction-only paths in our calculation. For these reasons, adding up printed R-values is necessary for some questions and insufficient for others.

Consider the ceiling of a house. Heat can conduct through insulation and framing. It can also leave with indoor air escaping through an attic hatch or plumbing penetration. More insulation may reduce the first flow while doing little to the second. Air sealing and thermal insulation are complementary controls, not synonyms.

### Why comfort and condensation enter the picture

Heat transfer is not only about a fuel bill. A thermal bridge can lower a room-facing surface temperature, changing radiant comfort and local condensation risk. If indoor air has a dew point above a cold spot's surface temperature, water can accumulate there. A corner that stays cold and wet may develop mold even when the surrounding wall appears dry. Indoor humidity, airflow, surface finish, and time also matter; a visible dark patch is not proof that a thermal bridge alone caused it.

Adding exterior insulation may warm structural sheathing during a cold season, but changing an assembly also changes its potential to dry. A wall that performs thermally could still fail in moisture control if water is trapped by incompatible layers. We must always ask about both thermal performance and moisture management. These are coupled systems, not interchangeable ones.

### Reading an insulation proposal

Suppose a contractor offers two upgrades. The first adds insulation in already-filled cavities. The second adds continuous insulation and details the window, door, foundation, and roof transitions. Before comparing prices, identify the existing assembly and ask how each option treats framing bridges, air leaks, moisture paths, and continuity at edges. Ask what numerical claim describes the insulation product and what claim describes the *assembly*. Ask what assumptions underlie a quoted savings estimate: climate, conditioned area, existing leakage, heating equipment, occupancy, and installation.

There is a final useful distinction. A highly insulated wall may occupy only a modest fraction of a home's thermal losses. Windows, roof, floor, foundation, air leakage, and mechanical ventilation contribute too. A whole-wall R-value is not a whole-house energy model. Our reasoning improves when we choose the system boundary explicitly.

When you inspect a wall, picture a network: layers in series and bridges in parallel. The most revealing question is not, “Is this material good insulation?” It is, “Where does heat have a connected low-resistance route, and what modification changes that route?” That is the beginning of credible building-envelope design.

## Air Leakage, Pressure Differences, and Stack Effect

Imagine a cold morning in a two-story house. A narrow crack at the attic hatch blows warm air into the attic. A nearly identical crack beside a basement window admits cold air. Neither crack contains a fan. The air moves because the pressure on one side differs from the pressure on the other. To understand the building, we need to understand where those differences come from.

A gap does not generate airflow on its own. It provides a path. Pressure supplies the push. The size and shape of the gap resist that push. Airflow depends on both. A large, unobstructed gap under a modest pressure difference may move more air than a tiny gap under a stronger one. That is why counting visible cracks is not a reliable measure of a building's leakage.

### The pressure map is not uniform

Start with warm indoor air. Heating generally makes a given volume of air less dense than the same volume of colder outdoor air, at comparable pressure and composition. A column of warm air therefore weighs less than a column of cold air of the same height. As elevation changes, pressure falls in each column, but the rates differ. Over the height of a house that difference matters.

In a typical cold-weather stack-effect pattern, indoor pressure relative to outdoors is negative near the bottom of the building and positive near the top. Air enters through available lower openings and leaves through upper openings. Somewhere between those regions is a neutral pressure level: at that particular height, the inside-outside pressure difference is zero, for the simplified case. The location is not automatically the middle of the building. The amount and distribution of leakage at different heights affect it.

Temperature difference and height matter. A taller, warmer building generally develops a stronger stack pressure pattern than a shorter building under otherwise similar conditions. But a building is not an isolated laboratory column. Doors, stairwells, ducts, chimneys, and room partitions change how well interior spaces communicate. A closed interior door can help create a local pressure relationship that differs from the simple whole-house picture.

### Wind adds a second pressure field

On a windy day, air piles up against the windward side of a house and flows around the edges and over the roof. Some exterior surfaces experience higher pressure; others experience suction. The actual pattern depends on wind speed, direction, nearby buildings, and the house's geometry. Consequently, wind may reinforce the stack effect at one opening and oppose it at another.

That is why one drafty window can seem quiet on Tuesday and troublesome on Wednesday. The opening has not changed. The conditions that drive it have. A tissue near a gap can indicate the direction of local air movement at that moment, but it cannot tell you the home's total leakage or identify the complete path.

### Fans can rearrange the entire map

Now turn on a kitchen exhaust hood. It sends indoor air outdoors. Unless another source supplies replacement air, the house tends to become more negative relative to outside. Replacement air enters through whatever paths offer the least resistance: a designed make-up-air opening, an open window, a building leak, or potentially an inappropriate route through a chimney or combustion appliance.

A forced-air heating system can also change local pressures. A supply duct leaking into an attic carries conditioned air outside the enclosure. A return duct leaking in a crawlspace can draw unconditioned air toward the system. The effects depend on where the leaks occur and whether those spaces are inside or outside the intended pressure boundary. Calling a duct leak 'inside the house' is not enough; one must know the boundary.

This matters because air can transport moisture, odors, particles, and combustion products along with heat. A cold-weather exfiltration leak near a roof is more than an energy concern. Warm indoor air moving into cold assemblies may cool below its dew point and deposit moisture there. A vapor-control material does not necessarily stop this flow: controlling diffusion through a material and controlling bulk air movement through a hole are different jobs.

### Follow the continuous boundary

An air barrier is best understood as a continuous system, not one magical product. Drywall, sheathing, membranes, sealants, and transitions may all help form it. The important question is whether the chosen boundary connects across floors, walls, roofs, openings, and penetrations. Where does it turn around a rim joist? How does it meet a window? Does the attic hatch join that boundary, or interrupt it?

Suppose you seal a visible crack around a window but leave a plumbing chase open from the basement to the attic. The large vertical pathway may continue to dominate the stack-driven flow. Conversely, a deliberate, well-connected air boundary can lower uncontrolled leakage even if the walls contain many different layers of material. Air tightness does not mean eliminating planned ventilation. It means separating accidental exchange from ventilation that can be measured and controlled.

### Testing separates stories from measurements

A blower door uses a calibrated fan to create a controlled pressure difference between a building and outdoors. With suitable instruments, it measures airflow needed to maintain that difference. A common diagnostic value is air changes per hour at fifty pascals, often called A C H fifty. It is a test quantity, not a claim that the house changes all its air at that rate during ordinary weather. Natural exchange depends on wind, temperature, and the actual pressure differences occurring over time.

A blower-door test tells you something about aggregate leakage. Smoke pencils, pressure measurements, and systematic investigation help locate particular paths. Thermal cameras can suggest where cold air may be entering, but a cool surface can also result from thermal bridging rather than airflow. The best diagnosis uses observations that distinguish explanations instead of treating every cold patch as the same defect.

Return to the basement crack and the attic hatch. Their behavior now has a causal account. They lie at different heights in a building with warmer indoor air; wind and fans modify the pressure field; the cracks supply paths. Repair is not a contest to apply sealant to the greatest number of spots. It is the work of identifying a continuous pressure boundary, finding consequential breaks, and preserving deliberate pathways for ventilation and safe equipment operation.

## Rain Control, Flashing, and Drainage Planes

A wall may look waterproof because its paint and siding are intact. But imagine wind pushing rain sideways against the wall, a hairline opening at a window trim joint, and water creeping into that joint. Has the wall already failed? Not necessarily. A well-designed exterior enclosure expects some water to get behind its outer cladding and gives it a continuous route back outside. A failing enclosure lets that water reach materials that cannot tolerate repeated wetting, or traps it where it cannot dry.

The first distinction is between a *rain-shedding surface* and a *water-control layer*. Siding, brick veneer, stucco, and similar claddings take the first beating. They reject much of the rain, and they also protect inner layers against ultraviolet light, impacts, and weather. But joints, cracks, laps, and penetrations make them imperfect barriers. Behind the cladding, the water-resistive barrier, or W R B, provides a second line of defense. In a drainable wall, that barrier connects to flashing and to an exit path. This is the drainage plane.

### A wall needs both down and out

Gravity is helpful only if we arrange the layers correctly. Imagine two overlapping sheets of paper standing vertically. If the upper sheet overlaps the outside face of the lower sheet, water can flow from one to the next without entering between them. Reverse that overlap, and the upper water flow meets a lip that can direct it behind the lower sheet. Builders call the correct arrangement shingle lapping. The goal is not merely to cover every piece of wood. It is to preserve a downhill path along the water-control layer.

That path must also lead *out*. A perfect vertical membrane that ends above an unflashed wooden sill can deliver rain straight onto the sill. A base flashing turns water away from the construction and includes a drip edge to shed it instead of allowing surface tension to carry it underneath. Flashings perform that turning function at windows, doors, roof-wall junctions, decks, and wall terminations. Where a roof runs into a taller wall, step flashing and properly detailed kickout flashing keep roof runoff from entering the wall. Without a kickout, a rain gutter or concentrated roof stream can wash into the siding edge repeatedly.

You can understand a flashing by asking three questions. What catches water arriving from above? What surface carries it across or around the interruption? Where is the exit, and can the water reach it without crossing into vulnerable materials? Those questions are often more illuminating than the brand name on the tape.

### The window is a small roof in the wall

A window interrupts the wall's water-control plane at the head, jambs, and sill. Flashing at the head diverts water arriving from above. The jamb details guide water toward the outside, while a sill pan gives incidental water a route to escape. The sequence of layers determines whether those details work together: each upper layer must drain over the next lower one. If those overlaps are reversed, the same water may be led behind the protection rather than out of the wall.

A window sealant joint is useful, but one bead of caulk is not a substitute for drainage. Sealants age, substrates move, and sun and temperature change adhesion. If the entire strategy depends on one inaccessible bead remaining flawless forever, a small defect can become a large hidden leak. The better question is what the assembly does *after* that bead fails. Does water find the pan and leave, or soak into the rough opening? A drained sill needs an exit path; plugging that exit while trying to make a window 'extra waterproof' can reverse the intended design.

Wind complicates the story. Wind-driven rain can be driven sideways and upward through discontinuities, rather than only falling down. A simple vertical overlap may still need compatible sealing, flanges, drainage gaps, or other details appropriate to exposure. The correct construction depends on the window type, cladding system, climate, and manufacturer instructions. The governing idea is continuity: transitions between materials are where water-control systems commonly lose their logic.

### Drainage needs space and time

A drainage plane is not always a wide visible cavity. Depending on materials and construction, water may pass along textured wrap, through small gaps, or through a designed rainscreen space. A ventilated or drained gap can reduce the time that cladding and underlying surfaces remain wet. But the gap must remain open where drainage is intended; mortar droppings, compressed insulation, misplaced adhesive, or sealed weep locations can obstruct it.

Some claddings absorb and temporarily store significant rainwater. When the sun warms a wet reservoir cladding, vapor can be driven inward as well as outward. That is a different problem from an obvious liquid leak, and its solution depends on the wall's vapor-control and drying strategy. The practical lesson is that a wall does more than repel rain in the instant a storm occurs. It must also drain and dry between storms.

### Diagnose the route, not just the stain

Suppose paint blisters below a second-story window. The water may enter at that window, at a roof-to-wall joint above it, through a service penetration, or through a defect much farther up the drainage path. Gravity usually moves water down, but framing cavities and horizontal interfaces can divert it. A stain identifies where water emerged or accumulated, not necessarily where it entered. Investigation should begin with the roofline, intersections, flashing continuity, and opening details before blaming the paint.

A useful exercise is to draw a wall section with a roof meeting it, a window, and a base flashing. Draw a blue line following any drop of water that gets past the siding. Do not stop at the membrane. Follow the water through every overlap, across the window head, around the jamb, onto the sill pan, and finally out beyond the wall. If your line reaches an unprotected wood edge or a sealed pocket with no drainage, you have discovered a design question worth asking.

The strongest mental model is simple: a weather-facing material sacrifices perfection so that a continuous secondary system can manage imperfection. A building lasts when its layers cooperate to keep liquid water moving down and out, while allowing wet materials to dry.

## Vapor Diffusion, Condensation, and Drying Paths

Rain control does not by itself explain water vapor or the assembly’s ability to dry.

A wet window on a winter morning is a small demonstration of a building-scale problem. Water that had been invisible in indoor air has become liquid on cold glass. There was no roof leak, no broken pipe, and no rain passing through the window. The air and the glass met under conditions where the vapor could not remain in the air at the local temperature. If that same change of state occurs inside a wall, nobody sees the droplets until damage has developed.

To understand it, we need to distinguish three ways moisture moves. Liquid water runs and is pulled through fine pores by capillary action. Air movement carries water vapor through cracks and openings. And water vapor can diffuse directly through materials even when the air itself is not flowing. Those mechanisms have different driving forces and require different controls. A sheet that slows diffusion may leave every electrical-box opening unsealed. An excellent air barrier can still permit vapor diffusion. A wall that drains rain can nevertheless trap water vapor.

### What the air can hold is not a fixed number

Warm air generally has a higher saturation vapor pressure than cold air. The actual water-vapor pressure expresses how much vapor is present; relative humidity compares that amount with the saturation level at the current temperature. When air is cooled without removing moisture, its relative humidity rises. At the dew point, the actual vapor pressure reaches saturation. Further cooling can produce condensation on a suitable surface. We have all seen this on a cold beverage glass.

Indoor air can feel reasonably dry at a comfortable temperature and still carry water vapor into a wall cavity. If it leaks through an outlet opening and reaches cold sheathing, condensation may form there. Moving air can deliver moisture rapidly to one concentrated cold spot; diffusion through intact gypsum board is a different and generally slower route. The distinction changes how we diagnose the wetting and how we try to stop it.

A vapor retarder changes the rate at which vapor diffuses through a material. Its permeability is often described in perms. Low-perm materials impede diffusion more strongly than high-perm materials. An air barrier, by contrast, is a continuous boundary designed to resist air movement under pressure differences. Some membranes can perform both jobs, but neither job follows automatically from the material's label. Taped joints, transitions, penetrations, and continuity across a whole assembly determine whether the air-control function is actually present.

### The cold surface moves when we insulate

Imagine an ordinary framed wall in winter. Indoor heat travels outward through the interior finish and insulation toward the colder sheathing and cladding. If most insulation is between the studs, the outer sheathing can become quite cold. Air leaking from the room may condense there, and diffusion can contribute to the moisture load depending on the materials and conditions.

Now add continuous insulation outside the sheathing. The thermal resistance has been redistributed. The sheathing may remain warmer in winter because a larger fraction of the temperature drop occurs outside it. That reduces condensation risk at the sheathing for a given indoor moisture condition. But exterior insulation may also slow outward drying if it is vapor-resistant. Whether this is a good assembly therefore depends on both its thermal proportions and its vapor permeability, not on the insulation's thickness alone.

The reverse seasonal problem also matters. In a humid summer, outdoor vapor pressure may exceed indoor vapor pressure, especially when air conditioning cools interior surfaces. Moisture can be driven inward. Rain-wetted cladding heated by sunshine can intensify the inward vapor drive. A wall designed solely around a winter picture may have an unexpected summer drying problem.

### A vapor barrier is not a universal improvement

Suppose somebody discovers a damp cavity and decides that adding an impermeable layer on each face will guarantee dryness. They may instead build a moisture trap. Even a carefully constructed wall experiences incidental wetting from construction moisture, small leaks, or imperfect details. If both directions of drying are severely restricted, the moisture that gets in may take a very long time to leave.

That does not mean low-permeability layers are always wrong. They can be essential where the climate, assembly and interior humidity require them. It means their placement and interaction must be deliberate. One wall might be designed primarily to dry outward. Another may be designed to dry inward. A third permits some drying in both directions. Ground-contact assemblies have yet another moisture source, and should not be treated as if they were above-grade walls.

This is the important design question: after plausible wetting, which way can the material dry, and how much drying capacity remains? The answer needs to consider both diffusion and moving air, as well as rain drainage and capillary separation. Moisture management is a system, not a single product.

### Diagnose moisture by its timing

If dampness appears only during cold weather, investigate indoor humidity, cold surfaces and leakage routes as well as exterior water entry. If it follows wind-driven rain, inspect flashing and rain-control continuity. If it emerges near a cool surface during humid weather, examine condensation and summer vapor drive. A musty basement wall can involve liquid groundwater, capillary rise, and humid air condensing on cool masonry at the same time.

Do not diagnose from a photograph of a stain alone. Measure where appropriate, inspect the sequence of materials, and track what conditions preceded the event. A wall section is a useful reasoning tool: draw the thermal boundary, air boundary, water-control layer, and places where vapor meets a cold surface. Then mark the paths that permit the assembly to dry.

The goal is not a perfectly impermeable building. It is a durable building that keeps large moisture loads out, limits hidden condensation, and can recover when small amounts of water enter. A wall succeeds not only because it stayed dry during a storm, but because it had a way to get dry afterward.

## Airflow Pathways in Rooms, Duct Chases, and Bypasses

Airflow can also take concealed routes that are not apparent from the visible room surfaces.

Imagine a house on a cold, windy evening. A bedroom is drafty even though its windows are closed. The hallway thermostat reads normally. In the attic, insulation looks deep and continuous, yet frost sometimes appears on the underside of the roof sheathing. These observations can share a cause: air moving through a hidden path between the heated rooms and an unconditioned space. Finding that path requires thinking about pressure, continuity, and the actual location of the building's air boundary.

### Follow the path, not the visible crack

A room is connected to other rooms by door undercuts, transfer grilles, plumbing penetrations, electrical openings, and intentional ventilation. It may also be connected to an attic, crawlspace, or wall cavity through an opening nobody intended to leave. Air moves through those connections when there is a pressure difference. Warm air rising through a stairwell and leaving at a high leak can draw replacement air in at lower levels. A windy wall can pressurize one side of a building and depressurize another. Mechanical fans add their own pressure fields.

A small crack is not necessarily the important leak. A modest opening that connects to a large vertical chase can move more air than several visible gaps that end in nearly closed cavities. Imagine a plumbing stack passing from a basement, behind a bathroom wall, into the attic. If the framing around it is open at both ends, the chase may become a vertical air corridor. Insulation tucked beside the pipe might reduce conductive heat loss locally, yet still allow air to flow along the pipe. Air-sealing and insulating are different tasks.

The first diagnostic question is therefore not, 'Where can I see a gap?' It is, 'Where is the continuous air-control surface supposed to be, and where might it have been interrupted?' At a vented attic, that surface is normally at the ceiling plane rather than the underside of the roof. The open top of a partition, a dropped soffit above kitchen cabinets, a chimney chase, and a duct boot can all interrupt that plane. The locations must be understood in their particular building rather than assumed from one textbook drawing.

### Pressure links rooms and equipment

A closed bedroom door can alter a heating system's airflow even if the supply register stays open. If supply air enters the bedroom but has no adequate return path, the bedroom may become relatively positive while the central hallway becomes relatively negative. Air then seeks unintended routes through exterior and interior leaks. In a leaky building, the result can include both uneven comfort and additional envelope leakage. The obvious complaint, a draft at the window, may be a downstream effect of the air-distribution system rather than a defective window seal.

Consider an illustrative room receiving one hundred cubic feet of supply air per minute with a substantially smaller intentional return path. Conservation of mass says the remainder must leave through some combination of door gaps, transfers, or leakage while the fan operates. It does not tell us which route dominates. That depends on the resistance of each path and the resulting pressure. Closing registers elsewhere to 'balance' the room can change static pressure and even harm system operation, so first determine the existing paths.

Duct leakage is another distinct problem. An airtight duct may pass through a poorly sealed building chase, or the chase may be tight while the duct itself leaks. A supply leak to an attic throws conditioned air away; a return leak may pull dusty, hot, cold, or humid air into the equipment. The two failures have different diagnostics. Looking only at the visible register does not establish either condition.

### Build evidence by changing one condition

Begin with a sketch showing the room, adjacent spaces, supply and return locations, ceiling penetrations, and likely vertical paths. Note whether a symptom appears only with the air handler running, only with certain doors closed, on windy days, or during strong indoor-outdoor temperature differences. These timing differences are valuable clues. An air movement that stops when the fan stops points toward a mechanical pressure contribution, though natural forces may still be present.

A trained energy auditor can use a blower door to impose a known building pressure difference, measure whole-building leakage, and help locate individual leaks with appropriate diagnostic tools. Pressure measurements across a closed door can reveal a return-path imbalance. A thermal image may reveal temperature patterns associated with air movement, but it measures surface radiation, not airflow. Temperature patterns also depend on insulation, moisture, material properties, and camera settings. Air movement should be corroborated with pressure and controlled-condition observations.

When investigating an attic, do not disturb loose insulation indiscriminately: identify possible asbestos or other legacy materials before disturbance, and respect structural limits on where it is safe to step. A gap around a metal flue is not simply an invitation to spray ordinary foam. Heat-producing equipment, chimneys, and fire-rated assemblies require their specified clearances and approved materials. An opening that carries intentional ventilation or drainage must remain able to perform that function. Sealing the wrong path can exchange an energy problem for a combustion, moisture, or indoor-air problem.

### Decide what a successful correction would prove

A credible correction identifies the actual boundary, closes the unintended bypass with durable compatible materials, and preserves required ventilation, combustion safety, and access. Its success is more than a photograph of neatly applied sealant. The original draft or room pressure imbalance should diminish under comparable conditions, and the surrounding enclosure should still have a workable drying path. If a repair changes building tightness substantially, ventilation and combustion-appliance operation deserve reevaluation.

Return to the cold bedroom. The solution might be an attic chase closure, a return-air path, a duct repair, or several coordinated changes. A window replacement chosen before testing could leave the dominant leak untouched. The larger lesson is that a building's hidden connections govern much of its behavior. Follow where air can go, what pushes it, and what boundary should have stopped it. That reasoning turns a draft from a complaint into a testable physical problem.

### A House Is a System of Flows: Heat, Air, Water, and Drying

**Track:** Building Science. **Background:** Everyday experience with a house; basic arithmetic. **Purpose:** establish a physical explanation of the building enclosure before recommending materials or repairs.

#### Technical companion: two separated mechanisms

For a simplified plane layer, steady conductive heat flow per unit area is `q/A ≈ ΔT/R_total`, where `R_total` is the thermal resistance per unit area in consistent units. This is not a model for air infiltration or for the full multidimensional thermal bridges around openings.

For idealized moist air, the dew point is determined by temperature, water-vapor partial pressure and total pressure; relative humidity is not an absolute moisture concentration. The numerical example in the lecture (20°C, 50% RH, dew point approximately 9.3°C) is an approximation, not a condensation prediction for a particular assembly.

### Heat Flow, Thermal Bridges, and Whole-Assembly R-Values

**Track:** Building Science. **Background:** Introductory heat transfer and building enclosure concepts. **Purpose:** replace the idea of “insulation as a single number” with an assembly-level account of heat flow.

#### Technical companion: assembly calculations and boundaries

For an ideal flat layer, `R = L/k` in SI thermal-resistance-per-area units, with `L` thickness and `k` conductivity. A steady approximation is `q'' = (T_in - T_out)/R_total`. For parallel area fractions `f_i` with paths `R_i`, `U_effective = sum(f_i / R_i)` and `R_effective = 1/U_effective`. Our illustrative example: `0.75/20 + 0.25/6 = 0.079166...`, so `R_eff ≈ 12.63`; with R-5 shared continuous insulation: `0.75/25 + 0.25/11 ≈ 0.05273`, so `R_eff ≈ 18.97`. The series/parallel approximation excludes junction losses, thermal storage and infiltration.

**Evidence and application boundaries:** Consult DOE building-envelope guidance and recognized assembly calculation methods before making quantitative performance claims. Surface temperatures and condensation require humidity, thermal-bridge geometry and boundary conditions. Adopted energy codes, climate zone, vapor-management strategy, and manufacturer assemblies can alter appropriate detailing.

**Exercise:** Draw the cavity, framing and window-header routes through an exterior wall. Mark which layers are continuous across every path. Explain why adding cavity insulation alone does not eliminate framing conduction.

**Claim map:** Parallel-path and reciprocal-U calculations: thermal conduction principles; building framing and material references in `metadata/sources.json`. Enclosure details: DOE and USDA reference records. The worked numerical model is original and illustrative.

### Air Leakage, Pressure Differences, and Stack Effect

**Track:** Building Science. **Background:** Elementary heat flow and enclosure concepts. **Purpose:** explain why the same gap behaves differently with wind, weather, and mechanical equipment.

#### Technical companion (non-spoken)

##### A simplified stack-pressure relation

For two approximately isothermal columns, the difference in inside-outside pressure change over height \(H\) is approximately \(\Delta p(H)=gH(\rho_{out}-\rho_{in})\). Location of the neutral pressure level determines the sign at a given elevation; real assemblies require a leakage-network model and may have multiple interacting zones. For fixed pressure, leakage flow through an opening is often approximated empirically as \(Q=C |\Delta p|^n\), with the exponent dependent on leakage geometry. Do not use a universal exponent or infer natural infiltration directly from ACH50.

##### Observation exercise

Sketch the intended air barrier in a vertical house section. Trace continuity through the attic access, top plates, wall-to-foundation junction, window perimeter, and service penetrations. Separately mark the paths for planned ventilation, drainage, and combustion exhaust. For a draft near a window, record outdoor temperature, wind direction, and active exhaust devices rather than assuming the cause from touch alone.

##### Scope and review

The qualitative stack-effect model is established building physics. Diagnostic thresholds, exhaust make-up air, and safe combustion venting depend on actual appliances, configuration, local adopted codes, and professional testing. This lecture provides no combustion-appliance commissioning protocol. No field design has been verified.

### Vapor Diffusion, Condensation, and Drying Paths

**Track:** Building Science. **Background:** Heat flow, air leakage, and rain control. **Purpose:** understand water-vapor transport without confusing it with bulk water or air leakage.

#### Technical companion (non-spoken)

##### Mechanisms and a diagnostic sketch

- **Diffusion:** vapor flow driven by a difference in water-vapor partial pressure; rate depends on permeability and area.
- **Air transport:** vapor carried with bulk air through an imperfect air barrier, potentially focused on cold surfaces.
- **Liquid transport:** gravity, hydrostatic pressure, surface tension and capillarity move liquid water through and along materials.
- **Dew point:** at a given vapor pressure, the temperature at which saturation occurs. Condensation risk depends on local temperature and vapor pressure, not just outdoor air temperature.

Draw a wall from interior to exterior and assign, separately, the thermal-control layer, air-control layer, rain-control layer and vapor-control strategy. Then mark drying paths. Do not use a single 'vapor barrier' symbol for all four functions.

##### Specificity and review

Assembly analyses must account for climate, indoor humidity, solar exposure, rain reservoir claddings, insulation ratio, and measured vapor properties. Simple steady-state dew-point sketches are teaching approximations, not substitutes for assembly-specific hygrothermal assessment. Review a complete detail, including windows, roof transitions, and initial moisture, before recommending a retrofit.
