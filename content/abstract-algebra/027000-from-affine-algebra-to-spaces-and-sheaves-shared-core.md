# Dimension, normalization, spectra, and geometric gluing

## Krull Dimension and Hilbert's Nullstellensatz

Suppose we write down several polynomial equations in several variables. Their common solutions form a geometric set: perhaps a few points, a curve, or a surface. But the polynomials themselves live in an algebraic ring. Can we recover the geometry by studying ideals in that ring? And how can we define dimension when no coordinate chart is given?

Two foundational ideas answer these questions. Krull dimension counts chains of prime ideals. Hilbert's Nullstellensatz links ideals of polynomials to their common zeros over an algebraically closed field. Together they give the dictionary that underlies affine algebraic geometry.

### Geometry reverses inclusion

Work initially over the complex numbers. Let R be the polynomial ring in variables x and y. An ideal I is a set of polynomials closed under addition and multiplication by arbitrary polynomials. Its vanishing set V of I consists of all points where every polynomial in I evaluates to zero.

If I is contained in a larger ideal J, then J imposes at least as many equations. Consequently V of J is contained in V of I. More equations mean fewer solutions. The correspondence reverses containment.

For example, the ideal generated by x describes the vertical axis. Add y to generate the ideal x, y, and the vanishing set shrinks to the origin. The zero ideal imposes no equations and has the whole plane as its vanishing set. The unit ideal contains the constant polynomial one and has no common zeros.

This reversal is why a chain of prime ideals measures geometry in the opposite direction to what one might initially guess. A small prime ideal describes a large irreducible geometric set; a maximal ideal, over an algebraically closed field, describes an individual point.

### What makes a prime ideal geometric?

An ideal P is prime when a product belonging to P forces at least one factor to belong to P. Equivalently, the quotient R modulo P is an integral domain. Algebraically, it says the quotient has no zero divisors. Geometrically, over an algebraically closed field, it says the corresponding algebraic set is irreducible: it cannot be written as the union of two proper algebraic subsets.

Consider the polynomial xy. Its zero set is the union of the two coordinate axes. The ideal generated by xy is not prime. The reason is immediate: xy lies in the ideal, but neither x nor y does. There are two distinct irreducible pieces, corresponding to the prime ideals generated by x and by y.

At their intersection sits the maximal ideal generated by x and y. The inclusions from the axis ideals into the origin ideal reflect the geometric containment of the origin in each axis.

### Defining Krull dimension

The Krull dimension of a commutative ring is the supremum of lengths of strict chains of prime ideals. Each strict containment counts as one step. The polynomial ring in one variable over a field has dimension one. The polynomial ring in two variables has dimension two.

To see why the dimension of the plane is at least two, write down a chain: the zero ideal is strictly contained in the ideal generated by x, which is strictly contained in the ideal generated by x and y. Every ideal in this chain is prime. Thus we have two strict inclusions.

The upper bound is subtler, but it follows from the dimension theorem for polynomial rings: adjoining n independent polynomial variables to a field produces a ring of Krull dimension n.

The coordinate ring of our two crossing axes is the quotient by the ideal generated by xy. It has dimension one, although the picture lives in the plane. That is the intended behavior. Dimension is measuring freedom *within* the algebraic set, not the number of coordinates used to draw it.

### The weak Nullstellensatz

Now the field hypothesis becomes important. Over an algebraically closed field k, Hilbert's weak Nullstellensatz says that every maximal ideal of the polynomial ring k in n variables has the form generated by the coordinate differences: x one minus a one, through x n minus a n, for some point a in k to the n.

A maximal ideal therefore corresponds to an ordinary geometric point. For one variable this is familiar: an irreducible polynomial over an algebraically closed field has degree one. The many-variable theorem extends the idea, and its proof uses a substantive fact about finitely generated field algebras, not simply a one-variable factorization argument.

Over the real numbers the statement fails. The ideal generated by x squared plus one in the real polynomial ring in one variable is maximal, because its quotient is a field isomorphic to the complex numbers. But it is not the ideal of any real point. Algebraic closure is essential, not decorative.

### The strong Nullstellensatz

A set of equations may conceal multiplicities. The ideals generated by x and by x squared have the same zero set, namely the vertical axis. If we know only which points solve the equations, we cannot distinguish those ideals.

The correct algebraic operation is radical. The radical of an ideal I contains every polynomial whose some positive power belongs to I. Hilbert's strong Nullstellensatz says: over an algebraically closed field, the ideal of all polynomials vanishing on V of I is exactly the radical of I.

Thus geometric zero sets correspond not to arbitrary ideals, but to radical ideals. Prime ideals among them correspond to irreducible algebraic sets. Maximal ideals correspond to points. This is a precise bridge, not merely an analogy between equations and shapes.

For our example, the ideal generated by x squared has radical generated by x. Squaring an equation changes algebraic multiplicity but not the classical set of zeroes. Later algebraic geometry restores that multiplicity information using schemes and nonreduced coordinate rings. The ordinary Nullstellensatz is the first level of the story, not the last.

### Why the dimension count matters

For an irreducible affine algebraic variety over a field, the Krull dimension of its coordinate ring matches the transcendence degree of its rational function field. Informally, this counts algebraically independent parameters. A curve requires one independent parameter generically; a surface requires two.

But do not confuse dimension with the number of generators of a ring, or with the number of equations. In a polynomial ring, equations may be redundant, and one equation can have several components. Prime chains and transcendence degree are the robust invariants.

The central lesson is a three-way translation. Ideals record equations, prime ideals record irreducible geometric pieces, and chains of primes measure dimension. The Nullstellensatz tells us exactly when passing from equations to their common zero set loses only radical information. It turns algebra into a language for geometry, with explicit hypotheses that explain when the translation works.

## Integral Dependence, Normalization, and Singular Curves

Picture a curve in the plane that comes into the origin and leaves along the same tangent direction. One elementary example is the cusp described by y squared equals x cubed. Its shape is controlled by polynomial equations, but there is a second way to describe it: let x equal t squared and y equal t cubed. As t moves through the real numbers, those formulas trace the cusp.

What looks like a change of coordinates hides an algebraic operation. The original curve's coordinate ring contains t squared and t cubed, but not t. Add t, and the resulting ring is simply the polynomial ring in one variable. The cusp's complicated local geometry has been replaced by a smooth parameter line. To understand why this works, we need the language of integral dependence and normalization.

### Algebraic does not mean integral

Let A be a domain inside a larger field. An element u of that field is integral over A when it satisfies a monic polynomial whose coefficients lie in A. Monic means that the coefficient of the highest power of u is one. This apparently small condition changes everything.

Every element of a field extension that is algebraic over a field satisfies a monic polynomial over the base field. But for a general ring A, an element of its fraction field might be algebraic without being integral over A. For example one half is in the fraction field of the integers and certainly satisfies a linear equation with rational coefficients. It is not integral over the integers. If it were, a monic polynomial with integer coefficients would force an integer divisibility relation that cannot hold.

The distinction appears even more sharply in the cusp. Let A be the ring generated over a field k by t squared and t cubed. The missing parameter t satisfies the monic equation T squared minus t squared equals zero; its coefficient t squared lies in A. Therefore t is integral over A. And t lies in A's fraction field, since away from t equals zero it is t cubed divided by t squared.

The ring A is therefore not integrally closed: there is an integral element in its own fraction field that A does not contain.

### A finite-module criterion

Why is the word integral important? If u satisfies a monic equation of degree d, every sufficiently high power of u can be reduced to a linear combination of one, u, up through u to the power d minus one, with coefficients in A. So A adjoin u is a finitely generated A-module.

Conversely, if the powers of u act on a finite faithful A-module in a suitable way, the determinant trick gives a monic polynomial satisfied by u. This is the practical principle behind integral extensions: integrality is tied to module finiteness, not merely to algebraic equations with arbitrary denominators.

For the cusp, adding t gives k[t]. As a module over k[t squared, t cubed], it is generated by one and t. Every higher power is already in the original ring or becomes so after one multiplication by t. Thus the passage from cusp coordinates to the parameter line is finite in the algebraic sense.

### The normalization

The integral closure of a domain A in its fraction field is the collection of all fraction-field elements integral over A. This collection is itself a ring. Passing from A to its integral closure is called normalization. A domain equal to its integral closure is normal.

In our example, k[t] is integrally closed, and t is integral over A. Consequently the normalization of k[t squared, t cubed] is k[t]. The map of coordinate spaces goes the opposite way: a point of the t-line is sent to the cusp point with coordinates t squared and t cubed.

The map is one-to-one on ordinary points over an algebraically closed characteristic-zero field, but it is not an isomorphism of algebraic curves. At the origin, the inverse parameter would be the rational function y divided by x, which is not regular there. In the coordinate ring, the missing t witnesses the failure.

The normalization separates issues that a drawing cannot. A cusp has one branch but a singular tangent behavior. A nodal curve may have two branches passing through the same point, and normalization can separate their preimages. Normalization is not merely a smoothing brush; it records how the algebraic branches fit together, and its precise effect depends on the dimension and hypotheses.

### A local view of the singular point

To detect the cusp in algebra, examine the maximal ideal generated by x and y at the origin. For the ring k[x,y] modulo y squared minus x cubed, the linear parts of that relation vanish. Thus, at the origin, the cotangent space has two independent generators: the classes of x and y modulo the square of the maximal ideal. Yet the curve has algebraic dimension one.

At a smooth point of a curve, the tangent space has dimension one. At the cusp, the tangent space has dimension two. This mismatch is one algebraic definition of singularity. By contrast, k[t] has a one-dimensional tangent space at each of its points.

There is a lesson here about coordinates. Introducing a parameter is not necessarily an invertible coordinate change. Sometimes it enlarges the ring of regular functions. The original singularity disappears on the normalized curve because the underlying local algebra has changed.

### Limits of normalization

For reduced curves of finite type over a perfect field, normalization gives a nonsingular curve, after treating components appropriately. In dimensions two and higher, normal varieties can still be singular. Thus normality is weaker than regularity in general, and normalization is not a universal resolution of singularities.

Nor should one assume the normalization is finite for every domain without hypotheses. It is finite for the finitely generated algebras over a field relevant here, but finiteness requires care in more general rings.

The central idea is useful far beyond the cusp: integral dependence allows us to enlarge a ring without changing its fraction field, while retaining enough finiteness to control the geometry. A singular point may be evidence that a coordinate ring does not yet contain every function that behaves algebraically like a regular one. Normalization makes that deficiency precise.

## Prime Spectra: Turning Rings into Geometric Spaces

Imagine being handed a commutative ring rather than a picture. It might be a polynomial ring, the integers, or a ring with elements whose squares vanish. What kind of space could this ring describe? The answer that underlies modern algebraic geometry is to treat prime ideals as points, and to retain enough algebra at every point to know which functions are locally available.

Why prime ideals rather than maximal ideals? A maximal ideal behaves like an ordinary point: evaluation at an algebraic point usually produces a field, and its kernel is maximal. But a variety also has irreducible pieces. Each such piece ought to have a generic point representing the entire piece, not a specially selected location on it. Prime ideals provide both kinds of points in one construction.

### A first picture made from the integers

Consider the ring of integers. Its prime ideals are the zero ideal and the ideals generated by individual prime numbers. The prime spectrum of the integers therefore has one point for each prime, together with one extra point corresponding to zero. That extra point is not an integer and not an additional prime number. It is the generic point of the entire space.

The word generic has a precise topological meaning. The closure of the zero-ideal point is the whole spectrum. The closure of the point corresponding to the ideal generated by seven consists only of that point. In the Zariski topology, therefore, the zero ideal is not closed, while the nonzero prime ideals of the integers are closed points.

One might try to draw these points on a line, but the ordinary Euclidean picture misleads. The Zariski topology is deliberately coarse. It records where collections of algebraic functions vanish, not how far apart points are. A basic open set determined by an integer six consists of all prime ideals that do not contain six. It removes the points two and three but retains every other prime point and the generic point.

### Closed sets and basic opens

Take any commutative ring with identity, called R. For an ideal I, consider the set of prime ideals containing I. We declare such sets closed. Their complements form the Zariski open sets. The fundamental basic opens have a particularly simple description: D of f consists of prime ideals at which f is not contained in the prime.

These basic opens are compatible with multiplication. Being outside both the vanishing set of f and the vanishing set of g is the same as being outside the vanishing set of their product. Thus the intersection of D of f and D of g is D of f times g. This modest identity will let us organize local algebra on overlapping regions.

A prime ideal p has closure equal to the set of primes containing p. The more ideals contain p, the more specialized those points are. An inclusion of primes therefore gives a specialization relation. This relation, rather than distance, controls much of the geometry.

Now try the polynomial ring in one variable over an algebraically closed field. It has the generic zero ideal and one maximal ideal for each scalar value of the variable. We recover a line of closed points, but with an extra generic point. Over a field that is not algebraically closed, closed points may correspond to irreducible polynomials of higher degree. That distinction matters: an affine scheme is not simply a set of solutions over the original field.

### What functions are allowed near a point?

Topology alone loses essential information. We must attach rings of functions to open sets. On D of f, the natural functions are fractions whose denominators are powers of f. Their ring is the localization R with f inverted. Near a prime p, a denominator is legal whenever it lies outside p, giving the local ring R at p.

This is an algebraic analogue of passing to functions that make sense in a neighborhood. A rational expression need not be defined everywhere. On an open where its denominator does not vanish, it becomes regular. The structure sheaf organizes these compatible local rings, together with the rule that a function restricted to a smaller region must agree with its original value.

For an affine scheme, the global regular functions are exactly the original ring R. Moreover, a ring map from R to S induces a map from the prime spectrum of S to that of R, by taking inverse images of prime ideals. Notice the reversal of arrows. Geometric maps go in the opposite direction from maps of function rings. This is not a quirk of notation: pulling functions backward along a map is fundamental in geometry.

### A point with invisible thickness

Here is the test that separates schemes from mere solution sets. Let k be a field and construct a ring by adjoining an element epsilon whose square equals zero. Its spectrum contains only one prime ideal, generated by epsilon. As a topological space it is a single point, indistinguishable from the spectrum of k.

But the rings of global functions are different. On the thickened point, epsilon is not zero, though its square is zero. A function can have a first-order infinitesimal part that no ordinary field-valued point detects. The structure sheaf preserves this information. Deleting the nilpotent would change the scheme, even though no point would move.

This explains why algebraic geometry asks for locally ringed spaces, not just point sets. It can study a curve touching another curve with multiplicity, a family degenerating at a special parameter, or an equation whose infinitesimal deformation matters. Such phenomena are lost if we remember only the visible solutions.

### The local-to-global promise

A scheme is a space that can be covered by regions of the form prime spectrum of a ring, with compatible structure sheaves. This generalizes the construction beyond one global coordinate ring. Like a manifold assembled from coordinate charts, a scheme can have no single coordinate system that covers it all.

We have gained a dictionary. Prime ideals become points. Localization becomes restriction to a basic open. Rings at primes describe local behavior. Nilpotents record infinitesimal thickness. Inclusions of primes encode specialization. And homomorphisms of rings turn into geometric maps with their direction reversed.

None of these replacements is decorative. Each answers a concrete problem left unresolved by describing a variety only as its field-valued solutions. The ring spectrum gives arithmetic and algebraic geometry a common language: one that can speak equally well about a polynomial curve and about the prime numbers in the integers.

## Sheaves and Quasi-Coherent Modules: Algebra that Glues

Suppose you solve an equation near one point and solve it again near a neighboring point. Is there a single solution covering both neighborhoods? Only if the two local solutions agree wherever their neighborhoods overlap. This is the gluing problem, and it arises in differential equations, geometry, and number theory. Sheaves are the language that turns the agreement condition into mathematics.

A sheaf does not merely record which functions exist on each open set. It knows how to restrict them, and it imposes two rules. If two sections agree on an open cover, they are equal. If compatible sections are given on a cover, they glue uniquely. These rules express a familiar idea precisely: a function is determined locally, and consistent local data define a global function.

### From modules to local modules

Let R be a commutative ring and M an R-module. We already know how to localize M. On a basic open D of f in the prime spectrum, use the module M with f inverted. Because the intersection of D of f and D of g is D of their product, the two localized modules restrict to a common module on the overlap. This is the core of the quasi-coherent sheaf associated to M, often pronounced M tilde.

For example, let R be the polynomial ring in one variable and let M be R modulo the ideal generated by x. On an open where x is invertible, the module vanishes: x annihilates M, while x has become a unit. So this sheaf is supported on the closed point x equals zero. A quotient module has become a geometric object localized at one place.

Compare that with the free module R itself. Its associated sheaf is the structure sheaf, whose sections are regular functions. A rank-two free module corresponds to two copies of the structure sheaf. A projective module gives a locally free sheaf of finite rank. Thus the algebraic distinction between free and projective turns into the geometric distinction between having a global frame and merely local frames.

### The affine theorem that makes the dictionary exact

On an affine scheme, the module and its associated quasi-coherent sheaf determine each other. Start with M, form the sheaf M tilde, then take global sections: you recover M. Conversely, every quasi-coherent sheaf on the affine scheme arises from its module of global sections. This is an equivalence of categories, not just a resemblance between examples.

The consequence is striking. Kernels, cokernels, exact sequences, tensor products, and many familiar module constructions can be read as operations on sheaves. Localization is flat, so exactness survives passage to an affine basic open. If a module map has a kernel, the corresponding sheaf map has the associated kernel sheaf. If we tensor two modules, their associated sheaves tensor to the sheaf associated to the tensor product.

There is, however, a limit to the dictionary. Global sections on a general scheme do not necessarily recover the whole sheaf. When there is no one affine chart covering the space, a sheaf may have interesting local sections but no useful global ones. The gluing geometry is no longer dispensable.

### Two charts for a projective line

Consider the projective line over a field. It has two standard affine charts, each an ordinary affine line. One chart uses a coordinate t, and the other uses s. On the overlap, neither coordinate is zero, and the coordinates satisfy s equals one divided by t.

Suppose we want a line bundle on the projective line. Trivialize it separately on each chart, then specify how the two local frames are identified on the overlap. Multiplication by a power of t gives a transition function. Different powers encode different twists. We cannot detect the entire projective line by choosing just one chart, and the transition function is information not contained in either chart alone.

This is the algebraic counterpart of the Möbius example, but the gluing is now performed in the Zariski topology, with regular functions rather than merely continuous functions. The Picard group of the projective line over a field is the integers; the twisting degree supplies the invariant. Its degree-zero member is the trivial line bundle. Other members may have quite different spaces of global sections.

In particular, the line bundle conventionally called O of minus one has no nonzero global sections, even though every point has a neighborhood on which it is trivial. The absence of global sections does not mean the bundle is empty or zero. It means that the local frames cannot be patched to a nonzero global section in the required way.

### Agreement is not automatic

Here is a useful miniature version of gluing. Cover the affine line by basic opens D of f and D of g, where f and g generate the unit ideal. Suppose a section of M localized at f and another localized at g agree after both denominators are inverted. Then the sheaf property says they come from a unique global section of M. For this two-open cover, the compatibility condition is the equalizer of a pair of restriction maps.

Why does the unit-ideal hypothesis matter? It ensures the two opens really cover every prime. If f and g vanish together at some prime, that point was never covered, and compatible data away from it need not determine what happens there.

This is the bridge to descent. A complicated object may be assembled from manageable pieces provided we have an overlap rule satisfying consistency conditions, especially on triple overlaps. The same principle recurs for bundles, modules, and more elaborate geometric structures. The hard part is often not solving the local problem; it is proving that the proposed identifications really glue.

### What sheaves make possible

Quasi-coherent sheaves turn algebraic geometry into a form of localized module theory. A coherent sheaf, when finiteness hypotheses are available, is a more controlled version useful for finite presentations and geometric equations. Both connect local linear algebra to global geometry without pretending that local triviality guarantees global triviality.

There is an important separation of responsibilities. On an affine scheme, the original ring and its modules are enough. On a scheme assembled from multiple affine charts, the transition data matters. That is exactly why algebraic geometry needs both a dictionary from rings to spaces and a theory of sheaves over those spaces. It lets us calculate locally without discarding what the global shape forces us to remember.

## Etale Maps, Derivatives, and Unramified Geometry

Consider the map that squares a number. Away from zero, the two square roots of a nonzero complex number move smoothly as that number changes. At zero, the two roots merge, and the derivative of the squaring map vanishes. The two-sheeted picture breaks at one special value. Algebraic geometers call this behavior ramification.

The distinction can be phrased entirely in terms of rings. We want an algebraic version of a local map that is nonsingular and has no infinitesimal directions along its fibers. The resulting maps are called etale. They are central to modern number theory because they describe families of algebraic solutions that vary without branching.

### Derivatives without calculus on a manifold

Start with a base ring R and construct a new ring S by adjoining a root x of a monic polynomial f. There is a formal derivative of f with respect to x, even if R is not a subring of the real numbers. A simple root is one at which this derivative does not vanish. If the derivative becomes invertible in S, the extension is an example of a finite etale algebra.

For a concrete model, let the base field have characteristic different from two. Take the polynomial x squared minus a parameter t. Consider the ring generated by t and x with the relation x squared equals t. The corresponding geometric map sends a point with coordinate x to the value t equal to x squared. Its derivative in the x direction is two x.

If x is nonzero, the derivative is invertible after restricting to an appropriate basic open. There the map is etale. At x equals zero, the derivative vanishes, and the map is not etale. The fiber over t equals zero contains a doubled root. The fibers elsewhere consist of two distinct roots over an algebraic closure.

This example is a good guide, but it is not the definition in full generality. An etale morphism is a smooth morphism of relative dimension zero. For affine rings one can express this through finite presentation and vanishing of the appropriate cotangent complex. In particular, etale morphisms are flat and unramified. Flatness rules out certain changes in the size of fibers; the unramified condition removes infinitesimal motion along the fiber.

### What the derivative detects

There is a useful algebraic object called the module of Kahler differentials. If S is presented as R with one variable adjoined and one polynomial relation imposed, then the differential of that relation produces the equation f prime of x times d x equals zero. When f prime is a unit, d x is forced to vanish. The extension has no first-order movement relative to the base.

For a finite presentation in several variables and equations, the Jacobian matrix plays the same role. If we have as many independent equations as variables and the Jacobian determinant is invertible, the resulting local algebra is etale. This is an algebraic form of the implicit-function theorem. Instead of assuming differentiable coordinates on a real manifold, it uses localization and a determinant inside a ring.

Do not infer that vanishing differentials alone always suffices. Outside suitable finiteness and flatness conditions, it does not characterize etaleness. The exact hypotheses are what make the Jacobian test reliable.

### Arithmetic ramification is the same phenomenon

Now take a number-field example. Adjoin a square root of two to the integers. Over most prime numbers, the resulting arithmetic map behaves like a pair of distinct algebraic roots after passing to an algebraic closure of the residue field. Over the prime two, the two roots coalesce in characteristic two. The derivative of x squared minus two is two x, which vanishes modulo two.

If we invert two in the base ring, the difficulty disappears. In the extension ring the adjoined square root of two is a unit, and so is its derivative two x. The resulting finite extension over the integers with two inverted is etale. The prime two has been removed from the base; the remaining map is unramified in this sense.

This is one reason number theorists distinguish ramified and unramified primes in field extensions. The arithmetic branching of primes is not merely a metaphor borrowed from Riemann surfaces. The same local algebra, differentials, and derivative conditions explain both settings.

### Stability under change of base

A major advantage of etale morphisms is their permanence. They survive composition and base change. If you have an etale family over a base and then replace the base by another ring or scheme, the pulled-back family remains etale. This is indispensable when comparing algebraic geometry over number fields, finite fields, and local rings.

The local lifting behavior is also remarkable. Simple roots often lift uniquely through sufficiently small nilpotent thickenings. That statement is formal etaleness, expressed through a uniqueness property for infinitesimal diagrams. In Henselian local settings, it resembles the root-lifting mechanism we met in Hensel's lemma, but the formal and Henselian lifting statements have distinct hypotheses.

A caution is needed about the word covering. Etale maps can resemble covering maps of topological spaces, especially finite etale maps over complex varieties. But an arbitrary etale morphism need not be a topological covering of an entire target, and the Zariski topology is too coarse to express the full analogy directly. Finite etale maps, and the etale topology built from them, have their own precise covering theory.

### The lesson from the squaring map

The derivative is not simply a calculus trick. It detects whether nearby algebraic solutions separate cleanly or collide, even over finite fields and arithmetic bases. Etale geometry extends the simple-root idea from an elementary polynomial to families of schemes.

The payoff is that smooth local behavior can be described without committing to Euclidean coordinates. The same definitions work over integers, finite fields, number fields, and geometric varieties. What begins with a failed derivative at zero becomes a general language for unramified arithmetic and for the local study of algebraic maps.

## Divisors, Line Bundles, and Picard Groups

A line on a surface may be given by one equation near one point and a different equation somewhere else. That sounds like an inconvenience of coordinates. But the failure to find one global equation is often the geometric feature we want to measure. Divisors and line bundles turn the difference between local and global equations into algebra.

Begin with the projective line. It has two standard coordinate charts. On one chart we use the coordinate t, and on the other we use its reciprocal. Neither coordinate is defined at every point. Nevertheless, the point at infinity is a perfectly legitimate geometric object. It is cut out by a local equation in the chart that contains it, though not by a single ordinary polynomial function on the entire projective line.

A divisor on a smooth algebraic curve can be pictured as a finite collection of points, each carrying an integer weight. A positive weight records a prescribed zero; a negative weight permits a pole. We can add divisors by adding weights. If a rational function has a zero of order three at a point and a pole of order three elsewhere, those orders form its principal divisor. A crucial distinction is that not every divisor comes from one rational function.

### Equations that agree up to units

To understand the geometry in more than one dimension, consider an effective Cartier divisor. Near each point it is defined by a single equation, and that equation is required to be a non-zero-divisor in the local ring. Two local equations for the same divisor differ on overlaps by multiplication by an invertible regular function, a unit. That unit is not arbitrary error: it is precisely the transition function needed to glue a line bundle.

A line bundle is a family of one-dimensional vector spaces over the points of our space, locally trivial but not necessarily globally trivial. A section chooses one vector from each fiber compatibly. On overlapping trivializations the coordinates of that vector are multiplied by an invertible function. Around a loop or across several charts, these multipliers may obstruct any single globally consistent nonzero section.

An effective Cartier divisor determines an invertible sheaf, conventionally called O of D. Its canonical section vanishes along the divisor. Conversely, a line bundle with a suitable regular section determines an effective Cartier divisor. These constructions explain why local equations and line bundles are two ways to encode related information, although a completely arbitrary line bundle need not come equipped with a section whose zero set is a divisor.

### How an integer emerges from a bundle

Return to the projective line. On the overlap of its two affine charts, the coordinate t is invertible. We may glue trivial line bundles using a power of t as transition function. Different powers produce the standard twisting bundles, usually written O of n. Tensoring two of them adds the exponents.

Over a field, every algebraic line bundle on the projective line is isomorphic to exactly one of these twists. The Picard group, which classifies line bundles up to isomorphism under tensor product, is therefore the integers. The integer is the degree of the bundle. The bundle O of one point has degree one; its inverse has degree minus one.

Here is a way to see the difference without computing the full classification. A line bundle with a nowhere-vanishing global section is trivial: that section gives a basis in every fiber. Yet a bundle of positive degree cannot have a nowhere-vanishing section on the projective line. Any nonzero section has zeros counted with total multiplicity equal to the degree. The obstruction is measurable, not merely a missing choice of coordinates.

### Cartier divisors are not always Weil divisors

There is another, closely related use of the word divisor. A Weil divisor is a formal integer combination of codimension-one integral closed subspaces. On a smooth variety, the local rings in codimension one are particularly well behaved, and Cartier and Weil divisors agree up to the usual equivalence. On a singular variety they need not. A codimension-one subspace may fail to be given locally by one non-zero-divisor equation, and a Weil divisor may not define a line bundle.

This distinction stops several tempting but false generalizations. Knowing which codimension-one pieces exist is not always enough to know which bundles glue. Nor does the existence of many local equations imply the existence of a single global equation.

The Picard group remembers the obstruction to globally trivializing locally free rank-one data. Divisor classes give concrete ways to calculate it. On curves, this bridge will let us translate a geometric question—how many functions can have specified poles?—into a theorem relating degrees, genus, and the dimension of spaces of functions.

### Krull Dimension and Hilbert's Nullstellensatz

**Track:** Abstract Algebra. **Background:** polynomial rings, prime and maximal ideals, quotient rings, fields, algebraic closure. **Central question:** how do algebraic equations determine geometry, and how can chains of prime ideals measure its dimension?

#### Mathematical companion (not spoken)

For \(R\) commutative, \(\dim R\) is the supremum of lengths \(r\) of strict chains \(\mathfrak p_0\subsetneq\cdots\subsetneq\mathfrak p_r\) of prime ideals. For a field \(k\), \(\dim k[x_1,\ldots,x_n]=n\).

For an ideal \(I\subseteq k[x_1,\ldots,x_n]\), define
\[V(I)=\{a\in k^n: f(a)=0\text{ for all }f\in I\},\qquad I(S)=\{f:f(a)=0\text{ for all }a\in S\}.\]
If \(k\) is algebraically closed, **weak Nullstellensatz**: every maximal ideal is \((x_1-a_1,\ldots,x_n-a_n)\), and **strong Nullstellensatz**:
\[I(V(I))=\sqrt I=\{f:\exists r\ge1,\ f^r\in I\}.\]
Thus radical ideals correspond to algebraic subsets with inclusion reversed; prime ideals correspond to irreducible algebraic subsets; maximal ideals to points. For \(k\) *not* algebraically closed, use the geometric points over an algebraic closure or a more general scheme-theoretic formulation, not the naïve assertion.

**Examples:** in \(k[x,y]\), \((0)\subsetneq(x)\subsetneq(x,y)\) is a chain of length two; \(k[x,y]/(xy)\) is reduced with minimal primes \((x)\) and \((y)\), and has dimension one. \(V((x^2))=V((x))\), and \(\sqrt{(x^2)}=(x)\). Over \(\mathbb R\), \((x^2+1)\) is a proper maximal ideal yet has no real zero.

##### Exercises with answers

1. What is \(\dim k[x,y,z]\)? **Answer:** Three; use the chain \((0)\subsetneq(x)\subsetneq(x,y)\subsetneq(x,y,z)\) for the lower bound and the polynomial-ring dimension theorem for the upper bound.
2. Over \(\mathbb C\), determine \(I(V((x^3)))\). **Answer:** \((x)\).
3. Is \((xy)\) prime in \(k[x,y]\)? **No:** \(xy\in(xy)\) while \(x,y\notin(xy)\).
4. Why does a maximal ideal of \(\mathbb R[x]\) not always correspond to a real point? **Answer:** the quotient can be a nontrivial finite field extension, for example \(\mathbb R[x]/(x^2+1)\cong\mathbb C\).

### Integral Dependence, Normalization, and Singular Curves

**Track:** Abstract Algebra. **Background:** rings and ideals, polynomial quotients, field extensions, affine coordinate rings. **Central question:** how can an algebraic curve have a singularity because its coordinate ring is missing an integral element?

#### Mathematical companion (not spoken)

An element \(u\) in an \(A\)-algebra \(B\) is **integral** over \(A\) if \(u^d+a_{d-1}u^{d-1}+\cdots+a_0=0\) for \(a_i\in A\). If \(A\) is a domain with fraction field \(K\), its normalization is \(\overline A=\{u\in K:u\text{ integral over }A\}\). For a Noetherian integral domain that is a finitely generated algebra over a field, the normalization is a finite \(A\)-module.

**Cusp in characteristic zero:** \(A=k[x,y]/(y^2-x^3)\cong k[t^2,t^3]\subset k[t]\), with \(x=t^2,y=t^3\). In \(\mathrm{Frac}(A)\), \(t=y/x\). It is integral because \(t^2-x=0\), and \(k[t]=A+At\) as an \(A\)-module. Since \(k[t]\) is integrally closed, \(\overline A=k[t]\). At \(\mathfrak m=(x,y)\), \(\dim_k\mathfrak m/\mathfrak m^2=2>\dim A=1\), so the cusp point is singular. The map \(\operatorname{Spec}k[t]\to\operatorname{Spec}A\) is finite and birational but not an isomorphism.

**Integral versus arbitrary algebraic element:** \(1/2\in\mathbb Q\) is not integral over \(\mathbb Z\). A rational number integral over \(\mathbb Z\) must be an integer (rational-root theorem).

##### Exercises with answers

1. Why does a monic equation make \(A[u]\) a finite \(A\)-module? **Answer:** reduce all powers \(u^n\), \(n\ge d\), to combinations of \(1,\ldots,u^{d-1}\).
2. Is normalization of an affine plane curve just a coordinate transformation? **No:** the induced coordinate-ring map need not have an inverse in regular functions.
3. In the cusp example, why is \(t\notin A\)? **Answer:** the monomials in \(A\) have exponents in \(\{0,2,3,4,\ldots\}\), never exponent one.
4. Does normal imply nonsingular in all dimensions? **No:** normal surface singularities exist; regularity implies normality for domains, but the converse fails in higher dimensions.

- [Stacks Project, Normalization revisited](https://stacks.math.columbia.edu/tag/082D), base-change behavior and exact hypotheses.

### Prime Spectra: Turning Rings into Geometric Spaces

**Track:** Abstract Algebra. **Background:** ideals, localization, Noetherian rings, affine algebraic sets. **Central question:** how can one recover a space, including its infinitesimal algebra, from a commutative ring?

#### Mathematical companion (not spoken)

Let R be a commutative unital ring. Its prime spectrum is the set 
\[\operatorname{Spec}(R)=\{\mathfrak p\subset R:\mathfrak p\text{ is prime}\}.\]
The closed sets are \(V(I)=\{\mathfrak p:I\subseteq\mathfrak p\}\). Standard opens are \(D(f)=\operatorname{Spec}(R)\setminus V((f))\), satisfying \(D(f)\cap D(g)=D(fg)\). The closure of \(\{\mathfrak p\}\) is \(V(\mathfrak p)\). The structure sheaf has \(\mathcal O_{\operatorname{Spec} R}(D(f))\cong R_f\) and stalk \(\mathcal O_{\operatorname{Spec}R,\mathfrak p}\cong R_{\mathfrak p}\). Its global sections recover R, including nilpotents.

**Three examples.** \(\operatorname{Spec}\mathbb Z=\{(0),(2),(3),(5),\ldots\}\), with \(D(6)\) omitting (2) and (3). If k is algebraically closed, closed points of \(\operatorname{Spec}k[x]\) correspond to \((x-a)\), but (0) is generic. In \(A=k[\varepsilon]/(\varepsilon^2)\), the unique prime ideal is \((\varepsilon)\); \(A\neq k\) as rings, hence the one-point schemes differ.

**Functoriality.** A ring homomorphism \(\varphi:R\to S\) induces \(\operatorname{Spec}S\to\operatorname{Spec}R\), \(\mathfrak q\mapsto\varphi^{-1}(\mathfrak q)\), compatible with sheaves. For a nilpotent n, every prime contains n; the underlying spectrum depends only on the reduced quotient, not the structure sheaf.

##### Exercises with answers

1. Determine \(\operatorname{Spec}(\mathbb Z/12\mathbb Z)\). **Answer:** two prime ideals, the images of (2) and (3). The ring is nonreduced: 6 is a nonzero nilpotent, since \(6^2=0\pmod{12}\). Its nilradical is the ideal generated by 6.
2. Prove \(D(f)\cap D(g)=D(fg)\). **Answer:** in a prime ideal, \(fg\in\mathfrak p\) exactly when \(f\in\mathfrak p\) or \(g\in\mathfrak p\).
3. Why do \(k[\varepsilon]/(\varepsilon^2)\) and k have the same underlying point set but different schemes? **Answer:** every prime contains epsilon, but the global section rings are nonisomorphic.
4. What happens to spectra when \(R\to R/I\) is surjective? **Answer:** \(\operatorname{Spec}(R/I)\cong V(I)\subseteq\operatorname{Spec}R\) as topological spaces, with its quotient structure sheaf.

### Sheaves and Quasi-Coherent Modules: Algebra that Glues

**Track:** Abstract Algebra. **Background:** modules, localization, prime spectra, projective modules. **Central question:** how do local solutions combine into coherent global algebra?

#### Mathematical companion (not spoken)

For an R-module M on \(X=\operatorname{Spec}R\), define \(\widetilde M(D(f))=M_f\). The restrictions localize further. There is an equivalence
\[\operatorname{Mod}(R)\simeq\operatorname{QCoh}(\operatorname{Spec}R),\qquad M\longmapsto\widetilde M,\quad\mathcal F\longmapsto\Gamma(X,\mathcal F).\]
In particular \(\Gamma(X,\widetilde M)=M\), and \(\widetilde{M\otimes_R N}\cong\widetilde M\otimes_{\mathcal O_X}\widetilde N\).

**Sheaf equalizer on an affine cover:** if \(D(f)\cup D(g)=X\), equivalently \((f,g)=R\), there is an exact sequence
\[0\longrightarrow M\longrightarrow M_f\oplus M_g\xrightarrow{(u,v)\mapsto u|_{fg}-v|_{fg}} M_{fg}.\]
The right-hand map is also surjective for a two-open principal cover of an affine scheme, giving an exact Cech complex in degree one.

**Projective line charts:** \(\mathbb P^1_k=U_0\cup U_1\), with \(U_0=\operatorname{Spec}k[t]\), \(U_1=\operatorname{Spec}k[s]\), \(s=t^{-1}\) on the overlap. Transition functions \(t^n\) define invertible sheaves; a choice of frame convention determines whether the resulting sheaf is \(\mathcal O(n)\) or \(\mathcal O(-n)\). Regardless of convention, \(\Gamma(\mathbb P^1,\mathcal O(-1))=0\) and \(\operatorname{Pic}(\mathbb P^1_k)\cong\mathbb Z\).

##### Exercises with answers

1. Why is \(\widetilde{R/(x)}\) zero on \(D(x)\)? **Answer:** localization makes x invertible, while x annihilates the module.
2. Why is a projective module locally free? **Answer:** finite projectives over a local ring are free; localize at primes, using finite projectivity to obtain a neighborhood of constant rank.
3. Must a locally free rank-one sheaf have a nonzero global section? **Answer:** no, e.g. \(\mathcal O_{\mathbb P^1}(-1)\).
4. What is missing if two trivializations agree pairwise but not on triple intersections? **Answer:** the cocycle condition; the proposed identifications do not define a global object.

### Etale Maps, Derivatives, and Unramified Geometry

**Track:** Abstract Algebra. **Background:** field extensions, localization, polynomial derivatives, affine schemes. **Central question:** when does an algebraic map look locally like several separate, nonsingular sheets?

#### Mathematical companion (not spoken)

For a ring map \(R\to S\), etale means smooth of relative dimension zero. Equivalently, it is flat, unramified and locally of finite presentation. If \(S=R[x,1/g]/(f)\), where f is monic and \(f'(x)\) becomes invertible in S, then R to S is **standard etale**.

The relative Kahler differentials for \(S=R[x]/(f)\) admit the presentation
\[S\xrightarrow{\cdot f'(x)}S\,dx\longrightarrow\Omega_{S/R}\longrightarrow0.\]
Invertibility of f' gives \(\Omega_{S/R}=0\); standard etaleness also supplies the required flatness and finite presentation. Vanishing of \(\Omega\) alone is not enough in arbitrary examples.

**The squaring map:** for \(k\) with \(\operatorname{char}k\ne2\), set \(R=k[t]\) and \(S=k[x]\) with \(t\mapsto x^2\). Then \(\Omega_{S/R}\cong S/(2x)\,dx\). On \(D(x)\), this is zero and the map is etale; at x=0 it is ramified. In characteristic two it is inseparable everywhere.

**Arithmetic example:** \(R=\mathbb Z[1/2]\), \(S=R[x]/(x^2-2)\). Here \(2x\in S^\times\) because x is a unit and 2 is a unit. Therefore S is finite etale over R. Over \(\mathbb Z\) the map is ramified at the prime over two.

##### Exercises with answers

1. Is \(k[x]\) over \(k[t]\), with \(t=x^3\), etale at \(x=0\) in characteristic zero? **Answer:** no; the derivative \(3x^2\) vanishes there.
2. Why do finite separable field extensions give etale maps? **Answer:** they are finite, flat, and their minimal polynomials have simple roots, giving zero relative differentials.
3. Give a permanence property. **Answer:** base change or composition of etale morphisms is etale.
4. What goes wrong with \(x^2-t\) in characteristic two? **Answer:** its derivative with respect to x is identically zero, so the generically separable simple-root model fails.

### Divisors, Line Bundles, and Picard Groups

**Track:** Abstract Algebra. **Background:** affine schemes, localization, invertible modules, projective spaces. **Central question:** how can locally defined equations determine a global geometric object?

#### Mathematical companion (not spoken)

Assume schemes are over a field unless otherwise noted. An **effective Cartier divisor** on a scheme X is a closed subscheme locally defined by a non-zero-divisor f in O_X(U). Its ideal sheaf I_D is invertible and O_X(D) = Hom_O_X(I_D,O_X). The inclusion I_D -> O_X gives a canonical section of O_X(D). For effective D,E, one has O_X(D+E) isomorphic to O_X(D) tensor O_X(E).

A **line bundle** is a locally free O_X-module of rank one. Its isomorphism classes form Pic(X), with group operation tensor product and identity O_X. A chosen trivialization on U_i has transition functions g_ij in O_X^*(U_i intersect U_j), satisfying g_ij g_jk = g_ik on triple overlaps. Up to coboundaries, this gives Pic(X) = H^1(X,O_X^*) in the sheaf-cohomology sense.

On an integral smooth projective curve, a Weil divisor is D = sum_P n_P[P], supported at finitely many closed points. The divisor of f in k(X)^* is div(f) = sum_P ord_P(f)[P]. The degree is sum_P n_P [k(P):k], with degree zero for principal divisors on a proper curve. Over an algebraically closed field the residue degrees equal one. For smooth curves, divisor classes modulo principal divisors correspond to Pic(X).

**Projective line:** Pic(P^1_k) is isomorphic to Z by [O(n)] -> n. A section of O(n) for n >= 0 corresponds to a homogeneous degree-n polynomial in two variables, with dimension n+1. Do not assert that Weil divisors coincide with Cartier divisors on arbitrary singular schemes; regular or locally factorial hypotheses matter.

##### Exercises with answers

1. Why can local defining equations differ by a unit? **Answer:** if two non-zero-divisor equations define the same effective Cartier ideal on an overlap, each is an invertible multiple of the other; the multipliers provide the gluing functions.
2. Compute O(P+Q) on P^1 up to isomorphism for two rational points. **Answer:** O(2), of degree two, whether or not P and Q coincide.
3. Does every line bundle admit a global nowhere-vanishing section? **Answer:** no; such a section would trivialize the bundle, while O(1) on P^1 is nontrivial.
4. Why does the local equation y=0 define an effective Cartier divisor on A^2_k? **Answer:** y is a non-zero-divisor in k[x,y]; its principal ideal is locally free of rank one.
