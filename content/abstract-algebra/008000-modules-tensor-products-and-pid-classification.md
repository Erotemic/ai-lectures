# Modules, tensor products, and PID classification

## Modules: Linear Algebra without Division

In linear algebra, it is tempting to treat every scalar like a nonzero real number: divide by it, cancel it, or pivot on it. That works over a field. But many structures we want to study have integer scalars, polynomial scalars, or matrix scalars. An equation such as two times x equals one need not have a solution, and cancellation can fail when a scalar is a zero divisor.

A module is what remains of a vector space when the scalars belong to a ring instead of a field. It captures systems as varied as abelian groups, integer lattices, polynomial recurrences, and linear operators. But the price of this generality is real: bases, dimensions, and complements are no longer automatic.

### The definition and first examples

Take a commutative ring R with identity. An R-module M is an abelian group under addition, with a scalar multiplication by elements of R. Scalar multiplication distributes over vector addition and scalar addition, multiplication of scalars is compatible with successive actions, and the ring's one acts as the identity.

A vector space over a field is a module, but there are many others. The integers themselves form a module over the integer ring. Integer pairs form another, as do integer triples and integer matrices. Any abelian group is a module over the integers: a positive integer acts by repeated addition, zero acts by zero, and a negative integer acts using additive inverses. Conversely, the axioms of an integer module force precisely that action. So integer modules and abelian groups describe the same structures.

The cyclic group of remainders modulo six is an integer module. The scalar two acts by doubling a residue. Notice that two times the residue three is zero even though three is not zero. Over a field, a nonzero scalar cannot kill a nonzero vector; here it can. Such elements are called torsion elements. Torsion is not a technical nuisance. It records information that ordinary vector spaces cannot possess.

### Submodules and quotient modules

A submodule is an additive subgroup closed under multiplication by every scalar in R. Quotients are formed exactly as in groups: two module elements become equivalent when their difference belongs to the submodule. Scalar multiplication descends to equivalence classes because multiplying a submodule element by any scalar stays inside the submodule.

In the integer module Z, the subgroup of even integers is a submodule. The quotient Z modulo two Z is the two-element cyclic group. We can now describe the short exact sequence: zero maps into Z, multiplication by two maps Z into Z, reduction modulo two maps Z onto Z modulo two Z, and then we map to zero.

The adjective exact means that at each position, the elements produced by the preceding map are exactly those killed by the following map. Multiplication by two is injective on the integers. Its image is exactly the even integers, which are the kernel of reduction modulo two. Reduction is surjective.

Why is this sequence interesting? Because it says a quotient need not be a direct summand. If there were a homomorphism from Z modulo two Z back into Z that reversed reduction, it would have to send the nonzero element of order two to an integer of order two. No such integer exists. So this sequence does not split. By contrast, every short exact sequence of vector spaces over a field splits: choose a basis of the image, extend to a basis, and construct a complement. Over a general ring we cannot simply assume a complement exists.

### The failure of automatic bases

A free module has a basis in the familiar sense: each element has a unique finite linear combination of basis elements. Integer pairs form a free module of rank two, with the two coordinate vectors as a basis. But Z modulo two Z is not a free integer module of any positive rank. Every nonzero free integer module contains elements of infinite order, while all elements of Z modulo two Z have finite order.

This gives a precise distinction between generating sets and bases. The group Z modulo two Z is generated by the class of one, but this generator satisfies the nontrivial relation two times one equals zero. In a free module, basis vectors cannot satisfy nontrivial linear relations.

Over more general rings, even submodules of free modules need not be free. Over the integers, submodules of finite-rank free modules *are* free, a special and powerful theorem connected to the structure of finitely generated abelian groups. The fact that it requires a theorem is itself a warning against importing field-based intuition without checking hypotheses.

### Homomorphisms and their kernels

An R-linear map preserves addition and scalar multiplication. The kernel is a submodule, and the image is a submodule. The first isomorphism theorem works again: M modulo the kernel is isomorphic to the image. The proof is exactly the same representative argument we used for groups and rings, now checking compatibility with scalar multiplication.

Consider multiplication by six on the integers modulo fifteen. Its kernel consists of residues x for which six x is divisible by fifteen. This occurs exactly for the multiples of five modulo fifteen: zero, five, and ten. The kernel has three elements. The image consists of residues zero, three, six, nine, and twelve, and has five elements. The quotient by the three-element kernel has five elements, matching the image.

This is a finite example of a general principle. A homomorphism organizes a module by what it loses and what it preserves. The role of exact sequences is to make such information available across several maps at once.

### Linear operators as polynomial actions

There is a less obvious module hiding inside any linear transformation. Let V be a finite-dimensional vector space over a field F, and let T be a linear operator from V to itself. We can let the polynomial ring F of x act on V by defining the action of a polynomial p to be the operator p of T.

For example, x acting on a vector means apply T. The polynomial x squared minus three x plus two acting on a vector means apply T twice, subtract three applications of T, and add twice the original vector. The polynomial-ring identities make the module axioms hold automatically.

Now the kernel of the action map from F polynomials to operators consists of polynomials that annihilate T. In finite dimension, that kernel contains a nonzero polynomial, because the list of powers of T must eventually become linearly dependent as operators. There is then a unique monic polynomial of least degree in the kernel: the minimal polynomial of the operator. A polynomial annihilates T exactly when the minimal polynomial divides it. Compare this with an algebraic element of a field extension: a number alpha and an operator T can both be studied through the polynomial relations they satisfy.

If T is represented by a matrix, the familiar questions of invariant subspaces, diagonalization, and Jordan form become questions about submodules and decompositions of an F of x module. This module viewpoint helps explain why a minimal polynomial is a structural invariant rather than merely another coefficient list to compute.

### What survives when division disappears

Some of the most reliable ideas in linear algebra require little or no division: kernels, images, compositions, quotients, exactness, and universal mapping properties. Others—automatic bases, complements, and a single numerical dimension—require a field or additional hypotheses.

Modules make that boundary visible. They are flexible enough to describe integer congruences, algebraic extensions, and linear operators in one language, while still distinguishing between relations that can be solved by division and relations that cannot. They are also the natural setting for the exact sequences and resolutions that appear in homological algebra. Their value is not that everything becomes a vector space. It is that we learn precisely which parts of vector-space reasoning are genuinely structural.

## Tensor Products: Encoding Bilinear Structure in a Linear Map

A linear map takes one vector as input and respects addition and scalar multiplication. But mathematics constantly asks about constructions involving two inputs: the dot product of two vectors, the product of two polynomials, or the operation that multiplies one function by another. Such a rule is often linear in each input separately, a property called bilinearity. If we fix one argument, the rule is linear in the other. It need not be linear in the pair considered as a single vector in the ordinary direct sum.

Tensor products are a way of organizing all bilinear rules into one universal linear construction. They also allow us to combine modules and representations in ways that direct sums cannot. The word universal has an exact meaning: a single object represents an entire family of compatible maps.

### Why pairs of vectors are not enough

Take two vector spaces V and W over the same field. A naïve attempt is to form pairs of vectors. But a bilinear rule treats a pair differently from an element of a direct sum. For a bilinear function B, if you double the first input, you double the output; if you double the second input, you also double the output. If you double both inputs, you multiply the output by four. A linear map on the direct sum would multiply its output only by two when both components were doubled.

Instead, introduce a formal symbol, spoken as v tensor w, for every pair of vectors. Impose the relations demanded by bilinearity. The symbol for v plus v-prime tensor w is the sum of the symbols for v tensor w and v-prime tensor w. Similarly in the second argument. And a scalar may pass from either input to the coefficient of the resulting symbol.

The space generated by these symbols, modulo those relations, is the tensor product, written V tensor W. Most of its elements are finite sums of elementary tensors. An arbitrary element need not be a single v tensor w. Restricting attention to elementary tensors discards most of the tensor product.

Now any bilinear map B from V times W to a vector space U extends uniquely to a linear map from V tensor W to U. The linear map sends each elementary tensor v tensor w to B of v and w. The imposed relations guarantee that this assignment is well-defined; uniqueness follows because elementary tensors generate the tensor product.

We could have started with a different construction and tried to prove it has the same property. The universal property tells us the result would be canonically isomorphic. This is what makes the tensor product a mathematical object rather than a particular collection of formal symbols.

### Count the dimensions

Suppose V has basis e-one through e-m and W has basis f-one through f-n. The elementary tensors e-i tensor f-j span the tensor product. Every elementary tensor is expanded by expressing its two factors in their bases and distributing bilinearly. The coefficient on e-i tensor f-j is the product of the corresponding coordinates.

Why are these m times n tensors independent? For each pair of basis indices, take the bilinear rule that multiplies the i-th coordinate of the first input by the j-th coordinate of the second. The universal property turns this into a linear functional on V tensor W. Applied to our list of basis tensors, it selects exactly one item. Hence no nontrivial linear relation among them exists.

So, in finite dimension, the dimension of V tensor W is the dimension of V times the dimension of W. For example, a two-dimensional vector space tensored with a three-dimensional one has dimension six, not five. The direct sum has dimension five; the tensor product has dimension six because it accounts for all pairwise interactions of coordinates.

Matrices offer a concrete realization. A two-vector tensored with a three-vector corresponds to an outer-product matrix of size two by three. A general tensor corresponds to a sum of outer products, which may have matrix rank greater than one. This is another reason not every tensor is elementary.

### Tensor products of representations

If a group G acts linearly on V and W, it acts on V tensor W by sending v tensor w to the image of v tensored with the image of w. This defines another representation because group composition respects both factors and the bilinear relations.

A one-dimensional sign representation of the triangle group tensored with itself is trivial: each group element acts by the square of positive or negative one. Thus tensor products can combine representations and produce new decomposition problems. In a fixed basis, the corresponding matrices are Kronecker products of the original representation matrices.

The character of a tensor-product representation is the pointwise product of the two characters. This follows from the matrix identity that the trace of a Kronecker product equals the product of the traces. Together with character orthogonality, this gives a practical method to decompose tensor products into irreducible representations.

This construction is not the same as taking a direct sum. Direct sum collects alternative degrees of freedom. Tensor product collects pairwise interactions, and often makes symmetry constraints on those interactions visible.

### Modules over the integers behave differently

Over a field, tensor products are well behaved with respect to dimensions. Over the integers, tensor products encode divisibility information that cannot be seen by multiplying ranks alone.

Consider the module of integers modulo two. It has an element of order two: twice that element is zero. Consider integers modulo three, with an element of order three. What is the tensor product of these two modules over the integers?

Take any elementary tensor a tensor b. The first factor is annihilated by two, so two times this tensor is zero. The second factor is annihilated by three, so three times the same tensor is zero. But two and three have integer coefficients whose combination equals one. Consequently the tensor itself must be zero. All elementary tensors vanish, so the whole tensor product is the zero module.

More generally, tensoring the integers modulo m with the integers modulo n yields the integers modulo their greatest common divisor. When m and n are relatively prime, there is no common torsion to retain.

Tensoring with integers modulo two has a simple effect on an integer module M: it identifies elements that differ by an even multiple, giving M modulo two M. This is sometimes called reduction modulo two. It is a change of coefficients; it can reveal arithmetic structures while erasing others.

### When tensoring loses exactness

Consider the injective map from the integers to the integers given by multiplication by two. If we tensor this map with integers modulo two, it becomes multiplication by two on the integers modulo two, which is the zero map. The original injection no longer remains injective.

This is the first sign of a broader principle. Tensor product over a ring preserves quotients and right exact sequences, but it need not preserve injections. Over a field tensoring by a fixed vector space is exact; over a general ring it may fail to be exact. The modules for which tensoring preserves injections are called flat modules.

The failure is not a technical inconvenience. It is a measurable phenomenon. Homological algebra introduces derived constructions, especially the functor Tor, to measure exactly what tensoring fails to preserve. The next part of the course will make that claim precise by using projective resolutions.

The tensor product therefore accomplishes three jobs: it turns bilinear rules into linear rules, it combines representations, and it exposes arithmetic torsion. Each job is a different face of the same universal object.

## Finitely Generated Modules over a PID: A Classification Theorem

Linear algebra offers a reassuring promise: a finite-dimensional vector space is determined up to isomorphism by its dimension. Choose a basis, count its vectors, and you have classified the space. Modules over general rings do not admit such a simple basis theorem. A module may have torsion, and even a torsion-free module may fail to be free.

But over a principal ideal domain, we recover a remarkably complete classification. Every finitely generated module is a direct sum of a free part and cyclic torsion parts. The relation between those parts is recorded by divisibility, in a form that is both theoretically canonical and computationally accessible through Smith normal form.

### What the principal ideal hypothesis buys us

A principal ideal domain, or PID, is a commutative integral domain in which every ideal is generated by one element. The integers are the standard example. Another is the polynomial ring in one variable over a field. A polynomial ring in two variables generally is not a PID: the ideal generated by x and y cannot be generated by one polynomial.

Why should a condition about ideals help classify modules? The central technical result is that submodules of finite-rank free modules over a PID are themselves free. Over the integers, this says any subgroup of a free abelian group is free abelian. That result makes finite presentations tractable. A finitely generated module has finitely many generators and, because a PID is Noetherian, finitely many defining relations. The relations can be written as a matrix.

Now the classification problem becomes: what can we do to a matrix without changing the isomorphism class of its cokernel?

### Smith normal form

A presentation matrix maps one finite-rank free module into another. Its cokernel is the module defined by the generators and relations. Change the basis of the domain or codomain, and the presentation matrix changes by multiplication on the left and right by invertible matrices. Because those transformations are isomorphisms, the cokernel does not change up to isomorphism.

Over a PID, successive divisibility arguments can diagonalize any matrix into Smith normal form. On the diagonal appear elements d one, d two, and so on, ordered so that each divides the next. Some diagonal entries may be units, producing zero summands in the quotient. Other entries may be zero, leaving free summands. The nonzero nonunit entries describe cyclic torsion pieces.

This is the invariant-factor form of the structure theorem. A finitely generated module is a free module of some rank, plus a finite direct sum of cyclic modules of the form R modulo d, with each d dividing the following one. The rank and the principal ideals generated by the d's are uniquely determined, up to multiplication of generators by units.

### Two different groups of order six

Take a module generated by two elements a and b with relations two a equals zero and three b equals zero. In matrix form the relation matrix is diagonal with entries two and three. The module is the direct sum of the integers modulo two and the integers modulo three.

But two and three are relatively prime. The Chinese remainder theorem says that their direct sum is cyclic of order six. The Smith normal form reflects this: after legitimate integer row and column operations, the diagonal entries become one and six. The entry one contributes nothing to the cokernel, leaving just the integers modulo six.

Compare this with the diagonal presentation having entries two and six. Because two divides six, it is already in Smith normal form. The resulting group is the integers modulo two plus the integers modulo six. It has twelve elements, not six. The divisibility chain provides a stable description, not merely a total count of elements.

### Invariant factors and prime powers

There is a second way to state the theorem. Factor each invariant factor into prime powers and use the Chinese remainder theorem to split coprime pieces. The result is the elementary-divisor form: a free part plus cyclic modules whose annihilators are powers of irreducibles.

Over the integers, these are cyclic groups of prime-power order. For example, a cyclic group of order twelve breaks into cyclic groups of orders four and three. Conversely, collecting prime-power summands into divisibility chains recovers the invariant factors. The two descriptions classify the same objects, but organize the data differently.

Uniqueness is not an afterthought. The ranks of the layers of p-power torsion, formed by repeated multiplication by p, determine how many elementary divisors have each exponent. That is why the multiset of prime powers is intrinsic, not a consequence of the diagonalization algorithm's particular choices.

### A connection to matrices and dynamics

Let F be a field and V a finite-dimensional F-vector space equipped with a linear operator T. Turn V into a module over the polynomial ring F of t by letting multiplication by t mean applying T. Because F of t is a PID, the structure theorem applies.

A cyclic module F of t modulo a polynomial q corresponds to a companion-matrix block. The invariant-factor decomposition produces rational canonical form. When the polynomial splits into linear factors over the field, the elementary-divisor decomposition leads to Jordan blocks. This explains why the Jordan form is not an isolated trick about matrices: it is a consequence of module classification over a PID.

The field matters. Over the real numbers, a rotation through a right angle has no real eigenvectors, so a real Jordan decomposition into linear factors is impossible. But it has a rational canonical form governed by the irreducible polynomial t squared plus one. The structure theorem does not require all polynomials to split.

### Know where the theorem stops

The finitely generated hypothesis is essential. Not every infinitely generated abelian group decomposes into the listed finite cyclic building blocks and a finite-rank free part. For instance, the additive group of rational numbers is not a direct sum of cyclic groups. The PID hypothesis is also essential. Over a general integral domain, torsion-free modules need not be free, and there can be nonprincipal ideal classes.

The deeper achievement is the passage from a matrix to a classification invariant. Generators and relations depend on choices; the abstract module does not. Smith normal form converts those choices into a canonical collection of divisibility data. Over the integers, that classifies finite abelian groups. Over a polynomial PID, it classifies linear operators up to similarity. A theorem about ideals becomes one of the most useful bridges between algebra, arithmetic, and matrix computation.

### Modules: Linear Algebra without Division

**Track:** Abstract Algebra: Structure, Symmetry, and Arithmetic. **Background:** fields, rings, quotient structures, and elementary vector spaces. **Question:** which linear-algebra arguments survive when the scalars do not form a field?

#### Mathematical companion (not spoken)

An \(R\)-module \(M\) is an abelian group with scalar action \(R\times M\to M\) satisfying distributive, associative, and unit axioms. A module is **free** if it is isomorphic to a direct sum of copies of \(R\).

**Non-split exact sequence over \(\mathbb Z\):**
\[
0\longrightarrow \mathbb Z\xrightarrow{\times2}\mathbb Z
\xrightarrow{\bmod2}\mathbb Z/2\mathbb Z\longrightarrow0.
\]
No section of reduction exists: a section would inject an element of additive order two into \(\mathbb Z\).

**Map on a finite module:**
\[
\varphi:\mathbb Z/15\mathbb Z\to\mathbb Z/15\mathbb Z,\quad [x]\mapsto[6x],
\]
\[
\ker\varphi=\{[0],[5],[10]\},\quad
\operatorname{im}\varphi=\{[0],[3],[6],[9],[12]\}.
\]

**Operator action:** for finite-dimensional \(V\), \(p(x)\cdot v:=p(T)v\) makes \(V\) into an \(F[x]\)-module. The annihilator ideal \(\{p:p(T)=0\}\) is generated by the minimal polynomial of \(T\).

##### Exercises with answers

1. Why is every abelian group a \(\mathbb Z\)-module? **Answer:** integer multiplication is repeated addition and additive inversion; this is forced by the axioms.
2. Is \(\mathbb Z/3\mathbb Z\) free as a \(\mathbb Z\)-module? **Answer:** no; it has torsion, while a nonzero free integer module is torsion-free.
3. What is the kernel of multiplication by four on \(\mathbb Z/12\mathbb Z\)? **Answer:** classes \(0,3,6,9\).
4. If \(T^2=I\), what polynomial annihilates \(T\)? **Answer:** \(x^2-1\), though the minimal polynomial may be a proper divisor.

Keith Conrad, [Expository papers](https://kconrad.math.uconn.edu/blurbs/), *Introduction to Modules*, *Modules over a PID*, and *Splitting of Short Exact Sequences for Modules*; Judson, *Abstract Algebra*, vector spaces and modules.

### Tensor Products: Encoding Bilinear Structure in a Linear Map

**Track:** Abstract Algebra. **Background:** modules, linear maps, quotients, and group representations. **Question:** how can a construction turn every bilinear question into a linear one, and what new information appears when tensoring modules?

#### Mathematical companion (not spoken)

For a commutative ring \(R\), modules \(M,N\), and an \(R\)-module \(P\), the tensor product \(M\otimes_R N\) is characterized by
\[
\operatorname{Hom}_R(M\otimes_R N,P)\cong \operatorname{Bilin}_R(M\times N,P).
\]
This is natural in \(P\). For a noncommutative ring, use a right \(R\)-module for the first factor and a left \(R\)-module for the second, with the balancing relation \((mr)\otimes n=m\otimes(rn)\).

**Construction.** Take the free \(R\)-module on pairs \((m,n)\) and quotient by the additive and balanced-scalar relations. Finite sums of pure tensors generate the quotient. A universal bilinear map descends uniquely through those relations.

**Bases.** For \(k\)-spaces with bases \((e_i)_{i=1}^m\), \((f_j)_{j=1}^n\), the elements \(e_i\otimes f_j\) form a basis for \(V\otimes_k W\). Coordinate product bilinear maps distinguish the basis elements.

**Cyclic abelian groups.** \(\mathbb Z/m\otimes_{\mathbb Z}\mathbb Z/n\cong\mathbb Z/\gcd(m,n)\). One derivation: \((\mathbb Z/m)\otimes N\cong N/mN\) with \(N=\mathbb Z/n\).

**Right exact but not left exact.** Tensoring \(0\to\mathbb Z\xrightarrow{\times2}\mathbb Z\to\mathbb Z/2\to0\) with \(\mathbb Z/2\) yields \(\mathbb Z/2\xrightarrow{0}\mathbb Z/2\to\mathbb Z/2\to0\), which remains right exact but the first arrow is not injective.

**Tensor characters.** For finite-dimensional complex representations, \(\chi_{V\otimes W}(g)=\chi_V(g)\chi_W(g)\).

##### Exercises with answers

1. Find \(\dim(\mathbb R^2\otimes\mathbb R^4)\). **Answer:** eight.
2. Is every element of \(\mathbb R^2\otimes\mathbb R^2\) a pure tensor? **No.** Under the matrix identification, the identity matrix has rank two, whereas every pure tensor has rank at most one.
3. Compute \(\mathbb Z/6\otimes\mathbb Z/10\). **Answer:** \(\mathbb Z/2\).
4. Compute sign \(\otimes\) sign for \(S_3\). **Answer:** the trivial one-dimensional representation.

Pavel Etingof and collaborators, [MIT 18.712 lecture notes](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/18-712-introduction-to-representation-theory-fall-2010/pages/lecture-notes/), tensor products and representations; Keith Conrad, [expository algebra notes](https://kconrad.math.uconn.edu/blurbs/), modules and tensor products.

### Finitely Generated Modules over a PID: A Classification Theorem

**Track:** Abstract Algebra. **Background:** Euclidean algorithm, ideals and modules, matrix row operations. **Central question:** when can we classify every finitely generated module from a finite list of arithmetic invariants?

#### Mathematical companion (not spoken)

Let \(R\) be a PID and \(M\) a finitely generated \(R\)-module. The invariant factor theorem gives
\[
M\cong R^r\oplus\bigoplus_{i=1}^sR/(d_i),\quad d_i\ne0,\ d_i\notin R^\times,\quad d_1\mid d_2\mid\cdots\mid d_s.
\]
The rank \(r\), the number \(s\), and the ideals \((d_i)\) are unique. An arbitrary presentation \(R^m\xrightarrow{A}R^n\twoheadrightarrow M\) admits invertible \(U,V\) for which \(UAV\) is diagonal with entries satisfying the divisibility chain. Unit diagonal entries give zero cokernel summands; zero entries give free summands.

Over \(\mathbb Z\),
\[
\operatorname{coker}\begin{pmatrix}2&0\\0&3\end{pmatrix}
\cong \mathbb Z/2\oplus\mathbb Z/3\cong\mathbb Z/6,
\]
whose Smith invariants are \(1,6\). By contrast \(\operatorname{diag}(2,6)\) already satisfies the divisibility ordering and yields \(\mathbb Z/2\oplus\mathbb Z/6\).

For \(V\) finite-dimensional over \(F\) and \(T\in\operatorname{End}_F(V)\), define the \(F[t]\)-action by \(t\cdot v=T(v)\). The PID decomposition yields rational canonical form; prime-power polynomial torsion yields primary blocks, and Jordan blocks occur when the relevant irreducibles are linear over \(F\). The finite-dimensional assumption makes the \(F[t]\)-module finitely generated and torsion.

##### Exercises with answers

1. Classify the abelian group \(\mathbb Z/4\oplus\mathbb Z/9\) by invariant factors. **Answer:** \(\mathbb Z/36\), by the Chinese remainder theorem.
2. Is \(\mathbb Z\oplus\mathbb Z/6\) finite? **No:** it has free rank one.
3. What is the module corresponding to a single Jordan block of size two with eigenvalue \(\lambda\in F\)? **Answer:** \(F[t]/((t-\lambda)^2)\).
4. Can the polynomial-ring structure theorem be applied directly to \(F[x,y]\)? **No:** this ring is generally not a PID.
