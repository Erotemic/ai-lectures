# How Large Is a New Number? Field Extensions, Degrees, and Conjugates

The symbol square root of two looks like one number. But when we add it to the rational numbers, it creates a whole new field, containing every expression a plus b times the square root of two, with a and b rational. It has infinitely many elements, just as the rationals do. In that sense, asking how many new numbers it contains is not very informative.

There is a better way to measure an algebraic extension: ask how many independent directions it has when viewed as a vector space over the original field. The field made by adjoining the square root of two has degree two over the rationals. Adjoin square root of three as well, and the degree becomes four. Yet adjoining square root of two plus square root of three does not make an eight-dimensional field; that single element already encodes both square roots.

These claims have a complete, calculable explanation. They lead from simple radicals to minimal polynomials, the tower law, and the symmetry groups of field extensions.

## Building a field from one algebraic element

Start with the rationals, denoted Q, and an element alpha satisfying alpha squared equals two. We could construct alpha as the positive real square root, but the algebra does not require this analytic description. We can also form rational polynomials modulo x squared minus two and call the residue class of x alpha. In either approach, every power of alpha can be reduced to a linear expression because alpha squared is two.

Thus every element has the form a plus b alpha for rational a and b. Addition is componentwise. Multiplication follows from distribution and alpha squared equals two: multiply the two expressions, collect rational terms, and replace alpha squared by two. We still need to know that every nonzero element has an inverse.

For a plus b alpha, multiply numerator and denominator by a minus b alpha. The denominator becomes a squared minus two b squared, a rational number. It cannot vanish unless a and b are both zero. If b were nonzero and the denominator vanished, alpha would equal the rational number a divided by b or its negative, contradicting the irrationality of square root of two. Therefore a plus b alpha is invertible whenever it is nonzero.

The field Q of square root of two is two-dimensional as a vector space over Q. The vectors one and alpha span every element. They are linearly independent: if a plus b alpha equals zero with rational a and b, then either b equals zero and a equals zero, or alpha would be rational. So they form a basis.

The extension degree, written bracket Q alpha colon Q bracket, is two. The word degree here is not counting field elements; it is counting basis vectors.

## A minimal polynomial measures the extension

Suppose alpha is an element of some larger field containing F. We call alpha algebraic over F if a nonzero polynomial with coefficients in F vanishes at alpha. Among all such polynomials there is a unique monic one of smallest degree. This is the minimal polynomial of alpha over F.

The minimal polynomial must be irreducible. If it factored into two lower-degree nonconstant polynomials, then evaluating at alpha would give a product of two elements equal to zero. In a field, one factor must evaluate to zero, contradicting minimality of degree. We therefore get an irreducible polynomial, and the quotient F of x modulo that polynomial is a field.

There is a natural map from F of x to the larger field: evaluate a polynomial at alpha. Its kernel is exactly the multiples of the minimal polynomial. The first isomorphism theorem for rings identifies the quotient with the subfield generated by F and alpha. As a vector space, the quotient has basis one, x, x squared, up to the power immediately below the degree of the minimal polynomial.

So the degree of the minimal polynomial equals the extension degree of F alpha over F. This is a beautiful translation: the complexity of a number's defining algebraic equation is exactly the number of basis directions required to describe its arithmetic over the original field.

For square root of two, the minimal polynomial over the rationals is x squared minus two. It is irreducible because square root of two is irrational. Its degree is two, agreeing with the basis we found.

Over a different base field, the minimal polynomial can change. Over Q of square root of two, the element square root of two already belongs to the base, so its minimal polynomial becomes x minus square root of two and has degree one. Algebraicity and degree are always statements relative to a specified coefficient field.

## Adjoining a second square root

Now build L by adjoining both square root of two and square root of three to Q. Write K for Q of square root of two. The central question is whether square root of three already belongs to K. If it did, there would be rational numbers a and b with square root of three equal to a plus b times square root of two.

Square both sides. We get three equal to a squared plus two b squared plus two a b times square root of two. Since square root of two is irrational, its coefficient must vanish: two a b equals zero. Thus a equals zero or b equals zero.

If b equals zero, we would have a rational square root of three, which does not exist. If a equals zero, we would have b squared equals three halves, and three halves is not the square of a rational number. The prime factorization of a rational square has even exponent at every prime, whereas three halves has an odd exponent at both two and three. Both possibilities fail. Therefore square root of three is not in K.

Consequently x squared minus three is irreducible over K, and L has degree two over K. We already knew K has degree two over Q. The tower law tells us that the total degree of L over Q is the product, two times two, which is four.

The field L therefore has a Q-basis consisting of one, square root of two, square root of three, and square root of six. Every element of L is a rational linear combination of those four basis elements. The proof of linear independence can be obtained from the two successive degree-two bases, rather than by solving a new four-variable equation from scratch.

## Why degrees multiply in a tower

The tower law is easy to remember, but its proof is worth learning. Suppose F lies inside K, which lies inside L. Let c-one through c-m form a basis for K over F. Let b-one through b-n form a basis for L over K. Claim that all products b-i times c-j, with one index from each basis, form a basis for L over F.

For spanning, write any element of L as a K-linear combination of the b elements. Then expand each coefficient in the c basis over F. We have expressed the original element as an F-linear combination of products b times c.

For independence, suppose an F-linear combination of those products is zero. Group terms by b. The coefficient of each b is now an element of K, expressed as an F-linear combination of c elements. Independence of the b basis over K forces every one of those K coefficients to vanish. Independence of the c basis over F then forces every original F coefficient to vanish.

Thus the product collection is a basis with m n elements. Degrees multiply. We did not rely on radicals or special polynomials; this is a theorem about composing finite-dimensional vector spaces in a tower of fields.

A useful warning follows. If two elements each have degree two over F, adjoining both need not give degree four. The second may already lie in the field generated by the first. Or it may genuinely double the degree. The tower law measures what happens only after we determine the degree of each *successive* extension.

## A single number that secretly contains two roots

Consider beta equal to square root of two plus square root of three. It looks like one algebraic element, but it generates all of L over Q.

The trick is to multiply beta by square root of three minus square root of two. Their product is three minus two, which is one. Therefore beta inverse equals square root of three minus square root of two. Both beta and its inverse belong to Q beta, since a field containing a nonzero element also contains its inverse.

Add beta and beta inverse. The square root of two terms cancel, leaving twice square root of three. Subtract beta inverse from beta. The square root of three terms cancel, leaving twice square root of two. By dividing by two, which is allowed in Q, we recover both original square roots from beta.

So Q beta contains square root of two and square root of three, and therefore contains L. The reverse inclusion is immediate because beta lies in L. Hence Q beta equals L. A single element generates a field extension of degree four.

Let's find its minimal polynomial. Square beta: we obtain five plus two square root of six. Thus beta squared minus five equals two square root of six. Square again: beta to the fourth minus ten beta squared plus twenty-five equals twenty-four. Rearranging gives beta to the fourth minus ten beta squared plus one equals zero.

That polynomial is monic of degree four, and beta generates an extension of degree four. Therefore it must be the minimal polynomial over Q. We have proved irreducibility without trying to factor a quartic directly. The degree calculation settled it.

The point is not that every quartic polynomial can be understood so quickly. It is that structural information about the generated field can be stronger than blind algebraic manipulation of a displayed equation.

## Conjugates and choices of sign

An algebraic number's minimal polynomial may have several roots in a larger field. For beta, the four numbers formed by independently choosing plus or minus signs in front of square root of two and square root of three all satisfy that quartic polynomial. They are distinct: none of the two square roots is zero, and their magnitudes differ.

These four roots are the conjugates of beta over Q. Each gives a possible image of beta under a Q-preserving field embedding into a field containing all the roots. Why must an embedding send a root to another root of the same polynomial? Because an embedding fixing Q preserves addition and multiplication. If the polynomial evaluates to zero at beta, applying the embedding gives the same polynomial evaluated at the image of beta, again equal to zero.

For our field L, independently flipping the signs of square root of two and square root of three gives four Q-automorphisms. An automorphism is a structure-preserving bijection from the field to itself that fixes the base field. Compose two sign flips and their signs multiply. The resulting group has four elements, every nonidentity element has order two, and the group is isomorphic to a product of two copies of the group with two elements.

This group acts on the field and on the roots of polynomials, linking our earlier study of group actions to field extensions. Some elements are fixed by certain subgroups of automorphisms. Flipping both square roots, for instance, leaves their product square root of six unchanged. The subfield Q of square root of six is exactly the collection of elements of L fixed by that particular two-element subgroup. Likewise, the other two nontrivial sign-flip subgroups fix Q of square root of two and Q of square root of three respectively.

That correspondence between subgroups and intermediate fields is the beginning of Galois theory. Our example makes it concrete: a field extension can carry a symmetry group, and the ways to forget some symmetries can correspond to fields lying between the original and extended fields.

## An extension need not contain all conjugates

It would be a mistake to assume that every field obtained by adjoining an algebraic element contains every root of its minimal polynomial. Consider the real cube root of two. Its minimal polynomial over Q is x cubed minus two, which is irreducible by Eisenstein's criterion at the prime two. The field Q of the real cube root of two is a three-dimensional extension of Q, with basis one, alpha, and alpha squared.

But the polynomial has two additional complex roots, obtained by multiplying the real cube root by nonreal cube roots of unity. Those are not in Q alpha, because Q alpha consists entirely of real numbers. To obtain a field containing all three roots, we must adjoin a primitive complex cube root of unity as well.

The extension generated by the one real root and the full splitting field are different objects. Indeed, the only Q-automorphism of Q alpha is the identity: an automorphism must send alpha to a root of its minimal polynomial that lies inside the same field, and the only such root is alpha itself. The field has degree three, but only one automorphism fixing Q.

This contrast explains why Galois theory has hypotheses. For an extension to be Galois, it must be normal and separable. In characteristic zero, algebraic extensions are separable, so in these examples the missing property is normality: the single-root cubic field has not yet included all the conjugate roots. Its full splitting field has a richer group of symmetries. The Galois correspondence applies in its clean form to the splitting field, not indiscriminately to every intermediate field generated by one root.

## From arithmetic to structure

We began by asking how much larger a field becomes when we adjoin an algebraic element. The answer was its dimension as a vector space. Minimal polynomials measured that dimension for simple extensions. The tower law explained why degrees multiply. A worked biquadratic extension showed how one generator can conceal several radicals. Conjugates showed that equations can have symmetries, and the example of the cube root of two showed why a field may fail to contain all its own conjugate possibilities.

This prepares the central question of Galois theory: given the field in which an equation fully splits, how do its automorphisms encode the possible intermediate fields? We now have all the ingredients that make the question meaningful—field quotients, minimal polynomials, basis degrees, towers, and group actions—and concrete examples against which the general theorem can be tested.

## Mathematical companion (not spoken)

For a field extension \(L/F\), \([L:F]=\dim_F L\). If \(\alpha\) is algebraic over \(F\) with monic irreducible minimal polynomial \(m_\alpha\) of degree \(n\), then
\[
F(\alpha)\cong F[x]/(m_\alpha),\quad
[F(\alpha):F]=n,\quad
\{1,\alpha,\ldots,\alpha^{n-1}\}\text{ is an }F\text{-basis}.
\]

**Tower law:** If \(F\subseteq K\subseteq L\) with finite degrees, \([L:F]=[L:K][K:F]\). If \(\{c_j\}\) is an \(F\)-basis of \(K\) and \(\{b_i\}\) is a \(K\)-basis of \(L\), then \(\{b_i c_j\}\) is an \(F\)-basis of \(L\).

**Biquadratic example:** Let \(L=\mathbb Q(\sqrt2,\sqrt3)\). Then \(\sqrt3\notin\mathbb Q(\sqrt2)\), and
\[
[L:\mathbb Q]=4,\qquad L=\mathbb Q\oplus\mathbb Q\sqrt2\oplus\mathbb Q\sqrt3\oplus\mathbb Q\sqrt6.
\]
For \(\beta=\sqrt2+\sqrt3\), \(\beta^{-1}=\sqrt3-\sqrt2\). Hence
\[
\sqrt3=\frac{\beta+\beta^{-1}}2,\quad \sqrt2=\frac{\beta-\beta^{-1}}2,\quad \mathbb Q(\beta)=L.
\]
Squaring \(\beta^2-5=2\sqrt6\) yields \(\beta^4-10\beta^2+1=0\). Since \([\mathbb Q(\beta):\mathbb Q]=4\), this is the minimal polynomial.

**Automorphisms:** The four maps \((\sqrt2,\sqrt3)\mapsto(\pm\sqrt2,\pm\sqrt3)\) define the four \(\mathbb Q\)-automorphisms of \(L\), giving \(\operatorname{Gal}(L/\mathbb Q)\cong C_2\times C_2\). The simultaneous sign flip fixes \(\mathbb Q(\sqrt6)\). The three order-two subgroups correspond to the three quadratic intermediate fields.

**Non-normal contrast:** \(\mathbb Q(\sqrt[3]2)\) has degree three (Eisenstein at 2 for \(x^3-2\)) but has only the identity \(\mathbb Q\)-automorphism, since its only root of that polynomial lying in the real field is the real cube root. Its splitting field is \(\mathbb Q(\sqrt[3]2,\zeta_3)\), not the simple real extension. In characteristic zero, finite algebraic extensions are separable; normality is the missing condition here.

**Worked checks and exercises**

2. Prove \([\mathbb Q(\sqrt2,\sqrt3):\mathbb Q]=4\) without assuming the four candidate basis elements are independent; use the tower law and the non-membership of \(\sqrt3\) in \(\mathbb Q(\sqrt2)\).
3. Derive the minimal polynomial of \(\sqrt2+\sqrt3\) and prove irreducibility using its generated extension degree.
4. Explain why the automorphism exchanging \(\sqrt2\) with \(-\sqrt2\) while fixing \(\sqrt3\) preserves all rational polynomial identities in the two generators.
5. State precisely why \(\mathbb Q(\sqrt[3]2)\) has degree three but lacks three automorphisms fixing \(\mathbb Q\).
