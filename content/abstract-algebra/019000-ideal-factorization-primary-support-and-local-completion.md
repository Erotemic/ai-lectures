# Ideal factorization, primary support, and local completion

## Dedekind Domains: Factorization Survives When Elements Fail

In the integers, a number has one prime factorization, apart from reordering and signs. Six is two times three, and no different collection of primes can produce six. This fact is so familiar that it is tempting to expect the same behavior in any ring of numbers.

But adjoin a square root of negative five to the integers. Elements now look like a plus b times the square root of negative five, where a and b are integers. Inside this ring, six has two factorizations: two times three, and one plus the square root of negative five multiplied by one minus that square root. Those four factors are irreducible, and the two factorizations are genuinely different.

What broke? And is the failure the end of unique factorization, or did we choose the wrong objects to factor?

### Verify the failure carefully

For a plus b times the square root of negative five, multiply by its conjugate. The result is a squared plus five b squared, a nonnegative integer called the norm. Norms multiply. A unit must have norm one; in this ring the only units are plus one and minus one.

The number two has norm four. A nontrivial factorization of two into nonunits would require two factors of norm two. But there is no element with norm two: a squared plus five b squared cannot equal two. So two is irreducible. The same reasoning handles three, whose norm is nine and would require factors of norm three. The two conjugate elements one plus or minus the square root of negative five have norm six. A nontrivial factorization would need a factor of norm two or three; neither exists. Thus those two elements are irreducible as well.

Yet six really is both two times three and the product of the conjugate pair. None of these factors is an associate of one in the other factorization. Unique factorization of elements has failed.

Notice the distinction between irreducible and prime. An irreducible element has no nontrivial factorization. A prime element has the stronger property that whenever it divides a product, it divides one of the factors. In an integral domain where unique factorization works, irreducibles are prime. Here two is irreducible but not prime: it divides the product of the conjugate pair, which equals six, but it divides neither member of that pair.

### Let ideals carry the factors

Instead of factoring an element, consider the ideal generated by that element. Ideals remember divisibility in a different language: if an element a divides b, then the ideal generated by b is contained in the ideal generated by a.

For the ring of integers with the square root of negative five, define an ideal P generated by two and by one plus the square root of negative five. It has index two in the ring, meaning the quotient contains two elements. There are also two different ideals of index three: Q generated by three and one plus the square root of negative five, and Q conjugate generated by three and one minus that square root.

These are nonzero prime ideals, in fact maximal ideals. The principal ideal generated by two equals P squared. The ideal generated by three equals Q times Q conjugate. And the ideal generated by one plus the square root of negative five equals P times Q. Its conjugate gives P times Q conjugate. Now the two factorizations of six produce exactly the same ideal factorization: P squared times Q times Q conjugate.

The apparent ambiguity has disappeared after passing from elements to ideals. An ideal factor is not required to come from a single element, and that extra freedom is exactly what this ring needs.

### Which rings permit this repair?

Not every integral domain has unique factorization of ideals. A Dedekind domain is a Noetherian integral domain that is integrally closed in its field of fractions and has Krull dimension one, apart from the harmless field case with dimension zero. Dimension one here means that every nonzero prime ideal is maximal. Being integrally closed means that if a fraction satisfies a monic polynomial equation with coefficients in the ring, the fraction was already in the ring.

Those hypotheses have consequences. Every nonzero ideal factors uniquely as a product of nonzero prime ideals. Every nonzero ideal is invertible as a fractional ideal: there exists another fractional ideal whose product with it is the whole ring. This is not the same as saying every ideal is generated by one element. In our square-root-of-negative-five example, the ideal P is not principal, because a generator would have to have absolute norm two, and we proved that no such element exists.

Why do the prime ideals behave so cleanly? Localization helps explain it. If we localize a Dedekind domain at a nonzero prime ideal, we obtain a discrete valuation ring. In such a local ring, every nonzero ideal is a power of its unique maximal ideal. It is a one-prime arithmetic world with a single exponent measuring divisibility. Globally, a nonzero ideal is supported at finitely many primes. Its list of local exponents gives the unique ideal factorization.

This local picture does not by itself replace the proof of the Dedekind factorization theorem; the Noetherian and integrally closed hypotheses are used to show that the local rings really are discrete valuation rings and to assemble the finite data. But it explains the structure of the result: prime ideal factorization is ordinary divisibility by one prime, carried out at every relevant location.

### The obstruction becomes a group

There is a further reward for treating ideals as objects. Take all nonzero fractional ideals and allow multiplication. In a Dedekind domain they form an abelian group because every nonzero fractional ideal has an inverse. Principal fractional ideals form a subgroup. The quotient is the ideal class group.

If the class group is trivial, every nonzero ideal is principal, and the ring is a principal ideal domain. In a Dedekind domain, that also implies unique factorization of elements. Conversely, a nontrivial class group records exactly why ideals cannot always be expressed using a single generator.

In the square-root-of-negative-five ring, the ideal P gives a nontrivial class, but its square is principal because P squared is the ideal generated by two. So the class of P has order two. One can show that the entire class group in this example has two elements, though that stronger computation needs an additional argument about all possible ideal classes.

The point is not merely that a theorem once thought false can be repaired. The new object exposes an invariant of the ring. Unique factorization of ideals does not hide the failure of element factorization; the class group measures that failure.

### Where the story goes

Dedekind domains include the rings of integers of number fields. Their prime ideals encode how rational primes split, ramify, or remain prime in an extension. Ideal classes connect arithmetic to lattices, geometry, and eventually class field theory.

We began with an equation involving the number six. It was not a minor arithmetic oddity. It revealed that elements alone are too restrictive as factors in some number systems. Enlarging the notion of factor to an ideal restores uniqueness under precise hypotheses and creates an invariant that tells us how far the ring lies from principal ideal arithmetic. The lesson is methodological: when an expected structure fails, ask whether the right mathematical objects have been chosen.

Unique ideal factorization depends on the Dedekind hypotheses. For more general Noetherian rings, primary decomposition replaces that picture with pieces organized by prime support.

## Primary Decomposition, Associated Primes, and Support

Over the integers, a composite number factors into prime powers. In a polynomial ring with several variables, the analogue of factorization is subtler. An ideal need not be generated by one polynomial, and two ideals can define the same set of solutions while carrying different algebraic information. Primary decomposition is a way of separating that information into pieces concentrated along prime ideals.

To see why, take a polynomial ring in x and y over a field. The equation x times y equals zero defines two coordinate axes. The ideal generated by x times y is the intersection of the ideal generated by x and the ideal generated by y. A polynomial belongs to both of those ideals exactly when it is divisible by both x and y; because x and y have no common nonunit factor, it is divisible by their product.

So the union of two geometric components corresponds to an intersection of two algebraic ideals. This reversal between unions and intersections is one of the first adjustments needed when passing from elementary geometry to algebraic geometry.

### Prime ideals as locations

An ideal P is prime if, whenever a product of two elements lies in P, at least one factor lies in P. In a polynomial ring, a prime ideal can be regarded as a generalized location: sometimes a single point, sometimes a whole irreducible variety. The prime ideal generated by x defines the entire y-axis; the prime generated by both x and y defines the origin.

A primary ideal Q weakens the product condition. If a product a times b lies in Q but a does not, then some positive power of b must lie in Q. The radical of Q, consisting of elements whose powers land in Q, is a prime ideal. Think of a primary component as structure supported along that prime location, possibly with multiplicity or nilpotents that ordinary zero sets cannot see.

For example, in the ring of polynomials in one variable, the ideal generated by x squared is primary, with radical generated by x. The equations x equals zero and x squared equals zero have the same solutions over a field. But their quotient rings are different. In the second quotient, the class of x is nonzero and has square zero. Geometry sees the same point; algebra remembers a thicker point.

### An embedded component you can calculate

Now consider the ideal generated by x squared and x times y in the polynomial ring k of x and y. Every generator is divisible by x, so the zero set contains the y-axis. In fact the set of zeros is just that axis: x squared vanishes only when x vanishes, and then x y vanishes automatically.

One decomposition of this ideal is the intersection of the ideal generated by x with the ideal generated by x squared and y. You can verify it directly. Intersecting with the multiples of x leaves precisely the combinations generated by x squared and x y.

The first component is prime, supported along the y-axis. The second is primary to the maximal ideal generated by x and y, so it is supported at the origin. The origin lies on the axis; it is not another irreducible component of the underlying zero set. It is called an embedded associated prime. Its existence records algebraic structure along the axis that is concentrated more tightly at the origin.

That example answers a recurring question: why not replace every ideal by its radical and work only with zero sets? Because the radical discards nilpotent and embedded information, exactly the information required to distinguish the two quotient rings in this example.

### What the decomposition theorem actually says

In a commutative Noetherian ring, every ideal admits a finite primary decomposition: an intersection of primary ideals. The Noetherian assumption matters. It constrains ascending chains of ideals and permits the finite decomposition theorem; it is not merely a convenient setting for notation.

Primary decomposition is not unique term by term. Some choices of primary components can differ, especially at embedded primes. In an irredundant decomposition satisfying the usual reducedness conditions, however, the set of radicals of the primary components is uniquely determined. These are associated primes of the quotient ring. Among them, the minimal ones describe the irreducible components of the support. The embedded ones can reveal additional module structure.

There is a useful second definition of an associated prime: a prime ideal that arises as the annihilator of an element of the module. For a finitely generated module over a Noetherian ring, this language makes associated primes intrinsic, rather than artifacts of a particular list of primary components.

### Support and localization

Suppose M is a module over a commutative ring R. Its support is the set of primes P for which the localization of M at P is nonzero. Localization asks which information survives when every element outside a chosen prime is allowed as a denominator. This is the right way to ask whether a module is present near a prime location.

For the cyclic module R modulo I, the support is exactly the set of prime ideals containing I, conventionally called V of I. For a finitely generated module, the support is also V of its annihilator. In our embedded-component example, the support is the y-axis; the embedded prime at the origin is contained in that support but contributes additional information not visible from the set of supporting primes alone.

That distinction is essential. Support says where the module lives. Associated primes can detect ways it lives there, including embedded concentrations. They are related invariants, not synonyms.

### What becomes possible

Primary decomposition lets one study polynomial equations component by component, but its main lesson is broader. A module can have a visible geometric skeleton and invisible algebraic thickness. Localization inspects one prime at a time. Associated primes flag locations where elements have a specified annihilator. Decomposition theorems recover a finite list of such structures when Noetherian hypotheses are available.

Even the elementary equation x y equals zero now looks different. It is not just a union of two lines. It is an invitation to keep track of what ideal cuts out those lines, which primary pieces appear, and what extra structure is lost when we replace an ideal by its set of solutions. The algebra is not pedantry added to geometry; it is a means of preserving information that geometry alone can forget.

## Completion, Discrete Valuations, and Hensel's Lemma

In ordinary numerical analysis, we regard two real numbers as close when their difference is small in absolute value. In number theory, there is another useful notion of closeness. Two integers may be called close when their difference is divisible by a very large power of a prime. The numbers one and one plus a million need not be close in the ordinary metric. But if the million is replaced by a sufficiently high power of seven, they can be extremely close in the seven-adic metric.

This is not just a change of vocabulary. It produces a new complete number system, new forms of Newton iteration, and a systematic method for lifting algebraic solutions through the powers of a prime.

### Measuring divisibility rather than magnitude

Fix a prime p. Every nonzero rational number can be written uniquely as p to an integer power times a fraction whose numerator and denominator are both not divisible by p. The integer exponent is the p-adic valuation. A high valuation means a large power of p divides the number. The p-adic absolute value assigns p raised to minus that valuation. A rational with a high power of p dividing it has small p-adic absolute value, even if its ordinary numerical magnitude is huge.

The defining inequality is stronger than the familiar triangle inequality. The p-adic absolute value of a sum is at most the maximum of the absolute values of its summands. As a result, every triangle is isosceles in a certain metric sense; distances behave differently from those on the real line. A sequence is Cauchy if its differences eventually become divisible by arbitrarily large powers of p.

The rational numbers are not complete in that metric. Completing them produces the p-adic field, conventionally written Q sub p. The closure of the ordinary integers is the ring Z sub p of p-adic integers. Its elements can be represented by compatible residue classes modulo p, p squared, p cubed, and so on. Compatibility means that each residue reduces to the preceding one. This inverse-limit description makes the ring tangible without requiring us to picture a mysterious infinite decimal.

### Localizing is not completing

There are two operations worth keeping separate. Localize the integers at the prime p: allow denominators not divisible by p. This gives rational fractions whose denominators are p-adic units. It is a local ring, with a unique maximal ideal generated by p, but it is not complete. Complete that local ring in its p-adic topology and you obtain Z sub p.

These constructions do different jobs in an approximation argument. Localization permits division by the appropriate denominators; completion ensures that a compatible sequence of increasingly accurate approximations converges to an element of the ring.

For a particularly transparent example, consider the geometric series one plus p plus p squared and so on. Its ordinary real series diverges when p is greater than one. But in the p-adic metric, the powers of p approach zero, so the series converges. Its sum is one divided by one minus p, an ordinary rational number whose denominator is not divisible by p. A single expression is divergent in one metric and convergent in the other.

### A polynomial root modulo seven

Consider the equation x squared equals two. Modulo seven, x equals three is a solution because nine differs from two by seven. Can we find an integer solution modulo forty-nine that reduces to three modulo seven?

Write x as three plus seven times t. Expanding the square and subtracting two gives seven plus forty-two times t plus forty-nine times t squared. For the expression to vanish modulo forty-nine, we need one plus six times t to vanish modulo seven. Choosing t equal to one works. Thus x equal to ten is a solution modulo forty-nine. Ten squared is one hundred, and one hundred differs from two by ninety-eight, which is twice forty-nine.

That is one step of Hensel lifting. A root modulo p has become a root modulo p squared. If the appropriate derivative is invertible modulo p, the process can be repeated to all higher powers of p. The compatible sequence defines an actual root in the p-adic integers.

### Why the derivative appears

The calculation resembles Newton's method. Suppose f of a is zero modulo p to the power k. Try correcting a by a multiple of p to the power k. To first order, f at the corrected point equals f of a plus the correction times f prime of a, while the remaining terms are divisible by at least p to the power two k. To cancel the residual modulo the next prime power, we must solve a linear congruence involving f prime of a.

If the derivative is not divisible by p, it has a multiplicative inverse modulo p, so there is a unique correction digit at each stage. This gives the simplest form of Hensel's lemma: a simple root modulo p of a polynomial with p-adic integer coefficients lifts uniquely to a root in Z sub p with the prescribed residue class.

The adjective simple is doing work. If the derivative vanishes modulo p, the lifting argument no longer guarantees existence or uniqueness. Some multiple roots do lift, some do not, and multiple lifts can occur. One should not silently apply the simple-root theorem to every congruence solution.

### A useful failure

Take the equation x squared equals p, and work modulo p. The residue x equals zero solves it, but the derivative two x is also zero modulo p. Over the p-adic integers there is no solution: the valuation of a square is even, while the valuation of p is one. Thus the modular solution does not lift to a p-adic root. The obstacle is arithmetic, not an artifact of a poor choice of approximation.

Here a valuation gives a stronger obstruction than the failed linear correction alone. It shows why some congruences have solutions at the first level that disappear at higher precision.

### Beyond isolated roots

Hensel's lemma is useful in local factorization, algebraic number theory, and computational algebra. A polynomial factorization modulo p can sometimes be lifted to a factorization modulo higher powers, provided suitable coprimality hypotheses hold. One can then combine local information with global arithmetic questions, while remaining careful that a solution in every local completion need not always give a solution over the rationals. Local consistency and global solvability are distinct questions.

Completion adds limits to arithmetic. Valuations measure how much divisibility a number contains. Hensel's lemma shows how a sufficiently nondegenerate local answer can be refined indefinitely. The result is a version of analysis in which divisibility, rather than Euclidean distance, determines what it means to approach the truth.

### Dedekind Domains: Factorization Survives When Elements Fail

**Track:** Abstract Algebra. **Background:** rings and ideals, modules, prime ideals, and localization. **Central question:** what can remain unique when elements cease to factor uniquely into primes?

#### Mathematical companion (not spoken)

Let \(A=\mathbb Z[\sqrt{-5}]\), \(\alpha=\sqrt{-5}\), \(N(a+b\alpha)=a^2+5b^2\). Then
\[
6=2\cdot3=(1+\alpha)(1-\alpha),
\]
with all four factors irreducible because there are no elements of norm \(2\) or \(3\). Their norms are \(4,9,6,6\), respectively. This does not assert they are prime elements.

Let
\[
P=(2,1+\alpha),\quad Q=(3,1+\alpha),\quad\bar Q=(3,1-\alpha).
\]
These ideals have norms \(2,3,3\), and
\[
(2)=P^2,\qquad(3)=Q\bar Q,\qquad(1+\alpha)=PQ,\qquad(1-\alpha)=P\bar Q.
\]
For example, \((1+\alpha)\subseteq PQ\) and both ideals have norm six; hence equality. Similarly, \(P^2\subseteq(2)\) and both have norm four. The identical factorization of \((6)\) follows.

**Dedekind factorization theorem.** A Noetherian, integrally closed integral domain of dimension one has unique factorization of every nonzero proper ideal into nonzero prime ideals; localizations at nonzero primes are discrete valuation rings. For a Dedekind domain, the fractional ideals form a group \(I(A)\), and the ideal class group is \(\mathrm{Cl}(A)=I(A)/\{(a):a\in\mathrm{Frac}(A)^\times\}\). The ideal \(P\) above is nonprincipal since an element generating it would have norm two; \(P^2=(2)\) makes its class 2-torsion. The stronger statement \(\mathrm{Cl}(A)\cong\mathbb Z/2\mathbb Z\) is not proved here.

##### Exercises with answers

1. Why is \(1+\sqrt{-5}\) irreducible? **Answer:** its norm is six; a proper factorization would require factors with norms two and three, neither possible.
2. Is every UFD a Dedekind domain? **No:** \(k[x,y]\) is a UFD of dimension two and not a Dedekind domain.
3. Does every Dedekind domain have a trivial class group? **No:** \(\mathbb Z[\sqrt{-5}]\) is a Dedekind domain with nonprincipal ideals.
4. What is the difference between \((p)\) and a prime ideal \(\mathfrak p\) lying over \(p\) in a number ring? **Answer:** \((p)\) may factor into several prime ideals, possibly with repeated exponents.

### Primary Decomposition, Associated Primes, and Support

**Track:** Abstract Algebra. **Background:** ideals, localization, prime ideals, Noetherian rings. **Central question:** how does an ideal encode irreducible geometric pieces and the embedded structure that a set of zeros hides?

#### Mathematical companion (not spoken)

For commutative \(R\), \(Q\subsetneq R\) is **primary** if \(ab\in Q\) and \(a\notin Q\) imply \(b^n\in Q\) for some \(n\ge1\). Its radical \(\sqrt Q\) is prime. For a Noetherian ring, every ideal has a finite primary decomposition \(I=\bigcap_i Q_i\).

In \(R=k[x,y]\):
\[
(xy)=(x)\cap(y),\qquad I=(x^2,xy)=(x)\cap(x^2,y).
\]
The first decomposition has two minimal prime components. In the second, \((x^2,y)\) is \((x,y)\)-primary; \(\operatorname{Ass}_R(R/I)=\{(x),(x,y)\}\). To see the embedded prime directly, the nonzero class \(\overline x\in R/I\) has annihilator \((x,y)\), while the class \(\overline y\) has annihilator \((x)\). (Check \(\operatorname{ann}(\overline y)=(x)\) by reducing modulo \(x\).)

The support of \(M\) is \(\operatorname{Supp}_R(M)=\{\mathfrak p:M_{\mathfrak p}\ne0\}\). For any ideal \(I\),
\[
\operatorname{Supp}_R(R/I)=V(I)=\{\mathfrak p:I\subseteq\mathfrak p\}.
\]
For a finitely generated module, \(\operatorname{Supp}(M)=V(\operatorname{Ann}M)\). Minimal primes of \(\operatorname{Supp}(M)\) are minimal associated primes (for nonzero finite modules over Noetherian rings), whereas embedded associated primes need not define new irreducible components.

##### Exercises with answers

1. Is \((x^3)\) primary in \(k[x]\)? **Yes:** it is \((x)\)-primary, and its radical is \((x)\).
2. What is the variety of \((x^2,xy)\) over any field? **Answer:** \(\{(0,y):y\in k\}\), the y-axis.
3. Does replacing an ideal by its radical preserve its quotient ring? **No:** \(k[x]/(x^2)\) has a nonzero nilpotent and \(k[x]/(x)\) does not.
4. Does a primary decomposition have unique individual primary ideals? **Not in general:** the associated prime radicals are intrinsic in a reduced irredundant decomposition, but embedded components can vary.

### Completion, Discrete Valuations, and Hensel's Lemma

**Track:** Abstract Algebra. **Background:** modular arithmetic, local rings, ideals, and Newton's method. **Central question:** when can a solution modulo a prime be promoted consistently to solutions modulo every prime power?

#### Mathematical companion (not spoken)

For \(x\in\mathbb Q^\times\), write \(x=p^{v_p(x)}u\) with \(v_p(u)=0\). Define \(|x|_p=p^{-v_p(x)}\) and \(|0|_p=0\). Then
\[
|x+y|_p\le \max(|x|_p,|y|_p),\qquad
\mathbb Z_p\simeq\varprojlim_n\mathbb Z/p^n\mathbb Z.
\]
The localization \(\mathbb Z_{(p)}=\{a/b\in\mathbb Q:p\nmid b\}\) is not complete; its \(p\)-adic completion is \(\mathbb Z_p\). Completing \(\mathbb Q\) gives \(\mathbb Q_p\).

**Simple-root Hensel lemma.** If \(f\in\mathbb Z_p[x]\) and \(a_0\in\mathbb Z_p\) satisfy \(f(a_0)\equiv0\pmod p\) and \(f'(a_0)\not\equiv0\pmod p\), there is a unique \(a\in\mathbb Z_p\) with \(a\equiv a_0\pmod p\) and \(f(a)=0\). A digit correction \(a_{k+1}=a_k+p^k t\) with \(t\in\{0,\ldots,p-1\}\) is chosen to solve
\[
f(a_k)/p^k+t f'(a_k)\equiv0\pmod p.
\]
For \(f(x)=x^2-2\), \(p=7\), \(a_1=3\), the correction satisfies \(1+6t\equiv0\pmod7\), so \(t=1\) and \(a_2=10\) mod \(49\). Indeed \(10^2-2=98\).

**Failure case.** \(x^2=p\) has solution \(x=0\) modulo \(p\), but none in \(\mathbb Z_p\), since \(v_p(x^2)=2v_p(x)\) cannot equal \(1\).

##### Exercises with answers

1. Evaluate \(|49|_7\). **Answer:** \(7^{-2}\).
2. Does \(1+p+p^2+\cdots\) converge \(p\)-adically? **Yes**, to \((1-p)^{-1}\).
3. Lift the other root \(x\equiv4\pmod7\) of \(x^2-2\) to mod \(49\). **Answer:** \(x=39\): \(39^2-2=1519=31\cdot49\).
4. Why is \(f'(a_0)\not\equiv0\pmod p\) central? **Answer:** it makes the linear correction uniquely solvable modulo \(p\).
