# The Ocean Runs on Plankton, Currents, and Falling Snow

Imagine hovering above the open ocean. There is no shore in sight. The water looks blue and comparatively empty. Now imagine trying to feed an animal weighing tens of tons in this apparent desert. A whale cannot survive by finding one enormous meal hidden under every wave. The ocean must turn sunlight and dissolved materials into living food on an enormous scale, then make enough of that food available to animals that may be millions of times larger than the organisms that first produced it.

The surprising part is that this production often depends on creatures too small to notice from a boat. Phytoplankton include many kinds of microscopic algae and cyanobacteria. They capture light and use it to build organic matter from inorganic carbon, given suitable nutrients and other conditions. They are not a single species, a single evolutionary group, or a miniature version of land plants. Different forms have different sizes, shells, pigments, ways of obtaining nutrients, and rates of growth. They occupy an ocean that can be nutrient-rich in one place and almost nutritionally starved in another.

To understand why, begin with two necessities that are often separated in the sea: light and nutrients. Sunlight enters the upper ocean but becomes weaker with depth. Nutrients such as nitrate and phosphate may be abundant in deeper water because sinking organic matter is broken down there. Yet the deep water may be too dark for photosynthesis. Near the surface, the light is available, but organisms may have consumed much of the usable nutrient supply. Life is limited not by a single shortage called 'food' but by the difficulty of obtaining several resources in the same place at the same time.

A physical boundary helps create this separation. Surface water heated by the sun is often less dense than cold water below it. Strong density differences can restrict vertical mixing. Wind, cooling, storms, and currents can change this structure, but the ocean is not constantly stirred from top to bottom like a cup of tea. In the sunlit layer, phytoplankton can grow only while nutrients remain accessible, and their cells must not be carried into darkness too rapidly. A bloom requires not only enough sunlight but enough time and resources in the right part of the water column.

Along some coasts, winds push surface water away and deeper water rises to replace it. This is upwelling. The newly arrived water is often cold and nutrient-rich. In a productive upwelling region, phytoplankton can multiply, small animals feed on them, fish feed on smaller animals, and large predators follow the prey. The famous abundance of some fisheries off the western edges of continents is tied to this machinery. You can have a relatively cool, cloudy coastal ocean supporting tremendous biological production, while a much warmer, brilliantly blue part of the open ocean produces comparatively little new biomass per unit area.

That contrast gives ocean color a biological meaning. Phytoplankton contain pigments, including chlorophyll, that affect the light reflected from the sea. Satellites can measure ocean color over huge regions and estimate aspects of surface chlorophyll. NASA uses such measurements to reveal swirls and blooms that ships could never map completely on their own. But a chlorophyll map is not a photograph of every creature in the food web. Pigment per cell can change, different species carry different pigment mixtures, suspended sediments can interfere in coastal waters, and satellites primarily see the illuminated surface. To learn who is there and what they are doing, scientists still collect water, examine cells, measure nutrients, and analyze genetic or biochemical signals.

A bloom itself can mislead us. If phytoplankton increase rapidly, it might mean conditions have favored growth. It might also mean their grazers have not kept pace. A particular group could flourish because another was suppressed. In some places, the spring bloom is associated with a seasonal combination of increasing light, changing mixing, and interactions with predators. Researchers still debate which processes dominate particular bloom events. A satellite showing an expanding green patch tells us that the light-absorbing material changed; it does not reveal which link in the food web caused the change.

Now shrink yourself to the scale of a copepod, a small crustacean drifting through this water. Many copepods feed on phytoplankton or other plankton, and some can consume surprisingly large amounts relative to their body size. A fish larva may feed on the copepod. A larger fish may eat that fish. Seabirds, seals, and whales may obtain energy from these longer chains or from shorter ones. The chain is useful for introducing energy transfer, but the ocean does not operate as a neatly numbered ladder. Some fish eat plankton directly. Predators change diets with age and season. Small organisms may prey upon each other while also being eaten by larger animals.

Consider a baleen whale feeding on dense patches of krill. Krill are small crustaceans, not fish, and many feed in large swarms. The whale has evolved enormous structures and muscular behaviors for concentrating tiny prey from seawater. At one end of the interaction is a bloom shaped by physics and nutrient chemistry. At the other is a huge mammal making the most of that concentrated energy. The whale's size is impressive, but the more interesting ecological question is why krill become dense enough, at certain times and places, for that feeding strategy to pay off.

Where the prey is sparse, searching may cost more energy than feeding returns. Where currents concentrate krill, the balance can change. This is why predator movement cannot be understood from a simple map of sea-surface temperature alone. Food webs occur in three dimensions and through time. A patch that was profitable yesterday may be unprofitable tomorrow. Life in the ocean depends on repeated coincidences of light, nutrients, current, behavior, and season.

Much of the ocean's food web is invisible even after we recognize phytoplankton. When photosynthetic organisms grow, some of the organic carbon ends up as dissolved molecules in seawater. Other organic molecules come from excretion, grazing, and the breakdown of dead cells. Many bacteria and archaea can use some of this dissolved material. Small protists graze on bacteria, and those protists may themselves be eaten. In 1983, Farooq Azam and collaborators helped put this system into an influential framework called the microbial loop. Carbon that might once have been imagined as lost from the familiar plankton food chain can pass through microbial populations and reenter other feeding pathways.

The microbial loop does not guarantee that every carbon molecule returns to a fish. Microbes respire some carbon dioxide, transform dissolved material, and compete for nutrients. Energy is dissipated at each step. Some organic compounds resist rapid processing or remain in circulation for long periods. The importance of different pathways varies between environments. The useful insight is that microbes form active trophic links rather than merely cleaning up after the animals and algae have finished eating.

A second pathway points downward. Dead plankton, fecal pellets, fragments of shells, and sticky aggregates may begin to sink. When illuminated by underwater lights, the descending particles can look like snowflakes drifting through darkness. Oceanographers call this marine snow. Some of the material is eaten or decomposed on the way down; some reaches deeper water or the seafloor. Small animals may intercept it. Bacteria grow on it and alter how quickly it falls apart. Larger sinking particles can carry carbon below the surface waters where it was originally fixed by photosynthesis.

This is part of the biological carbon pump. The phrase sounds like a machine powered by a motor, but it means a set of processes through which biology moves carbon from the surface toward the ocean interior. Some carbon returns to the atmosphere relatively quickly. Some enters water masses that remain isolated from the surface for long periods. Some reaches sediment and may eventually be buried. The amount exported out of the sunlit zone is not the same as the amount stored for centuries. These are different measurements with different ecological and climate implications.

Zooplankton complicate the process. Some undertake daily vertical migrations, rising toward surface waters to feed during darkness and moving deeper during daylight. This movement can reduce exposure to visual predators, although the balance of costs depends on food and habitat. Migrating animals can carry carbon in their bodies and release it at depth through respiration and excretion. Other organisms package carbon into fecal pellets that sink at different rates. Deborah Steinberg and Michael Landry's review of zooplankton and the carbon cycle shows why treating all plankton as stationary particles misses important biological transport.

The deep sea has another problem. Most deep water has too little light to support ordinary photosynthesis. Many animals therefore depend, directly or indirectly, on material that was manufactured nearer the surface. A deep-sea fish may eat another animal that once ate a sinking particle or a smaller consumer. A sea cucumber may sift through sediment for edible organic matter. The amount and quality of downward food supply can influence growth, reproduction, and the abundance of bottom organisms. The deep ocean is not empty, but food scarcity can be a defining condition in many of its habitats.

In 1977, researchers working near the Galápagos Rift encountered a different route into the food web. Hydrothermal vent systems were associated with remarkable communities of animals, including giant tube worms, clams, and other invertebrates. The discovery did not mean that life had abandoned chemistry or energy budgets. Instead it demonstrated a pathway by which microorganisms could build organic matter using energy released by chemical reactions involving reduced compounds, without depending on local sunlight.

In some vent communities, microbes oxidize hydrogen sulfide or other substances and fix inorganic carbon. Giant tube worms such as Riftia house bacteria inside specialized tissues. The worm supplies access to relevant compounds, while the microbes make organic material that supports the partnership. Adult Riftia do not feed through a conventional mouth and digestive tract as many animals do. Their lifestyle depends on these symbionts. But one must avoid an attractive exaggeration: the worms are not living inside boiling water. They occupy places where warm, chemically rich vent fluids mix with colder seawater, creating a combination of conditions their tissues and partners can tolerate.

The vent discoveries expanded the map of possible ecosystems. They did not establish that the deep ocean everywhere is powered by sulfur-based chemosynthesis. Most deep-sea animal communities continue to depend strongly on food that came from above. Even chemosynthetic habitats have physical and chemical boundaries. A vent can shut off or move; a species that depends on it must disperse and find suitable new habitat. Chemical energy is not free of constraints. It becomes biologically useful only when organisms can exploit an energy-releasing reaction and obtain the other elements they need to build and maintain cells.

Ocean chemistry also places limits from another direction: the availability of oxygen. In the upper ocean, photosynthesis and exchange with the atmosphere can supply oxygen. At depth, oxygen depends partly on the history of the water mass and on the respiration occurring along its path. In certain regions, oxygen is naturally low; in some coastal waters, excessive nutrient delivery increases organic production and subsequent oxygen consumption. Animals differ greatly in their tolerances. Fish may avoid a low-oxygen area that smaller or less mobile organisms cannot escape. The phrase 'dead zone' is a shorthand for habitat stress, not a claim that every microbe has died.

How do ocean scientists untangle these overlapping processes? They combine direct water samples with sensors for temperature, salinity, oxygen, and chlorophyll. They use drifting floats and instruments attached to ships or moorings. They examine plankton under microscopes, follow genetic markers, trace stable isotopes, and count animals with nets or acoustic devices. Carbon flux can be estimated using sediment traps and other methods, each with biases. Satellite views reveal surface patterns but not all the processes beneath them. There is no single instrument that can tell you everything about the ocean's food supply.

Predicting changes in plankton communities requires tracing several connected processes. If upper-ocean warming increases stratification, nutrient delivery to some sunlit regions may decrease. Elsewhere, altered winds or currents might increase upwelling or change the timing of blooms. Changes in predators can alter the grazers, which can change how blooms develop. Fisheries remove organisms that participate in food webs; pollution can change chemistry; ocean acidification affects organisms differently depending on their physiology and mineral structures. A responsible explanation has to state which region, species, mechanism, and timescale is being considered.

Imagine again the whale at the beginning of the lecture. Its last meal may trace back to sunlight captured by organisms invisible to human eyes. That energy passed through krill and through currents that concentrated them. The same plankton community also released dissolved molecules to bacteria and produced particles that began sinking toward the deep. Some of those particles were recycled rapidly; others followed longer paths. A few kilometers away, a different current or a different nutrient history might have produced a very different food web.

The ocean is not one giant aquarium. It is an enormous collection of living habitats, linked by water movement and by transformations of chemical matter. Its great animals are possible because of processes measured in individual cells and because of planetary circulation measured across ocean basins. A blue surface may appear quiet. Underneath it, microbes recycle molecules, grazers track blooms, animals move between depths, particles fall, and chemical reactions create entire communities where the sun never reaches. Learning to see those connections is the beginning of understanding how the ocean lives.

## Research and listening companion (not spoken)

**Questions:** Why are light and mineral nutrients often separated? How do winds produce upwelling? What does ocean color reveal and what can it not reveal? What is the microbial loop? How is carbon export different from long-term sequestration? Why are vent communities not evidence that every deep-sea habitat is independent of surface productivity?

**Vocabulary:** phytoplankton, primary production, nutrient limitation, mixed layer, upwelling, chlorophyll, copepod, krill, microbial loop, marine snow, biological carbon pump, diel vertical migration, chemoautotrophy, hydrothermal vent, hypoxia.

**Evidence boundaries:** Satellite color is a proxy rather than a species census. Spring-bloom mechanisms vary. Carbon sinking below the surface is not equivalent to permanent storage. Hydrothermal vent animals inhabit mixing zones rather than boiling discharge. Vents are specific habitats; most deep-sea food webs depend on surface production. A 'dead zone' need not be entirely without life.
