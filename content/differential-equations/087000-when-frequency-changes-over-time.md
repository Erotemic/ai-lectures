# When Frequency Changes over Time

Suppose you listen to a recording that begins with a low whistle and ends with a high one. You can hear when the change happens. A single Fourier transform of the entire recording is less informative about that question. It can tell you which frequencies occurred, but it mixes contributions from the beginning, middle, and end. In fact, two recordings can have precisely the same Fourier magnitudes while playing their sounds in a different order.

How do we keep the frequency information without throwing away the clock? The short-time Fourier transform starts with an idea simple enough to perform by hand. Look at a short piece of the recording, calculate its Fourier transform, move a little way forward, and calculate again. Each transform answers a local question: which oscillations were present around this time? The succession of answers makes a picture of frequency changing over time.

The phrase around this time matters. An interval has width. A transform of that interval does not identify the exact instant at which every oscillation began. It describes the contents of the window. We will learn both the construction and its limitations, beginning with eight numbers.

## An eight-sample recording

Imagine samples arriving eight times per second. Our recording lasts one second, so it has eight samples. In the first half-second, the samples are zero, one, zero, negative one. In the second half-second, they are one, negative one, one, negative one. Those two four-number sequences could be generated by different discrete oscillations. The first is a two-hertz sine sampled at eight hertz. The second alternates signs and lies at the four-hertz Nyquist frequency of this sampler.

First take a Fourier transform of only the first four numbers. With four samples, the frequency bins are zero, two, and four hertz on the nonnegative side, because the sampling rate divided by the transform length is two hertz. The coefficient at the two-hertz bin is negative two times the imaginary unit. Its magnitude is two. The coefficient at the four-hertz bin is zero. If we convert that positive-frequency coefficient to a one-sided sinusoidal amplitude, we double the magnitude and divide by four: amplitude one, exactly what we put in.

Now transform the last four samples. Their four-hertz coefficient is four, and their two-hertz coefficient is zero. At Nyquist, a real signal's positive and negative frequency bins coincide, so we do not double that coefficient. Divide four by four and the amplitude is again one. The first local transform says two hertz. The second says four hertz. We have recovered not only which frequencies occur but also their order.

These two transforms use nonoverlapping rectangular windows: each window selects four consecutive samples and gives each selected sample weight one. A window is simply a collection of weights, multiplied pointwise into a piece of the signal before its Fourier transform. Moving a four-sample window by four samples is called a hop of four. Here the windows cover the record with no overlap.

This small example is deliberately exact. For real recordings, frequencies are rarely aligned so neatly with the chosen bins, and transitions may occur in the middle of a window. We need to understand those cases, not hide them.

## Move the window, not the physical signal

Write the sample sequence as x of n. Choose a window w of length L and a hop of H samples. For window number m, take the samples beginning at position m times H, multiply each by its window weight, and compute an L-point discrete Fourier transform. The result has two indices: m for the time window and k for the frequency bin. It is a complex number, not just an amplitude. Its magnitude describes the strength of a component in that time slice; its angle records phase relative to the local window convention.

When we slide the window by fewer than L samples, consecutive windows overlap. Suppose L is four and H is two. Then the first window uses samples zero through three, the second uses samples two through five, and the third uses samples four through seven. An event near the middle contributes to more than one transform. That is expected. Overlap produces a denser sampling of the same evolving local spectrum; it does not create new independent information about the original recording.

If windows are labeled by their central times, our original nonoverlapping half-second windows are centered approximately at a quarter-second and three-quarters of a second. Using centers rather than starts is a plotting convention, but it changes how a displayed spectrogram should be read. The program must tell you which convention it uses, especially near the edges where it may extend the signal with zeros or reflected samples.

Now replace the rectangular window with a gentle taper. One popular choice is a Hann window, whose weights are small near the ends of each piece. The taper reduces the strong sidelobes caused when a finite segment is treated as if it repeated abruptly. But it also broadens its central frequency response. A taper does not make the frequency estimate simply better in every sense: it trades some ability to separate nearby tones for less spectral leakage from strong distant tones.

The price is visible at the start and end of a finite signal. If a window goes to zero at its ends and we neither pad nor overlap appropriately, some boundary samples receive no weight at all. No Fourier calculation can recover a sample it never observed. Reconstruction conditions therefore matter just as much as forward-transform formulas.

## A spectrogram is a view, not the whole transform

A short-time Fourier transform is a two-dimensional collection of complex values, one for each time and frequency bin. To draw a spectrogram, we often square their magnitudes. Time runs horizontally, frequency vertically, and brightness or color represents local power. In some conventions the plotted quantity has already been scaled as a power spectral density; in others it is just squared transform magnitude. These are not interchangeable without normalization.

For a steady tone, the spectrogram contains a horizontal ridge. For a rising whistle, it contains a rising ridge. For a sharp click, energy spreads across many frequencies for the windows that touch the click. We should not interpret every bright pixel as an independently existing physical oscillator. Spectrograms tell us about the interaction between the signal, the window, the Fourier basis, and the plotting scale.

The phase values are usually hidden in a spectrogram. That can be convenient for seeing frequency patterns but dangerous for reconstruction. Imagine taking the eight-sample example and moving the last four samples to the front. The full eight-point Fourier magnitudes remain the same, because the new sequence is a circular shift of the old one. Yet the local transforms reveal a reversed sequence of events. The global Fourier phase, which changed under the shift, was carrying important time-order information.

There is a useful numerical check. If a signal has N samples and we circularly shift it by four samples, its eight-point Fourier coefficient at bin k is multiplied by a complex exponential of phase negative pi times k. That multiplier has magnitude one. Every full-record magnitude is therefore unchanged. By contrast, the first four-sample local transform now sees four hertz rather than two. The distinction is mathematical, not just something we hope a plot will show.

## Can we turn the windows back into a signal?

A conventional short-time Fourier transform can be inverted when the window and hop have sufficient coverage and appropriate synthesis weights. Take the inverse Fourier transform of each local spectrum to recover its windowed block. Shift those blocks back to their original positions, overlap them, and correct for the total window weight accumulated at each sample. With an analysis window used again in synthesis, the normalizing denominator is the sum of the squared window weights at that sample. Where this denominator is strictly positive, the original sample can be recovered in exact arithmetic.

Notice what we did not do: invert the magnitude-only spectrogram. The complex local coefficients contain phase; their squared magnitudes generally do not. Recovering a waveform from magnitude alone is a different, harder problem, and the answer may not be unique without additional assumptions.

Modern software exposes more than one short-time Fourier convention. A window length, a hop length, a sampling rate, a number of FFT points, and an amplitude or power scaling together determine the meaning of the numbers. If the FFT is zero-padded beyond the window length, it provides a denser set of plotted frequency samples but does not add information from outside the observed interval. If the hop is reduced, the spectrogram gets more columns but not a shorter fundamental time blur.

One should always ask: how many physical samples went into this column? At what time is it centered? What window weighted them? What units appear on the frequency and intensity axes? And does this application keep phase if I intend to reconstruct the signal?

## Choosing the right question

A single Fourier transform is excellent for measuring the spectral content of a record that is approximately steady, or for solving linear equations where frequency separates the mathematics. A sliding transform is better when the occurrence of the frequency matters. Neither is an all-purpose microscope. Very short windows make rapid events easy to locate and nearby pitches harder to distinguish. Very long windows sharpen the frequency picture but mix together changes over more time.

Our eight-sample experiment gives the essential idea. The whole record contains both a two-hertz and a four-hertz component, but its global magnitudes do not tell us which came first. The first local spectrum and the second do. We gained a clock by deliberately giving up some frequency precision. The following question is whether that trade is merely a practical inconvenience or a mathematical limit. It is a real mathematical limit, and we can derive it.

## Mathematical companion: definitions and exact calculation

For sample rate \(f_s\), \(L\)-sample analysis window \(w[n]\), hop \(H\), and \(L\)-point DFT,
\[
X[m,k]=\sum_{n=0}^{L-1}x[mH+n]w[n]e^{-2\pi i kn/L},
\quad f_k=k f_s/L,\quad t_m\approx(mH+(L-1)/2)/f_s.
\]
The time stamp is convention-dependent; the indicated value is the discrete window's center of mass for symmetric windows, not a universal API definition. With \(L=4\), \(H=4\), \(f_s=8\), \(w=(1,1,1,1)\), and
\[
x=(0,1,0,-1,\,1,-1,1,-1),
\]
the local DFTs are
\[
X[0,:]=(0,-2i,0,2i),\qquad X[1,:]=(0,0,4,0).
\]
For a real-valued block of length four, a non-DC, non-Nyquist sinusoidal amplitude at bin 1 is \(2|X[1]|/4\); at Nyquist it is \(|X[2]|/4\). Thus the two blocks show unit-amplitude two- and four-hertz components respectively.

A circular shift by four samples of the complete length-eight record satisfies \(Y[k]=e^{-2\pi i k4/8}X[k]=(-1)^kX[k]\); hence \(|Y[k]|=|X[k]|\) for every full-record bin.

A simple synthesis relation for a full complex STFT is obtained by inverting each block, multiplying by a synthesis window \(v\), and dividing the overlap sum by \(\sum_m w[n-mH]v[n-mH]\) wherever that denominator is nonzero. For \(v=w\), the denominator is \(\sum_m w[n-mH]^2\). Consistent endpoint handling and a nonvanishing denominator are essential. This is a reconstruction statement about the *complex* coefficients, not magnitudes alone.

### Exercises with answers

1. With \(L=4\), \(f_s=8\), what are the nonnegative frequency bins? **Answer:** 0, 2, and 4 hertz.
2. At four samples per window and hop two, which samples enter frame 1 using zero-based indexing? **Answer:** 2, 3, 4, 5.
3. What happens to the global DFT magnitudes after a circular four-sample shift of an eight-sample record? **Answer:** nothing; each coefficient is multiplied by \((-1)^k\).
4. Why must a magnitude-only spectrogram not be described as exactly invertible? **Answer:** it discards phase; many signals can share magnitudes, and overlap constraints do not generally determine a unique phase.
5. Does FFT zero-padding improve the inherent resolution of a four-sample window? **Answer:** no. It interpolates the already windowed frequency response; it adds no measured samples.
6. If an analysis and synthesis window leave some sample with zero total overlap weight, can a general signal be recovered there? **Answer:** no, that sample has not been observed with nonzero weight.
