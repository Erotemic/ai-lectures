# A Snowflake on a Rabbit Hair

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

On March 12, 1936, a snow crystal grew in a laboratory in northern Japan. It was attached to a thin filament. The air around it was sixteen degrees below freezing, in Celsius, and the water supplying its vapor was kept at nine degrees above freezing. The crystal formed six branches.

The filament was an unlikely piece of scientific equipment: a rabbit hair.

The experiment took place at Hokkaido Imperial University, where the physicist Ukichiro Nakaya and his colleagues had been trying to persuade water vapor to build the kinds of crystals that ordinarily form inside clouds. They could make frost readily enough. Frost spread across the threads and surfaces of their apparatus. What they wanted was a single crystal that could grow with something approaching the freedom of a snowflake suspended in air.

The rabbit hair gave a crystal a point of attachment without putting a large solid object in its way. It also provided enough support that the researchers could keep the crystal in view while it grew. They could adjust the cold air, change the supply of water vapor, and watch what happened.

A snowflake is a familiar object, but making one deliberately was a demanding experiment. The challenge was not getting water cold. It was making water construct a particular shape.

There are two questions in the story. Why does a snow crystal often begin with sixfold symmetry? And how does that small, regular beginning sometimes turn into a feathery structure of branching arms, not one of them quite like any arm you have seen before?

The first question is about the structure of ice. The second is about the behavior of a growing crystal in moving air. The distance between the two questions is where most of the interesting physics lives.

## Six corners in Prague

In 1611, Johannes Kepler published a short work called *The Six-Cornered Snowflake*. Kepler was already an astronomer of formidable accomplishment. He had worked out mathematical laws for planetary motion, but he was also willing to spend his attention on the small things that landed on his coat.

Kepler's essay takes the form of a New Year's gift. He was thinking about a present for a friend and patron, and snow suggested an ingenious solution: something modest in cost and vast in questions. Why, he asked, should snow keep returning to a shape with six corners? Why six, rather than five or seven?

One of his ideas concerned the packing of little spherical particles. If you put identical balls on a table and push them together, each ball in a tightly packed layer can have six neighbors. The resulting arrangement contains hexagons. Kepler suspected that a comparable economy of packing might account for the snowflake.

He was reaching toward a structural explanation without knowing how a water molecule is built, much less how molecules hold together in ice. His proposed mechanism was not the modern explanation. The useful part of his question survived the incorrect mechanism. There was evidently something in the material itself that favored six directions.

Ice, under the ordinary conditions in which atmospheric snow forms, has a hexagonal crystal structure. Water molecules bond through an ordered network, and that network has sixfold symmetry when viewed along a particular axis. The crystal has distinct faces. Its top and bottom are called basal faces; the faces around its sides are prism faces. If the crystal grows more readily in some directions than others, the microscopic ordering appears in its larger shape.

This does not mean the molecules are tiny snowflakes, or that the shape of the water molecule is a six-pointed star. It is the arrangement of very many molecules, held by the geometry and energy of their bonding, that matters. Individual water molecules are not six-sided tiles.

A small crystal can therefore grow as a hexagonal plate, a short solid prism, or a longer column. The symmetry tells us something about which directions are equivalent in the material. It does not tell us whether the crystal will become a flat star or a slim needle. There is still a world of air around it, and the air has opinions about where the next molecule gets attached.

Kepler's essay could ask why the six directions existed. It could not tell a forecaster which clouds would produce a snowfall of plates and which would produce columns. The answer to that would require another three centuries of instruments, cold rooms, and photographs.

## The man with the camera

In Jericho, Vermont, Wilson Bentley became fascinated by snow crystals when he was young. He used a microscope to examine what was otherwise a fleeting white blur. The trouble was that looking was easier than keeping a record. The crystal might melt. It might sublime, lose its sharp edges, or be damaged when he tried to move it. A drawing required him to choose details while the subject was disappearing.

Bentley eventually adapted a camera to his microscope. In 1885 he obtained a successful photograph of an individual snow crystal. The Smithsonian's account describes the long, cold sessions, the feather he used to handle crystals, and exposures that could take around a minute and a half. He was photographing outdoors because the specimen had to stay frozen while he worked.

Imagine what the old photographic process required. You were not looking at a glowing digital screen, deciding whether to press the shutter again. You selected a tiny object, positioned it, exposed a plate, and later discovered whether you had managed to capture its shape. There was no guarantee that the best crystal would wait for the apparatus.

Bentley went on to make more than five thousand snow-crystal photographs. In 1931, the year he died, he and the meteorologist William J. Humphreys published *Snow Crystals*, a large collection of images. You can look at Bentley's work today and still be surprised by the variety. There are broad plates with geometric markings, elaborate stars, branching shapes like ferns, and crystals whose outlines look almost architectural.

There is a complication in those images. Bentley sometimes prepared or retouched the photographic negatives so the crystals would stand out against a black background. The resulting pictures are striking, but they are not a random sample of everything that fell from the sky. He was selecting crystals worth showing and preparing them to be seen. Later photographers have likewise tended to choose the most symmetrical examples.

That matters because the familiar snowflake image is only one entry in a large catalog. Plenty of atmospheric ice crystals are lopsided, clumped, broken, or coated in frozen droplets. Many are short columns. Some are plates so plain that they look like tiny hexagonal coins. A falling snowflake can also be an aggregate of several crystals stuck together, in which case the whole object has no obligation to be six-sided.

Bentley made the complexity visible. He did not settle the question of what conditions built each shape. His camera preserved the results of experiments that the sky had performed without telling him what settings it used.

A photograph can describe an intricate branch down to its smallest visible forks. It cannot, on its own, tell you whether that branch grew in unusually moist air, or whether the air changed temperature halfway through. For that, somebody would have to arrange for a crystal to grow under known conditions.

## How water builds an arm

It helps to begin with a process that happens even when no picturesque snowflakes are involved.

In a cold cloud, water vapor can be deposited as ice. Instead of first becoming liquid and then freezing as a raindrop might, vapor molecules join a growing ice surface. Many atmospheric clouds also contain supercooled liquid droplets, and the initial ice particle may appear through freezing or other nucleation processes. Once an ice crystal is present, it can continue growing by taking up vapor. When people describe snow crystals as frozen raindrops, they are mixing up two different routes through the phases of water.

For growth to proceed, the surrounding air must supply water molecules faster than they leave the crystal. Physicists describe the excess vapor available above equilibrium as supersaturation. The exact meaning of the word depends on what the vapor is being compared with. Since water vapor can be in equilibrium with ice at a lower vapor pressure than it would need for equilibrium with liquid water at the same subfreezing temperature, a cloud can supply ice-crystal growth even in conditions that are not supersaturated with respect to liquid water.

A new ice crystal tends to have broad flat faces because molecules attach to some arrangements of its surface more readily than others. If you imagine an isolated little hexagonal plate, its six corners all have a chance to receive incoming vapor. But the vapor is not delivered equally to every microscopic point.

Here is the useful physical picture. Water molecules travel through the air by diffusion. A face of ice that consumes water vapor lowers the local vapor concentration around it. A small corner that sticks into the surrounding air reaches farther into vapor that has not yet been depleted. That corner can grow faster. As it gets farther ahead, it gains an even better position for collecting vapor. A small projection amplifies its advantage.

This is one route to branching. Under suitable conditions the tips of the six corners extend outward, and then smaller tips begin to develop along those arms. The basic process has a name in the physics of pattern formation: a growth instability. Snow has a particularly elegant way of making the instability visible.

If you stop the explanation there, you miss something. Vapor diffusion is not the only influence. Attaching a water molecule to ice is a microscopic event governed by the structure of the ice surface. Attachment can be easier on the basal faces than on the prism faces, or the other way around, depending on conditions. As ice grows, it also releases latent heat, which must be carried away. Temperature changes can affect how rapidly molecules arrive and attach.

The visible shape emerges from a competition among these processes. Diffusion can reward a projecting corner. Surface kinetics can make a crystal grow thin or thick, long or broad. The warmth released during growth changes the immediate environment. Small differences at the surface can become conspicuous after enough growth.

A useful way to avoid getting lost in this is to keep three separate questions in mind. What directions does the ice lattice permit? Where can incoming vapor reach? What does a water molecule have to do to become part of a particular ice face? All three are involved, and they need not favor the same kind of growth.

A six-sided crystal is therefore not evidence that an invisible hand carved six matching branches. It is a structure that grows under local physical conditions, with each new bit of growth changing the conditions for the next.

## Needles at five below, stars at fifteen

The cold itself has a surprising effect. Colder does not simply mean more elaborate.

Around five degrees below zero Celsius, snow crystals grown in moist conditions tend toward slender columns and needles. Around fifteen degrees below zero, highly branched plates and stellar dendrites are common. At warmer subfreezing temperatures, plates can appear again. At much lower temperatures, the diagram changes again.

This alternation is the peculiar result shown in the snow-crystal morphology diagram. On one axis is temperature. On the other is the amount of water vapor available for growth, often expressed as supersaturation. The various regions tell you which broad shapes tend to form under which conditions.

If the humidity is low, a crystal may keep an orderly, comparatively simple outline. Increase the available vapor and the protruding parts can grow more aggressively. A relatively plain plate can sprout points; the points can grow sidebranches; the crystal can become a fernlike star. The exact boundaries between these shapes are not hard walls. The diagram is a record of observed tendencies, not a machine for predicting the geometry of every falling flake.

The alternation between plates and columns has remained an active research problem long after its discovery. Kenneth Libbrecht, a physicist at Caltech, has spent years investigating how the rates at which molecules attach to ice faces change with temperature. His experimental and theoretical work shows how surface effects can be amplified by the geometry of a growing, thin plate. The central difficulty is that the ice surface is only a few molecular layers thick, while the finished crystal may be millimeters across. A small effect at the surface can produce a large difference in the visible result.

That is why a tidy explanation such as 'cold makes six points' is inadequate. Sixfold symmetry belongs to the lattice. The preferred growth habit belongs to the conditions. The spectacular branch patterns are generated by instabilities during growth. These are related facts, but they are not the same fact.

If you want a particular kind of snow crystal, it is not enough to turn the temperature dial down as far as it will go. You need to know which temperatures favor the faces and edges you want, how much vapor is available, and how to keep an individual crystal growing long enough to reveal the pattern.

This is what brought the problem into Nakaya's laboratory.

## The cold room in Hokkaido

Nakaya had begun studying natural snow crystals after moving into physics work at Hokkaido University in the early 1930s. The winter environment offered an abundant supply of specimens. He and his colleagues observed crystals, photographed them, and examined features such as size and falling behavior. A sufficiently cold place can provide natural snow. It cannot make snow arrive on a physicist's timetable, or hold its humidity constant while a crystal grows.

A cold laboratory built during the mid-1930s allowed the team to try the other approach. They made a chamber for growth experiments and built apparatus that combined cold air with a controlled source of water vapor. The designs evolved. One arrangement let vapor rise from warm water through glass cylinders while air cooled around it. The goal was to provide a growing crystal with an environment that could be adjusted instead of merely recorded.

It was more difficult than it sounds. When they suspended ordinary cotton filaments, frost would form in many places along the fibers. The crystal they wanted was supposed to grow almost in isolation. Instead, they got a little forest of competing crystals, each affecting the vapor supply of its neighbors. In his later account, Nakaya compared some failed filaments to hairy caterpillars.

Then, on March 12, 1936, they obtained a crystal that resembled a natural six-branched snow crystal. Nakaya's published account gives the air temperature as minus sixteen degrees Celsius and the water temperature as plus nine. The first success did not mean they could thereafter summon a perfect snowflake whenever they wished. Repeating the result was difficult. Their early report explicitly called the work preliminary. But the event established that such a crystal could be grown in a controlled apparatus rather than only observed after falling from a cloud.

The rabbit hair often appears in retellings as a charming bit of laboratory improvisation. It is charming. It was also a solution to a technical problem. The researchers needed a thin support on which one crystal could begin and then develop while surrounded by vapor. A much thicker support would have changed the geometry of the local environment. A crowded filament would have made neighboring crystals compete for water molecules. The hair helped isolate the phenomenon they wanted to study.

By changing temperature and humidity, Nakaya's group eventually learned how to produce many of the crystal forms they had collected outside. Their experiments gave rise to the diagram associating plates, needles, columns, and branched stars with ranges of temperature and supersaturation.

The distinction between observing and producing is important here. Bentley had photographed what clouds delivered. Nakaya could ask for a change in the environment and see whether the crystal changed with it. If lowering the temperature in a particular range tended to switch a plate into a column, that was evidence of a causal relationship. If increasing the vapor supply brought out sidebranches, the lab could repeat the test. The apparatus turned the shape of a snow crystal into an experimental result.

Nakaya later used the phrase 'a snow crystal is a letter from the sky.' It is a good description of what the diagram made possible. The outline of a crystal can carry information about the conditions in which it grew. But a letter is not a complete weather report. A crystal may have traveled through changing layers of air, and two different histories can sometimes leave similar visible structures. The laboratory gave the researchers a vocabulary for reading the traces, not perfect knowledge of the whole journey.

## Why the six arms resemble each other

There is another small puzzle. The six arms are far apart in molecular terms. No mechanism sends instructions from one arm to another saying what branches to grow next. Yet the most elaborate stellar crystals can look uncannily balanced. One arm acquires a sidebranch; five other arms develop something like it. Then the air changes, and the next set of structures appears farther out.

Part of the answer is scale. A crystal only a few millimeters across moves through a cloud as a small object. Its six arms generally experience nearly the same sequence of temperatures and vapor concentrations. If a shift in the environment makes all the arm tips branch more rapidly, it affects them at about the same stage in their growth.

Their resemblance is a record of a shared history. It does not require communication between branches, and it does not guarantee mirror-image perfection. The arms compete for vapor locally and can shade one another. Tiny differences in their positions can accumulate. Crystals collide with droplets, break, stick to neighbors, or tumble into a less favorable orientation. Perfect symmetry is the exception photographers hunt for.

This also helps with the famous claim that no two snowflakes are alike. For a large, complex snow crystal, an exact duplicate is extraordinarily improbable. The shape records many small events of growth, and any change in those events can change the branching. In addition, exact molecular identity is a stronger demand than a visual resemblance through a microscope.

But saying every snowflake is unique as if it were a proved theorem creates a problem that science did not give us. Simple hexagonal prisms can be visually indistinguishable at ordinary resolutions. Two crystals might have matching external shapes without having identical molecular compositions. A snowflake can be a clump of several crystals, making the question of sameness still less clear. The right account depends on what counts as a snowflake and what counts as identical.

The more interesting question is not whether somebody could find two objects that appear the same. It is how a material that repeatedly begins with the same crystalline symmetries can generate such a huge range of forms. Each growing tip responds to its surroundings. The shape changes those surroundings. The interactions continue until the crystal reaches the ground or ceases growing.

A computer can draw a decorative six-pointed star by repeating one branch six times around a center. That operation gives the eye the symmetry it expects. An actual snow crystal has to acquire every new molecule through the physics of a cloud. The symmetry of the crystal is a consequence of that physical process, with its imperfections included.

## The snow that is not in the picture

I want to return to the broader category we call snow. The picture in a holiday card is one possibility. Snow in the real atmosphere includes small crystals, irregular crystals, crystals coated with rime from freezing cloud droplets, and aggregates formed when crystals collide and stick. Different combinations affect how snow falls, packs, reflects light, and interacts with weather systems.

A high, cold cloud might produce slender ice forms. They can travel into warmer or moister air and acquire new structures as they fall. Some may melt partly and refreeze; others reach the ground intact. What ends up on a coat sleeve is a survivor of a complicated journey, not a standardized product of a cloud factory.

This is why cloud scientists cannot dispense with the unphotogenic crystals. The lopsided ones are part of the precipitation, and their sizes and shapes matter to how they fall and how a cloud evolves. A beautiful specimen may be an excellent illustration of one growth process and a poor illustration of the proportions of different crystals in an actual storm.

The physics also offers a caution to a listener who now knows the morphology diagram. You cannot look at one snowflake from a driveway and reliably recover a cloud's complete temperature profile. Crystal shapes carry clues, sometimes valuable ones, but growth conditions may have varied along the path. Photographs and laboratory measurements are evidence to be combined with measurements of the atmosphere, not replacements for them.

The greatest contribution of the rabbit-hair experiment was therefore more precise than proving that snowflakes are beautiful or confirming that they have six sides. It showed that at least some of the bewildering variety of natural snow crystals could be reproduced by changing a manageable set of physical conditions. What the sky made could be investigated in a laboratory.

The same insight made possible more sophisticated questions: exactly how does an ice face change its attachment behavior with temperature? What produces a thin plate instead of a broad one? What initiates a sidebranch, and what selects the spacing between branches? Researchers continue to study those details because the first successful artificial crystal did not answer everything.

And that is where I want to leave the crystal: growing on its fine support, with enough space around it for six tips to advance. The cold room is a very small imitation of a cloud. The six branches have no plan. Each is collecting water from the air. Their branching patterns will be determined in the minutes that follow.

Outside the laboratory, the snow keeps falling, whether anyone has a camera ready or not.
