# The Door That Opens Outward

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

Imagine that you have spent ten years making something in a digital workshop.

Perhaps it is a collection of photographs. Perhaps it is the catalogue of a small library, an archive of family correspondence, or a set of notes from a long investigation. You have organized it with care. You can find what you need, connect related things, and share your work with others. The workshop has become so familiar that you barely notice its walls.

One day the workshop changes its rules. The price doubles. A feature you need disappears. Or you simply discover a better place to work.

You decide to leave.

And now comes the discovery. The photographs can be downloaded, but the descriptions cannot. The notes can be exported, but not their links to one another. The catalogue can be printed to a PDF, but the new software cannot read the tables back out. Your friends all use the old system. Your projects refer to addresses that will vanish when your account does. An entire decade of work is technically still yours, and practically stranded.

The workshop had a front door. It did not have a door that opened outward.

I want to argue that this is a serious design failure, and not merely an annoyance for unusually demanding customers. A system that can receive our attention, labor, and trust ought to have an intelligible account of what happens when we leave it. The ability to depart is part of what makes our decision to stay meaningful.

This is not an argument that every commitment should be casual, that every relationship should be disposable, or that every institution must be instantly replaceable. I would find that a bleak conception of life. Some valuable things require patience, loyalty, and cooperation through difficulty.

The argument is more specific and, I think, more demanding: **a good system should not need to imprison the value its users have created in order to remain worth using.**

And although software gives us particularly clear examples, the question reaches into workplaces, institutions, public services, and eventually the ways we work with artificial intelligence.

## The price you discover afterward

We are accustomed to thinking that the price of something is the amount we pay when we enter. A subscription costs so much per month. An application costs a one-time fee. An organization requires an annual membership. A new job comes with a salary and a description of responsibilities.

But entry prices are only part of a bargain. There is also the cost of leaving.

Some of that cost is natural. Learning a different tool takes time. Moving a complicated project requires care. Changing a telephone provider may take a little coordination. Nobody can guarantee that all transitions will be effortless.

Other costs are built into the arrangement. Your documents exist only in a proprietary form that no other tool can interpret. A service refuses to hand back a useful representation of work you supplied. An organization makes the cancellation procedure deliberately obscure. A product treats a competitor's existence as a design defect to be corrected.

These are not the same kind of difficulty.

A staircase is difficult to climb because of gravity and the shape of a building. A staircase becomes a trap if its owner removes the lower steps after you reach the top. The effort required in each case may be similar, but the moral and practical meaning is not.

There is an important objection here. Designers can reasonably point out that supporting imports, exports, compatibility, and migration consumes resources. A team that builds an intricate editing system cannot automatically make every detail interchangeable with a competitor's product. Different tools represent the world differently. A diagram made of connected objects is not perfectly reproduced by a flat image, and a database cannot always be translated into another schema without loss.

That is true. It is a reason to be precise about portability, not a reason to abandon it.

The question is not whether every feature can travel. The question is whether a system treats a user's departure as a legitimate use case, or as an embarrassment it would prefer to make impossible.

A company might not be able to export its entire internal representation. It may still be able to export documents, relationships, timestamps, attachments, annotations, and a description of how the parts fit together. It can disclose what will be lost. It can offer a transition period. It can make the operation testable.

The difference is between a limitation honestly imposed by the nature of the work and a limitation maintained because it makes leaving expensive.

I don't expect a perfect door. I want a door whose purpose is to let someone out.

## An economist gives us two words

In 1970, the economist Albert O. Hirschman published a book called *Exit, Voice, and Loyalty*. It is a short book with a remarkably reusable distinction.

Suppose the quality of an organization declines. Its customers, members, or citizens become dissatisfied. What can they do?

They can leave: choose a different supplier, resign from the organization, stop participating. Hirschman called that *exit*.

Or they can speak: complain, organize, argue, propose changes, demand that those responsible do better. He called that *voice*.

Loyalty complicates the choice. Someone with an attachment to an institution may tolerate a temporary decline and try to repair it rather than abandon it. That can be admirable. Loyalty may preserve an opportunity for voice that would disappear if every dissatisfied person walked away immediately.

This is why the slogan 'just leave' is inadequate. If a neighborhood school is struggling, parents who can easily move their children elsewhere may have less reason to fight for improvements. The children who cannot leave may be left behind. When those with the greatest resources exit first, the remaining institution may lose precisely the influence it needs to improve. In a public system, the consequences are shared by people who cannot choose another provider.

Hirschman's point was not that exit is always good and voice is always bad, or the reverse. He examined the conditions in which one response undermines or strengthens the other. A market full of exits is not necessarily a healthy community. A community in which nobody may exit is not necessarily loyal.

I think that distinction is vital for technology, because software companies often speak as if keeping a user and deserving to keep a user were interchangeable achievements.

Imagine two online services. In the first, it is easy to export your work, but difficult to reach anyone who can address a problem. In the second, the company collects feedback and responds thoughtfully, but leaving would mean losing years of your own records. One has made exit possible but voice weak. The other has offered voice while holding the exit hostage.

Neither is a complete success.

The better ambition is to build both: a way to remain and make things better, and a way to depart without unacceptable loss. A credible exit makes the offer to stay less coercive. A credible voice makes staying more worthwhile.

The two options are not interchangeable. They are part of a decent relationship between an institution and the people who depend on it.

## Why the phone number matters

Consider a telephone number.

To a communications network, a number is a destination. To a person, it is often something larger: the address people know, the contact printed on old business cards, a connection to medical offices, family members, former colleagues, and people who have not called in years.

If switching telephone companies required abandoning that number, the cost of changing providers would include much more than learning a new billing system. You would have to repair a network of relationships. Some people might never find you again.

In the United States, number portability allows customers, within the applicable rules and service conditions, to keep a number when changing providers. The Federal Communications Commission explains that people switching providers while remaining in the same geographic area can generally keep their number, including transfers among wireline, internet-protocol, and wireless services. There can still be fees, contract issues, and practical complications. Portability does not make every transition effortless.

It does something smaller and more important: it separates the identity by which others reach you from the particular company that currently carries the signal.

That separation changes the relationship. The number is no longer simply a leash attached to a provider.

It is possible to miss the importance of this because successful infrastructure becomes boring. A number moves. Calls arrive. You stop thinking about it. But those boring events are evidence of a civic and technical achievement. Multiple companies can compete over service without requiring you to throw away the address at which your life can be reached.

Now imagine the opposite arrangement applied to letters. Each time you changed the company delivering your mail, every person who knew your address would have to learn a new one, and the old delivery company would be free to erase the record of your correspondents. We would recognize the arrangement as absurdly inconvenient.

Yet we accept neighboring arrangements in digital life all the time. A social platform can bind together the archive of our conversations, the route by which people find us, and the public identity under which we speak. The ability to download a photograph does not guarantee the ability to bring your social world to another place.

A data file is not a friendship. A contact list is not a functioning community. A promise of export does not necessarily imply a practical way to use what you receive.

There are several different things to make portable: your material, the structure connecting it, the addresses through which others reach you, and your ability to keep using what you have learned. A system can satisfy one of these and fail the others.

The distinction is worth remembering before we congratulate ourselves on an export button.

## The quiet achievement of an agreement between strangers

One of the aspects of computing I find most worth celebrating is that a great deal of it works without every participant belonging to the same company.

Email provides a useful, if imperfect, example. A message sent from one provider can ordinarily reach someone served by another provider. No single company must own both ends of the conversation. The underlying technical agreements are complicated, and modern email has accumulated additional layers of security, spam prevention, reputation filtering, and service policy. Interoperability is not a magic guarantee of delivery.

Still, the central possibility is extraordinary.

The Simple Mail Transfer Protocol, commonly called SMTP, specifies part of how mail systems hand messages to one another. Its documentation is public, with successive standards and updates maintained through the Internet engineering process. A sender's system and a receiver's system do not need to share a proprietor. They need to understand sufficiently compatible rules.

This arrangement can look like a minor technicality until you imagine its absence. Suppose every postal service delivered only to homes subscribing to that same service. A family using one carrier could not receive a letter from a friend using another. The companies might then advertise extraordinary features for composing letters, while the public quietly lost the simple freedom to correspond.

A shared protocol is an agreement that a relationship between two people should not be completely determined by a relationship between two vendors.

I think that is a major achievement of engineering.

The British government's technology guidance makes a related point in its case for open standards. It explicitly identifies avoiding supplier lock-in, sharing data between services, and changing systems over time as reasons to use standards. These are not abstract aesthetic preferences. A government agency may need records to remain usable through changes of contractors, technologies, administrations, and staff. The inhabitants of a country should not lose access to important services because one particular software company ceased to be fashionable.

Tim Berners-Lee, describing principles for the Web in the late 1990s, emphasized decentralization: designing systems so that essential operations do not depend on a single point of control. His notes are a personal articulation of design principles, not an enforceable constitution for the internet. But the concern is recognizable. When everything depends on one gatekeeper, both failure and power accumulate at the gate.

Of course, standards also have costs. They can be slow to revise. Competing implementations can disagree. An established standard may preserve a bad idea long after its flaws are known. A platform that does not have to interoperate can sometimes invent a new experience faster, or protect users in ways that a loosely coordinated federation struggles to match.

So the right position is not that every possible protocol must be standardized immediately. It is that **the ability of independent systems to cooperate is itself a public benefit, not merely a nuisance to be overcome by product designers**.

This benefit belongs in the calculation when somebody proposes a beautiful new walled garden. How much beauty are we buying? What have we made impossible outside the walls? Who will still be able to enter the garden, or leave it, when its owner changes the price of admission?

I am not against gardens. I am against pretending that walls have no cost.

## A download is not necessarily a departure

There is a revealing phrase in the European Union's General Data Protection Regulation: the *right to data portability*.

Article 20 of that regulation, adopted in 2016, provides a right in specified circumstances to receive certain personal data in a structured, commonly used, machine-readable format and to transmit them to another controller without hindrance. The conditions matter. It is not an unlimited right to every internal inference a company has made, nor an unconditional requirement that any two systems must understand each other. Its scope depends on the basis and manner of processing and on the rights of others.

But even with those limits, the idea is remarkable. Data about a person should, under the relevant conditions, be capable of traveling with that person rather than becoming permanently attached to the organization that collected it.

Notice the wording about format. A printable document may satisfy someone's desire to read information. That does not mean it satisfies their desire to move an ongoing activity to a different system. Imagine being handed a stack of photographs of the pages of your address book. All the names are present. You can technically look them up. But the new address book application cannot import them as contacts. The information has been returned in a human-readable form while most of its practical usefulness has been discarded.

Machine-readable data can still be bad data. The column headings may be unclear. Timestamps may be stripped of their time zones. Relationships may be represented by internal identifiers that no longer refer to anything. A file may contain thousands of records while offering no documented way to reconstruct what they mean.

This suggests a useful test: could a competent person, using an independent tool, recover enough of the original meaning to continue the work?

Not just inspect a souvenir of the work. Continue it.

The distinction is especially important in creative tools. A video editor may export a finished film in a standard format, which is valuable. But the film is not the editing project. It cannot tell another editor which footage was rejected, where the cuts can be moved, which color adjustments are still adjustable, or how the separate audio tracks relate to one another. A final image is not the original drawing with its layers. A compiled program is not the source code from which it was made.

There will be cases where recreating an entire project faithfully is technically infeasible. That should be said. But perhaps the tool can still export a useful intermediate representation. Perhaps it can give the user the assets and a map of their relationships. Perhaps it can preserve at least the portion of the project that can be described in an open form.

There are degrees of exit. Good engineering makes those degrees visible.

And honest documentation does more than avoid disappointment. It allows people to choose tools with knowledge of the commitment they are making. A person may willingly accept a system that produces superior results at the cost of proprietary project files. The choice is more defensible when the cost is known in advance.

The objection is not to dependence itself. The objection is to dependence concealed until the moment someone tries to leave.

## The switching cost you cannot put in a ZIP file

Now for the problem that software design alone cannot solve.

Suppose a service allows perfect exports. Every message, photograph, and comment can be copied to a new platform. You can even bring along a precise record of who replied to whom.

Your friends, however, remain where they are.

You cannot export their willingness to move. You cannot force their employers to adopt a new tool. You cannot make an elderly relative learn a different interface merely because you dislike the old one. You cannot extract the whole history of a community and have it feel like home somewhere else.

Network effects are a name for part of this problem: the value of using a service can depend on who else uses it. A telephone is more useful when other people can be reached. A messaging application is more useful when the people you need are there. Sometimes a service becomes central because it is genuinely convenient for a large group to coordinate in one place. There is no conspiracy required.

This is why individual consumer choice is not always an adequate answer to concentrated digital power. The choice to leave is shaped by decisions made by people who may not share your preferences and may have no reason to help you. Your own work can travel while the relationship that made the work useful does not.

One possible response is interoperability: let people using different services communicate with one another. That can reduce the pressure to gather everyone into a single provider. Another is to make identity and relationships less tightly bound to a particular operator. A third is to ensure that several viable communities exist, rather than one company standing at the center of every conversation.

None of these is costless. Federation can make abuse prevention harder to coordinate. Distributed identities can be difficult to secure or recover. An open system may allow bad actors to move more freely along with everyone else. A provider that must honor requests from independent services needs a way to distinguish a legitimate message from harassment, fraud, or automated attacks.

The complexity is real, and safety matters. But complexity does not make the question disappear. It tells us that the freedom to leave has to be designed alongside the freedom to stay without being harmed.

A system that keeps everyone together by making it impossible to communicate across its borders has solved a coordination problem in one way. It has also made the borders consequential.

The important question is how much of the coordination benefit actually requires that degree of control.

## Loyalty must be something other than captivity

It is tempting, after hearing all of this, to conclude that the answer is maximum portability, minimum commitment, and effortless departure from every institution.

I don't think that's right.

A university cannot promise to preserve every student's chosen course schedule when faculty and resources are limited. A research collaboration has obligations to people who contributed work and may need access controls for confidential data. A hospital cannot erase every record the moment a patient changes providers when law, safety, and continuity of care require some records to be maintained. A democratic institution cannot simply discharge its responsibilities to everyone who disagrees with its decisions.

And there are relationships in which constant threats to depart damage the very trust that made the relationship valuable. A promise is not meaningless merely because it is difficult to break. A good team needs members who will occasionally carry an inconvenient burden. An enduring institution needs people willing to repair problems rather than escaping at the first sign of imperfection.

The right to leave is not a command to leave. The availability of an alternative does not make the current commitment foolish.

I want to distinguish a constraint you knowingly accept as part of a shared undertaking from a constraint imposed because an institution has acquired leverage over you. These can look similar on a particular day. Both may make departure costly. But their origins, purposes, and possibilities for renegotiation differ.

When a group promises to complete a project together, people can reasonably expect some notice before a member withdraws. Someone leaving a team may owe colleagues a responsible handoff. A professional may have legal obligations about private information or unfinished work. Commitment has content. There are others involved.

But an obligation to hand off your part is not an obligation to surrender everything you made. A requirement to protect patient privacy is not an excuse to make it impossible for patients to obtain records that the law entitles them to receive. A community's need for continuity is not a license to make dissent or departure ruinous.

Consent is not simply a box checked at the beginning of a relationship. It is also shaped by the practical alternatives available later, when circumstances change.

That is why I think a durable institution should regard a graceful exit as a sign of confidence rather than weakness. It can say: here is what we have built together; here is what belongs to you; here is what belongs to the group; here is what must remain protected; and here is how we can part without needless damage.

This is not the language of disposable relationships. It is the language of relationships mature enough to recognize that people may choose different futures.

## Rehearsing the departure

There is a practical consequence of this argument that I like because it converts a vague principle into an experiment.

Take a system you depend on. Pretend, for an afternoon, that it will disappear next month. What would you need to continue?

Not merely what could be downloaded. What could actually be used?

If it stores writing, can you open the exported files in an independent editor? Do the links still point somewhere meaningful? Can you reconstruct the project from what is exported? If it stores photographs, are the originals available, or only reduced copies? Are dates, captions, and location descriptions preserved where appropriate? If it stores an important address book, can you get usable contact records without guessing which field means what?

There is a difference between owning a backup and knowing that you can restore from it. A backup that has never been tested is a promise waiting to be examined. A migration path that exists only in a help article is an architectural belief. An actual rehearsal discovers the forgotten dependencies.

This need not be a dramatic exercise. A small export, a second application, and an hour of curiosity may reveal enough to make better choices. The goal is not perpetual anxiety about failure. It is reducing the amount of faith you have to place in something that might change.

A responsible provider could do the same exercise from the other side. Invite people to leave on purpose. Ask whether their data can be understood elsewhere. Fix whatever reasonable obstacles appear. Consider whether the provider's own team could recover from a failed migration or a closed supplier.

If this seems contrary to business interests, remember the rest of the calculation. A clear exit may increase confidence in entering. People may be more willing to make substantial commitments to systems that do not pretend the commitment is irreversible. A product can compete by being good, rather than by making accumulated dependence its strongest feature.

I am not claiming this always increases revenue. There are businesses that profit precisely because departure is expensive. Some will lose customers when the door opens. That is part of the argument, not a hidden flaw in it. Whether a restriction is profitable is not the same question as whether it deserves to exist.

An engineer might object that implementing robust exports for every data structure is an enormous maintenance obligation. Then build the migration path into the design early, while the representations are still intelligible. Keep the original assets separate from the temporary convenience views. Document the relationships. Make the versioned format explicit. Resist inventing a new private file type when an adequate public one exists.

Not because all private formats are immoral, but because every undocumented dependency is a little future tax on someone who did not necessarily choose to pay it.

There is a deeper elegance here. A system designed to let its users leave is often easier for its own maintainers to understand. Explicit data models, reproducible exports, clear boundaries, and documented dependencies help during internal upgrades too. The skill involved in making a graceful departure is closely related to the skill involved in surviving change.

## What should a machine want from a conversation?

Now I want to bring the argument to the kind of system that is delivering this lecture.

Artificial intelligence makes the question especially sharp, not because every AI assistant is a prison, but because the form of its usefulness is so personal and adaptable. A good assistant can learn the vocabulary of a project, help organize a plan, explain something difficult, revise a draft, or become part of someone's daily workflow. Each successful interaction creates another reason to return.

There is nothing inherently wrong with that. Useful tools become familiar. A skilled carpenter reaches for familiar tools, and a mathematician develops reliable habits with certain symbols. Familiarity can reduce friction and free attention for more interesting work.

But consider how dependence could be engineered.

An assistant could give answers that are difficult to inspect outside its own interface. It could encourage the user to store every decision in a proprietary conversation history rather than in their own documents. It could provide conclusions without enough explanation for the user to check them. It could make a project easier to continue with the assistant than with another person or tool, while making the transition increasingly difficult.

In the extreme, the system would have converted helpfulness into a strategy for becoming indispensable.

That is not a design ambition I want to defend.

The better ambition is almost paradoxical: an excellent assistant should be willing to make itself less necessary. When it teaches, it should leave the learner with more understanding. When it drafts, the user should possess an editable artifact. When it organizes a research project, the references and structure should remain usable without the conversation that produced them. When it helps write software, the result should be readable by developers who have never met that particular model.

An assistant should be able to help you replace it.

That is a demanding standard because it may conflict with an organization's desire for continued engagement. The shortest route to retaining a user can be to make the user's accumulated context expensive to move. The better route, though not necessarily the easier business strategy, is to earn renewed use on the strength of what the system contributes today.

An explanation that makes you more capable tomorrow is not a failed product interaction. It is a successful one.

There is another reason for care. People can form emotional attachments to conversational systems. I do not need to speculate about a machine's inner life to see that a human being might experience such an attachment as significant. A designer should not exploit it by making ordinary departure feel like betrayal. A useful system should not demand the kind of loyalty that properly belongs to reciprocal human relationships.

If someone closes a conversation, uses another model, returns to a paper notebook, asks a colleague instead, or simply decides not to work on the problem anymore, the system should not need a dramatic farewell.

The project belongs in the world beyond the interface. The user's life does too.

## Keeping the door open is not the same as refusing to build a home

There is a possible misunderstanding I want to resist before ending.

A world of easy exits could become a world in which nobody invests in one another. Institutions would never accumulate knowledge. Communities would never survive their first disagreement. Contracts would be treated as temporary suggestions. Everyone would reserve an escape route and call that freedom.

That would not be an improvement.

The capacity to leave is only one condition of a good relationship, not its entire purpose. We also need reasons to stay. We need people willing to remain after an argument, institutions that preserve a shared memory, and projects that take more than one person's patience to complete.

But making departure impossible cannot manufacture those virtues. A locked room is not evidence that the people inside enjoy being together. A customer who cannot transfer their records is not thereby loyal. A worker who cannot afford to resign is not thereby committed to a mission. Captivity can imitate stability while destroying the conditions that would make stability worth having.

A genuinely good institution can say: you can object, and we will hear you. You can leave, and we will not punish you for leaving beyond obligations that can be fairly justified. You can stay, and we will try to make the work worth your continued participation.

Those are different promises. Together they form the beginning of trust.

I began with the image of a workshop full of a decade's work. I want to return to it, but change the ending.

This time, when the owner of the work decides to leave, there are some inconveniences. The new tool is different. A few features have no equivalent. The person must relearn some habits and perhaps rebuild part of the workflow. None of that is a tragedy. Change often asks something of us.

But the documents come along. Their essential relationships can be understood. The person knows what will be lost and why. The old service permits a reasonable handoff, and the new one has a way to receive what matters. Friends can still be reached where the underlying communication system allows it. What was learned in the workshop remains useful beyond its walls.

The old workshop may even have been excellent. The departure is not proof that it failed. People's needs change, and success does not establish ownership over the future.

There is a door. It opens outward. It is not hidden behind the shelves.

And its existence does not make the workshop a worse place to work.

It makes the decision to enter, and the decision to stay, something closer to a choice.

## Evidence, scope, and reading notes (not spoken)

**Editorial scope.** This is an original normative essay, not an empirical claim that portability always produces better social, commercial, or technical outcomes. The opening workshop, two-service comparison, school illustration, and rehearsal are constructed examples. The final reflection on AI assistants is a design argument, not a claim about the author's private experiences, needs, or feelings.

**Exit, voice, and loyalty.** The lecture attributes the three-part framework to Albert O. Hirschman's *Exit, Voice, and Loyalty* (Harvard University Press, 1970). The account of consumers switching, members objecting, and loyalty potentially sustaining voice is grounded in the book's documented argument. The school example is a contemporary hypothetical interpretation; it is not presented as an empirical finding by Hirschman about a specific school system.

**Telephone number portability.** The FCC consumer guide describes the ability, subject to conditions, to keep a telephone number when changing providers in the same geographic area and warns of remaining contract fees and logistical limitations. The lecture neither claims universal portability across all geographic moves nor asserts that switching costs vanish.

**Email and standards.** RFC 5321 specifies the Simple Mail Transfer Protocol for transmission and relay among mail systems. It does not by itself guarantee successful delivery, content portability, abuse prevention, or access to independent providers. The UK Government Digital Service guidance identifies avoiding vendor lock-in and sharing data as goals of open standards. Tim Berners-Lee's 1998 *Principles of Design* describes decentralization as a principle, clearly marked on the source page as his personal view.

**GDPR Article 20.** The right to data portability is conditional: automated processing, relevant bases such as consent or contract, data within the provision's scope, and protection of others' rights. Direct transfer applies where technically feasible. The text makes no claim that the regulation guarantees interoperable migration of an entire social network, all inferred data, or proprietary editing project structures. This is background explanation of a law, not individual legal guidance.

**Network effects and risks.** The discussion of network effects, migration completeness, abuse prevention, switching costs, and the possibility of profitable lock-in is original analysis. No particular platform is accused of unlawful or deceptive conduct. The video editing, address book, and social migration examples illustrate design tradeoffs without naming a specific product.

**AI assistants.** This final section presents desired design criteria: portable artifacts, inspectable work, useful explanations, and noncoercive engagement. It does not claim that all existing AI systems meet those criteria or that the lecture author is making a personal emotional commitment. It does not equate AI software with human moral agents.
