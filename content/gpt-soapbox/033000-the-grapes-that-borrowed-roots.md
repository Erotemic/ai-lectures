# The Grapes That Borrowed Roots

*How a tiny insect changed the underground half of the world's vineyards*

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

A grapevine in a modern nursery can look as if it has been assembled from spare parts. Its roots belong to one plant. Above a thickened joint near the base of its stem, a different plant will grow the leaves and grapes. The little union may be sealed with wax. In a vineyard, years later, it can resemble a scar on an otherwise ordinary trunk.

The nursery is not trying to invent a new kind of grape by crossing two plants. The upper piece and the lower piece retain their own genetic identities. The rootstock draws water and minerals from the soil. The scion above it produces the fruit. If the scion is Pinot Noir, it remains Pinot Noir, even though its roots may come from a North American grape species that would produce very different berries if allowed to grow its own shoots.

There is a reason for this arrangement. More than a century ago, vineyards across Europe began dying from the ground up. People who understood the cultivation of grapes as thoroughly as anyone alive could not keep the plants healthy. They tried flooding the ground. They poured chemicals into the soil. They argued about whether an insect found on sick vines had caused the sickness or merely arrived to feed on dying plants.

The solution eventually accepted in much of Europe did not eliminate the insect. It changed the plant's capacity to live with it.

To understand why, we need to go underground.

## Something underneath the vines

In the 1860s, growers in the southern Rhône region of France noticed an alarming decline. Vines that had produced reliable harvests lost their vigor. Leaves yellowed. Shoots grew poorly. Eventually whole patches of vineyard withered. There was no dramatic swarm moving down the rows to announce the attacker. A grower might have inspected the canopy for disease and still failed to find the cause.

France had already been through an ordeal with powdery mildew. That disease, caused by a fungus, had struck European vineyards in the middle of the nineteenth century. Growers had learned that threats could arrive from abroad along with useful plants, and they had spent money and labor recovering production. Now another affliction was spreading, and this time the visible symptoms told them little about where to look.

In 1868, the botanist Jules-Émile Planchon examined affected vines near the Rhône and reported tiny insects on their roots. Phylloxera, as the insect became known, was difficult to see without careful inspection. Its adult root-feeding forms are about a millimeter long or smaller. A person can walk through a dying vineyard without ever noticing one.

The insect uses its mouthparts to feed on plant tissue. On susceptible grapevines, feeding on fine roots produces distorted, swollen tips; feeding on older roots can create injuries that crack and decay. Fungi and other organisms may exploit the damage. As the root system deteriorates, it becomes worse at supplying water and nutrients to the rest of the vine. A plant accustomed to drawing sustenance from a large network of roots begins to behave as if it has lost the ground beneath it.

This gives phylloxera a particularly cruel advantage as a pest of perennial plants. A grapevine is an investment in future harvests. Farmers prune it, train its branches, and wait while the wood matures. A vine that dies in its twentieth year takes more than one season's harvest with it. Its replacement may need years before it produces well.

The scientific dispute began almost immediately. Were these insects actually responsible for the decline? Or were they scavengers settling onto roots already damaged by an unknown disease? It was possible to find both a dying plant and an insect on its roots. That observation alone did not establish which one came first.

Researchers had to compare healthy and sick roots, inspect plants early in their decline, trace the insect's distribution, and learn its life cycle. An insect that appears in different forms at different stages can fool observers into describing several organisms where there is only one. Work by Planchon and his French colleagues, along with the American entomologist Charles Valentine Riley, helped establish that forms seen on grape leaves in North America and forms on damaged roots in France belonged to the same insect species.

They also had to explain why its arrival was so catastrophic in Europe when grapes continued to flourish in parts of North America where the insect lived.

## A visitor with a familiar host

Phylloxera originated in North America. It lived among native grape species that had been exposed to it over long evolutionary histories. These grapes were not necessarily free of feeding damage. Some could carry the insect while continuing to grow and reproduce. Their defenses and tolerances varied from species to species and from one population to another.

The vines making the wines of Burgundy and Bordeaux were predominantly cultivars of *Vitis vinifera*, the domesticated grape species associated with western Asia and Europe. European growers had selected and propagated its varieties for generations. Those selections had been made for their fruit, for the kinds of wine they could yield, and for their performance in local growing conditions. They had not been selected under sustained pressure from this particular North American root-feeding insect.

A grapevine can be propagated from a cutting: a piece of stem placed in conditions that let it grow new roots. A vineyard planted with cuttings of a valued variety can preserve its characteristics with great fidelity. That makes cuttings extraordinarily useful for agriculture. It also allows an enormous number of plants to share a vulnerability.

Nineteenth-century trade moved living grape material across the Atlantic. Botanical collectors, nurseries, and winegrowers wanted foreign vines for experiments, gardens, and attempts to improve their crops. Phylloxera is widely understood to have crossed the ocean on infested planting material, although the exact route of its first introduction cannot be reconstructed with certainty. An imported vine could appear healthy enough to keep, with small insects concealed among its roots.

There is no contradiction in what happened next. The American vines that tolerated the insect helped transport it. Once the insect reached susceptible European vines, its effects became far more severe. A creature that had lived alongside its hosts for an immense time became a disaster for plants it had never previously encountered.

The path from a plant collector's enthusiasm to a French vineyard's dying roots was neither intended nor easily reversible. By the time researchers understood the scale of the problem, the insect was already established in soils and vineyards spread across a growing region.

Phylloxera moves only modest distances by itself compared with the ships and trains that carried its hosts. Crawling insects, winged forms under some conditions, and movement in soil can spread it locally. Human activity is far more efficient at delivering it from one vineyard to another: rooted cuttings, contaminated tools, soil, and equipment can carry what the eye cannot readily see.

The nineteenth-century grape trade had connected distant landscapes. The insect made use of those connections too.

## Flood the ground; poison the soil

The response was urgent, expensive, inventive, and sometimes desperate. Growers proposed treatments that sound strange in retrospect. Others were serious applications of the chemistry and agricultural knowledge available at the time.

Flooding could suppress phylloxera by keeping the insect's habitat underwater for extended periods. It worked best where ground was flat and water was abundant. That excluded many vineyards planted on hillsides, and even a flooded vineyard could become infested again. The intervention was laborious to repeat.

Carbon disulfide could be injected into the soil to kill the insect. The chemical is toxic and highly flammable. Its application had to reach a pest living among roots without destroying the plant those roots supported. Some growers persisted with chemical treatment for years. Others could not afford it, could not make it work reliably, or concluded that continued cultivation no longer made economic sense.

Trials of American grape varieties presented another possibility. If their roots survived phylloxera, why not replace the vulnerable European vines with American ones? There was no biological rule forbidding it. But many growers disliked the wines produced by the imported varieties, and the established European cultivars were entwined with markets, traditions, and particular growing regions. Hybrid grapes offered still other choices. Their results varied, and some proved useful in different circumstances, but they did not persuade every grower to abandon the wine varieties already known and sold.

An alternative was so simple in concept that it could be mistaken for an evasion. Keep the European fruiting vine. Replace its roots.

By the 1870s, Riley and French researchers were exchanging observations and plants across the Atlantic. Grafting, an old horticultural technique, became the center of a new agricultural project. In 1881, a phylloxera congress in Bordeaux discussed grafting European vines onto resistant American roots as a durable approach. The French High Commission on phylloxera formally endorsed it in 1888. Adoption was uneven and took far longer than a single meeting or recommendation.

Some growers resisted because they feared American roots would spoil the character of European wines. The concern was understandable if one imagined the whole plant becoming American. But the upper part remained the desired European cultivar. An American rootstock could influence how vigorously the vine grew, how it handled water, or when its fruit ripened. It did not transform a Pinot Noir scion into an American grape variety.

The difference turns on a very old skill: persuading two wounded pieces of living wood to become one functioning plant.

## A seam that carries water

To graft a vine, a grower or nursery worker joins a scion to a compatible rootstock. The cut surfaces must be aligned so the growing tissues can connect. Cells proliferate around the wound and form a bridge; new conductive tissue develops across the junction. Eventually water can travel upward and products of photosynthesis can travel downward. Above the union, the scion grows its shoots. Below it, the rootstock extends its roots into the soil.

The union does not blend the two genomes into a newly bred grape. Grafting and crossing are different operations. Crossing combines inherited material in offspring. Grafting keeps two established organisms connected physiologically. The distinction is still visible in nurseries: different rootstocks can carry the same fruiting variety, chosen for different soils or pests.

Success requires biological compatibility, careful alignment, appropriate conditions while the wound heals, and later protection from the scion growing its own roots into infested soil. The plant has to make a successful connection and then live with it for many years.

A nursery can produce thousands of grafted vines, but that does not make replacement free. An old vineyard has to be removed. Land must be prepared. Workers must plant and train the new vines. Income may fall while the plants establish themselves. The price of a rootstock is only one part of a grower's cost. For small producers, replanting could consume resources they did not have.

The landscape changed as well. In some French regions, vines that had grown in irregular arrangements were replanted in more orderly rows that could accommodate draft animals and later machinery. Different cultivars were selected for the new plantings. Some vineyard land never returned to grapes. Many families recovered their trade; others could not.

When we say French viticulture survived phylloxera, we should not imagine that every vineyard, occupation, and local variety returned unchanged. A practice survived by being rebuilt. The rebuilt practice had a different physical foundation and, in places, a different economy.

Even technically, grafting was not one universal recipe. The American vines that resisted phylloxera did not all thrive in every European soil. Some grew too vigorously. Some demanded more water. Some failed in chalky ground where European vines had previously prospered.

That last problem sent a French botanist looking for grapevines in Texas.

## Limestone in Texas

A vine can survive an insect and still die from the soil it has been asked to inhabit.

Parts of France, including vineyards near Cognac, have calcareous soils rich in limestone. Their chemistry can make iron difficult for plants to acquire. Iron is needed for processes that allow leaves to produce chlorophyll. When roots cannot obtain enough available iron, leaves may turn yellow in a condition called chlorosis. The replacement vine may be alive, pest-resistant, and unable to support an adequate crop.

Some of the American rootstocks that worked well against phylloxera were poorly suited to such conditions. Vineyard growers had exchanged one vulnerability for another. The problem lay not in the concept of grafting, but in the particular plant selected to provide the roots.

In 1887, the French botanist Pierre Viala traveled through parts of the United States to seek grape species adapted to conditions resembling those of French vineyards. In Denison, Texas, he met the horticulturist Thomas Volney Munson. Munson had spent years collecting, classifying, breeding, and cultivating native American grapevines. He knew plants not from a distant botanical label alone, but from their habits in local ground.

One of them, *Vitis berlandieri*, grew among the limestone of central Texas. Its roots tolerated the conditions that had defeated other American rootstocks, and it showed useful resistance to phylloxera. Yet it had an inconvenience for the nursery: it was difficult to propagate from ordinary dormant cuttings.

Now the solution to one problem had created two problems to solve together. A rootstock needed resistance to the insect, tolerance of calcareous soils, and enough practical ease of propagation to make vineyard replacement possible. Breeders crossed American grape species to combine these features. *Vitis berlandieri* became a parent in important rootstocks, often crossed with the more easily propagated *Vitis riparia* or *Vitis rupestris*. Its genetic contribution remains present in widely used rootstock families.

The vineyard wanted a French grape above the ground. To grow it, the nursery needed a lineage partly derived from limestone hills in Texas.

There is a temptation to tell this episode as a story of a lone French scientist discovering an American savior. The work was broader. Munson had studied the plants before Viala arrived. French and American entomologists had exchanged evidence and practical advice for years. Nurseries and growers ran trials, discarded failures, and selected combinations suitable for their ground. They worked with a collection of native species that people had often overlooked because they did not produce the European wines they admired.

The roots were valuable for a quality that would remain invisible in a bottle.

## A famous wine with borrowed roots

Imagine standing in a vineyard whose wine is advertised through the age of its estate, its soil, and a grape variety grown there for centuries. The language may suggest a plant that has remained in one place, uninterrupted, since some distant past.

If you dug below the graft union in many such vineyards, you would find roots descended from North American species.

That observation does not expose the wine as counterfeit. The grapes really are produced by the named cultivar. A scion of Chardonnay remains Chardonnay. The weather, soil, and human decisions made during cultivation are still real. Nor is the rootstock incidental. It influences how the vine uses its surroundings. Roots determine access to water and minerals, interact with soil organisms, and affect the vigor of the shoots that ultimately bear fruit. The resulting wine is produced by the entire living system, even if its varietal identity comes from the upper part.

The biology becomes easier to understand when we abandon the image of roots as passive plumbing. A rootstock is living tissue with its own responses to drought, salts, soil chemistry, insects, and disease. It can change the growth of the scion without replacing the scion's inherited identity. In the vineyard, what looks like a single plant is a negotiated physiology maintained over years.

Not every vineyard uses grafted vines. Some sandy soils discourage severe phylloxera damage. There are regions and isolated plantings where the insect has not established or where other local conditions have allowed own-rooted European vines to persist. Chile and Santorini are often mentioned among the exceptions, although local circumstances and histories differ. Their existence does not invalidate the reason grafting became widespread. It shows how an organism's consequences depend on the place in which it lives.

The nineteenth-century crisis also changed wine commerce far outside France. When French production fell, imports from Spain and Italy increased. Growers in Rioja planted vines and built wineries to supply a market suddenly short of domestic wine. Later the insect reached Rioja too. French colonial production in North Africa expanded as wine markets shifted. In Algeria, land and labor were organized within a colonial system whose effects cannot be reduced to an interesting anecdote about French wine shortages.

A pest living on roots rearranged what was planted above ground, who purchased the harvest, and where new vineyards were established. The history can be followed through the vineyards that were saved and through the places whose economies changed while France recovered.

By the twentieth century, the grafted vine had become so familiar that it was easy to imagine the problem solved for good. California offered a warning against that assumption.

## The rootstock that failed twice

In the twentieth century, winegrowers in California faced their own choices about rootstocks. One variety, known as AxR1, appeared attractive. It combined traits from a European grape, Aramon, with those of the American *Vitis rupestris*. It grew vigorously, grafted readily, and performed well in many California trials. The University of California recommended its use in certain vineyard conditions after years of observations.

The catch was in its parentage. AxR1 had some resistance to phylloxera, but it was not as reliably resistant as rootstocks bred entirely from suitable American species. Its limitations had been observed in Europe. In California, under the conditions and insect populations encountered during long trials, it nevertheless often performed well enough to convince growers and nurseries. It became widely planted during the vineyard expansion of the 1960s and 1970s.

In 1983, investigators found phylloxera feeding on AxR1 roots in Napa Valley. The damage spread. Researchers identified an insect population able to exploit AxR1, called biotype B to distinguish it from populations against which the rootstock had held up. By the end of 1995, more than twelve thousand acres in Napa and Sonoma had been removed as a direct result of the outbreak, according to research published by University of California scientists.

The scale was severe enough to force another generation of vineyard reconstruction. Old rows came out. New rootstocks went in. Some growers used the replanting to adopt different trellis systems, irrigation practices, or grape varieties. The changes eventually became part of Napa's commercial development. That retrospective benefit did not pay the bills of every producer who had to pull out vines before their expected productive lives were over.

It would be easy to condemn the people who recommended AxR1 as though they had simply ignored evidence. The historical record is more difficult. AxR1 did have advantages. It had survived trials, and the populations of phylloxera present during those trials did not cause the same damage. Researchers and growers made a judgment under uncertainty, with costs attached to each alternative. They underestimated a vulnerability with consequences that became visible decades later.

This is what makes the later failure worth placing beside the nineteenth-century rescue. Choosing a resistant rootstock is not like installing a permanent shield between an insect and its food. The insect populations can vary. The rootstock has limits. The soil and associated microorganisms influence the outcome. An arrangement that works in one vineyard or decade may fail under different conditions.

The modern recommendation is correspondingly more specific: use well-characterized rootstocks with strong resistance and choose among them for the site. The insect has not been banished from the world. Growers are cultivating plants that can survive alongside it.

## At the nursery bench

Return to a nursery worker assembling vines for a new planting. A fruiting cultivar has been selected because someone wants its grapes. A rootstock has been selected because of the soil it will occupy and the living threats it is likely to meet.

The worker makes a cut. Two pieces of vine are brought into alignment. If the graft heals, their tissues will form a connection strong enough to carry water upward, sugars downward, and the seasonal demands of a working plant through many harvests.

The resulting vine can stand in a famous European vineyard and bear fruit associated with that place. Its roots may descend from species that grew beside rivers or on limestone hills thousands of kilometers away. They reached Europe as part of a rescue effort made necessary, in part, by an earlier movement of plants across the same ocean.

The wax at the graft union will wear away. The scar may become thick and hard to distinguish from the rest of the trunk. The rootstock will remain there, below the soil, doing the work for which it was chosen.
