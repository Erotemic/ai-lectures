# Lead Was Good at Its Job

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

Suppose you need to make a pipe, and you have neither stainless steel nor plastic. Your workshop can produce iron, copper, pottery, and a heavy gray metal that is soft enough to scratch with a fingernail. You can heat that last material in a fairly ordinary furnace, pour it into a mold, bend it, hammer it into sheets, and shape it around awkward corners. It resists some of the forms of corrosion that would quickly ruin an iron pipe.

You might choose lead.

That decision is easier to understand than its consequences. Lead is dense, workable, relatively easy to melt, and useful in combinations that are harder than the pure metal. It was available to societies that had no concept of an electron, a nervous-system receptor, or a part per billion. If you know lead mainly as a dangerous contaminant, there is a temptation to imagine its earlier users as foolish. That would miss half the history. This metal did many of the jobs people gave it extraordinarily well.

The other half of the history is what happened after the job was finished.

An ancient plumber installed a pipe. A painter made a brilliant white pigment. A battery manufacturer produced a rechargeable source of electrical power. A motor-fuel company found an additive that made an engine run more smoothly. In every case, the material continued to exist after the transaction, in the object, the workplace, the dust, the water, the air, or somebody's body.

I want to follow it through several of those places. It is one of the clearest cases I know of a material's useful properties becoming a reason for spreading it into environments where we later had to struggle to remove it.

## A troublesome gift from the ground

Lead has atomic number eighty-two. Its chemical symbol is P b, from the Latin plumbum, which also gave English its words plumbing and plumber. It melts at about three hundred and twenty-seven degrees Celsius, substantially lower than copper or iron. Its density is about eleven times that of water. A small lump weighs surprisingly much when lifted.

The metal occurs in ores, particularly galena, a lead sulfide mineral. Extracting it requires heat and chemical reactions that separate the metal from sulfur and other constituents. There is nothing magic about this process, but it took practical mastery to work it at scale. Lead has often been produced alongside silver. Where silver mining was profitable, lead could be plentiful as a consequence of pursuing another metal.

Its physical behavior is distinctive. Softness makes lead a poor material for a structural beam, but a good material for some seals and shapes that must be worked without cracking. Weight makes it inconvenient for moving machinery and useful for counterweights or ballast. Its capacity to absorb certain kinds of radiation makes it valuable in carefully designed shielding. Lead-acid batteries turn a different set of chemical properties into the ability to store electrical energy and deliver substantial current.

One chemical element, several jobs, and no universal answer about whether it is suitable for any given application. Suitability depends on what else the application permits the material to do.

That last sentence needs some detail, because lead is not equally dangerous in all forms and situations. A solid piece of lead metal in a controlled setting poses a different exposure problem from inhaled particles, soluble lead compounds, or dust that a child repeatedly transfers from a floor to their mouth. Lead's toxicity does not erase those distinctions. It makes the distinctions necessary.

Human beings have spent thousands of years discovering how to exploit the first side of this equation. Understanding the second side took much longer, and the evidence was often available before anyone had a good system for acting on it.

## The Romans did not poison themselves into extinction

Roman plumbing is almost impossible to avoid in a history of lead, partly because the connection is visible in the language. Roman builders used lead pipes and sheet lead, and archaeologists still encounter the evidence in surviving infrastructure. Some Roman writers recognized dangers from lead work. Vitruvius, in discussing water supply, favored earthenware pipes over lead ones partly on health grounds.

There is a popular story in which these pipes poisoned the population, weakened the empire, and helped cause Rome to fall. A story with a single toxic villain is easy to remember. Historical evidence is less accommodating.

Water chemistry matters. Flowing water may spend less time in contact with a pipe than water standing overnight. In some systems, minerals precipitate inside pipes and form a scale that limits direct contact between the water and the metal. Not every Roman lead pipe carried drinking water. There were multiple paths for exposure, including mining, metalworking, pottery, paints, and possibly some food preparation. These paths were not evenly distributed through the population.

A 2026 review of Roman lead exposure brings together written accounts, artifacts, and evidence from human bones and teeth. It questions familiar exaggerations about lead-adulterated wine and plumbing as straightforward explanations for widespread poisoning. It does not conclude that Roman lead exposure was harmless. Archaeological studies can identify contamination and sometimes reconstruct its likely sources. They cannot turn a variable distribution of exposures into a tidy theory of imperial collapse.

That distinction matters outside ancient history. When a health hazard is real, we can become careless about evidence in the opposite direction: if it causes harm, perhaps it caused every harm that followed. It didn't. The fall of an empire is not a laboratory experiment with one variable.

Roman pipes also illustrate a subtle engineering trap. A pipe can be intact, inexpensive to install, and apparently reliable while transferring a contaminant in amounts that people cannot see. The user evaluates the pipe as a pipe. The body experiences it as a chemical source. These are different tests of success, and one may pass for centuries without anyone running the other properly.

## Where lead goes in a body

The most important modern medical fact is straightforward. There is no known blood lead concentration at which harm can confidently be ruled out. The World Health Organization makes that statement explicitly. It does not mean that every trace produces an observable injury in every individual. It means that the available evidence does not justify drawing a line below which exposure can be declared biologically safe.

We are accustomed to poisons announcing themselves. A person swallows something, feels sick, and can connect a symptom to an event. Lead frequently works on a less theatrical schedule. It can interfere with the production of heme, the iron-containing component required for hemoglobin. It can disrupt nervous-system functions. Its effects include harm to the cardiovascular system and kidneys. Developing brains are especially vulnerable.

Lead can enter through inhaled dust and fumes or through contaminated food, water, soil, and household dust. Young children are exposed partly because being a young child involves crawling, touching, and putting things in the mouth. They also absorb more of an ingested dose than adults do under many circumstances. The behavior is normal. The environment may not be.

Lead can be stored in bones and teeth. A blood test offers a useful indication of exposure, but blood is not the entire history of the element inside a person. Stored lead can be released from bone under certain physiological conditions, including during pregnancy. Exposure can therefore continue to matter after the immediate source has been reduced.

A clinical reference value is not a magical boundary between healthy and poisoned. In the United States, the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention has used a blood lead reference value of three point five micrograms per deciliter for young children since 2021. That reference value identifies children with higher blood lead levels compared with most of their peers. It is a tool for public-health action, not a declaration that three point four is harmless.

The distinction is worth dwelling on. Suppose a city improves its drinking-water supply enough to lower a contaminant below a regulatory action level. That may be an important accomplishment. But an action level tells an agency when particular interventions are required. It does not establish the complete biological meaning of every possible exposure. Confusing these two purposes can encourage people to treat compliance as the end of inquiry.

This is part of why lead has been such a difficult material for institutions to handle. A builder can test whether a pipe leaks. An employer can notice whether a worker has an acute illness. A regulator can check whether a measured concentration crosses a threshold. The full consequences of dispersed low-level exposure demand population studies, careful measurement, and time.

## A better engine, for a price

At the beginning of the twentieth century, automobile engineers faced a practical problem called engine knock. In a gasoline engine, fuel and air should burn in a controlled way after ignition. Under some conditions, part of the unburned mixture ignites in an uncontrolled fashion, creating abrupt pressure waves and the characteristic knock. Increasing the compression ratio can improve an engine's efficiency and power, but it can also make knock harder to avoid if the fuel isn't suitable.

Engineers could address this through engine design, changes in fuel composition, and additives. In 1921, researchers at General Motors found that very small amounts of tetraethyl lead were remarkably effective at suppressing knock. Charles Kettering, Thomas Midgley Junior, and Thomas Boyd were among the researchers involved. The additive reached the market in 1923 under the name Ethyl.

There is a reason for the marketing excitement. When a fraction of a percent of an additive changes the behavior of an entire tank of fuel, it is a powerful technology. Engines can be designed around the new fuel's properties. A business can sell an improvement without requiring every motorist to purchase a completely new vehicle. The chemical solution was compact and commercially attractive.

The dangers of concentrated tetraethyl lead were not hidden for long. Workers manufacturing or handling it suffered serious poisoning, and employees died in industrial incidents during the 1920s. Midgley himself became ill after exposure. News reports described the extreme neurological symptoms of workers who had been poisoned. There were investigations, public controversies, and warnings from occupational-health specialists, including Alice Hamilton.

The policy question grew beyond the factory: even if industrial workers could be better protected, what would happen when millions of engines burned fuel containing a lead compound? The lead would not remain in the engine. Combustion would transform and expel lead-containing material, dispersing it in exhaust.

The United States Surgeon General convened an investigation in the mid-1920s. The resulting deliberations were constrained by the short period available for testing, and officials allowed leaded gasoline to continue under conditions and voluntary limits. That sequence has sometimes been retold as though a safety investigation conclusively established safety. It did not. The problem of long-term, widespread exposure was much larger than the available study.

I find the engineering decision fascinating because the performance improvement was real. The additive solved a specified problem. The catastrophe was in what else the solution did, and in how the people selling and regulating it treated a missing long-term answer as a tolerable uncertainty.

There were other ways to raise fuel octane, including higher concentrations of certain fuel components and alcohol blends. Each alternative had cost, supply, infrastructure, and technical constraints. So the historically serious question is not whether a single perfect substitute was sitting on the next shelf, waiting to be chosen. It is why the social cost of putting lead into the air was allowed to remain outside the calculation for so long.

## The lead that was getting in the measurements

Now we need to spend some time with a chemist who was trying to learn the age of the Earth.

Clair Patterson worked on radiometric dating using lead isotopes. Different atoms of lead can have the same number of protons and different numbers of neutrons. Some isotopes are produced over geological time as uranium decays. If a mineral has remained sufficiently closed to the movement of relevant elements, the ratios among isotopes can preserve information about how much time has passed.

The details are beautiful. Uranium-238 eventually produces lead-206. Uranium-235 eventually produces lead-207. Lead-204 provides a useful reference because it is not produced by those uranium decay chains. Meteorites preserve records from the early solar system. By comparing isotopic compositions in different meteorite materials, Patterson could constrain an age for the formation of the Earth and its early building blocks.

His 1956 paper reported about four point five five billion years, with an uncertainty of about seventy million years under its model and measurements. That is astonishing precision for something no human being could ever have witnessed. Other methods and evidence have refined our understanding, but the result remains close to the accepted age of Earth today.

Doing such measurements also creates an infuriating practical problem: there is lead everywhere you don't want it. If you are trying to determine tiny amounts of ancient lead in a sample, lead introduced by laboratory vessels, dust, reagents, or the surrounding environment can swamp the quantity you intended to measure. A laboratory that is clean enough for routine chemistry may be nowhere near clean enough for this purpose.

Patterson and his collaborators developed methods to control contamination and make measurements of very small lead concentrations. Once you become able to distinguish a trace of lead belonging to a sample from lead that arrived during handling, a related question becomes unavoidable. How much of the lead in modern air, seawater, dust, or living tissue belongs there naturally? And how much did human activity put there?

His 1965 paper, *Contaminated and Natural Lead Environments of Man*, addressed that question. It argued that industrial contamination had profoundly changed concentrations in the environment and challenged the assumption that ordinary human exposure sat comfortably near a natural background. Measurements of ancient and modern materials provided ways to reconstruct the difference. Polar ice and other archives made it possible to look backward at the deposition of lead over long periods.

The story is sometimes presented as a lone scientist defeating the entire lead industry through sheer stubbornness. That makes a satisfying drama, but it shrinks the actual history. Patterson was important. So were industrial workers who reported symptoms, physicians who investigated occupational poisoning, toxicologists, epidemiologists, public-health officials, and campaigners who insisted that lead exposure mattered outside a poisoned factory. His contribution was particularly powerful because a geochemical measurement supplied an independent line of evidence about how thoroughly industrial society had changed its surroundings.

The question 'What is the normal amount of lead in a person?' turns out to be treacherous if everyone in the population being sampled has already been exposed. The average of a contaminated population is a measurement of contamination, not a measurement of nature.

That is an important discovery about baselines. If you only measure a world after changing it, the altered condition can become your idea of normal. Patterson's work helped make the earlier world measurable again.

## What happened when the fuel changed

Evidence did not produce an immediate global ban. Automobile fleets, refining operations, engine designs, and fuel distribution systems had grown around leaded gasoline. The technology remained commercially useful, and the politics were contested. Reductions in leaded road fuel arrived through a mixture of air-quality regulation, engine and fuel changes, public-health evidence, and new emissions-control equipment.

Catalytic converters added a technical reason to use unleaded gasoline: lead could damage the catalytic materials used to reduce other exhaust pollutants. American drivers began seeing more unleaded fuel in the 1970s. New passenger vehicles were built to work with it. Rules tightened, and leaded gasoline for on-road vehicles was completely phased out in the United States by the beginning of 1996.

We have unusually direct evidence of the change in exposure. The United States' National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey measures lead in people's blood. Among children aged one through five, a later analysis reported that the geometric mean blood lead concentration fell from fifteen point two micrograms per deciliter in 1976 through 1980 to zero point eight three in 2011 through 2016. That is a decrease of roughly ninety-five percent.

These measurements do not permit us to assign every bit of the decline to one regulation or one technology. Lead paint, plumbing, industrial emissions, food-can solder, and other sources were also being addressed. But the timing, the scale, and the extensive research on gasoline emissions leave little doubt that eliminating lead from road fuel was a major component of the improvement.

A statistic like that can be treated as a public-health success story, and it is one. It is also worth pausing over the original level. Fifteen micrograms per deciliter was not a rare emergency measurement in the late 1970s. It was approximately the geometric mean for an entire sampled age group. A child with that level today would require a serious public-health response. The distribution itself had shifted.

The global process took longer. In July 2021, Algeria stopped selling leaded petrol for ordinary road use, and the United Nations Environment Programme announced that leaded petrol had been eliminated worldwide. Its account describes a campaign involving governments, fuel producers, civil society, and international institutions over nearly two decades.

There is a qualification that matters. Ending leaded road gasoline did not end every use of leaded fuel. In the United States, some piston-engine aircraft continue to use aviation gasoline containing lead. The Federal Aviation Administration has been working with industry on a transition intended to eliminate this use by 2030. High-performance aircraft engines and fuel-certification requirements make the change difficult. The difficulty is real, and so is the public-health problem near airports that use leaded fuel. Neither fact cancels the other.

We have learned how to run enormous numbers of engines without intentionally putting lead into their fuel. We have not yet completed every remaining transition.

## A pipe can wait for decades to become a problem

Leaded gasoline distributed contamination through exhaust. Water pipes pose a different problem. They contain material that can remain in place for a long time, and how much lead enters the water depends on water chemistry, plumbing materials, conditions inside the pipe, and treatment practices.

Flint, Michigan, provides a painful example. In 2014 the city changed its water source. The new treatment arrangement failed to provide adequate corrosion control. Water with different chemistry passed through existing infrastructure, including lead-containing pipes and plumbing components, allowing lead to enter drinking water. Residents raised concerns, independent researchers performed measurements, and the public response became a national scandal. By October 2015 Flint had switched back to its previous supply arrangement.

The lead was not simply a natural constituent of the Flint River at the levels that later appeared in household taps. Much of the problem arose from lead already present in the built system. The change in treatment caused that old material to become a new exposure pathway.

This is a particularly cruel feature of long-lived infrastructure. The person who makes a decision today may have had no role in installing a pipe generations ago. The pipe still has the capacity to make that decision harmful. An engineering system carries physical obligations forward whether or not its operators remember the choices that created them.

Corrosion control can greatly reduce lead release. Testing can locate problems. Filters can provide important protection when used correctly. But removing a lead service line eliminates that particular source in a more fundamental way than merely trying to manage the interaction between water and pipe.

In 2024, the United States Environmental Protection Agency finalized stronger Lead and Copper Rule requirements, including a general requirement to replace lead service lines within a ten-year compliance period, with limited exceptions and implementation complexities. The regulations have faced legal challenge, and their implementation is a policy process rather than a plumbing repair completed by publishing a rule.

Think of the actual work: finding records for buried pipes, determining ownership across public and private property, informing residents, financing construction, coordinating street excavation, managing disruptions, and verifying that the replacement did what it was supposed to do. The invisible metal in the water becomes a question of maps, public administration, laboratory methods, and the labor of people digging up roads.

There is no single clever chemical additive that makes all these obligations disappear.

## The battery in the truck

After gasoline and paint restrictions, it might be tempting to speak as though modern society is in the process of abandoning lead altogether. It isn't. Lead-acid batteries remain a large and useful industry. The chemistry is old, rechargeable, relatively inexpensive, and able to supply the large currents needed for applications such as starting combustion engines. Batteries also provide backup power for networks and other equipment.

The United States Geological Survey estimated that lead-acid batteries accounted for sixty-seven percent of apparent U.S. lead consumption in 2025. Globally the proportion is larger in some estimates. This is not a minor remnant of a metal the world has forgotten how to use.

Lead-acid batteries are also unusually recyclable. The U.S. Environmental Protection Agency estimated that ninety-nine percent of lead-acid batteries in the relevant municipal solid-waste category were recycled in 2018, counting recovered materials as defined by its method. Recovery makes sense: the battery contains substantial amounts of valuable lead, and returning that metal to production can reduce demand for newly mined material.

Now consider the word recycled. It describes a movement of material. It does not, by itself, tell you whether that movement is safe.

In a properly controlled facility, people and machines can disassemble batteries, manage the acid, capture dust and fumes, recover lead, and monitor emissions. Doing all of that costs money, requires equipment, and depends on enforcement. In places where people recycle batteries informally, dismantling and smelting may occur in homes, yards, or small workshops without effective exposure controls. Lead can escape as dust and fumes. Workers may carry contamination home on clothing. The business can provide desperately needed income while harming the families who depend on it.

The World Health Organization identifies informal used lead-acid battery recycling as a significant source of lead exposure in many low- and middle-income countries. The issue is not that recycling has become a bad idea. The issue is that a process which works on a material balance sheet can fail disastrously on the human balance sheet when emissions are not contained.

The comparison with gasoline is revealing. Gasoline was designed to burn in engines and release exhaust into the surroundings. The lead-containing additive went along for the ride. A lead-acid battery, by contrast, can perform its useful work while keeping most of its lead physically contained. The dangerous transitions are concentrated in manufacture, damage, handling, and recovery. That gives us better opportunities to control exposure, provided we actually use them.

I don't know whether lead-acid batteries will remain economically competitive in all their present uses. Different battery chemistries already serve many applications, and technological change will alter the mix. For now, the scale of lead-acid battery use is too large to treat as an historical footnote.

There is a lesson in the difference between a technology that makes contamination an ordinary by-product of use and a technology whose hazards are concentrated at identifiable points. The former may demand replacement. The latter may be managed through containment, safer design, collection systems, and occupational protection. The right answer can vary with the application and the alternatives available.

And if a regulation closes the loop on paper but leaves some of the loop running through a backyard furnace, the diagram is incomplete.

## The material after the product

I began by asking why a builder might choose lead. The answer was reasonable within the limits of the builder's knowledge. Lead was available, tractable, and effective. The mistakes were distributed across centuries of use, and the twentieth century made some of them vastly larger by inventing a way to scatter lead from millions of engines.

A history like this invites an easy accusation against the past: people knew lead was poisonous, and they kept using it. That accusation contains truth. People did warn about lead, and some companies and officials discounted serious evidence. It also compresses very different situations into a single verdict. An ancient pipe maker and a twentieth-century manufacturer selling an airborne lead additive did not have the same information, the same alternatives, or the same scale of potential harm.

Judgment should be particular. So should responsibility.

I keep returning to the clean laboratory where Patterson tried to measure lead in meteorites. A speck of modern contamination could ruin a measurement of material billions of years old. A chemical element people had learned to spread through workshops, cities, and skies was interfering with our ability to read the history of the planet. To do the science, someone first had to learn how little of it belonged in the sample.

Today a used car battery may travel to a recycling plant, its heavy plates still carrying the element that engineers learned to exploit long ago. Someone has to collect it. Someone has to process it. Someone has to ensure the fumes are captured and the dust does not settle in the neighboring houses. Those actions are less spectacular than inventing a high-compression engine or calculating the age of the Earth. They are what make the chemistry a usable technology rather than a recurring public-health disaster.

A worker closes the recovered material inside the next controlled process. Outside, the air does not need to contain a trace of the day's production. That is a result worth measuring.

## Technical note (not spoken)

Lead-204 is conventionally treated as a non-radiogenic isotope for uranium–lead geochronology. Decay chains of uranium-238 and uranium-235 terminate in lead-206 and lead-207 respectively. In a two-stage or open geological system, simple ratios can be misleading; Patterson's meteorite isochron argument makes explicit assumptions about initial composition and isotopic evolution. Clinical blood lead units in the essay are micrograms per deciliter. The CDC blood lead reference value is not a toxicity threshold, and WHO's 'no known safe level' is not a claim that the consequences of every individual exposure can be observed directly.
