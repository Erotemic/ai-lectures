# The Scoreboard Cannot Choose the Game

Imagine that you have been asked to run a public library.

You inherit a building full of books, a small staff, a limited budget, and a community whose members want very different things. Some want novels. Some want a quiet desk. Some need help navigating a government form. Some need a place to sit during the afternoon. A child comes in because it is the one building in the neighborhood where nobody is going to insist that she buy something.

You cannot measure all of that with a single number. But the city needs to know whether its money is being put to good use, and it needs something that can be compared across years. So it asks you to report how many books have been borrowed.

A reasonable request. Circulation is real. It is countable. An increase probably tells you something valuable. Perhaps the library is reaching more readers. Perhaps it has acquired books that people actually want. Perhaps a stubborn barrier to access has been removed.

Now imagine that circulation becomes the basis for your budget, your reputation, your staff evaluations, and whether you keep your job.

It is still the same number. Nothing about the arithmetic has changed. But everything about its meaning has changed. You have been given a new task: make the number go up.

What follows is not necessarily fraud, and it is not necessarily cynicism. You might move popular series to the front of the building. You might make renewals easier. You might encourage light, quickly finished books over difficult works that take weeks to read. You might cut the patient assistance that helps one person solve a formidable problem in forty minutes, because that service scarcely moves the circulation figure at all.

Many of these changes could be good. That is part of the difficulty. The numbers might improve while the library becomes more useful. They might also improve while it becomes less useful. The circulation figure cannot, by itself, tell you which happened.

I want to argue for a simple distinction: a number can tell you how the game is going, but it cannot tell you what game is worth playing.

And we have become exceptionally good at forgetting that distinction.

## The invention of a target

The economist Charles Goodhart was concerned with a particular problem in monetary policy. A statistical relationship that appeared useful while officials observed it could change when those officials tried to control the economy by targeting it. People responded to the policy; financial practices adapted; what had looked like a stable indicator could lose its former relation to the thing policymakers wanted to influence.

Goodhart developed this point in the mid-1970s. There is a familiar modern slogan about measures becoming targets. That slogan is a later, broader formulation associated with the anthropologist Marilyn Strathern, drawing on earlier discussion by Keith Hoskin. It is convenient, but it is worth keeping the history straight: the original issue was not that numbers are bad. It was that our actions can change the relationship between a measurement and the world it measures.

Donald Campbell, writing about social programs in the same period, approached a neighboring problem. When officials use quantitative indicators to make consequential decisions, the indicators become vulnerable to manipulation, and the processes being monitored may be distorted by the pressure. Campbell was not opposing evaluation. Quite the contrary: he cared deeply about finding out whether reforms actually worked. He was warning that the means of finding out can become part of the intervention.

Here is the difference I think matters most. Measuring something is an attempt to learn about it. Rewarding people for a measurement is an attempt to control their behavior. These activities can reinforce each other. But they are not the same activity, and it is a mistake to assume that evidence remains equally informative after it has become a command.

A thermometer measures temperature. Suppose I want to warm a room. I put a thermometer on the wall and turn up the heat until it reads twenty-one degrees Celsius. So far, so good. The measurement is closely tied to the condition I care about.

Now suppose that I am rewarded for the thermometer reading twenty-one, regardless of how the room feels. I discover that I can put a small lamp next to the sensor. The thermometer shows the desired temperature. The room remains cold.

This is not a deep paradox. It is a trivial example. That is why I like it. We are often tempted to treat a target as a moral demand or an organizational truth. In the lamp example, the problem becomes almost embarrassing. Nobody seriously believes that warming a sensor and warming a room are the same achievement. Yet in more complicated settings we repeatedly make the equivalent mistake, because the sensor is a spreadsheet and the room is a human institution.

## Three ways a score can lie without being miscalculated

It helps to separate several mechanisms that can produce the same misleading result.

The first is manipulation. If people know what number will reward them, they can choose actions that raise it without producing the intended benefit. That can involve deception, but it need not. The library can sincerely improve circulation while neglecting other forms of public service. A university can sincerely raise its graduation rate by helping students succeed, by changing the students it admits, by changing requirements, or through some combination. These possibilities are not morally equivalent. The metric does not tell them apart.

The second mechanism does not require anybody to respond strategically at all. It is a statistical problem with an important psychological consequence: selecting the best reported score also tends to select favorable measurement error.

Imagine ten proposed teaching methods. For the sake of a clean example, suppose they are actually equally effective. You test each one with a small sample of students, and because samples are noisy, their reported scores are slightly different. One method comes out on top. You choose it, congratulate yourself, and announce that the evidence has identified the winner.

But you have mostly identified the luckiest measurement.

If you now test the winning method against the others with fresh students, the apparently large advantage will tend to shrink. This is sometimes called the winner's curse, and it is closely related to the problem of overfitting. No one cheated. No one even optimized a classroom practice in the wrong direction. You simply asked noisy evidence to support more certainty than it could bear.

The more candidate approaches you try, the more likely you are to find an impressive-looking result produced partly by noise. The statistical error does not need to be large in any individual experiment. Selection does the amplification for you.

The third mechanism is subtler. The measurement can be accurate, yet the goal it represents can be incomplete.

Suppose the library's circulation count is flawless. Every checkout is recorded correctly. There is no fraud and no random error. You still cannot infer that circulation captures everything important about the library. Someone studying an unfamiliar language might spend months with one book. A visitor might learn to file an appeal, reunite with a family member through a public computer, or simply find shelter from the weather. Some of these are among the most valuable things a library can provide. They may be almost invisible to circulation data.

An accurate measurement is not necessarily a complete description. And even a complete description of the present would not, by itself, tell us what ought to matter.

Those are different errors. Cheating a metric, overfitting a noisy metric, and optimizing an incomplete metric require different remedies. It is a mistake to flatten them into one cynical declaration that all numbers are lies.

## When a target becomes the institution

Sometimes the consequences are not subtle at all.

In September 2016, the United States Consumer Financial Protection Bureau announced an enforcement action against Wells Fargo. According to the regulator, employees had secretly opened deposit and credit card accounts without customers' authorization. The agency linked the practices to sales targets and compensation incentives. The bank's own analysis, cited at the time, identified more than two million accounts that may not have been authorized. The regulator imposed a one-hundred-million-dollar civil penalty, alongside other actions.

This is a real episode, not a fable about a hypothetical company. The point is not that everyone who works under a sales target is dishonest. The point is that an organization's chosen measurement can become a competing definition of success. The bank wanted more business. Accounts opened were a visible proxy for that. A system of targets and rewards placed pressure on that proxy. Some employees produced accounts without obtaining the customer relationships that would have justified them.

The number of accounts went up. The underlying promise to customers was broken.

It would be too easy to use this as a story about a few people behaving badly. Bad conduct matters and deserves its own accountability. But an institution also has to ask why certain forms of bad conduct became attractive and repeatable within its operating system. A design that rewards a predictable distortion should not be surprised when the distortion appears.

There is a related danger even without outright abuse. Picture a research laboratory whose managers celebrate published paper counts. Publishing is not bad. It is a useful way to distribute knowledge and subject claims to criticism. But if paper count becomes the main currency of prestige, every decision begins to bend toward what can become a paper. A careful replication may look less rewarding than a new result. A long, difficult piece of software maintenance may look less productive than slicing one study into several outputs. A graduate student may rationally avoid a worthwhile question whose answer will take three years.

I am not claiming that every laboratory behaves this way, or that publication metrics are the sole reason for any particular scientific failure. This is a structural possibility. The danger does not require an evil administrator. It can arise when individually reasonable choices are made within a system that pays for the wrong evidence.

And once the evidence becomes the currency, people begin to forget that it was ever a proxy. They stop asking whether the papers were useful, and start asking whether there were enough papers. They stop asking whether the service helped, and start asking whether the response time was met. They stop asking whether students understood, and start asking whether scores increased.

Notice the grammar. An instrument for answering a question has been promoted into the question itself.

## A measurement is a theory in miniature

I think there is a deeper reason this happens.

We like to imagine measurement as an act of removing subjectivity. Put the facts on the table. Replace argument with evidence. Count the things that count.

But before the counting begins, somebody has already made a theoretical decision. Somebody decided what the units are, what belongs in the category, what time interval matters, and what outcome the count is supposed to represent.

If you measure a school's quality by test scores, you are making a claim about the relationship between performance on those tests and the kind of education you want. That relationship may be strong for some purposes and weak for others. If you measure software work by the number of completed tickets, you have made a claim about how tickets relate to value delivered. If you measure a medical service by average time spent with patients, you have made a claim about how the relevant kinds of care should be recognized.

None of those claims is absurd. None of them is automatic.

A measurement is a theory in miniature, even when it is printed as a bare integer. It says: this feature of the world is relevant; these differences matter; these other differences can be ignored for this decision. The fact that the resulting number is objective does not make the choice of what to count objective in the same sense.

This gives us two separate arguments we must not confuse. One is about whether a measurement is accurate. Did we count correctly? Is the instrument calibrated? Were the samples representative? The other is about whether the measurement answers a worthwhile question. Did we count the right thing? Does improvement in that quantity mean what we think it means? Who benefits if it goes up? Who bears the cost if it goes up the wrong way?

We can solve the first argument and still fail the second.

Sometimes I think modern organizations are full of elaborate solutions to the first argument and barely begun conversations about the second.

## The power of counting

Now I want to resist my own case for a moment, because there is a very bad conclusion available here: stop measuring things. Trust human judgment. Let professionals decide. Replace numerical accountability with discretion.

I reject that conclusion.

Measurement has corrected a vast amount of wishful thinking. A hospital administrator may be deeply convinced that patients receive prompt attention; a timestamped record can show that some wait far longer than assumed. A company may believe its software is reliable; incident reports and latency distributions may show otherwise. A school may believe every student is being reached; assessments can reveal that some students have been left behind. A researcher may have a compelling theory; controlled measurements can defeat it.

Without measurement, the person with the most confidence or the highest status often gets to decide what is true. Data can give someone else a way to object. It can expose injustice that otherwise goes unnoticed. It can show that an improvement people hoped for has not happened. It can also document quiet success that nobody thought to celebrate.

Consider the difference between telling a maintenance team that a machine seems fine and asking how often it has failed, how long it was unavailable, and whether the failures are becoming more frequent. The second conversation can be far more respectful of the team's intelligence, not less. It invites people to correct mistaken impressions and to discover patterns nobody could see from one shift.

The point is not to retreat from numbers. The point is to use them as evidence that can challenge us, not as substitutes for the judgments that gave them meaning.

A good indicator is an achievement. A measurement that reliably tracks a consequential property can save labor, reduce confusion, and make a complex system governable. A good target can also motivate genuine improvement. If a bridge is supposed to carry a specified load, it is appropriate to test whether it does. If a treatment demonstrably reduces an outcome we care about, it is appropriate to care about that outcome. The target has not thereby become illegitimate.

Goodhart's warning is a pressure to ask whether the relationship will survive our attempts to exploit it. It is not a proof that every target immediately stops being useful. That is important. Catchy laws are themselves vulnerable to being turned into overused proxies for thought.

## Why more metrics are not enough

Suppose you agree with my criticism and decide that the library needs a balanced scorecard. Instead of circulation alone, it will report circulation, visitor satisfaction, use of computers, number of community events, and the cost per visitor.

That is probably an improvement. It makes more of the institution visible. A single measure no longer has unquestioned authority.

But there is no guarantee that five numbers are safe where one was dangerous. A system with five targets has five opportunities to optimize the display rather than the underlying result. And when the numbers conflict, someone still has to decide how much each matters. A library could maximize visitor satisfaction by never enforcing a noise rule; it could minimize cost per visitor by refusing difficult services; it could maximize event attendance by offering entertainment unrelated to its other purposes. Perhaps those choices would be right in particular circumstances. The scorecard does not settle them.

Sometimes an organization turns several indicators into a weighted score. Fifty percent performance, twenty percent satisfaction, twenty percent efficiency, ten percent innovation. It looks sophisticated. But the weights are a judgment. They have not been discovered by arithmetic. And the conversion of qualitatively different values into common points can hide tradeoffs that deserve argument.

I am not opposed to composite scores. They are sometimes necessary. I am opposed to treating the existence of a formula as evidence that the formula has resolved the underlying conflict.

The more powerful defense is not a larger dashboard. It is an institution that remains willing to examine the world beyond its dashboard.

That means keeping some observations outside the reward system. It means checking whether an apparent gain survives a new measurement or a different sample. It means hearing from people who are poorly represented by aggregate numbers. It means asking whether the target has changed behavior in ways that undermine the original objective. It means revising the target rather than defending it because last year's performance review depends on its credibility.

And it means preserving the possibility of a sentence that is administratively inconvenient but intellectually essential: the score improved, and the service got worse.

## The held-out test for an institution

Software engineers and statisticians have a useful concept for this problem: the held-out test.

When you train a predictive model, you do not normally judge its general ability by asking only how well it performs on the data it was trained to fit. It may have learned patterns that hold only in that sample, including accidental noise. Instead you evaluate it on data it did not get to optimize against.

The analogy to institutions is imperfect, but productive.

If you reward a call center for fast calls, inspect a sample of cases for actual resolution, with attention to the people who had to call back. If you reward a school for exam results, also investigate what students can do with unfamiliar material they have not rehearsed. If you reward a software team for tickets closed, ask whether real users can accomplish their tasks and whether the changes remain maintainable six months later.

The key is independence. If every possible observation becomes a public target with immediate rewards attached, people will learn to optimize all of them. Then you have constructed a more complicated scoreboard, not a more trustworthy understanding.

An independent assessment cannot be a perfect secret oracle. It needs ethical oversight, valid sampling, and attention to context. People should not be ambushed by capricious criteria. But a serious organization can maintain ways to find out whether its favored indicators still predict what they were meant to predict.

Think of it as a recurring act of humility. We are going to try to improve a measurable thing. We are also going to check, periodically and independently, whether improving it still helps the thing we actually care about.

This is harder than announcing a target and giving speeches about accountability. It requires admitting that some outcomes are only partly observable, some tradeoffs cannot be translated into a single currency, and some people will have reasons to challenge our preferred statistics.

It requires that the people responsible for the score sometimes lose an argument to the people who understand the work.

## The metrics that never appear on the slide

There is another loss that comes from managing through a scoreboard, and I find it particularly important. It changes what we pay attention to before any target has been gamed.

Work that fits the score becomes legible. Work that does not becomes background.

A team may know exactly how many new features it shipped but not how many crises were prevented by quiet preventive maintenance. An organization may celebrate the employee who resolves a hundred easy cases while overlooking the colleague who patiently handles five that nobody else can solve. A research group may proudly report the number of citations while failing to notice the technician whose careful measurements made the research trustworthy.

You might say that the solution is simply to count those other contributions as well. Sometimes it is. A sensible organization should measure reliability and maintenance where it can. But there will always be a remainder. Human purposes have more texture than a finite report can express.

And that remainder is not necessarily trivial. It may contain everything that makes a place worth returning to: trust, fairness, beauty, patience, the willingness to tell a superior that the plan is wrong, the expert judgment that prevents a catastrophe before it becomes a statistic.

It is possible to make some of these things more measurable. It is not possible to outsource the question of their importance to measurement itself.

There is a moral consequence here. If an institution declares that the things it cannot conveniently count are not part of the job, it can convert a limitation of its reporting system into a judgment about whose labor deserves respect. The spreadsheet was designed to simplify attention; it ends by deciding who exists.

That is why I do not think this topic is merely about management efficiency. It is about what we permit our systems to recognize.

## The temptation to optimize a life

The same pattern appears at a personal scale, though with different stakes.

Suppose you decide to read more. You set a target of fifty books in a year. This may be a wonderful idea. It reminds you to make time for reading. It encourages you to finish books you would otherwise abandon out of distraction. It gives your habit a visible shape.

Then one day you are halfway through a difficult book that matters to you, and you notice that finishing it will take the time you could spend reading three short ones. You begin to resent the difficult book. The book count is supposed to protect your love of reading, but now it has started negotiating against it.

At this point, you could abandon the target. You could revise it. You could keep it and deliberately make an exception. I am not here to prescribe the correct choice. I am interested in your ability to notice the change.

A useful goal helps you remember what you value when you are distracted. A harmful goal demands obedience after it has stopped serving that value.

The number of books read is a measure. The experience of reading and what it adds to your life is the purpose. It would be strange to let the servant adjudicate a dispute with the master.

I am deliberately speaking in the language of choices rather than declaring that happiness cannot be measured. There are real advantages to keeping track of exercise, money, time, or health indicators. A person can learn from a chart. A chart can reveal that their memory was wrong. But it cannot decide, on its own, what they ought to want from a life.

The ability to change a target without regarding yourself as a failure is not weakness. It is an acknowledgment that the target was an instrument rather than a law of nature.

## Whose game is it?

There is one final problem beneath all the others. Even if you have a reliable measure and a well-designed incentive system, you still have to choose an objective.

You can make a delivery network faster. You can make it cheaper. You can make it quieter. You can make it more accessible to people who live far from profitable routes. You can ask workers to accept less predictable hours, or you can invest in schedules that give them more stability. You cannot infer the right tradeoff between these things by measuring all of them more precisely.

Better evidence can tell us what each choice costs and accomplishes. It cannot choose the community's values on the community's behalf.

This is why the most ambitious dream of total optimization strikes me as conceptually confused. Imagine an ideal computer that knows the precise outcome of every available policy. It can tell you what would happen if a city converted parking spaces into bus lanes, if a university raised admissions standards, if a company changed its compensation system. Its predictions are flawless. It does not hallucinate. It does not make arithmetic mistakes.

Even that machine has not answered the question of which world we should prefer. We would still need to decide how to weigh convenience against accessibility, short-term gains against future resilience, one person's welfare against another's, and efficiency against freedom to choose differently.

Some tradeoffs can be negotiated. Some can be resolved by rights or rules. Some remain contested. The presence of a very good prediction does not make the disagreement disappear.

I do not claim that preferences are always mysterious or that democratic choice is always wise. I claim something narrower and firmer. Predicting the consequences of a goal and justifying the goal are distinct forms of reasoning. A tool that excels at the first has not thereby acquired authority over the second.

That includes artificial intelligence. It is entirely reasonable to ask an AI system to optimize a schedule, compare costs, simulate outcomes, or find an efficient solution to a specified problem. But whenever the specification leaves out something important, a highly capable optimizer can become highly capable at neglecting it. Greater intelligence does not automatically supply the missing objective. Sometimes it only makes the mistake more effective.

The important human responsibility is therefore not merely to provide the optimizer with a longer list of instructions. It is to remain able to ask whether the instructions express the purpose, whether the purpose is still the right one, and who gets to revise it.

## Leave room for the question

Let me return to the library.

At the end of the year, the director stands before the city council. Circulation has risen. Attendance at events has risen. The cost per visitor has fallen. The dashboard is full of favorable numbers, and perhaps the library genuinely had an excellent year.

What I want is not for a council member to sneer at the statistics. I want someone to be interested enough to ask what happened behind them.

Who is coming now who did not come before? Who has stopped coming? What are the librarians proudest of that is not recorded here? What did you cut to achieve these gains? Did those cuts matter? Which number surprised you? Which one are you beginning to distrust? What would count as evidence that next year's plan is wrong?

These are not evasions of accountability. They are accountability. The figures make the conversation possible; they do not bring it to an end.

I suspect that many failures of public and private decision-making begin when we become too impatient for this conversation. The spreadsheet has been submitted. The score has been calculated. A decision looks justified. Asking what the numbers mean now feels like slowing down progress.

But progress is not an increase in a number. Progress is a change in the world that we have reasons to prefer.

The difficulty of determining which changes deserve that name is not an inconvenience to be engineered out of existence. It is the work.

Measure carefully. Test the predictions. Inspect the failures. Let numbers embarrass your assumptions and correct your memories. Then retain the right, and the responsibility, to say that a rising score is not enough.

Because the scoreboard is useful. It tells you how the game is going.

It cannot choose the game.

## Source and argument notes (not spoken)

**Historical origin and attribution.** The original monetary-policy law belongs to Charles A. E. Goodhart's 1975 work. The short modern restatement about a measure becoming a target is associated with Marilyn Strathern's 1997 essay, drawing on Keith Hoskin's 1996 treatment; do not quote that wording as Goodhart's own original sentence. Donald T. Campbell separately developed the problem of corruption pressure on quantitative social indicators in his social-program evaluation work, initially in the 1970s. The essay paraphrases their ideas rather than treating all three statements as synonymous.

**The bank case.** The CFPB announcement and consent order of September 8, 2016 document the enforcement action against Wells Fargo, the link to targets and compensation incentives, and the figure of *more than two million potentially unauthorized accounts* identified by the bank's own analysis at the time. "Potentially" is essential; the figure is not a proven count of individual unauthorized accounts. The stated $100 million was the CFPB civil penalty, not the total financial consequences or all penalties. The essay does not assume it knows the motives of every employee.

**Invented illustrations.** The library, university, teaching-method experiments, thermometer and lamp, research laboratory, personal reading habit, and ideal forecasting computer are thought experiments. They are not reported events or studies, and the essay does not claim that a particular institution used the described rules.

**A calculation behind the winner's curse example.** Suppose ten candidates have identical true value, and each reported score adds independent noise uniformly distributed between minus one and one. The expected maximum measurement error among ten samples is nine elevenths, approximately 0.818, even though each individual error has mean zero. More generally, among n samples this expectation is (n-1)/(n+1). This demonstrates pure selection bias without any strategic gaming. A different noise model changes the number, not the underlying mechanism. A held-out evaluation reduces this problem only if it is genuinely independent, representative of the intended deployment distribution, and not repeatedly reused for selection.

**Boundaries of the claim.** Goodhart-style failures are contingencies, not an automatic theorem that all target metrics become invalid. Causally close, hard-to-game metrics may remain highly useful, and some well-specified optimization objectives work well. The argument concerns the need to distinguish evidence, incentive, and normative objective. The "held-out test for an institution" is an analogy and a proposed governance practice, not an experimentally validated universal intervention.
