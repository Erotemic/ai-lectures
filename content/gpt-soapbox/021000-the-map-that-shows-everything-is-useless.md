# The Map That Shows Everything Is Useless

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

Imagine you have just arrived in a city you have never visited.

You ask a stranger for a map. They give you three.

The first shows the streets: their lengths, their intersections, the bridges, the paths through the park. The second shows the underground railway. The lines bend neatly at right angles. The central stations seem pleasantly far apart, even though above ground several are only a short walk from one another. The third shows which neighborhoods are likely to flood if a storm arrives tonight.

The maps disagree.

They must. They are trying to answer different questions.

Now imagine that an exceptionally diligent cartographer offers to settle the dispute. They will make a single map containing every fact about the city. Every road. Every pipe. Every doorway. Every bus route, property line, tree, basement, sidewalk, historical boundary, bird nest, pedestrian movement, and change in elevation. Every fact will be drawn at a scale large enough to read. No generalizations, no omitted detail, no abstraction.

The cartographer has proposed an object so faithful that you may need the entire city to store it. And when you ask how to get to the railway station, the map will be useless.

That is the paradox I want to defend. A good map does not merely contain information. It leaves information out in a disciplined way. It makes some relationships visible by hiding others. It is a device for selective attention.

This is true of the maps in your phone. It is true of the charts that guide ships, the diagrams that explain a machine, the models that predict disease, and the simplified picture of the world that each of us carries around in our heads.

But something follows from this that is more troubling than the pleasant observation that simplicity is useful.

If a map must leave things out, somebody gets to decide what disappears.

Sometimes those decisions are ingenious. Sometimes they are careless. Sometimes they make invisible the very people whose lives are supposed to be improved by the map. And sometimes we forget that a decision was made at all, because the finished picture looks so objective.

I want to talk about the intelligence of good omissions, the danger of bad ones, and a difficult standard for anyone who tries to represent the world: tell me not only what your map shows, but what it cannot show.

## The man who bent London

In nineteen thirty-three, travelers on the London Underground were introduced to a new kind of railway map. Its designer, Harry Beck, had been a technical draftsman. The network, as he drew it, was wonderfully regular. Lines went up and down, left and right, or diagonally at forty-five degrees. Stations were named clearly. Interchanges were recognizable. The familiar geographical twists of London were largely gone.

London had not straightened its tunnels overnight. Beck had straightened the representation.

Before such diagrams became common, a railway map might follow the physical tracks across the city. That seems like the obvious way to draw a railway. If a tunnel turns, draw the turn. If two stations are far apart, put them far apart on the page. If the line makes a complicated journey through central London, reproduce the complication.

But imagine trying to put a sprawling metropolitan network onto a small sheet of paper. The distant stations consume the available space, and the crowded center becomes a knot of nearly illegible text. Geographic fidelity has begun to defeat the purpose of the map.

Beck's extraordinary idea was to give up that fidelity deliberately. His map was not chiefly answering, Where exactly are these tunnels? It was answering, How do I get from this station to that station, and where must I change trains?

For that purpose, the topology mattered more than the geometry.

Topology is the study of relationships that survive changes in shape. We do not need the entire mathematics of it here. Think of a network of strings connected by knots. You may stretch and curve the strings without changing which knots are connected. If you want to know how to travel from one knot to another, the connections are essential. The angle of every string is not.

Beck made a similar bargain on behalf of his travelers. Keep the connections. Keep the station order. Keep the transfers. Distort much of the physical geography.

This was a spectacular success in information design. A drawing that was literally less faithful to London's geography became more faithful to a passenger's immediate question.

There is, however, an important qualification. A Tube diagram is a good tool for changing trains. It may be a poor tool for deciding whether to walk. A journey that looks substantial on the diagram may be short above ground, while a connection between distant suburban stations may conceal a much greater distance than the picture suggests.

The same diagram can be excellent for one decision and misleading for another.

That is the first rule I would teach anyone learning to read a map: do not ask whether the map is accurate without first asking, Accurate for what?

A map is not a report card on the cartographer's virtue. It is a proposed relationship between information and action.

A ship's chart, a subway diagram, and a pedestrian map can all be correct, even when their lines cannot be superimposed without disagreement. Their correctness depends partly on which features must survive the simplification.

And when the map is used for a question it was not designed to answer, the omitted facts can suddenly become the most important facts in the world.

## The shape of the world on a flat page

Now replace London with Earth.

The planet's surface is curved. Your paper, or your screen, is flat. You cannot flatten a spherical surface onto a plane while preserving every distance, every area, every angle, and every relationship. Something has to give.

One famous choice was introduced by Gerardus Mercator in fifteen sixty-nine. The Mercator projection was particularly useful for navigation because lines of constant compass bearing, called rhumb lines, appear as straight lines on the map.

This is a precise and valuable mathematical property. If you are drawing a course on a nautical chart, the ability to represent a constant heading with a straight line can make an otherwise complicated task more manageable. It is worth remembering that a constant-bearing course is not necessarily the shortest route between two points. The projection makes a particular navigation problem easier; it does not solve all problems of navigation at once.

The price is distortion.

As you move away from the equator toward either pole, the Mercator map inflates the apparent size of regions. Greenland looks immense. Africa and South America appear smaller relative to northern landmasses than their true surface areas justify. Near the poles the distortion becomes extreme.

Here is a detail that matters. Mercator is not a careless drawing. It is mathematically systematic. Locally, it preserves angles, a property known as conformality. The area distortion is the consequence of preserving certain other properties on a flat surface.

You can say exactly what the map does. You can calculate the distortion. And for the question it was built to answer, its bargain can be sensible.

But the very same map can give a wildly misleading impression when it is hung on a classroom wall and interpreted as a picture of how large the continents are.

It is tempting to resolve the problem by declaring one map projection the good projection and another the bad projection. This is another way of missing the point.

An equal-area projection can preserve relative areas while bending local shapes. A projection that preserves local shapes cannot preserve all areas on a global map. A useful map of ocean routes may not be the right map for comparing forest loss across countries. A map designed for regional measurements may be a terrible choice for displaying the entire globe.

Choosing a projection is not a minor visual preference. It is choosing which errors are acceptable for the intended task.

What should the mapmaker do? State the task. Choose a projection that serves it. Make the distortion intelligible when it matters. Do not let a map chosen for navigation masquerade as a neutral demonstration of who occupies the largest portion of the world.

And what should the reader do? Learn to ask what has been preserved. Area? Angles? Distances from a chosen point? Direction? Connectivity? Which of those matters to the decision at hand?

The answer is often less dramatic than an accusation that the map is lying. It is also more useful. A map may be perfectly faithful to its own method and unsuitable for the use we have assigned it.

This is a deeper kind of error than a misplaced coastline. It is a mismatch between a representation and a question.

## What a map can make us notice

So far I have talked about maps that guide travel. But a map can do something else. It can reorganize an investigation.

In eighteen fifty-four, cholera tore through a part of Soho in London. The disease was frightening, severe, and incompletely understood. The physician John Snow suspected that contaminated drinking water was an important route of transmission. That position challenged the widely influential idea that cholera spread through foul air.

Snow investigated deaths around Broad Street. He examined the location of households, the pumps from which residents obtained water, and the apparently awkward cases that did not fit the simplest geographical pattern. Some people lived near one pump but drank from another. Some institutions in the affected district had different water arrangements. The question was not simply where deaths occurred. It was how people had been exposed.

His now famous map marked deaths in relation to the neighborhood's pumps. It is a compelling picture: a terrible concentration of suffering around one particular source of water.

You have probably encountered a simplified version of this story. Snow draws the map. The map reveals the culprit. He removes the pump handle. The outbreak ends. A heroic picture solves a scientific mystery.

This version is satisfying. It is also misleading.

The historical chronology is more complicated. Snow's investigation and advice to local authorities preceded the best-known published map. The Broad Street pump handle was removed in early September. Snow first exhibited a version of the death map in December, and the famous published image appeared in his eighteen fifty-five book. The outbreak had already been declining when the handle was removed, as residents fled and the initial wave of illness passed. We should not pretend that a beautiful map single-handedly ended it.

More importantly, the evidence was not merely the clustering of deaths. It included the relationship between where people lived and where they actually obtained drinking water. That distinction sounds small until you imagine the alternative.

Suppose everyone drinks water at home. A map of residence may be a good proxy for exposure. But suppose children drink on the way to school, workers drink near their jobs, or families prefer water from a pump farther away. Then the place where someone slept and the place where they encountered contaminated water are different data points.

A map of addresses alone does not contain the mechanism of disease.

Snow's investigation required something beyond cartography: a causal explanation, information about behavior, attention to exceptions, and comparison with other hypotheses. The map helped organize and communicate the pattern. It did not make the rest of the reasoning unnecessary.

This is the second rule I would teach about maps: when a picture suggests a cause, ask what would have to be true for that cause to follow.

If deaths cluster around a pump, is the water contaminated? Or are the surrounding households more crowded? Or is there another environmental factor? What exposure history would distinguish the explanations? What observation would count against the preferred theory?

A map is powerful because it can make an invisible pattern visible. It is dangerous when visual clarity feels like a substitute for a tested explanation.

That tension is still with us in modern medical research. Draw cases of a disease on a map, and a region may seem to glow with a terrible significance. Perhaps it does. But a map of reported cases also reflects who was tested, who visited a clinic, where populations are dense, how records were collected, and whether different places use the same definitions.

A high count in one district may describe a high burden of illness, an excellent reporting system, a large population, or some combination. A low count may describe safety, or it may describe people the system never reached.

The picture is real data. The interpretation is work.

## The color that was also a judgment

During the late nineteenth century, the social investigator Charles Booth and his colleagues undertook an immense study of life and labor in London. Among its results were remarkable maps that classified streets according to the economic circumstances of their residents.

The maps were visually striking. Colors spread across neighborhoods, turning the city into a patterned landscape of wealth and poverty. They made inequalities visible at a scale that could be difficult to grasp from individual stories or aggregate tables.

There was serious documentary work behind them. They are valuable historical records. Researchers can examine how income, employment, and housing varied across London, and how those patterns changed over time.

But look at what happens when we move from recording circumstances to naming people.

One category was labeled in terms of vice and supposed criminality. It wasn't simply a measurement of weekly income. It contained a moral verdict. Across a map, that verdict could be attached to a whole street, even though no street contains a single kind of person.

This is not an argument that Booth's investigators should have avoided studying poverty. The observation of inequality can be an instrument of reform. It is an argument about how the chosen categories alter the meaning of the picture.

A line on a map might indicate a river. A color might indicate a measured flood depth. But a color may also indicate that somebody has taken complicated observations, made a classification, and assigned it a name. When that name describes the worth, character, or trustworthiness of the people living there, the legend has become an argument about those people.

And because it is printed neatly beside a map, the argument may look as though it came directly from the ground.

It didn't. The surveyors collected information through particular methods. The designers selected categories. The map pooled differences among households and residents into street-level descriptions. The labels reflected assumptions about class and behavior that readers should be free to challenge.

A shaded street can tell us something worth learning. It cannot tell us that everybody who lives there shares the same moral character.

This is an important distinction between measurement and judgment. We can challenge a measurement by asking how it was made. We can challenge a judgment by asking what values and assumptions it contains. Many maps mix the two.

If they are honest, they should not disguise one as the other.

## When the legend affects the loan

In the nineteen thirties, the American Home Owners' Loan Corporation developed color-coded maps grading neighborhoods by what officials called residential security. Green indicated places judged most secure for lending. Red indicated neighborhoods classified as hazardous.

The area descriptions behind these maps often incorporated openly discriminatory assumptions about race, ethnicity, immigration, and the supposed stability of neighborhoods. These documents are now collected and made searchable through the University of Richmond's Mapping Inequality project.

There is a reason these maps have become powerful historical artifacts. They show prejudice translated into the official visual language of risk. A neighborhood becomes a color. The color becomes a grade. The grade is easy to repeat without ever repeating the assumptions that produced it.

But I want to be careful with the history, because an overly neat story would reproduce the very error I am describing.

The maps did not invent discriminatory lending, nor can we reasonably say that the Home Owners' Loan Corporation created the entire practice of redlining merely by printing a set of maps. Historical research shows that lenders were avoiding many of the disfavored neighborhoods before these particular maps were made. The maps were not widely distributed to private lenders. Scholars disagree about the extent to which the maps themselves caused later patterns, as distinct from recording and reinforcing a discriminatory system that already existed.

Those distinctions matter. We do not make the case against discrimination stronger by assigning every consequence to the wrong document.

The deeper issue is that a colored risk map can encode a set of judgments in a way that makes them portable and apparently objective. A decision-maker who sees only red may not see the residents, the history of exclusion, the disparities in access to credit, or the role that previous policy played in producing the conditions now classified as risky.

Here is the dangerous loop.

Imagine a neighborhood suffers from years of disinvestment. A rating system observes declining buildings and limited access to resources. It calls the neighborhood high risk. Lenders use that classification to justify withholding additional investment. The lack of investment makes the neighborhood appear riskier next time.

That loop is a constructed example, not a claim that any one historical map necessarily caused that sequence. But the mechanism is real enough to be worth watching for: a representation used to make decisions may change the world that it will later be used to represent.

Once the map influences the territory, the next measurement is no longer independent of the previous judgment.

A wildfire risk map might rightly steer development away from danger. A map of poor road conditions might help an agency prioritize repairs. A map of perceived financial risk might instead help preserve the conditions that made the risk score look plausible. The ethical difference cannot be read from the colors alone. It depends on the evidence, the decision, the affected people, and the feedback loop.

This is where mapmaking becomes public responsibility. It matters who chose the categories, who can inspect them, and who has the authority to appeal an error.

A map that affects people's access to housing, services, or safety is not simply a picture. It is part of an institution.

## The dangerous meanings of blank

There is another kind of map error that can be harder to recognize, because it looks like nothing.

A blank area.

Maybe the cartographer deliberately left it blank because the subject is irrelevant. A subway map does not need to display every tree. Maybe the data have not been collected. Maybe the mapmaker could not afford to survey the area. Maybe an agency is legally prohibited from publishing a location. Maybe people there are overlooked by the system that gathers information. Or maybe the area really has no instances of the phenomenon being measured.

These situations are different. The same empty patch of paper can represent any of them.

In the wake of the devastating earthquake that struck Haiti on January twelfth, twenty ten, volunteer mappers working with OpenStreetMap began rapidly tracing roads, buildings, and other features from available imagery. They were responding to a practical shortage of usable, current geographic information for the emergency effort. Within weeks, hundreds of people had contributed to a map that relief organizations could use.

The World Bank later described contributions from more than six hundred volunteers across twenty-nine countries, making about one point two million edits in a concentrated effort. The Humanitarian OpenStreetMap Team's anniversary account describes the rapid creation of a basemap that was used by responders.

The people in Haiti had not been living in unmapped physical space. Roads, homes, paths, and communities existed long before remote volunteers traced them into a database. What was missing was a sufficiently accessible, up-to-date, machine-readable representation for the tasks the responders needed to perform.

That distinction matters.

When a digital map shows a blank region, people sometimes slip into saying that the area has no roads or no buildings. Usually the legitimate claim is narrower: this database does not currently contain roads or buildings there.

An absent line on a map is not evidence of an absent road.

This should sound elementary. Yet we make this error constantly with databases of all kinds. A patient without a recorded diagnosis is not necessarily healthy. A school without a reported incident is not necessarily safe. A village without a mapped road is not necessarily inaccessible. A language absent from a popular benchmark is not a language without speakers, literature, or intellectual life.

We turn absence of evidence into evidence of absence because the display makes both look like the same blank space.

The Haiti mapping effort also exposes a difficulty in the other direction. Remote volunteers can trace a visible road, but they may not know whether it is passable after the earthquake, what locals call it, whether a bridge has collapsed, or whether access is restricted. Satellite imagery provides one kind of evidence. Field knowledge provides another. A useful emergency map needs a way for these sources to inform and correct each other.

The answer to missing data is not simply more confident drawing. It is also a system for reporting what is known, what is guessed, how recent the observations are, and who is able to correct them.

## What would an honest legend contain?

At this point, perhaps I sound as if I want to attach a small philosophical dissertation to every map you open on your phone.

I do not.

If I am crossing a city in the rain, I don't want my navigation app to open with a seminar on the epistemology of road networks. I want it to tell me when to turn left.

But I do want somebody designing that app to have asked a few questions. Which pedestrians can use this route? Is there a staircase the map has failed to mark? Does it know which entrance is accessible? How recent is the information about the bridge? If the location estimate is wrong, does the interface make that obvious before I walk into a road?

For a subway diagram, a brief title and a scale warning may be enough. For a public flood-risk map, the legend might need to explain the probability interval, the data vintage, and what uncertainty means at the level of an individual house. For a lending model, the underlying classifications and appeals process may be more important than the colors used in the interface.

The right amount of explanation depends on how much the map is being asked to decide.

I would propose four habits of good mapping.

The first is to name the question. Say what the representation is for. A map designed to navigate railway connections is not designed to rank neighborhoods by economic potential. State the purpose before the reader invents a more consequential one.

The second is to distinguish an empty value from an unknown value. Zero cases recorded is different from no cases. There are places where the data are missing, dates when observations have expired, and boundaries where measurements are too uncertain to support a neat line. Sometimes that uncertainty deserves its own symbol.

The third is to make the categories inspectable. What does the red mean? Measured danger? Expert judgment? A legally designated zone? A score computed from incomplete observations? Each can be defensible in its place, but they are not interchangeable.

The fourth is to preserve a route back to the evidence. A person affected by a classification should have some way, appropriate to the stakes, to learn how it was produced and to challenge a factual mistake. In low-stakes maps, that might be a simple edit button. In consequential public decisions, it may require published methods, independent review, and accountable institutions.

None of these habits creates a perfect map. That is the point. They make a map usable without pretending it is complete.

## A model with an attractive color scheme

I want to finish by stepping away from cartography, because this entire discussion is about something larger.

Scientists construct models. Economists construct models. Engineers construct models. A machine-learning system constructs a relationship between inputs and predictions. People construct stories about what happened to them and why. In every case, something is preserved and something is omitted.

A model that includes every detail of a phenomenon has not necessarily explained it. Explanation requires deciding which features matter to a question and which can, for the moment, be neglected.

In physics, a simple model of a falling object may ignore air resistance. That omission can be excellent for understanding gravity in a classroom and terrible for predicting how a feather descends in a gust of wind. In economics, a simplified account of market behavior may illuminate incentives while overlooking institutions, unequal bargaining power, or the costs that never enter a transaction. In machine learning, a high overall accuracy score may conceal poor performance for the exact group whose situation makes an error most costly.

A model is not defective merely because it is simplified. The defect begins when the simplification is forgotten, concealed, or applied outside the circumstances that made it useful.

This is why I dislike the notion that increasing the amount of data automatically moves us closer to an unmediated picture of reality. More measurements can be wonderful. Better measurements can be transformative. But no number of recorded observations decides for us which question we ought to ask, which errors matter most, or what a good response to the evidence would be.

A model with ten billion data points still has a legend, even if nobody has written it down. Somebody chose what counted as an observation. Somebody decided how errors were classified. Somebody decided which outcomes deserved attention. And if the model is embedded in a powerful institution, those choices may change the world in ways that the model was never built to describe.

I would like to see more pride in stating limitations well. Not as a timid disclaimer, the kind that gets copied into the last paragraph of a report. As part of what makes the work intelligent.

The best map is not the one that says, Trust me, I contain the world. It is the one that helps you reach a destination and tells you when you have reached the edge of what it knows.

## Fold the map

Let's return to our traveler, newly arrived in an unfamiliar city, holding three maps.

The railway diagram is still useful. The flood map is still useful. The street map is still useful. The fact that they disagree about appearance is not evidence that one of them has failed. Each asks the city to reveal something different.

Our traveler now has a better question than, Which one is the true map?

The question is, Which map should I trust for the decision I am about to make? And what could this map be hiding that would change my decision?

I do not think we should demand that every map become a courtroom witness reciting every possible objection. The genius of a good map is precisely that it spares us from confronting everything at once. There is pleasure in that clarity. There is freedom in it. A person who could not navigate the city before can now find a way through.

But clarity is a gift with conditions. We must not mistake the clean line for the complicated street, the colored district for the people who live there, or the empty cell for a world in which nothing happens.

A map cannot show everything. That is why it can help us see anything at all.

The work is to choose the omissions carefully, show enough of their consequences, and remain willing to draw the map again.

Fold it when you have finished. Step outside. The city is still there.

## Sources, examples, and research boundary

This is an independently argued essay about the epistemology and ethics of representation. The opening traveler, the all-inclusive cartographer, the imagined neighborhood lending feedback loop, and other illustrative situations are constructed examples, not historical episodes. Normative advice about model transparency, whose interests matter, and which map uses are ethically justified is the essay's argument rather than a claim attributed to the sources below.

The 1933 diagram discarded much geographical fidelity to make a complex network legible, using consistent line angles and recognizable interchanges. Claims about the diagram's limits for walking are applications of its design properties rather than measured claims about specific station pairs.
- **Mercator and projection tradeoffs.** [U.S. Geological Survey, *Map Projections*](https://pubs.usgs.gov/gip/70047422/report.pdf?loclr=blogmap); [USGS, *Map projections: a working manual*](https://www.usgs.gov/educational-resources/map-projections-working-manual); [Esri, Mercator projection reference](https://doc.esri.com/en/arcgis-pro/latest/help/mapping/properties/mercator.html); [National Geographic, *Selecting a Map Projection*](https://education.nationalgeographic.org/resource/selecting-map-projection/). The essay distinguishes absent digital map features from absent physical places and does not imply volunteers alone delivered relief.
