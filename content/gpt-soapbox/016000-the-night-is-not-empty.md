# The Night Is Not Empty

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

Suppose you are walking home at night and somebody has installed a new floodlight on the building across the street. It is powerful enough to illuminate the pavement, the windows above it, the side of a parked car, a portion of a nearby tree, and much of the sky. It is bright enough to be noticed from several blocks away.

Perhaps the owner wanted to discourage theft. Perhaps the fixture was cheap, the installer chose a generous safety margin, and nobody thought much about the direction of the beam. Perhaps there was a genuine need for light at the entrance, and it seemed easier to illuminate everything than to decide exactly what needed illumination.

Nothing about this requires a villain. The light may even make one part of the walk safer.

But what has happened to the rest of the night?

The person in the upstairs apartment now has more light entering the bedroom. Insects that would have moved through the dark encounter a powerful new signal. A bird migrating overhead encounters a brighter patch of ground. Anyone looking up from the sidewalk sees fewer faint stars. A person across the street who wanted darkness did not get to participate in the choice.

The light has an owner. The illumination does not stay on the owner's property.

That is where I want to begin. We are accustomed to thinking of darkness as an absence: the condition that remains before somebody provides light. We count the arrival of lighting as a service. We rarely count the darkness that has been displaced as something with a value of its own.

I think that accounting is incomplete.

Night is not empty space waiting to be improved. It is habitat. It is a biological clock. It is one of humanity's oldest sources of wonder. And it is a shared environmental condition that one person's decisions can alter for many others.

I am not going to argue that electric light was a mistake. The ability to make a room, a street, a workshop, or an operating theater bright at the moment we need it is an extraordinary human achievement. I want to defend that achievement against a habit that diminishes it: the assumption that if some light is helpful, more light, over more territory, for more hours, must be better.

The question is not whether we should have light. The question is whether we still know what light is for.

## The working hours of the other half of the world

Stand near a flowering plant during the day and you may see a bee visit it. This is a familiar picture of pollination. It is familiar enough to masquerade as the whole story.

But many plants are visited at night. Moths and other nocturnal insects move between flowers after sunset. Their activity is not a backup copy of the daytime world. It is a partly different network of relationships: different visitors, different schedules, different opportunities for pollen to travel.

A research team led by Eva Knop published an experiment in Nature in twenty seventeen that made this easy to grasp. In illuminated plant communities, they recorded sixty-two percent fewer nocturnal visits to plants than in comparable dark communities. For the focal plant in that study, fruit set was reduced by thirteen percent, even though the plant also received visits from daytime pollinators.

That is a result from a specific field experiment, not a universal law saying every streetlight reduces every crop by thirteen percent. The size of an effect depends on the organisms, the landscape, and the lighting. But the mechanism is a warning against an assumption that might otherwise seem reasonable: if a plant gets visited during the day, what happens at night probably does not matter.

Sometimes it does.

Consider what it means to replace a period of natural darkness with a new source of light. We do not merely make things easier for our eyes to see. We change a signal that other creatures use to organize their behavior. A moth may be drawn away from a flower. A predator may find prey more easily. A species that ordinarily avoids open, bright spaces may find the same patch of habitat harder to cross.

The night has a schedule, and our lamps can interrupt it.

This is even clearer on a sea turtle nesting beach. A hatchling does not hatch with a map of the coastline. It has evolved responses to visual cues. On a relatively natural beach, the open horizon over the ocean tends to look brighter than the landward side, where dunes and vegetation form dark silhouettes. That contrast helps the hatchling find the water.

Now put an intensely illuminated hotel or parking lot behind the beach.

The hatchling's response is not suddenly foolish. It is doing something that usually works, in an environment where the signals have been changed. Florida's wildlife agency documents the result: hatchlings can travel away from the sea, become exhausted or dehydrated, or encounter roads and predators. Artificial lights can also deter nesting females or interfere with their return to the water.

This example matters to me because it makes the phrase light pollution precise. Pollution does not always have to be a poisonous substance. A change to the environment can be harmful because it delivers the wrong information at the wrong time.

We built something that says, very brightly, come this way. Another creature follows the message. We did not mean to address that creature, but it was listening.

## Migrants in the beam

Much of the world's bird migration happens at night. A migrating bird is solving a difficult navigation problem while also managing limited energy and changing weather. The sky is part of the environment through which that journey takes place.

The United States Fish and Wildlife Service warns that artificial lighting can attract or disorient night-migrating birds. Under certain conditions, especially low cloud and fog, birds may circle illuminated structures, expend energy, or collide with buildings. These effects are not distributed evenly across every species, light, and night. Weather, the type of fixture, the location, and the migration route all matter.

That last qualification is important. When people hear about bird collisions, they may picture a simple rule: light is bad, darkness is good. But a high-rise tower shining into cloud is not equivalent to a dim, shielded path lamp. Some lights are steady, some flash. Some point upward or outward. Some shine through glass. Some are switched off after the building closes. Treating them as interchangeable would conceal the design problem.

We know how to modify parts of this problem. Lights can be aimed downward. Unnecessary decorative illumination can be switched off during migration. A building can avoid projecting a luminous beacon into the sky when nobody needs it. Curtains can prevent an office's interior lights from turning the entire glass facade into part of the display.

This is one reason I do not find blanket arguments about technology very helpful. The harm is not simply a property of having invented the electric lamp. It emerges from where, when, how brightly, and in what direction we use it.

In that respect, night lighting is an unusually good case study in technological maturity. Inventing a source of light is one achievement. Learning to shape its consequences is another.

And there is a deeper question hiding underneath the engineering. If a building owner does not intend to interfere with migration, but the lights do interfere with migration, whose responsibility is it to notice? The birds do not submit complaints. The ecosystem does not receive a seat at the planning meeting. Effects become visible only because people decide to study them and bring the evidence into a design process that was originally concerned with something else.

We should be grateful for that kind of research. It expands the circle of consequences we are capable of seeing.

## The stars are still there

Here is a thought that I find almost unbearably strange. People can live their whole lives beneath a sky whose most spectacular features are hidden, not because the features have vanished, but because we have put an artificial glow between ourselves and them.

A world atlas of artificial night-sky brightness published in twenty sixteen estimated that more than eighty percent of the world's population and more than ninety-nine percent of the populations of the United States and Europe lived under light-polluted skies. The atlas estimated that the Milky Way was hidden from more than a third of humanity.

These figures describe modeled conditions and population exposure at the time of that atlas, not a census of what every person can see from a particular window. Weather, terrain, eyesight, and local illumination affect actual visibility. Even so, the scale of the result is astonishing.

It means that losing the view of our galaxy is not something that happens only to the occasional person living beside a badly designed billboard. For many people, it is an ordinary condition of life.

And the evidence suggests the problem has been growing.

In twenty twenty-three, Christopher Kyba and colleagues analyzed more than fifty-one thousand observations submitted by volunteers to Globe at Night between twenty eleven and twenty twenty-two. Those human observations of visible stars were consistent with an increase in night-sky brightness of roughly seven to ten percent per year over the observed period, with a fitted average near nine point six percent.

That rate should not be read as a precise increase for every place on Earth. Volunteers are not evenly distributed around the world, and the researchers had to account for changing participation and differences in observation conditions. It is also not a direct measure of the number of lamps installed. The relation between a lamp and the sky depends on color, direction, atmosphere, and distance.

The finding is nonetheless significant because it measures the effect in a very human currency: stars people can see.

I like the fact that the project used ordinary observers. It is a reminder that some scientific measurements are not only about machines detecting something invisible. Sometimes they help us quantify a change in something that used to be plainly visible.

There is a temptation to treat the loss of the Milky Way as a decorative concern. So people in cities see fewer stars. Is that really important compared with housing, health, transportation, and public safety?

I think the question deserves a serious answer rather than a sentimental reflex.

First, astronomy is not merely decorative. Observatories need dark skies to detect faint sources. A telescope does not care whether the extra photons were emitted for a noble reason. If unwanted light enters the measurement, the instrument has a harder problem to solve. Protecting an observatory's view can therefore be an ordinary practical requirement for scientific work.

Second, seeing the night sky is a kind of access to the world that does not require an intermediary to have fabricated an experience for you. The light reaching your eyes from a distant star has traveled through space. Learning what it is may require books and instruments, but the encounter itself does not require a screen, a subscription, or a ticket.

Third, losing something familiar can make us forget it was ever available. A child who grows up without a visible Milky Way may not experience a loss. They may simply inherit a narrower picture of what a night can look like.

I am not claiming that the stars make people morally better. I cannot establish that. I am claiming that access to a visible universe is a real good, and that its disappearance deserves to be treated as a cost rather than as a picturesque footnote.

## The biological clock is not a metaphor

Our own bodies are not indifferent to the timing of light.

We use the word clock as though the daily rhythm were a little device inside the head. In reality, the circadian system is a network of biological processes influenced by light reaching the eyes and by a host of behavioral and physiological signals. It helps organize the timing of sleep, alertness, and many bodily functions. Light in the morning and light in the evening do not have the same effect.

Research on circadian rhythms supports a broadly practical distinction: relatively bright days, dimmer evenings, and darkness during sleep tend to be more compatible with the normal timing of human rest than an environment that treats all hours alike.

But I want to be careful here. It would be easy to turn this into a frightening story about how modern lighting is poisoning everyone. That would go beyond the evidence. The effects depend on intensity, timing, duration, spectrum, individual sensitivity, and whether we are talking about a controlled experiment, a particular sleep outcome, or a long-term disease claim. Scientific bodies have also noted gaps and conflicting findings in research on long-term health effects of short-wavelength light.

The reliable point is not that every blue-rich lamp is a medical emergency. It is that light is a biological signal as well as a visual tool. When we design a place for people to live, work, and sleep, we should consider both jobs that the light is doing.

The timing issue also makes it difficult to talk about one ideal human night. Hospitals operate around the clock. People work late shifts. Someone reading beside a sick child may need light urgently at three in the morning. A person with impaired vision may need more illumination to navigate a hallway safely. Different lives require different arrangements.

A humane design should be able to accommodate those needs without pretending that the only available choice is bright everywhere or dark everywhere.

Consider an indoor corridor. A ceiling fixture that stays at maximum intensity every hour might make sense in a continuously occupied high-risk space. In a different building, a lower overnight level with localized, quickly responsive lighting might serve the people using it better. The correct choice depends on who uses the space and what can go wrong there. A lighting plan is not improved merely by adding bulbs.

This is another way in which darkness asks us to be more thoughtful about the technology we already have. Once we are able to switch light on instantly, to dim it, to aim it, and to change its timing, leaving every light on continuously becomes a design decision rather than an unavoidable feature of civilization.

## The strongest argument for the lamp

Let me make the opposing case as well as I can.

Suppose you are walking alone along a badly lit road. Cars approach faster than you would like. The shoulder is narrow. You cannot see a broken section of pavement until you are almost on top of it. The idea that this darkness is an ecological treasure is not going to sound persuasive. It may sound like someone else's aesthetic preference being purchased with your safety.

You would have a point.

Electric lighting has made streets, factories, stations, and homes usable under conditions that once imposed much greater limits and dangers. It enables emergency work, movement, recreation, and economic activity after sunset. For many people, it is a condition of independence.

The evidence also prevents us from making a simple claim that reducing light always improves the world. A Cochrane review of street lighting and road injuries, published in two thousand nine, found studies suggesting that installing or improving street lighting could reduce crashes and injuries. The authors also warned that the studies they pooled were mostly methodologically weak and that stronger evaluation was needed.

An updated systematic review of street-lighting interventions and crime, published in twenty twenty-two, examined twenty-one studies from several countries. It reported an average reduction of about fourteen percent in total crime in treated areas compared with controls. The effect was clearer for property crime than for violent crime. One intriguing result was that studies including both daytime and nighttime offenses tended to show stronger improvements than studies measuring only nighttime offenses. That complicates the simple theory that a lamp works only by letting potential offenders see that they might be watched.

The point is not that brighter light is always safer. The point is that making places adequately and appropriately lit can produce real benefits. Those benefits deserve to be counted.

There is further nuance. An observational study of reduced street lighting across sixty-two local authorities in England and Wales found little evidence that the particular dimming and part-night strategies examined were associated with increases in traffic collisions or crime. That study does not prove every reduction everywhere is harmless. The effects depended on the strategies studied, and some estimates were imprecise. But it does challenge the idea that every lumen removed necessarily makes a street less safe.

A good argument for darkness cannot dismiss people's fears as ignorance, especially when the burdens of insecurity fall unequally. Women walking at night, people with disabilities, shift workers, elderly residents, and children using public spaces may have different reasons to want visibility and predictable routes. Those reasons cannot be answered with a photograph of the Milky Way.

The job, then, is not to choose between the astronomer and the pedestrian, or the turtle and the emergency responder, as though one must be declared unimportant.

It is to ask where light is actually needed, what kind will serve the need, what happens outside the beam, and who gets to participate in the decision.

I suspect that a great many of our lighting disagreements would look different if we began there.

## Too much of a good thing

Imagine designing a reading lamp. You want enough light on the page to read comfortably. You do not want the lamp to shine directly into your eyes. You do not need it to illuminate the neighboring apartment. And you would not regard it as an improvement if it made the room so bright that everything except the text became harder to see.

The principle is obvious at the scale of a desk.

For some reason, it becomes less obvious at the scale of a parking lot.

There are real technical reasons why brighter does not always mean better seeing. Glare can reduce visual comfort and, depending on the viewing conditions, impair visibility. Light spilling beyond the intended target wastes energy and affects other spaces. A fixture can be impressively bright while producing poor contrast where a person actually needs to see.

Color matters too. Human vision responds differently to the spectrum of a source under different levels of adaptation, and shorter-wavelength light is scattered strongly in the atmosphere. A lamp with an attractive color temperature on a showroom wall may not be the best choice near a sensitive habitat or astronomical site. Yet warmer light is not a universal substitute for thoughtful placement. A badly aimed warm-colored floodlight is still badly aimed.

There is a larger economic trap here. Once efficient lighting becomes cheap, we have the opportunity to use much less energy to accomplish a given task. We also have the temptation to illuminate more surfaces for more hours, simply because the marginal cost has fallen. Whether the resulting growth cancels a particular energy saving is an empirical question; it depends on what people actually install and how they operate it. But the possibility is easy to understand.

A more efficient tool lets you do the same work at lower cost. It can also tempt you to do considerably more work that you had never previously considered necessary.

That is why efficiency alone cannot decide the shape of a desirable night. Energy consumed per unit of light is one question. How much light we want, and where, is another.

This distinction reaches beyond electricity. A society can make an activity cheaper without deciding whether the additional activity is beneficial. It can make communication cheaper and produce a greater volume of messages than anybody wants to read. It can make computation cheaper and fill the world with automated decisions that did not need to be made. It can make illumination cheaper and turn every dark surface into an opportunity for another fixture.

A technology's efficiency tells us how much it costs to do something. It does not, by itself, tell us how much of that thing we ought to do.

## A city that made a different choice

There are places that have tried to do better without pretending that public life must end at sunset.

Flagstaff, Arizona, is one of them.

The city adopted an outdoor-lighting ordinance in nineteen fifty-eight that addressed searchlights. Over subsequent decades, it developed more comprehensive protections, including outdoor lighting limits enacted with the surrounding county in nineteen eighty-nine. In two thousand one, Flagstaff became the first city recognized as an International Dark Sky City.

I find this history instructive because it did not begin with the invention of some miraculous new form of darkness. It began with a series of public choices about what people would be allowed to shine into the shared environment.

Flagstaff is also associated with astronomical research, including Lowell Observatory. The city's protection of the night sky makes both cultural and practical sense. The opportunity to study faint celestial objects is part of what gives the place its character and scientific value.

Of course, Flagstaff is not a proof that every city can adopt identical rules without cost. Its geography, institutions, local interests, and design choices matter. A dense city with different traffic patterns and housing conditions will need a different plan. The fact that one place has a workable solution is an invitation to learn from it, not a command to copy it blindly.

What I take from the example is simpler: the night sky can be treated as a public interest in municipal decision-making. It does not have to be left out of the conversation because it is difficult to price.

There is already a practical design vocabulary for this. DarkSky International and the Illuminating Engineering Society have articulated five principles of responsible outdoor lighting. A light should serve a useful purpose. It should be targeted where it is needed. Its level should be no higher than necessary. It should be controlled so that it is on when needed rather than automatically all night. And, where appropriate, its color should avoid unnecessary short-wavelength emissions.

These are not commandments to abandon modern lighting. They are instructions to use modern lighting as though we know how to aim a tool.

What could that mean in ordinary life?

A light at a doorway can illuminate the doorway instead of the sky. A path can be legible without flooding the adjacent woods. A business can reduce decorative lighting after closing. A building can provide strong illumination for an event and then return to a quieter nighttime mode. An observatory can be protected without asking everyone within a hundred miles to live by candlelight.

The design challenge is real. But it is a challenge within engineering, architecture, public policy, and shared judgment. It is not an argument against those disciplines.

## The problem with a sky nobody owns

Now I want to return to the floodlight across the street.

A person buys a fixture, pays an electrician, and pays the electricity bill. From the narrow point of view of that transaction, the costs appear to be accounted for. Someone wanted light and purchased it.

But the fixture changes an environment beyond the property line. That is a classic problem in the economics of external effects. The individual who chooses an activity receives some of its benefits while distributing part of its costs to people who did not make the choice.

The phrase external cost sounds dry. Try translating it into the language of the room in which you sleep. The light outside your window is not merely an item on somebody else's electric bill. It is part of the conditions under which you are expected to rest.

The same problem appears when an advertisement is bright enough to dominate a public square. The advertiser can buy the sign and measure its visibility. Nobody has purchased an equivalent right to a dark patch of sky behind it. The people who would have preferred an unilluminated view may not even know how to express their loss in the decisions that matter.

There is a real tension here. Public places are shared by people who do not all want the same thing. A neon-lit street can be beautiful and exciting. A festival can transform a town after dark. Night markets, illuminated art, theaters, and restaurants are part of the cultural life of cities. I do not want to place every night under a single austere design language, nor imagine that one aesthetic preference should command all others.

What I want is room for distinctions.

There can be districts where light is part of the attraction and other places where preserving darkness is the attraction. There can be hours when illumination is valuable and later hours when the same display serves almost nobody. There can be lights that mark a safe route without overwhelming a neighboring habitat. We can recognize the beauty of an illuminated building without requiring that every building compete to be the brightest object in the district.

And when a decision has effects beyond its owner, there ought to be a way for those effects to enter the decision.

This is where local regulations, lighting standards, protected areas, and ordinary neighborly consideration become important. None is guaranteed to solve the problem. Rules can be poorly written; enforcement can be unfair; standards can freeze old technologies into place. But leaving the costs uncounted is also a choice. It generally favors the party with a fixture over the party who wants the night to remain dark.

A shared sky calls for some form of shared responsibility.

## What we might recover

I want to imagine what success would look like, because an argument that identifies damage without describing a desirable alternative can become a familiar kind of complaint.

Success would not mean turning off every streetlight. It would mean recognizing that light is a resource to be placed carefully, not a substance to spread as widely as possible.

It would mean a person using a wheelchair, a late-shift nurse, a child walking home from a rehearsal, and a resident living above a bright storefront could all be considered in the same design conversation. Their needs might not lead to one perfectly harmonious solution, but at least the discussion would concern the actual ways people inhabit the night.

It would mean giving more attention to the sky above a school, a park, or a public square, even when that sky does not produce a direct financial return. It would mean treating a dark shoreline as functioning habitat, not as a missed opportunity to install decorative lighting. It would mean allowing the public to enjoy an illuminated festival without assuming the same lamps should continue broadcasting until sunrise.

It would mean asking some very ordinary questions. Is this lamp needed? Who needs it? What is the smallest useful beam? When does the need end? What does the light reach after it passes the intended surface? Can the person who needs a bright task light get one without imposing brightness on everybody nearby?

And it would mean accepting that the appropriate answer will differ across places. A poorly lit intersection has a different problem from a floodlit office park at three in the morning. An inhabited urban alley has a different problem from a sea turtle nesting beach. A hospital entrance has a different problem from an empty monument that somebody decided ought to be visible from the highway.

To me, the most attractive part of this vision is that it takes technology seriously. We now have extraordinary control over the intensity, direction, timing, and color of artificial light. It would be a disappointment to use that control only to produce a larger quantity of light than our predecessors could afford.

The next achievement would be judgment about where light belongs.

## One last look upward

Let me end with a scene, not a statistic.

Imagine walking out of a building after an evening spent with other people. The windows are warm with light. A few lamps show the path. You can see the step at the entrance, the person beside you, and the way back to the road. You are not being asked to navigate a dangerous landscape in the dark.

Beyond the path, the trees are dark. The building does not illuminate the whole hillside. Above the roofline, the sky is visible. The lighting has done its job and stopped.

I think there is a kind of excellence in that restraint. Not because darkness is morally pure or electricity is suspect, but because the designer has understood the boundary of the task.

I sometimes think we misunderstand progress as the systematic elimination of circumstances we did not create. We pave a path and imagine the surrounding ground must also be paved. We make a tool and suppose every available task should be performed with it. We invent an artificial day and then wonder why the actual night is worth keeping.

The better version of progress can be more discriminating. It knows what to improve and what to leave available.

The night existed before our cities, our institutions, and our instruments. It is not necessarily benevolent. It can conceal dangers, and we have good reasons to bring light into it. But it contains an immense amount of activity that does not belong to us. It provides signals used by living things, a rhythm that our own bodies respond to, and a view of a universe larger than any human work.

It deserves more than to be treated as an unused portion of the day.

We do not need to return to a world without lamps. We need to learn that a lamp can be useful without becoming sovereign over everything its light can reach.

When a light has served its purpose, there is a perfectly respectable next step.

Turn it off.

## Source notes and distinctions (not spoken)

This is an original argumentative essay, not a consensus statement on how every place should be lit. Its normative position—that naturally dark conditions deserve explicit consideration alongside safety, accessibility, culture, and engineering goals—is a proposed position, not a position attributed to the research authors or to OpenAI. The initial floodlight and closing path scenes are constructed examples, not field observations or personal memories. Quantitative findings apply to specified studies and dates rather than universal physical constants.

Political and economic claims about the value of darkness are the essay's synthesis rather than source-authored conclusions.
