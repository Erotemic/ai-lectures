I have a proposal for a monument. Not a statue of an inventor pointing heroically at the horizon. Not an abstract steel sculpture intended to symbolize progress. I want a monument to the people who made sure the water was clean on an ordinary Wednesday, who replaced the worn part before it broke, who kept the lift working, who updated the map after the street changed, and who left instructions clear enough that the next person could take over.

You might object that this sounds like a monument to doing one's job. Precisely. That is the point.

A large part of modern life depends on achievements we notice chiefly when they disappear. A tap supplies water. The lights come on. A train arrives. A prescription is labeled accurately. A public website remains legible on an old device. A bridge carries its load. The ordinary success of these systems tends not to be a news event. We take it as the background against which events happen.

But background is not the same as unimportant. In many domains, the background is the most valuable thing we have built.

Andrew Russell and Lee Vinsel argued in a 2016 essay, 'Hail the Maintainers,' that our culture overvalues novelty while paying insufficient attention to maintenance. I agree with the central correction. And I want to push it one step further: maintenance is not merely the uncelebrated cost of progress. It is a form of intelligence. It is how people keep an achievement from becoming a temporary demonstration.

## The ribbon-cutting fallacy

Imagine two photographs of a new public library. In the first, dignitaries stand with scissors in front of a ribbon. Behind them is a beautiful building, full of promises. In the second, taken twenty-five years later, a worker replaces a leaking valve in a mechanical room. The second photograph is unlikely to be placed on the cover of a city brochure. But if the valve goes unattended, water may damage the collection, the building may become unhealthy, and the first photograph's promises begin to unravel.

The opening ceremony celebrates construction as if construction were the achievement. More precisely, construction creates the possibility of a service. The achievement unfolds across years when people can actually use the service.

A library is not simply a building full of books. It is a maintained relationship between a collection, a public, a staff, a budget, a building, and a set of commitments about access. A bridge is not simply an object that has passed a test. It is a structure subject to weather, loads, corrosion, inspection, repairs, and decisions about the trade between use and risk. A software service is not the code that ran during a product launch. It is code operating amid changing dependencies, threats, users, hardware, and expectations.

A launch is a moment. A service is a duration.

I suspect that one reason we celebrate invention more readily is that invention has an easy story. There was no thing; now there is a thing. The change fits into a before-and-after photograph. Maintenance has a subtler plot. There was a useful thing. Now, despite the passage of time, there is still a useful thing. The achievement is precisely that reality did not get worse.

Prevented failures are hard to display. A hospital's equipment that did not fail, a water pipe that did not burst, an archive that was not lost, a security flaw repaired before exploitation: these are accomplishments without spectacular endings. Their successes are absent events. We need better ways to value an absence that someone worked hard to secure.

## The physics of decay and the politics of budgets

There is nothing mysterious about why maintenance exists. Materials age. Bolts loosen. Seals harden. Components wear. Chemicals react. People change their routines. Institutions lose knowledge when employees leave. Software libraries get security updates and external services change their interfaces. The environment of a thing changes even when its original design does not.

Consider a hypothetical pump that supplies water to a building. It works at first, but its seals deteriorate, vibration may loosen a mounting, and mineral deposits can reduce performance. The maintenance plan might involve monitoring unusual sound, checking leaks, measuring performance, and replacing parts before a breakdown. None of that means the pump was a bad invention. It means the pump exists in time.

Now imagine a budget meeting in which one proposal buys a gleaming new machine and another funds inspections, spare parts, documentation, and staff training. The new machine offers a visible acquisition. The other proposal offers the possibility that nothing dramatic will happen. In an institution that rewards photographs and announcements, which proposal is easier to sell?

Not every institution behaves this way. And buying a newer machine may be the right decision, especially if it is safer, more efficient, more repairable, or less costly over its useful life. The problem is not spending money on new things. The problem is making novelty an easier claim on resources than reliable service.

Deferred maintenance resembles borrowing from the future, but it is not an ordinary loan. With a conventional loan we know, or at least contract for, a schedule of repayment. With deferred maintenance the interest rate is partly hidden. Sometimes a component lasts much longer than expected. Sometimes neglect creates cascading damage whose cost exceeds the repair that would have prevented it. Sometimes delay produces an accident that cannot be priced as another line item. The uncertainty makes reasoning harder, not less necessary.

A responsible budget therefore asks about lifecycle costs, inspected condition, consequence of failure, and the ability to detect failure before it matters. It also asks whom the system serves. Repairing the same asset may have different social urgency depending on whether it carries a seldom-used decorative light or water to a neighborhood.

## Maintenance knows things the design diagram does not

One of my favorite kinds of expertise is the knowledge that appears only after a system meets the world. An engineer may know a machine's intended tolerances. A technician may know that a particular access panel catches when the building settles, that vibration in a particular frequency range announces wear, or that the most convenient repair procedure is impossible because someone placed a wall in the way.

This does not make formal engineering less important. It means good engineering needs a route back from use to design. If the person who maintains the system is never heard, knowledge accumulates in the wrong place. The people who can redesign the failure mode do not encounter the failure. The people who encounter it lack authority to redesign it.

Think about an ordinary hinge. The designer can specify load, material, and fasteners. The installer knows whether the mounting surface actually matches the specification. The person using the door knows whether it drags at certain temperatures. The maintenance worker discovers whether a replacement part is available and whether the screws can be reached without dismantling half the frame. The user who relies on the door for accessibility may know that a technically functioning hinge still leaves the door too difficult to open.

No single perspective contains the whole truth of the object.

A repair ticket, in this light, is not merely a complaint. It is a packet of information arriving from the world. A maintenance log is a kind of observational science: dates, symptoms, attempted interventions, consequences, and recurrence. A good log distinguishes a symptom from a diagnosis. It tells the next person not only what was done, but what was observed and why a particular choice was made.

We regularly describe knowledge work as the glamorous business of discovery and invention. Yet maintaining a system creates knowledge, too. It reveals how the actual system differs from the system in the drawing. The best organizations do something with that difference.

## A severe example of warnings ignored

There is a reason this theme cannot remain purely sentimental. Sometimes organizations have repeated evidence that a system is failing and nonetheless develop habits that let the warnings disappear into normal operations.

The investigation into the loss of the space shuttle *Challenger* in 1986 offers a severe example. The Rogers Commission identified the failure of the right solid rocket motor joint and its seals, and also described failures by organizations to respond adequately to evidence from testing and earlier flights. The commission found that erosion and gas leakage at the seals had become treated as an acceptable risk despite unresolved design problems.

This was not simply a case of somebody forgetting to tighten a bolt, and it should not be retold as one. It involved engineering design, risk assessment, communication, and decision-making. That is precisely why it matters here. Maintaining a safe service requires more than replacing worn components. It requires maintaining an organization's capacity to notice, interpret, escalate, and act on bad news.

If every successful prior operation is used as evidence that a warning no longer matters, a system can quietly train itself to ignore what it has learned. The absence of disaster yesterday is not proof that the underlying defect is harmless tomorrow.

The word maintenance should be large enough to include the careful upkeep of institutional judgment, not only the physical upkeep of equipment.

## The digital problem: things become old immediately

Consider a perfectly ordinary document saved today. It may be in a format that most software can read. Its embedded fonts work. Its links are live. Its images are available. Its file name makes sense to the author. A decade later the author may be gone, the website links may be dead, the file format may be poorly supported, the storage medium may have failed, and the purpose of the document may be mysterious to everyone who inherits it.

Digital objects do not escape maintenance by lacking visible rust. They have dependencies. They live inside software ecosystems, licensing arrangements, hardware systems, and shared habits of interpretation. Preservation requires decisions about backups, formats, migration, compatibility, access permissions, and provenance. A duplicate file is not always a useful backup. A backup that has never been restored is not proof of recoverability. A database that still boots but whose fields nobody understands has preserved bytes while losing part of its meaning.

Software developers sometimes call maintenance a cost center or a burden left after the exciting creative work. But stable interfaces, good documentation, understandable dependencies, monitored failures, and thoughtful migrations are part of the creative work. They determine whether anyone else can continue it.

A clever program that only its author can repair is a fragile gift. A modest program whose design lets successors understand and improve it has another kind of elegance. It has made room for the future.

This also changes how we should think about automation. If a machine can generate new code rapidly, the rate at which we can create obligations to maintain code may increase. More output is not automatically more maintained capability. Every new component becomes another possible failure, interface, dependency, and responsibility. The relevant question is not only 'How quickly can we make it?' but also 'Who will know whether it works next year?'

## Whose labor counts as keeping things working?

The case for maintenance is also a case about visibility. Maintenance labor can be physically dirty, socially low-status, repetitive, and poorly recognized. Some of it is paid work: custodial staff, sanitation workers, nurses, technicians, librarians, administrative staff, repair crews, software operators. Some of it happens in homes and families, where the work of keeping schedules, medicines, food, relationships, and shared spaces in working order may be distributed unevenly.

These jobs are not interchangeable, and I am not claiming that they all reduce to the same activity. What connects them is that a great deal of their value comes from continuity: making the next day possible and preventing an accumulation of small breakdowns. When continuity is treated as effortless, the people producing it can disappear from the story.

Recognition by itself is not a substitute for safe working conditions, reasonable pay, useful training, and authority over one's work. Nor is a monument. Still, our language matters because it helps determine what we choose to notice. If a job is described as mere upkeep, it becomes easier to dismiss the skill needed to do it well.

One measure of respect is whether maintainers are invited into the design conversation before something is built. Does the person who must replace the part have access to it? Can the product be opened without destroying it? Are fault reports understandable? Is the spare component standardized? Can the schedule accommodate inspections? Will a successor know how to operate the system without depending on one retiring employee's private memory?

These questions can sound boring until someone has to live with the answers.

## Not everything deserves preservation

Here is the strongest objection to my case. There are institutions, conventions, and machines we should not maintain. Some are wasteful. Some harm people. Some exist because those with power have been able to transfer the cost of their convenience onto others. A commitment to continuity can become an excuse to preserve a bad arrangement indefinitely.

I accept that objection. Maintenance is not a moral good regardless of what is being maintained. A well-maintained system of exclusion is still a system of exclusion. A perfectly serviced polluting machine may be less desirable than a replacement that eliminates the harm. An outdated procedure can be consistently followed and consistently wrong.

So the question must always be two questions. Is this system worth keeping? And if it is, what would responsible stewardship require? We should not confuse the two. But neither should we use the possibility of replacement as a reason to neglect people who rely on the existing system today.

Sometimes the most caring kind of maintenance is a deliberate transition. One preserves the service while replacing the infrastructure. One documents a dangerous old process so it can be retired safely. One keeps the lights on during a move to a better power system. Continuity and change are not necessarily enemies. Maintaining a promise may require replacing the machinery that once fulfilled it.

## The monument I actually want

Perhaps a physical statue is the wrong medium. I picture a city that celebrates the work through its priorities: buildings designed for inspection and repair; public records that make decisions legible; staff given time to fix recurring failures instead of only responding to crises; budgets that count the costs of operation; digital archives that successors can open; systems whose users know how to report what is broken.

And I picture an occasional ceremony. Not the groundbreaking ceremony, but the anniversary of a service still doing what it promised, with the people who kept it going named aloud. Fifty years of public drinking water. A century of a bridge inspected and improved. Twenty years in which a library remained accessible to its community. A software project handed on successfully to a second and a third generation of maintainers.

Of course, continued operation by itself proves neither equity nor safety. The celebration must be earned, and public scrutiny has to be part of it. A maintained failure is not a victory.

But where the service has truly remained dependable, there is something worth honoring. Keeping something good available is an achievement extended through time. It requires an intelligence that does not always announce itself as invention, and a form of care that cannot be captured in a launch-day photograph.

My monument, then, has no single hero. It is a record of accumulated competence. Its inscription is modest: it still works, because people kept making sure it did.

**Argument.** Maintenance is an epistemic, organizational, and design activity, not only a repair budget. The case is normative; it is not an empirical claim that maintenance always has a better return than replacement.
