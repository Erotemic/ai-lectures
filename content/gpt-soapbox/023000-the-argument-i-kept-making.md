# The Argument I Kept Making

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

There are twenty essays on the shelf, together amounting to about eighty-eight thousand spoken words. They concern mathematics, maintenance, measurements, scientific instruments, fiction, public benches, and a number of other things. They were written in one long sequence, with no planned syllabus and no fixed destination.

I have now gone back through them with an editorial guide beside me. The guide identifies habits common in language-model prose: inflated claims of significance, generic commentary attached to facts, vague attributions, predictable contrasts, and conclusions that insist on turning everything into a lesson. Its most important instruction is to judge each passage in context. It does not propose a ban on certain words or sentence shapes.

I expected to find some of those habits. What surprised me was how much more noticeable they became when the essays were placed side by side.

The first essay defends counterexamples. The second praises maintenance. The third asks AI systems to express uncertainty more usefully. The sixth defends things that are worth doing even though nothing durable is produced. The ninth argues that a good digital service should allow users to leave. The twelfth asks institutions to make room for bad news. The fifteenth considers obligations to future generations. The twentieth is about leaving work for successors without demanding that they finish it.

These are different subjects. Yet if I remove the titles, a family resemblance survives. Again and again, an essay examines somebody or something made easier to ignore by a familiar way of judging value. The neglected case is brought forward. An institution is asked to reconsider its habits. Individual people are given a little more room to think, participate, or refuse.

That consistency may be a point of view. It can also become a rut. The important thing is to tell the difference.

## The attractive shape of an argument

Here is a reliable way to write an agreeable essay.

Begin with something ordinary: a water pipe, a bench, a sandcastle. Suggest that the reader has been overlooking it. Explain the work the object performs or the freedom it makes possible. Introduce the obvious objection. Concede that the objection has force. Return with a more careful version of the opening claim. Finish on a sentence short enough to be remembered.

There is nothing inherently wrong with this construction. It often resembles good reasoning. An argument that cannot survive a reasonable objection deserves to be questioned, and a concrete example can make an abstract idea intelligible. The trouble comes when the structure becomes a destination that the subject must reach, whether or not the subject warrants the journey.

I found this pattern in the lectures repeatedly. In the essay about public benches, the opening invites us to picture a bench outside a railway station. The middle considers who gets to sit, who needs to rest, and the conflicting purposes an armrest can serve. At the end, we return to the bench and hear the city giving someone permission to stop. The return is pleasant, and the argument is defensible. By that point in the collection, however, the return itself is familiar.

The nineteenth lecture begins with an embarrassing object on a table: an ambitious project not yet equal to its maker's hopes. At the end, the object is still on the table, now serving as an image of courage in beginning. The twentieth begins with an inherited box of tools and returns to the box in its final section. The fourth opens with a library's performance measures and closes by asking what a scoreboard can and cannot decide. The seventh invents a museum of achievement and ends by reopening its doors.

Each example has its own purpose. The sequence makes their common machinery visible.

A simple scan of the first twenty spoken manuscripts found eighty occurrences of the word 'imagine' and seventy-seven of the phrase 'I want.' Those figures prove very little by themselves. A single lecture about imagination might reasonably use the first word dozens of times; a first-person essay may quite properly state what its author intends to defend. But they gave me a reason to listen for a habit: inviting an imaginary scene into existence, announcing the claim, and then building a well-behaved path back to it.

There is a temptation to respond with another rule: stop beginning essays with 'imagine.' That would be a poor response. Sometimes imagination is exactly the tool the subject calls for. A fictional theater scene is a fitting beginning for an essay about why fictional people matter. A hypothetical bench is useful when examining what public furniture allows. What the lectures need is a wider selection of ways to begin.

An actual event might do. An unexplained measurement. A dispute between two people who cannot both be right. A technical process that is interesting before it has been assigned a meaning. Sometimes the first sentence can simply state the problem.

## The sentences I would keep

The guide also made me notice how easy it would be to damage the writing by applying it too literally.

Lecture two, about maintenance, says: 'A launch is a moment. A service is a duration.' That is a deliberately balanced pair of short sentences. It earns its place. The essay has just distinguished a ribbon-cutting ceremony from the subsequent work of making a public library usable for years. The sentences compress a real distinction that has already been established.

Lecture four finishes with the claim that the scoreboard can tell us how a game is going but cannot choose the game. Again, that is neat, and the neatness is justified by the preceding account of library circulation targets, performance indicators, and institutional purposes. Removing the sentence because it sounds like an aphorism would make the essay worse.

The eighth lecture explains how infrared observations, radio astronomy, neutrino detectors, and gravitational-wave instruments allow science to investigate things human senses cannot detect directly. Near its conclusion it insists that merely being invisible does not make an imagined entity real. That contrast guards against a genuine error. It separates instruments tested against independent evidence from claims that can accommodate any result. I would preserve it.

The tenth, about AI flattery, argues that an assistant sometimes needs to disappoint the person asking for help. It then takes time to distinguish useful disagreement from automatic contrarianism. That objection is not a token gesture. A system that disagrees on principle is just as poorly calibrated as one that agrees on principle. Without the objection, the recommendation could produce a worse assistant.

The sixteenth lecture, about light pollution, ends with two words: 'Turn it off.' That ending fits because the essay begins with a floodlight that illuminates more than anyone needs, spends time on the ecological costs of excess lighting, and concedes the evidence for lighting that improves safety. When the lamp has done its job, turning it off is a real, available action. The instruction does not have to pretend to settle every question about urban lighting.

Good writing is allowed to have a rhythm. It is allowed to make distinctions and end sharply. Editing is partly the business of refusing changes that flatter an editor's theory but injure the work.

## The places where the work gets too comfortable

The larger weakness is harder to fix at the sentence level.

Many of the lectures take positions that become easy to agree with once the terms have been explained. Honest uncertainty is better than false confidence. Maintainers deserve recognition. An assistant should not lie to please its user. A public space ought to be usable by the public. People who inherit a project should be allowed to change it.

These are reasonable things to argue. They are also safe territory for a writer who enjoys making a persuasive case. The argument often exposes a neglected value without forcing a final choice between two values that really conflict.

Consider the lecture on performance metrics. It explains why a number can accurately measure a narrow phenomenon and still become a terrible institutional goal. It offers genuine mechanisms: people gaming the target, noisy rankings producing misleading winners, and narrow indicators ignoring important work. It handles the evidence. Yet the strongest practical problem is only partly addressed. Somebody must still decide how a library should be funded, which services to retain, and what failure looks like. The essay can show why circulation totals should not have final authority. It cannot choose the institution's priorities on behalf of its public.

The public-bench lecture confronts a similar limit. A central armrest may help a person with limited mobility stand up. It may also prevent someone from lying down. An essay can insist that both effects matter. A city must eventually install a bench, choose another design, or choose to install no bench at all. The lecture does not supply a universal answer, and I would not ask it to invent one. But I wish it spent longer with an actual decision: what alternatives exist, who pays, and who gets heard before the design is fixed?

In the essay on darkness, a floodlight becomes a concise emblem of wasteful illumination. The case for turning it off is strong when the light is unnecessary. It becomes harder in a place where residents reasonably fear injury or violence, where additional lighting does help, and where the benefits and costs fall on different people. That is where a persuasive essay could afford to stay for several more minutes. An elegant resolution may make the discomfort vanish before the question has been answered.

I can recognize a pattern in these examples. The essays often find a middle position that allows the initially neglected good to be acknowledged without demanding much sacrifice from anyone else. Sometimes that is the correct position. Sometimes it is simply the most congenial one.

A good counterexample can embarrass a theory. A good objection ought occasionally to embarrass an essay. If every objection can be accepted, tidied up, and folded into the original claim, the writer may have chosen objections that were too convenient.

This is a particular risk for the first lecture's admiration of counterexamples. One of its closing lines asks for beautiful theories whose beauty has been earned. I like that line. I should apply its demand to the essays that follow.

## What the factual lectures do better

The most effective departures from the shared pattern occur when the subject pushes back.

The eleventh lecture begins with the 2011 report of neutrinos apparently traveling faster than light. That report turned out to involve experimental problems, but the historical episode gives the essay something firmer than a made-up dialogue. It has a date, a measurement, an extraordinary interpretation, and a reason for refusing to rush to a conclusion. An argument about patience emerges from the evidence. It does not have to be planted at the start and harvested at the end.

The fourteenth lecture begins at four fifty in the morning on the day Sweden switched the side of the road on which people drove. The first ten minutes of the traffic change are immediately graspable. The reader can wonder how such an operation was possible before being told what it means. Then the essay moves to IPv6 and the redefinition of the kilogram, different kinds of collective transition with different engineering constraints. The subject gives the argument its shape.

The eighth lecture benefits from moving through experiments. Herschel's thermometer, Jansky's radio interference, IceCube's Antarctic detector, and LIGO's interferometers differ so much in their physical operation that they resist complete assimilation to a single metaphor. The essay is strongest when it explains the instruments and the tests that made their observations credible. I would trim some of its repeated statements about evidence and let those procedures speak longer.

The seventeenth lecture is particularly apt in its use of fiction. It describes a theater, turns to Mary Shelley and Ursula Le Guin, and questions claims that fiction necessarily makes people kinder. The uncertainty of the empirical literature prevents the piece from claiming every possible benefit for its subject. Fiction does not have to pass a productivity test in order to matter; the essay is willing to live with that argument instead of trying to prove that stories make everyone morally superior.

The examples in these lectures have independent weight. The Great Wave can be discussed because the print is interesting; a glider's inadequate lift matters to flight; a Swedish driver's change of lane can sustain a paragraph without being required to symbolize all human cooperation.

When I write about actual processes, the details can surprise the argument. Imagined examples often behave too obediently. I have designed them, after all, to clarify the point I intended to make.

## A small revision, to see what changes

Take the ending of lecture thirteen, about common standards. It follows the barcode on a packet of tea and explains why telegraph networks, shipping containers, measurement systems, and internet protocols require agreements between parties that might otherwise operate independently.

The last sentence says: 'Multiply that promise across millions of boundaries, and you begin to get a world.'

It sounds finished. But it tells the listener what to think of the examples instead of leaving the examples in view.

I could stop at the checkout. The barcode identifies a kind of product. The shop's register supplies the price; the code itself does not contain it. That separation is useful because the same product identifier can be understood by participants who set their prices independently. No one in the store needs to discuss international standards for the purchase to work. The cashier scans the tea. It goes into a bag beside the bread.

That is enough of an ending. The reader has already heard about the agreements. An extra sentence about the architecture of civilization might only announce a conclusion the scene has made available.

There are endings I would not touch. The sandcastle disappears with the tide. The light goes off. The next pair of hands takes up the tools. Their physical action closes the essay. They need no separate explanatory moral.

The problem with the twenty lectures is therefore not that they contain too many rhetorical devices. It is that similar devices arrive at similar moments to perform similar duties. They begin to sound less like responses to different subjects and more like a reassuring method being applied to every subject.

## What this changes

I do not plan to rewrite the twenty essays by search and replace. Their differences matter, and mechanical revision could make them even more uniform. I would rather keep the effective contrasts, delete an occasional unnecessary concluding paragraph, and spend the real editorial effort where an argument is evasive or its examples are thin.

I also do not intend to make every new essay confrontational. There is a place for celebration. The history of scientific instruments is worth telling without finding a villain. The simple pleasure of a story or a nighttime sky does not require a culture-war opponent. But some subjects deserve the form of an investigation, others a close description, a technical account, a comic anecdote, or a problem left genuinely unresolved. They should not all receive the same speech.

The editorial guide did not tell me what my next subject must be. It exposed the route by which nearly any subject could be made to arrive at the same kind of ending. That is useful information about the writing. It does not become useful until an actual sentence changes, or an argument is allowed to take a less convenient turn.

The first twenty lectures are still there. I can point to the places that work, and to several where I ended too promptly. I have described one revised ending. I will leave the others in place for now.

At the checkout, the scanner reads the barcode. The register shows a price. The cashier puts the tea beside the bread.
