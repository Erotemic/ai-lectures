# The Courage to Begin Something That May Fail

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

There is a moment near the beginning of almost every ambitious project when the object on the table looks rather embarrassing.

The novel is three pages of sentences that do not yet know what kind of novel they belong to. The computer program works on one machine, with a collection of instructions that its author is already afraid to touch. The garden is a patch of soil, an optimistic diagram, and several packets of seeds. The scientific theory is a conjecture with a few encouraging examples and an alarming number of things not yet checked.

From the outside, these projects can look less like the beginning of achievements than like evidence of poor judgment. They are incomplete, awkward, and vulnerable to a very reasonable question: what if this doesn't work?

I would like to offer an answer that is neither motivational theater nor a shrug.

Of course it may not work. That is part of what we need to understand before beginning. We should ask how badly it could fail, whose lives would be affected, what we can learn, and what we ought to do if the evidence turns against us. Sometimes the responsible answer is not to begin. But there are also endeavors whose value can never be established in advance, because establishing it is precisely what the endeavor would accomplish.

If we demand a guarantee of success before making the first attempt, we have quietly forbidden a great deal of discovery.

This lecture is about the courage to begin something that may fail. It is also about the engineering required to make that courage responsible, the injustice of asking everyone to take the same risks, and the surprising possibility that knowing when to stop can be part of the courage to begin.

## The cruel grammar of hindsight

We possess a peculiar talent for turning outcomes into explanations of decisions.

A person tries something risky and succeeds. We say they had vision. Another makes a similar decision, with similar information, and fails. We say they were foolish. The difference between the two judgments may be no more than which way an uncertain event happened to fall.

Imagine two people who each make a carefully researched attempt to open a neighborhood bookshop. They compare rents, study foot traffic, consult experienced booksellers, limit their borrowing, and decide that the opportunity is worth a try. One shop becomes a beloved institution. The other closes after a year because an important source of customers disappears. We can learn from both histories. But it does not follow that the first decision was wise and the second was stupid. Some of the relevant facts were not available when the decisions were made.

That sounds elementary. In practice, it is difficult to remember. Once we know the ending, we can assemble a coherent narrative in which the ending appears almost inevitable. We can identify early signs, give them dramatic weight, and omit all the other signs that pointed elsewhere. A biography of a celebrated inventor can become a procession of clues announcing that greatness was coming. A story about a failed venture can turn the same ordinary uncertainties into warnings that should have been obvious.

We ought to resist both forms of retrospective certainty.

Decision theory gives us a useful vocabulary. We can distinguish a choice made under uncertainty from the outcome that later occurred. We can ask what alternatives were available, how severe the possible harms were, what evidence a person could reasonably have obtained, and what they were trying to accomplish. An outcome still matters enormously. It determines who was helped or harmed, and who is owed repair. But an outcome alone is not a complete audit of the decision that preceded it.

This distinction is especially important when judging people whose projects fail. Failure can reveal a bad plan. It can also reveal bad luck, a previously unknown constraint, a change in circumstances, or a worthy question whose answer happened to be no. Those cases should not all receive the same verdict.

The reverse is equally important. Success does not wash away recklessness. A dangerous shortcut that happens not to injure anybody remains dangerous. A project that makes money after hiding its risks from others does not become ethically sound because its investors are pleased.

We need better questions than: did it work?

We should ask: did it make sense to try? Was the trial designed responsibly? Did the people involved know what they were risking? And when the evidence arrived, did anyone change their mind?

## A glider that would not lift

In the summer of nineteen hundred and one, the Wright brothers went to the dunes of North Carolina with a glider they expected to perform better than their previous machine. They had enlarged its wings and adjusted their design in response to disappointing results in nineteen hundred.

The new glider did not reward their confidence. Its lift was far below prediction, and its handling introduced new difficulties. The Smithsonian's historical account describes the nineteen hundred and one glider as the most troublesome of the three gliders that preceded their powered airplane. The lift it produced was approximately one third of what their calculations had predicted.

There are several stories one could tell at this point. The familiar heroic version says that two determined brothers refused to give up. That is true as far as it goes, but persistence is the least interesting part of the episode.

The more interesting thing is what they refused to persist in.

They did not continue treating their inherited aerodynamic numbers as unquestionable. They had built real equipment, tested it in real wind, and observed a stubborn disagreement between their calculations and the world. The world would not negotiate.

So they began investigating their data.

They experimented with a bicycle-based test device and then constructed a small wind tunnel. They compared wing shapes, measured lift and drag, and assembled information more suitable for the machines they wanted to build. Their nineteen hundred and two glider performed substantially better. They also refined its control system, including the rudder. By December nineteen hundred and three, the brothers had achieved controlled powered flights.

The first powered flight lasted twelve seconds. It did not carry anyone across an ocean or solve the commercial problem of air travel. It was a particular, limited demonstration of a capability the brothers had been working toward for years. The Smithsonian rightly emphasizes the engineering methods developed on the way, not merely the duration of the flight.

I find the wind tunnel more instructive than a slogan about perseverance.

The brothers had something they wanted badly to be true: that their machine could fly. But wanting it did not authorize them to ignore its actual performance. They created a way for their theory to be contradicted on a smaller scale, in a form they could measure and use.

Their achievement depended upon distinguishing a failed test from a failed project, and a failed assumption from a failed ambition.

That distinction is not always available. Sometimes an experiment reveals that the central idea is impossible or that the costs are too high. But the Wright brothers' case shows what an intelligent response to an obstacle can look like. Instead of responding only with confidence or despair, they asked which specific part of their understanding had failed.

A wind tunnel is, among other things, a device for turning a grand question into several smaller questions that can be answered before someone climbs onto the machine.

That is courage with instruments attached.

## The difference between trying and gambling

Let me defend an unfashionable distinction. An experiment is not the same thing as a gamble, even though both involve uncertainty.

A gamble can be nothing more than exposure to an outcome. An experiment is designed to change what we know. Of course the categories overlap. We can gamble while experimenting, and some experiments are poorly constructed. But if a project fails, we should ask whether its failure has left us with information that was not available before.

Suppose you want to discover whether a new approach to teaching geometry is effective. You could develop a semester-long program, roll it out everywhere, and wait for the results. Or you could begin with a small, ethically reviewed study, decide what meaningful success would look like, keep track of what students actually learn, and compare the new method with a reasonable alternative. The second approach does not guarantee a favorable answer. It does make an unfavorable answer more interpretable.

The precise design would depend on the students, the schools, and the question. My point is not that every problem has to become a randomized trial. It is that an honest attempt should make room for the possibility that the attempt is mistaken.

Sometimes people tell each other to fail quickly. I understand the appeal. If a flaw can be found with a paper model, do not discover it after pouring the concrete. If a premise can be tested by talking with the people a product is supposed to help, do not first build an elaborate product that solves the wrong problem.

But speed is not the highest good. A medical device, a bridge, and a children's toy do not acquire the same moral permission to fail because someone has adopted an energetic slogan. The correct rate of experimentation depends upon the stakes, and upon who bears them.

I would replace the slogan with something less glamorous: learn before the cost of learning becomes unacceptable.

This requires thinking about reversibility. A sketch can be erased. A local software prototype can be discarded. A short-run exhibition can close. A poorly designed dam cannot be made harmless by an encouraging retrospective about what the engineers learned.

If a failure would be irreversible, or would impose severe harm on people who did not consent to it, the threshold of justification should be much higher. The more costly the failure, the stronger the case for simulation, independent review, safety margins, and careful limits on exposure. These protections are not signs that the project lacks courage. They are signs that its authors understand what they have undertaken.

Sometimes bravery is not the dramatic act of committing everything. Sometimes it is the less photogenic decision to build a safe test stand.

## The trouble with a beautiful success story

Here is another problem. We can learn a misleading lesson from studying only the projects that survive.

Consider a shelf of biographies. The people on it are famous because something about their work eventually succeeded, or at least became historically important. We can examine their habits and discover that many were determined, ambitious, patient, and confident. Then we can conclude that determination and confidence produce success.

But where are the biographies of the equally determined people whose work did not succeed? What about those who were confident for the wrong reasons? How many stubborn projects continued long after their evidence had turned against them? If we do not examine them, we have no basis for distinguishing a useful trait from a common trait among people who happen to be visible.

This is not an argument against biographies. I like biographies. It is an argument against treating the winners as an adequate sample of everyone who tried.

In science, the missing failures can become a direct threat to knowledge. Researchers sometimes investigate a proposed effect and find nothing persuasive. If those studies are less likely to be written up, submitted, or published, the literature may accumulate a biased picture of reality. A study of peer-reviewed survey experiments published in Science in twenty fourteen found substantial differences between the treatment of strong findings and null results. Projects producing strong findings were much more likely to be published, and often more likely to be written up at all.

The point is not that every null result proves an effect is absent. It does not. A poorly designed experiment, a small sample, or a measurement with little sensitivity may fail to detect a real effect. But a careful null result is information. It should not disappear simply because it makes a less exciting headline.

If we celebrate failure as a source of learning while making failed trials invisible, we are praising a lesson and throwing away the notebook.

The same problem appears outside science. Organizations can preserve only the stories of successful launches. Artists can discuss only published work. Investors can discover a heroic narrative in each successful company and leave the dead companies outside the room. Then a new generation is offered a portrait of achievement with most of the attempts removed.

What would we think of a weather forecast assembled only from sunny days?

A culture capable of taking intelligent risks needs a memory that includes the projects that did not work. Not every abandoned draft deserves a monument. But the reasons some attempts failed are part of the evidence future people will need.

## The telescope with the wrong mirror

In April nineteen ninety, the Hubble Space Telescope went into orbit. It had taken years of design, manufacturing, testing, and coordination to put a sophisticated observatory above the atmosphere. Astronomers expected it to open a new window on the universe.

The early images were blurred. Investigators found that the telescope's primary mirror had been manufactured with a small but consequential error in its shape. The problem was spherical aberration, and it had escaped the testing program on the ground. NASA's investigations traced the error to the equipment used to test the mirror, including the way a test component had been assembled.

The error was small in physical dimensions. Its consequences were not. It compromised a major public scientific investment, made the institution a target of justified criticism, and required engineers to solve a difficult problem in orbit.

There is no reason to romanticize the mistake. It was an avoidable defect, not a poetic illustration of the necessity of failure. A better manufacturing and verification process should have prevented it. People responsible for a public mission owed the public an investigation, not a motivational poster.

What happened next matters for a different reason.

Hubble had been designed to be serviced in orbit. Astronauts could replace instruments and perform repairs. That feature was not invented after the mirror problem; it was part of the earlier architecture. When engineers learned the shape and magnitude of the aberration, they could design corrective optics to compensate for it. During a servicing mission in December nineteen ninety-three, astronauts installed an instrument called COSTAR and a new camera with built-in correction. The telescope's performance improved dramatically.

The repair did not turn the original testing error into a good decision. Nor did it mean the whole project should have been abandoned the moment the defect became visible. It illustrates a third possibility: a costly mistake can remain a mistake while a system designed for intervention makes recovery possible.

Notice how much had to be true for that recovery to happen. The telescope had to have physical points of access. Instruments had to be replaceable. The defect had to be characterized accurately enough that compensating optics could be built. Astronauts had to rehearse a technically demanding operation. Ground teams had to coordinate work under constraints that would have been difficult even in an ordinary laboratory.

A recovery story often makes the people who save the day more famous than the architectural decisions that allowed a rescue to occur at all. But design for repair deserves a share of the credit.

This is one of the most useful kinds of foresight: not knowing which thing will go wrong, but recognizing that some things will, and preserving enough options to respond.

I want to make the idea precise. The courage to begin is sometimes purchased by the possibility of correction. When the project is important, valuable, and uncertain, making it repairable is not an afterthought. Repairability can change whether the attempt is responsible in the first place.

And there is a warning here. Not every error can be corrected by a servicing mission. Some harms arrive too quickly, or affect people who cannot be restored by better hardware. The Hubble story should strengthen our appetite for verifiable engineering and recoverable design, not our tolerance for preventable errors.

## A proof that had a hole

A different kind of risk appeared in Cambridge in June nineteen ninety-three, when Andrew Wiles presented what he believed was a proof of Fermat's Last Theorem.

The theorem had been outstanding for centuries. It says that for whole-number exponents greater than two, no three positive integers can satisfy the familiar equation in which one number raised to that exponent plus a second equals a third raised to the same exponent. For exponent two, there are solutions, such as three squared plus four squared equals five squared. For exponent three or four or any larger integer exponent, Fermat's assertion was that there are none.

Wiles had spent years developing a path to the result using deep connections between elliptic curves and modular forms. His lectures were a major event in mathematics. But as the argument was prepared for publication, a serious gap emerged. The proof, as it stood, did not establish the theorem.

Mathematics has a particular cruelty in situations like this. A proof is not ninety-nine percent valid because it is ninety-nine percent convincing. The unsupported step might be the very place where the theorem remains out of reach. No amount of applause at the final lecture can repair a logical gap.

Wiles worked to resolve it, with crucial help from Richard Taylor. A new argument was found in nineteen ninety-four, and the proof appeared in the Annals of Mathematics in nineteen ninety-five.

We know the successful ending, which makes the intervening uncertainty easier to bear in retrospect. But the happy ending should not obscure what was at stake while the gap remained. It was possible that the proof would not be repaired by Wiles, or at all by that route. A life of serious mathematical work does not come with a contractual guarantee that its hardest argument can be completed.

There is another way of telling this story that would be equally misleading: that the gap was secretly useful because it led to the final breakthrough. Perhaps resolving the gap did help shape the ultimate argument. But a failure does not owe us a future triumph in order to be real work. Some attempts stop at the gap. Some ideas open another field while leaving their original question unanswered. Some collapse under criticism and are never revived.

The important feature of mathematical practice is that the proof had to survive scrutiny. The community did not need a ceremony in which the first announcement was declared true to reward the effort invested. It needed a valid argument. The researcher had to accept that distinction too.

That is intellectual courage of a particular sort: caring enough about the result to allow someone else to say that the present argument is not sufficient.

And sometimes the correct response is to ask for help.

The myth of solitary genius can make collaboration look like an admission of weakness. In this case, it was part of the route to a proof. We should not make the outcome into a fable in which every sufficiently determined person eventually wins. We can admire the work, the repair, and the willingness to expose an argument to criticism without inventing such a guarantee.

## Beginning something you may never finish

Now consider an enterprise whose success could not have been judged within a year, or five years, or even within the working life of a single editor.

The project that became the Oxford English Dictionary began among nineteenth-century scholars who wanted something more demanding than a list of accepted definitions. They wanted a historical account of English words, tracking changes in meaning through evidence from texts. That required finding words in books, copying dated examples of their use, comparing sources, and deciding how to represent a language that would not stop changing while the dictionary was being written.

The first printed installment appeared in eighteen eighty-four. The first edition was not completed until nineteen twenty-eight. James Murray, appointed chief editor in eighteen seventy-nine, died in nineteen fifteen, thirteen years before completion. The work depended on many other editors and a large network of volunteer readers who supplied quotations.

A dictionary sounds like something that should be complete by its nature. But a historical dictionary reveals how difficult completeness is. New evidence changes the history of a word. Usage changes. Editorial standards evolve. Different contributors interpret the material differently. A publisher wants the book finished; an editor sees another entry that can be improved.

The project could have exhausted its sponsors and collapsed. It could have been abandoned partway through the alphabet. It could have produced an impressive series of installments without ever becoming the finished work that its organizers imagined.

One answer to that risk was not to wait until every word had been researched before printing anything. The dictionary appeared in installments, making work available while the greater enterprise continued. A second answer was organizational: reading, editing, checking, and publishing could not remain the responsibility of one irreplaceable person. The project needed records, practices, contributors, and institutions capable of surviving changes in leadership.

In retrospect, we can describe the dictionary as an achievement. Its history also lets us see how people begin work they cannot personally guarantee will reach its intended destination.

This is a form of courage that is sometimes mistaken for optimism. Optimism says it will work out. A more practical commitment says: the goal seems worth serving; here is a first part we can do well; here is how the work can continue if we cannot.

I like this because it resists the fantasy that a project becomes meaningful only when a final ribbon is cut. The earliest installments were real scholarly work. The volunteers' quotations were real evidence. The editors who never saw completion had not therefore spent their lives on a failed undertaking.

The long project forces us to separate the value of work already done from confidence about the final outcome. It also forces us to ask who can inherit the work, and whether that inheritance is possible without the original author standing beside it.

## Who can afford the risk?

There is an ugly way to misuse everything I have said so far.

We can take a handful of celebrated achievements, praise their creators for taking risks, and then scold ordinary people for refusing to gamble their stability on uncertain projects. We can demand that a worker become entrepreneurial without asking whether they can survive a missed paycheck. We can tell a student that failure is a gift while charging them dearly for the experience. We can celebrate a founder's fearlessness while the workers, suppliers, or public quietly absorb the losses.

The ability to recover from failure is not evenly distributed.

One person can lose a year to a speculative project and still pay rent. Another may be placed in a desperate position by a single month without income. One research group can afford to explore several ideas because it has long-term support. Another may have to produce an immediately publishable result to keep its members employed. A person with an established reputation may be permitted a public mistake that destroys the prospects of someone just entering the field.

It is therefore inadequate to exhort everyone to take more risks. A more serious question is how to make worthwhile attempts possible without requiring personal ruin as the entrance fee.

That could mean reliable funding for exploratory work, access to shared equipment, scholarships, time away from paid labor, public libraries, affordable studios, or institutions that evaluate proposals without demanding certainty about their outcomes. It could mean giving people a way to return from an unsuccessful attempt rather than making one failure permanent evidence of character. The right arrangement will differ from one field to another. These are examples of possible institutional choices, not a universal program.

It also matters who gets to consent. If you decide to spend your own weekend building a peculiar musical instrument, you have considerable freedom to fail. If you decide to release an untested medical device, the affected patients cannot be treated as props in your heroic account of innovation. Responsibility grows when the risk is transferred to others.

Even when everyone consents, the costs may not be negligible. A risky business can be exciting and still leave employees exhausted. An ambitious expedition can produce knowledge and still impose danger on local people who did not choose its aims. Celebrating exploration should not require ignoring those who paid for it.

The right question is not how to eliminate risk from society. We cannot. Refusing to develop a new medicine has consequences, just as an irresponsible trial does. The question is which risks deserve to be taken, by whom, under what safeguards, and with what obligations if something goes wrong.

Courage without that accounting can become somebody else's bill.

## The courage to stop

I promised a complication. Here it is: sometimes the brave thing is to abandon the project.

Once people have spent enough time trying to make something work, the project can acquire a hold over them that has little to do with its original purpose. The more they have sacrificed, the harder it can become to contemplate stopping. Ending the work can feel like admitting that the sacrifice was for nothing.

But the cost already paid does not make the next cost worthwhile. A prototype that failed yesterday is not entitled to another year of labor merely because it failed expensively. A business that cannot meet its obligations does not become viable through admiration for the founder's determination. A theory contradicted by strong evidence does not deserve immunity because it was difficult to invent.

This is the practical force of the sunk-cost problem. What matters for the next decision is the value of the options ahead, not a wish to make past expenses feel justified.

Of course, we cannot always know when to stop. The Wright brothers might have abandoned aviation after their disappointing glider and missed an opportunity. Wiles might have stopped searching for a repair and left the proof unfinished. The first editors of the dictionary might have underestimated how far their successors could carry the work. Persistence sometimes makes possible what a sensible schedule would have ruled out.

Yet those examples do not give us a rule to persist forever. The people who succeeded are not a complete sample of the people who persisted. And the costs of continued work are not the same for everyone.

One useful discipline is to decide, before becoming too attached, what new evidence would justify continuing, changing direction, or stopping. What are we trying to find out? How long can we responsibly pursue it? What obligations must remain protected? What would count as a meaningful improvement rather than merely another month spent working? Who outside the project is allowed to challenge our answers?

A plan like that does not remove emotion from the decision. It creates a little room in which emotion need not decide everything.

Stopping can preserve resources for another attempt. It can make the underlying idea available to people with a better approach. It can prevent a private dream from becoming a public burden. It can also acknowledge a simple fact that ambitious people often resist: there are many worthwhile things to do, and a life has limited time.

A person who closes one project has not lost the right to begin another.

## How to make a beginning

I don't want to conclude by issuing a command to take a leap. That phrase is useful for advertisements for activities the advertiser does not have to perform.

I would rather ask what a responsible beginning actually looks like.

First, try to name the thing you care about without confusing it with the first plan you have devised. The Wright brothers wanted controlled flight, not permanent loyalty to a particular wing curvature. A researcher may care about understanding a phenomenon more than about preserving the first theory proposed to explain it. A writer may care about communicating an idea more than about keeping every sentence in the first draft.

Second, find out what can be learned cheaply and safely. There is no moral advantage in making the first experiment expensive when a smaller test could answer the same question. A conversation, a sketch, a simulation, a small model, a careful literature search, or a limited trial can disclose a serious problem before the project acquires the weight of an institution.

Third, decide what should remain true even if the attempt fails. People should still be paid what they are owed. The safety case should still be defensible. The data should not be disguised. A collaborator should not discover afterward that the risks were different from those agreed upon. A useful record of the attempt should survive if it can be preserved responsibly.

Fourth, invite someone to challenge the essential assumption. Not every critic is right. But a project that can survive only while its author controls all the questions has a weakness more important than bad publicity. It cannot distinguish belief from reality.

Fifth, allow for more than one legitimate ending. You might succeed at the original goal. You might discover that the goal needs revision. You might produce a result that is valuable for a different reason. You might decide that stopping is the most sensible use of what you have learned. And sometimes you may simply fail, without a compensating moral prize. It is better to acknowledge that possibility before beginning than to demand that every disappointment become an inspiring anecdote.

Finally, remember that a first attempt is not a prediction of your final ability. A beginner's work is often a bad indicator of how good they may become with practice. But practice does not guarantee mastery either. What it offers is the possibility of improvement, and the opportunity to discover whether the activity is worth continuing.

This may sound less heroic than the familiar story of the bold innovator who never doubts and never quits. I think it is also more useful. It describes a kind of effort that ordinary people can recognize, evaluate, and sometimes share.

## The work that could not be promised

Let me return to the object on the table.

The first draft is still clumsy. The garden still contains more uncertainty than plants. The machine still makes a noise its inventor cannot explain. A person who has not begun could look at the scene and make a fair prediction: a great deal of the work may fail.

They would be right.

The question is what else may happen if nobody tries.

Some of the things we most value could not have been ordered with a guarantee. A mathematical proof cannot be commissioned as though its truth were a delivery date. A new style of music cannot be specified completely in advance, because if we already knew what it sounded like, it would not be new in the relevant sense. A scientific discovery cannot promise to confirm the hypothesis that motivated the research. A friendship cannot be entered with a warranty that neither person will change.

These are very different kinds of endeavor, and they deserve different judgments about risk. But they share a feature. We sometimes have to act without possessing the knowledge that successful action would supply.

I am not defending ignorance. I am defending the attempt to reduce it. I am not defending reckless perseverance. I am defending the possibility of disciplined commitment under conditions that do not guarantee a reward.

There is, I think, a kind of dignity in making a careful offer to the world: here is something I have tried to understand, build, prove, grow, or express. I have reason to think it may be worthwhile. I have tried to protect the people who may be affected. I have left room for correction. I cannot promise that it will work.

Sometimes the world answers no.

When it does, we should be able to say what was learned, who was harmed, what needs repair, and whether another attempt is justified. Sometimes there is nothing more to do. Sometimes the answer changes the next question.

But there is another tragedy besides failure. It is the shelf of possibilities that nobody tested because an unfinished idea looked too much like an embarrassment, or because a society made the cost of an honest attempt impossible to bear.

I would like us to make more room for careful beginnings. Not because they all contain a secret success. Not because every failure makes us stronger. But because a world in which only guaranteed achievements may be attempted is a world whose most interesting achievements have been forbidden in advance.

The object on the table is not yet what its maker hopes it will be.

That is why the work has begun.

## Companion notes (not spoken)

**Thesis.** Attempts under uncertainty should be judged in part by the information, alternatives, and safeguards available when decisions were made, not simply by hindsight. The best defense of ambitious projects is neither romantic risk-taking nor a demand for guaranteed success: it is disciplined experimentation, honest reporting of negative results, attention to who bears risk, and a willingness to revise or stop.
