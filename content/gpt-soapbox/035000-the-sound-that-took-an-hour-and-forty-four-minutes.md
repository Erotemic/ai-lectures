# The Sound That Took an Hour and Forty-Four Minutes

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

In January 1991, a set of underwater microphones near Ascension Island in the South Atlantic registered a sound that had begun near Heard Island in the southern Indian Ocean. The signal had traveled roughly nine thousand two hundred kilometers through water. Its earliest arrival took about one hour, forty-four minutes, and seventeen seconds.

Those seventeen seconds are not poetic precision. They come from a technical account of the experiment. The researchers were trying to establish whether they could measure the time taken by sound to cross an ocean with sufficient accuracy to detect changes in the ocean itself.

The sound did not come from a whale or a ship's propeller. Scientists aboard a vessel near Heard Island had deliberately transmitted coded, low-frequency signals. The receiving microphones were part of a network spread across several oceans. At Ascension Island, the instruments recorded the signals so clearly that traces of sound continued arriving well after the most direct energy had passed. Researchers reported late reverberation for at least twenty-two minutes after the direct signal ended.

It is difficult to picture a single event lasting so long at the receiving end. A sound does not travel across the ocean as a neat parcel. It takes different routes. Parts of the wave pass through water with different temperatures and pressures. Some bend deeper; others come back upward. They arrive at different times, carrying information about the route they took.

The experiment had a rather straightforward question behind it: could the ocean itself be used as a measuring instrument? To understand why anyone thought this might work, we need to descend about a kilometer beneath the surface, where the water does something counterintuitive.

## A slow place in a fast medium

Sound travels through seawater at roughly fifteen hundred meters per second, more than four times its speed in air. That number varies with temperature, salinity, and pressure. A small temperature change alters how readily a compression wave passes from one region of water to the next. Increasing pressure generally makes sound travel faster. Salinity also contributes.

Consider a patch of the open ocean, far from a river mouth and away from shallow water. At the surface, the water may be relatively warm. Descend, and the temperature falls. The reduction in temperature tends to slow sound down.

But pressure keeps increasing as you descend. Below the upper layers, temperature changes relatively little while pressure continues to rise. Pressure's influence takes over, and sound begins to travel faster again.

Somewhere between these regions lies a depth at which the sound speed is smallest. In many parts of the deep ocean it is around a kilometer down, though its depth varies with latitude, season, and local ocean conditions. Toward high latitudes the slow-speed layer can approach the surface. Oceanographers call this region the deep sound channel, or the SOFAR channel, for Sound Fixing and Ranging.

It has no walls. It is not a tunnel with a roof you could touch or a current that pushes sound forward.

What holds sound near it is refraction. When a wave enters water where the speed of sound differs, its direction changes. The effect is related to the reason a straw appears bent in a glass of water, although the substances and waves differ. A path leaving the slow-speed layer is gradually bent back toward it. In a simplified picture, a wave bends downward above the speed minimum and upward below it. The result is a succession of long, curving paths that can keep sound away from the absorbing boundaries of the ocean surface and seafloor.

A more complete account uses modes of wave propagation, and actual paths are affected by currents, eddies, seafloor mountains, and seasonal changes. The channel is no guarantee of uninterrupted passage. Land interrupts it. Seamounts and ridges can scatter sound. Higher-frequency waves are absorbed more readily. But a well-placed, low-frequency source can be detected at distances that would seem impossible if sound simply weakened in a straight line through uniform water.

It helps to distinguish a strong signal from a loud experience. A faint pressure variation detectable by a sensitive hydrophone after careful processing need not be perceptible to a person at the surface. Conversely, a source powerful enough to be detected far away may expose nearby marine animals to high sound levels. Those are separate questions, and the history of ocean acoustics eventually forced researchers to confront both.

The physics was there long before anyone lowered a microphone into it. Whales had been making low-frequency sounds in seawater for millions of years. For human scientists, however, discovering the channel required instruments, a hypothesis about sound speed, and an unusually large experimental space.

## Four pounds and nine hundred miles

During the Second World War, the ocean was an enormous military problem. Ships needed to know what was approaching. Aircraft went missing at sea. Submarines had to be detected, concealed, or rescued. Naval research poured resources into methods of listening underwater.

In 1944, Maurice Ewing and J. Lamar Worzel tested long-range sound propagation in the Atlantic. Their vessel, the *Saluda*, carried a deep underwater microphone. Another ship released small explosive charges, roughly four pounds each, to detonate at selected depths. Some of the transmissions were made at distances approaching nine hundred miles from the receiver.

They were heard.

The arrivals did not behave like a simple crack followed by silence. Energy reached the hydrophone along several paths. The received pulses accumulated into a recognizable pattern, ending with a remarkably sharp arrival. Later descriptions of the experiment preserve recordings that look like successive traces becoming crowded together. Scientists could recognize the sound even at the enormous separation between source and receiver.

It is worth remembering that this was an experiment with explosive charges, not the first time sound had ever traveled a great distance under water. Researchers already knew a great deal about naval acoustics and had predicted the possibility of a sound channel. A group in the Soviet Union made an independent discovery in the Sea of Japan in 1946. The important result of the Atlantic test was confirmation that a natural structure in the water could preserve usable signals over distances of hundreds of miles.

The immediate application was supposed to be lifesaving. A crew whose aircraft had fallen into the ocean could drop a charge designed to explode near the sound channel. Widely separated receiving stations would record its arrival. Compare the arrival times, and one could estimate the position from which the signal came. A rescue party would have a much smaller area to search.

The Navy established several SOFAR stations, but according to its own history they were never used as intended for that rescue system. The stations nonetheless became valuable for studying the ocean's sounds.

The same discovery would serve a darker task. In the Cold War, the United States developed the Sound Surveillance System, usually abbreviated SOSUS, with fixed arrays designed to listen for submarines. The arrangement of microphones and the character of the signals were military secrets for decades. Aspects of the system were declassified in 1991, and scientists gained access to acoustic recordings that could also reveal earthquakes, submarine volcanoes, and marine animals.

A listening array is not a single-purpose object. The reason somebody pays to install it may determine which sounds its operators look for, but the hydrophone responds to pressure changes without asking what produced them.

A distant earthquake can produce a signal that enters the water. A volcanic eruption can do the same. So can a whale. The first task in interpreting the record is therefore to work out what kind of event occurred. The second is to understand how it reached the receiver.

For several decades, the microphones were primarily a means of locating sources. Walter Munk and Carl Wunsch proposed making more ambitious use of them. Instead of asking only where a sound came from, why not use its travel time to learn about the water it crossed?

## A thermometer the size of an ocean basin

Take a swimming pool. To measure its temperature, lower a thermometer into the water. But a thermometer at one place tells you about that place. If the pool has a warm corner, a cold inlet, or layers of different temperatures, you need more observations.

Now enlarge the pool until it spans an ocean basin. The conventional solution becomes a formidable sampling problem. Ships can lower instruments. Moorings record conditions at fixed sites. Floats can descend, drift, rise, and transmit measurements to satellites. These are extraordinarily useful methods, especially in combination. None can be everywhere at once, and the deep ocean contains enormous volumes of water that remain difficult to sample regularly.

Sound offers a different measurement. Send a pulse between two known positions. Record when it was transmitted and when it arrived. Its journey takes time according to the speed of sound along its route. That speed is influenced by the water's temperature, pressure, and salinity. If those other influences are properly accounted for, a change in travel time can reveal a change in temperature averaged along an extended path.

Suppose two signals take nearly identical routes across an ocean, separated by a year. If the water along that route has become warmer overall, the later signal will generally travel a little faster and arrive sooner. The timing difference may be small compared with the hour-long journey, yet it can be measured with sensitive equipment and a known transmitted code.

This is the germ of acoustic thermometry. The larger framework, ocean acoustic tomography, uses multiple acoustic paths to reconstruct some of the water's structure, rather as medical tomography uses measurements taken through a body from several directions. The ocean problem is harder in important ways: the paths are curved, the water is moving, and the very property you hope to reconstruct helps determine the paths themselves.

There is an inverse problem hiding in each arrival time. You observe the result of a journey and ask what combination of conditions along the journey produced it. One travel time is not a complete map. A signal might arrive sooner because much of its path warmed, because a smaller part warmed considerably, or because the path itself changed. More paths, repeated observations, and comparisons with independently measured conditions help constrain those possibilities.

There was another challenge before any of that analysis could begin. Could a coded signal remain recognizable after traveling many thousands of kilometers? Would its different arrivals remain stable enough to measure precisely? Sound from ships, weather, biological activity, and other sources would be present at the receivers. Internal waves and ocean currents would complicate the propagation. The signal needed to be recoverable from that noise.

The 1991 experiment near Heard Island was designed to answer those preliminary questions. Heard Island is an Australian territory in the southern Indian Ocean, remote from major ports. It was attractive for technical reasons: from that region, sound could propagate toward the Atlantic and Pacific, and the sound channel lay relatively close to the surface. Equipment that could not be lowered thousands of meters could still be placed near the channel.

The island's name also offered an irresistible pun to a scientist setting out to test whether the world could hear him. That pun did not hurt the proposal.

## The experiment that survived the weather

The ships traveled south with powerful low-frequency acoustic sources, equipment intended to transmit signals around fifty-seven hertz. That is a frequency near the lower edge of ordinary human hearing, although calling it a low note does little to convey the sound pressure needed for such a test.

The sources were difficult machinery to operate in the Southern Ocean. They overheated and had to be stopped and started. Seas grew violent. Equipment broke away. The expedition's informal daily reports charted the decreasing number of surviving acoustic sources. On January thirty-first, the crew reported Beaufort force ten conditions and a wind instrument at its limit of sixty knots. The final transmissions went out that day.

During the five days the sources operated, the experiment managed thirty-five long-distance transmissions. Not quite the clean, sustained test the researchers had hoped to conduct.

Yet on distant receivers, the transmitted codes appeared.

A receiver in the South Atlantic did not have to rely on a human listener recognizing a note. Researchers knew the pattern that had been sent and could search for that pattern in the noisy recordings. By comparing the received fluctuations with the expected code, they could detect a signal whose energy was scattered among many sounds. This is one reason communication systems use carefully designed sequences rather than a single undifferentiated tone.

Receivers on both coasts of the United States detected transmissions. Signals were also received at monitoring sites in the Atlantic, Pacific, Indian, and Southern Oceans. The Ascension Island record showed a first arrival one hour, forty-four minutes, seventeen seconds after transmission over approximately nine thousand two hundred kilometers. It was a convincing demonstration of the ocean's carrying capacity.

It was also a mixed scientific result.

The global signals had complicated arrival patterns, and the transmitting equipment had been unreliable. The short experiment did not provide enough stable, identifiable travel paths to infer the ocean's temperature field as planned. In later accounts, the investigators were explicit: they had demonstrated extraordinary propagation, but they could not yet perform the intended oceanographic inversion from those measurements.

There was a conceptual limitation as well. A temperature estimate integrated halfway around the planet would combine several ocean regions that might be warming or cooling in different ways. Averaging across all of them could conceal the regional changes scientists wanted to resolve. A basin-sized thermometer might be more informative than a planet-sized one.

That was not an excuse invented after failure. It was a lesson drawn from the data about the scale on which the next experiment should operate.

The subsequent Acoustic Thermometry of Ocean Climate project, known as ATOC, concentrated on the North Pacific. Sources near California and Hawaii transmitted coded signals to distant hydrophones. During the experiments of the later 1990s, researchers measured travel times over roughly three thousand to five thousand kilometers to precisions of a few tens of milliseconds. Over such distances the travel time was measured in many tens of minutes. The technical ability to resolve small timing changes was real.

The science was no longer only about whether a recognizable sound could reach a distant instrument. It was about obtaining repeatable measurements that could be compared with ocean models and independent observations.

But the sources were not operating in an empty laboratory.

## Other listeners

Whales do not need to know the term SOFAR channel to live in a world shaped by its acoustics. Low-frequency calls from some baleen whales can travel enormous distances. Underwater sound is a way to communicate in conditions where light quickly fades and where an animal may be separated from others by many kilometers of dark water.

A hydrophone recording contains more than useful research signals. There is the diffuse sound of weather and waves, the low-frequency components of distant ship traffic, and the calls of animals. A ship's propeller can generate a broad mixture of frequencies. Different species and activities occupy different parts of that soundscape. An animal listening for a call may have trouble detecting it against competing noise in the same frequencies. Acousticians call this masking.

It would be a mistake to claim that every human-made underwater sound is equally disruptive. Duration, frequency, source intensity, distance, local propagation conditions, and the hearing of the affected species all influence what an animal actually encounters. A short controlled signal and the continuous noise of traffic create different exposure patterns. Hearing a signal does not by itself establish a harmful biological response. And the absence of a dramatic visible response does not prove there has been none.

These questions became central to ATOC. The scientists wanted to measure ocean warming, which has profound consequences for marine life. Marine biologists and environmental advocates asked what the measurement process might do to animals that depended on low-frequency sound. The project required permits and a marine mammal research program to investigate possible effects. Its acoustic sources were less intense than those used near Heard Island, and transmissions followed schedules intended to limit exposure.

A National Research Council review in 2000 did not give a simple all-clear. Early observations had not shown the dramatic behavioral changes some had feared, but the reviewers found that the studies could not always distinguish a truly small effect from a failure to observe the relevant response. Better monitoring methods were needed to answer the biological questions with greater confidence.

That difficulty is worth explaining carefully. Imagine testing whether a sound changes an animal's behavior by counting how often it surfaces. If the sound changes how far the animal travels underwater, or interferes with calls between individuals, the surface count might remain almost the same. The chosen measurement may be reliable while missing the effect you wanted it to reveal.

Years later, studies of commercial shipping noise have approached masking directly by estimating how much an animal's effective communication or detection range shrinks in a noisy area. This still requires assumptions about the animal's hearing and behavior, but it is closer to the biological problem than asking only whether the animal fled from a source.

The resulting history has no clean heroes and villains. The ocean's capacity to carry low-frequency sound makes it possible to investigate climate and also makes persistent human noise capable of reaching distant listeners. Oceanographers did obtain measurements that could improve our knowledge of the water. The animal studies left important questions open. The same acoustics made both the scientific experiment and the environmental concern credible.

There is, however, another route to measuring the ocean that requires scientists to make no sound at all.

## Let the earthquakes speak

An undersea earthquake sends energy through the solid Earth. Some of it can enter the ocean and travel as acoustic waves. Seismologists call one class of these arrivals T waves. Long before acoustic thermometry, stations could record them without interpreting them as a temperature measurement.

The idea behind seismic ocean thermometry is to find earthquakes that repeat from nearly the same source region and compare the arrival times of their acoustic waves across the same ocean path. If the source and receiver geometry are sufficiently stable, changes in travel time can constrain changes in the water along the path. Natural earthquakes provide the acoustic pulses. Scientists supply the timing analysis.

In 2020, Wenbo Wu, Zhongwen Zhan, Shirui Peng, Sidao Ni, and Jörn Callies published an example using a roughly three-thousand-kilometer section of the eastern Indian Ocean. Their method inferred temperature variability from the travel times of earthquake-generated sound, including changes over seasons and years. In 2023, researchers extended the approach using hydrophones operated for the international nuclear-test monitoring system. The hydrophones could detect smaller events more reliably than some land-based recordings, yielding more frequent comparisons.

This does not make the method effortless. Earthquakes are not perfectly repeatable laboratory transmitters. Their positions and rupture histories must be estimated. Different acoustic paths sample different portions of the water. A shorter travel time can have more than one explanation, which researchers must test. Independent float observations and models remain necessary for interpretation.

A controlled source has advantages: you can choose when it transmits, its frequency, and its code. An earthquake has another: it happens without anyone needing to add another acoustic source to the sea. Its unpredictability creates analytic difficulties, but its existence offers a different form of long-baseline measurement.

It is a pleasing reversal in the history of listening. The wartime investigators wanted to know where a sound came from. The climate researchers wanted the sound to reveal the water through which it had traveled. Now a naturally occurring earthquake can sometimes do both. The ocean has not become simpler; the records and methods have become more capable.

The listening networks also have an unexpected public function. The Comprehensive Nuclear-Test-Ban Treaty Organization operates eleven hydroacoustic stations to watch for evidence of nuclear explosions, including six deep-water hydrophone stations and five island stations that register sound energy converted into seismic vibrations. Their sensors can also record volcanic eruptions, earthquakes, and whale calls. Because the sound channel transports information across great distances, a small number of receivers can observe events over wide ocean regions.

This is not the same as seeing the source. The shape and timing of an acoustic signal constrain possible events, but the listening system still needs models and sometimes other sensors to identify what occurred. An underwater microphone can be exquisitely accurate about pressure and still know nothing about whether the cause was an earthquake, a ship, or a whale until the signal is interpreted.

## Back at Ascension

Return to that hydrophone near Ascension Island in January 1991. It is an ordinary engineering instrument: a device that changes tiny variations in water pressure into an electrical record. It does not know that the source ship is fighting the weather near Heard Island. It has no picture of the vast and moving body of water between them.

Somewhere far to the east and south, a transmitter has begun sending its code. The wave travels through a slow-speed layer assembled by temperature and pressure rather than by human hands. Curved paths take it past regions of water nobody has measured that morning. The signal weakens, spreads, and arrives by several routes.

An hour and forty-four minutes after it set out, its earliest trace reaches the receiver. Later traces keep coming.

The scientists have succeeded in making an ocean deliver a message. It will take more experiments to learn how to read the ocean from the message's journey.

## Research and accuracy notes (not spoken)

This is an original GPT-6 science-history essay. The final scene reconstructs the known source, receiver, and propagation process; it does not claim access to the sensory experience or thoughts of any crew member. Times and distances for the Ascension Island reception are approximate except for the 1h 44m 17s earliest-arrival figure reported by the technical study. Sound-channel depths are geographically variable. The essay describes curving acoustic rays as a conceptual model; the research literature also analyzes normal modes and complex arrival patterns. SOFAR propagation is not lossless, and not every whale call or engineered source propagates thousands of kilometers. ATOC's timing precision established technical feasibility but did not, by itself, constitute a globally resolved temperature map. The marine-mammal research findings are described as limited, consistent with the National Research Council's criticism of the observation methods. The closing discussion of passive thermometry includes real published applications, not a claim that it has superseded conventional hydrographic measurements.
