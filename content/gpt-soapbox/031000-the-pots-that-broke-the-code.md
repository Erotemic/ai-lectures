# The Pots That Broke the Code

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

In the spring of 1953, a letter arrived in London from an archaeological excavation in Greece. The recipient was Michael Ventris, an architect who had spent years trying to read a script from the Bronze Age. The letter came from Carl Blegen, the archaeologist excavating the ancient palace at Pylos.

Blegen had been examining clay tablets that had not yet been made available to the people attempting the decipherment. On one of them, little drawings of vessels accompanied lines of mysterious signs. Applying Ventris's proposed values to the signs, Blegen found something astonishing. Next to the drawings of three-legged vessels was a word that appeared to read *ti-ri-po-de*. There were other words that seemed to describe vessels with handles, including how many handles they had.

The Greek word *tripodes* means tripods. A tripod is a thing with three feet, and the tablet was making distinctions among actual pots. In his letter, Blegen asked whether the fit could really be accidental.

This was not how the story had been expected to end. Archaeologists had found documents that nobody could read, and after half a century of argument, the proposed decipherment was being tested against a list of kitchen equipment.

There is a splendid disproportion between the scale of the mystery and the modesty of the answer. We can spend a lifetime wondering what a vanished civilization might have believed about death, power, or the gods. The civilization sends back a tablet about pots.

To understand why those pots were so persuasive, we have to go back much further than the letter.

## Words that survived the destruction of their office

The people who wrote in Linear B lived in the late Bronze Age, more than three thousand years ago. Their world occupied parts of mainland Greece and Crete. Its great centers included Pylos, Mycenae, Thebes, and Knossos. They built administrative complexes and storerooms, managed agricultural production, and recorded quantities of goods and the movement of labor. Later Greeks would inherit some names and words from this earlier world, but the palatial system itself did not survive the end of the Bronze Age.

The writing was made by pressing or cutting signs into damp clay. A scribe could form a small, flat tablet, mark it with a pointed tool, and keep it as part of a working record. Many were temporary documents. A record of an allocation of wool or an inventory of animals was useful to an administrator at the time. It did not necessarily need to last a thousand years.

The catastrophe that ruined the palace could preserve its paperwork. Clay tablets that had not been deliberately fired sometimes became harder when the buildings around them burned. Archaeologists later recovered those baked fragments from the debris. What survived was shaped partly by an accident of destruction.

The result is a wonderfully lopsided archive. We have administrative lists. We do not have a Mycenaean library of surviving novels, theatrical scripts, or long poems written in Linear B. No tablet records an eyewitness description of a king's coronation for posterity. The people who made the marks were usually recording business that a central authority needed to manage.

That limited record is still informative. The tablets name individuals, groups of workers, places, materials, quantities, and deities. They contain the ordinary bureaucratic distinctions of a society that could organize production across a territory. They also expose how much of the population appears in the records primarily because it had something to deliver, something to receive, or work assigned to perform.

We should not imagine every person on Crete or the Greek mainland reading these documents. Palace administration was a specialized activity. Written evidence from palaces tells us much about palaces, and much less about the conversations people had outside their walls.

Before the decipherment, even those narrow records were inaccessible as language. A researcher could look at a drawing of an animal and suspect that a line concerned livestock, but the sequence of signs beside it remained mute.

## The man who named the problem

In 1900, the archaeologist Arthur Evans began extensive excavation at Knossos on Crete. A Greek antiquarian named Minos Kalokairinos had excavated parts of the site earlier, in 1878. Evans's work brought an immense quantity of material to scholarly attention, including tablets covered in signs unlike the familiar Greek alphabet.

Evans distinguished three writing systems: Cretan Hieroglyphic, Linear A, and Linear B. The names were organizational tools, not descriptions left by the ancient scribes. The scripts differ, and the distinction between the two linear systems proved essential. Linear A is older and is associated mainly with a language that remains poorly understood. Linear B is a related script used to write another language.

For decades, nobody knew what that second language was. Evans favored the view that it was not Greek. He was trying to recover a civilization he called Minoan, and the tablets' unfamiliar signs seemed to support the idea of an unfamiliar language.

There were reasons to be cautious about a Greek identification. Linear B did not resemble the Greek alphabet. The best-known surviving Greek texts were written centuries later. The Bronze Age political world had disappeared. Nor was there a convenient bilingual inscription that set the same message side by side in a known language, as the Rosetta Stone had helped do for Egyptian hieroglyphs.

The word *code* can mislead here. There is no evidence that the palace scribes were trying to keep these records secret. Their writing was perfectly ordinary to the people trained to use it. The knowledge of how to read it disappeared with the institutions that had sustained it.

A decipherer did not know the language or the values of the signs. The task was therefore more difficult than matching an unknown alphabet to an already familiar sentence. A proposed reading had to account for the whole surviving system, including repeated words, grammatical variations, numerals, and drawings.

Even the number of signs needed careful work. If one scribe made a mark with a long loop and another made it with a short loop, were these different signs or handwriting variations of the same one? The answer changes every calculation of sign frequency. You cannot study how often a symbol appears until you can decide when two marks count as the same symbol.

Meanwhile the source material was scattered, and publication was slow. Evans died in 1941 before the principal collection of Knossos tablets had appeared in a usable form. New tablets found at Pylos in 1939 opened another enormous body of evidence, but the Second World War interrupted excavation and publication. Researchers had to wait for reliable copies, not simply demand to see the whole archive at once.

The problem attracted scholars with strikingly different habits. Some looked for languages they already knew. Others looked for repeated symbols and numerical patterns. Several announced solutions that failed when applied to more than a few convenient examples.

Two American scholars began to make the task more manageable without claiming to know the answer.

## Alice Kober's boxes

Alice Kober taught classics at Brooklyn College in New York. She spent years studying Linear B, reproducing signs, comparing tablets, and recording recurring sequences. Her surviving papers include thousands of small slips of paper arranged in reused cigarette cartons. These were tools for sorting information before a modern searchable database existed.

The detail is charming, but it can also mislead. It is easy to picture one dedicated scholar surrounded by paper, and then treat the paper itself as an explanation for the discovery. Filing cabinets do not decipher languages. Kober's achievement was in deciding which differences among signs were worth recording.

She resisted the temptation to decide prematurely what language the signs represented. At a time when scholars proposed Greek, Etruscan, and other candidates, she wanted relationships that could be established without guessing a language at all.

She noticed groups of words that appeared in related forms. In some places the signs were almost identical except for their endings. The pattern recurred too systematically to dismiss as scribal accident. It looked like grammatical inflection: the way a language alters the form of a word to mark number, gender, or another grammatical distinction.

Consider an example from Italian. The adjective *buono* can mean good in the masculine form, and *buona* is a feminine form. If you wrote those words in a system with one sign for each consonant-and-vowel syllable, the endings would include a sign for *no* in one case and a sign for *na* in the other.

Suppose you knew nothing of Italian or of the sound values in that writing system. You might still notice that two words often occurred in the same circumstances, with endings that alternated. The two ending signs could plausibly share a consonant, with a different vowel. You would not know that the common consonant was *n*. You would know something about the relationship between two signs.

Kober identified more elaborate families of related Linear B words, later nicknamed Kober's triplets. By following the recurring variations, she could group signs that likely shared a consonant or a vowel. She began arranging them in a table, leaving the sound values blank.

A blank table may sound like a thin achievement. Imagine, though, that every sign is an unknown symbol, and that the ancient scribe has left no pronunciation guide. If you can establish that one set of signs belongs together because their consonants match, and another set belongs together because their vowels match, you have already imposed a considerable structure on the problem. A later clue can give one sign a sound, and relationships in the table can carry that information outward.

Kober published the method in a series of studies in the 1940s. In her 1948 article she showed a small part of the sign table. Her private notes contained additional tentative placements. Some were right; some were incomplete. It was working research, not a secretly finished decipherment waiting for someone else to publish it.

She died in 1950, at forty-three, before the breakthrough. The story is sad enough without inventing an inner monologue for her final months. Her articles and notes show what she had accomplished. They do not tell us how she would have completed the task if she had lived longer.

## Cleaning the data before asking the question

Kober's tables required a reasonably reliable list of signs. That brings in Emmett L. Bennett Junior, another American scholar working with the tablets from Pylos.

Bennett studied the shapes of the Linear B characters, separating distinct signs from variants made by different hands. He helped establish a reliable sign inventory and worked on the tablets' weights and measures. Those advances could look more like catalog maintenance than decipherment. Yet they made it possible to count and compare the inscriptions without repeatedly confusing a spelling variation with a different word.

Think of an English document written by several people in different handwriting. Someone writes a lowercase *a* as a small circle with a stroke; someone else draws a form that looks more like a printed single-story *a*. If a researcher treats these as different letters, patterns in the document get scrambled. Multiply the problem across dozens of unfamiliar signs, some damaged and some copied from broken clay, and you have an idea of the difficulty.

The new Pylos material was important for another reason. It came from a site on the Greek mainland, not merely from Crete. The growing corpus allowed scholars to compare where sign sequences appeared, what sorts of commodities were listed, and which features might belong to the writing system itself.

Kober and Bennett exchanged information. Their contributions were complementary. Kober made relationships visible among signs whose values were unknown. Bennett improved the reliability of the categories those relationships depended on. They did not need to agree on a hypothetical translation before their work could be useful.

By the early 1950s, the problem had acquired a firmer foundation. It was still a problem no one had solved.

## An architect proposes a language

Michael Ventris had been fascinated with the script since adolescence. In 1936, as a schoolboy, he attended an exhibition about Greek archaeology and encountered Evans and the Knossos finds. He later trained and worked as an architect, while continuing to study the undeciphered writing. The work was serious and sustained, even though his paid profession was elsewhere.

Ventris spoke several languages and was confident enough to attempt comparisons that specialists regarded skeptically. He also held a favored hypothesis for a long time: that Linear B might record a language related to Etruscan. That idea was wrong. He did not begin with an accurate vision of the answer and patiently await its confirmation.

In 1950 he circulated questions to scholars working on the problem. He followed this with a series of research notes, sent to colleagues for criticism. The notes let other people see the changing state of his guesses. By February 1952 he had developed a substantial grid of signs, building on Kober's structural work and the increasingly reliable evidence provided by Bennett.

Imagine the grid as a set of intersecting rows and columns. A row represents signs thought to share one consonant; a column, signs thought to share one vowel. At first the row and column labels are unknown. You may be fairly confident that two signs belong together without knowing whether they are *na* and *ni*, or *ta* and *ti*, or something else entirely.

Ventris tried attaching tentative values to a few positions. He compared some Linear B signs with signs from the Cypriot syllabary, a later script whose use for Greek was already known. A visual resemblance between signs is not proof of a shared value. Writing systems change, and similar shapes can arise by accident. But a comparison can supply a hypothesis that the grid and the tablets may accept or reject.

Then he considered an idea about the words that appeared in particular positions on tablets from Knossos. What if some were place names? An administrative center recording the delivery of goods would have reasons to name places. Some names in Crete might have survived in later Greek sources even if the spoken language had changed.

The harbor town of Amnisos was one candidate. In a simple syllabic spelling, its name might appear approximately as *a-mi-ni-so*. Other candidate locations offered further constraints. When Ventris assigned values to signs on this basis, recognizable names began to emerge: Knossos among them.

That was exciting but dangerous. The first reading of a place name can be a coincidence, especially if a decipherer has the freedom to change sound values until a name fits. The honest test is whether the same assigned values work elsewhere. Every time you assign a sign a sound, you have made a commitment about every other occurrence of that sign on every other tablet.

Ventris pushed beyond the place names. As readings accumulated, more words and grammatical forms suggested Greek. This surprised him, because the language he had long expected was not Greek.

On June 1, 1952, he circulated a work note titled *Are the Knossos and Pylos tablets written in Greek?* The title was still a question. The grid was turning into a decipherment, and its author was finding himself forced to change his mind about the language.

He announced the result on the BBC in July. It was a compelling claim: some of the earliest known writing in Greek had been lying unread in museums and excavation stores for decades.

It was still necessary to test it.

## A philologist and an unexpected witness

John Chadwick, a scholar of Greek at Cambridge, heard about the announcement and obtained Ventris's materials. Chadwick was trained in the language and its history in ways Ventris was not. He examined the proposed readings and recognized features of Greek that could help explain their forms.

This partnership added an important check. Bronze Age Greek was not going to look exactly like the Greek of the classical authors taught in schools. Languages change over centuries, and the syllabic script could not represent every detail of pronunciation. A form might look unfamiliar because it was old, because the writing system suppressed sounds, or because the decipherment was wrong. Sorting these possibilities required linguistic expertise.

Linear B is not an alphabet. A sign usually represents an open syllable such as *pa* or *ti*, or a single vowel. Greek words, however, often contain adjacent consonants and consonants at the ends of syllables. When a scribe fitted Greek into Linear B, some sounds had to be handled imperfectly. The written sequence *ti-ri-po-de* is not a phonetic recording of every sound in the word *tripodes*. A reader who demanded a one-to-one correspondence with later Greek spelling could miss a genuine match.

Ventris and Chadwick worked together, publishing a scholarly account in 1953 and eventually a large joint study of the tablets. But the decipherment's decisive test did not depend solely on what its advocates could make of the signs they had already examined.

This brings us back to the clay pots.

In the spring of 1953, Blegen was preparing previously unpublished tablets from Pylos. He tried the new sign values on one marked Pylos Ta 641. Beside drawings of vessels, words emerged that seemed to refer to tripods and different configurations of handles. There were details no decipherer could have arranged in advance to make a known example fit.

The Greek reading predicted meaningful labels on material that had not been used to devise it. The pictures and the words agreed. Blegen wrote to Ventris. Chadwick later recalled how excited Ventris was when he called with the news.

A language can be guessed from a handful of names. A system that repeatedly predicts what a new document says is much harder to dismiss. The 1953 tablet did not prove that every sign had been correctly understood, or that every disputed word would now be easy to translate. It did something more precise. It showed that the proposed values worked on genuinely new evidence in a way an arbitrary collection of guesses was unlikely to do.

Blegen's letter asked whether the result could be coincidence. With the wider body of Greek-looking readings, and the new tablet as a test, the skeptical answer became increasingly difficult to maintain.

## What the tablets finally said

The decipherment opened a window onto the administration of Mycenaean palaces. The records tracked livestock, textiles, metal, agricultural produce, and personnel. Some named deities familiar from later Greek religion. Other words revealed terms for officials and allocations whose meaning has to be reconstructed from repeated administrative contexts.

It changed the chronology of Greek as a written language. A language previously known from much later alphabetic texts now had written evidence from the Bronze Age. But the revelation did not turn the palaces into the world of Homer's poems in any simple way. A palace list is not an epic. A familiar divine name does not establish that people told exactly the same stories about that deity centuries later.

Take an administrative tablet in the Heraklion Archaeological Museum. It records a group of men under several district headings, using a repeated pattern in which an individual's name appears beside the sign for a man and a numeral. Once read, the tablet yields names and administrative categories. It still does not tell you what those men said about the system or how they felt about its demands.

That silence is part of the evidence. Some people appear as quantities in an institution's records because the institution needed to count them. Their experiences are not necessarily recoverable from the very documents that prove they were there.

And there is a second limit. Solving Linear B did not solve Linear A. The scripts share many sign shapes and appear to be historically related. That lets scholars make tentative comparisons. It does not follow that the earlier language is Greek or that values transferred from Linear B will yield a straightforward translation. Linear A remains undeciphered in the sense that its underlying language has not been securely identified and read. The available texts are fewer, often short, and do not provide the same body of grammatical patterns and independent tests.

Decipherment depends on a meeting of surviving evidence, good classification, productive guesses, and opportunities to discover that a guess is wrong. Linear B happened to provide enough of those opportunities. For Linear A, the remaining obstacles are part of a genuine scholarly problem, not a failure of imagination.

The people who solved Linear B did not give the Bronze Age a new voice by inventing words for it. They recovered a small but direct part of what ancient scribes had put down. The voice was largely bureaucratic. That is what survived.

I return to the letter from Pylos because it is such a satisfying ending to the investigation. On a clay tablet made for a palace administration, a scribe had once distinguished among pots with different arrangements of feet and handles. A fire preserved the account. Archaeologists found it. Decades passed while researchers learned which signs were distinct, which endings belonged together, and which syllables those signs might represent.

Then Blegen tried the new values and read *ti-ri-po-de* beside the picture of a tripod.

Somewhere in the ruined office at Pylos, thousands of years earlier, somebody had needed to know how many pots there were. The answer survived long enough to tell us how to read the language.
