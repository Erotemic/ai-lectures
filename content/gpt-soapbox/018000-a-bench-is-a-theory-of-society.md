# A Bench Is a Theory of Society

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

Imagine a bench outside a railway station.

It is not especially beautiful. Its paint has faded where people have rested their hands. The wood has been replaced at least once. The city has never put it on a postcard. It exists mainly because, at some point, somebody decided there should be a place to sit.

One person uses it while waiting for a bus. Another stops because the walk from the station is tiring. A third is early for an appointment and has twenty minutes with nothing to do. Two friends meet beside it. A stranger sits at the other end, and nobody speaks. In the evening a cleaner works around it. A person with no safe place to sleep may see it as a possible refuge, though a very poor one.

No grand theory is visible in the object. But there is a theory in the decision to put it there, and another in the decision about who may use it, how long they may remain, and what happens when their presence makes someone else uncomfortable.

I want to talk about this extraordinarily ordinary piece of furniture because it concentrates a set of questions we usually reserve for constitutions and manifestos. Who is a city for? What may a person do there without paying? How much inconvenience should strangers be expected to accommodate? Does public space mean somewhere you may pass through, or somewhere you may actually be?

My claim is not that a bench can solve the problems of urban life. It plainly cannot. My claim is that the small decisions which shape public space reveal what our much larger declarations about belonging are prepared to mean in practice.

A bench is a theory of society you can sit on.

## The discovery that people sit

There is a story about urban planning that I like because it begins with an embarrassment. In the early nineteen seventies, William H. Whyte and the Street Life Project studied how people actually used plazas and other public spaces in New York City. Rather than beginning with a designer's intentions, they watched the inhabitants of the spaces. They mapped activity, counted people, and studied the arrangements that attracted them.

Whyte's account, published in nineteen eighty as The Social Life of Small Urban Spaces, contains a finding that sounds almost too elementary to count as research: people tend to sit where they have somewhere to sit.

It is worth resisting the temptation to laugh and move on. The observation was important partly because urban design could become so absorbed in buildings, monumentality, sight lines, and abstract open space that the elementary question of whether a visitor could comfortably stay there was neglected.

It was not enough to draw a generous-looking plaza on a plan. A plaza could be magnificent in an architectural photograph and unpleasant to occupy. A tall building could be surrounded by expensive stone paving that provided no shade, no comfortable ledges, and no inviting place to pause. When the design had been judged mainly from above, the view from a seated human body might be missing.

Whyte's team also noticed that people often preferred to be near other people. They watched activity from the edges of busy routes. They gathered where circulation and sitting overlapped. Movable chairs gave them some control over how close they sat to strangers, whether they faced the sunshine, and whether they joined a conversation or watched one from a distance.

There is no need to turn these observations into a universal law of human behavior. A plaza is not a controlled experiment that reveals every person's preferences. New York in the nineteen seventies was not every city, in every climate, for every population. People need shade in one season, shelter in another, and peace from traffic in a third. The right result cannot be a single bench design copied everywhere.

The more useful lesson is methodological. A place is not successful merely because its designer can explain why it ought to work. Go there. Watch what people do. Ask what they need. Notice where they sit when the seats are occupied, and where they refuse to sit when the seats are empty. Find out whether the supposed public benefit is available to actual members of the public.

A familiar engineering discipline appears in this story. The drawing is a hypothesis. The place itself is the test.

I especially like the modesty of that lesson. A professional can have an advanced vocabulary and still be corrected by a person who wants to put down a shopping bag. A long conversation about urban vitality can be improved by a short walk through the neighborhood at three in the afternoon.

## The distance between two resting places

Now consider two people living on the same street.

One can walk for an hour without difficulty. The other can walk for ten minutes and then needs to sit for a while. Both have access to the same sidewalks. On a map, the library, post office, park, and grocery store are equally distant from their homes.

But the map is missing something essential.

If the second person cannot reliably rest along the way, a destination that looks nearby on paper may be practically unreachable. They may stay home, take a more expensive trip, depend on somebody else, or decide the effort is not worth the risk of being stranded without somewhere to stop.

Put a well-placed seat halfway along the route and the geography changes. No road has moved. No building has been demolished. The distance in meters remains identical. Yet a journey becomes possible that was previously too demanding.

This is an illustration, not an estimate of how many people gain access from any particular bench. Real mobility depends on many things: crossing times, slope, weather, sidewalk conditions, pain, fatigue, transport, and individual needs. But I think the example reveals something true about infrastructure. A network's reach is not determined only by the length of its connections. It is also determined by what the traveler can do along them.

The World Health Organization recognized this in its two thousand seven guide to age-friendly cities. Drawing on consultations with older people in cities across the world, it included well-situated, maintained public benches among the features of a city that people can use as they age. Seattle's age-friendly street-design guidance likewise treats seating at transit stops and on walking routes as a practical support for older adults, people with disabilities, and families with young children.

Notice what kind of claim this is. We are not being asked to regard seating as a luxury to make a neighborhood more picturesque. It is sometimes part of the equipment that allows a person to participate in public life.

And that equipment should not require the person to perform a need for our approval. Nobody asks a pedestrian to demonstrate sufficiently tired legs before using a traffic signal. A bench should not ordinarily require a performance of deservingness either.

There is a broader principle here. An environment can be officially open to everyone and functionally available only to those whose bodies and resources happen to match its design. Accessibility is not just the removal of a prohibition. It is the positive provision of the conditions under which an invitation can be accepted.

A city that advertises walking while offering nowhere to rest has made a peculiar assumption about the walkers.

## The permission to be somewhere without buying anything

A bench is also economically interesting. Most of the places in which modern urban residents can sit comfortably are tied, in some way, to transactions. A café has chairs, but they belong to a business. A restaurant has tables, but it expects customers. A hotel lobby may be welcoming until a visitor becomes a person with no evident reason to be there.

There is nothing inherently wrong with a business having conditions for using its furniture. A café needs to support its staff, pay rent, and leave room for the next customer. I am not arguing that every private enterprise should operate as an unrestricted public lounge.

But it matters whether a city contains places where the right to stay does not begin with a purchase.

Think about how many ordinary situations contain a few minutes of unproductive time. A person is between appointments. Two people need to talk about something difficult. A parent wants to let a child finish a snack. Someone has received bad news and does not want to immediately resume walking. A tourist is lost, but not ready to ask for directions. A person simply likes watching the world.

None of these situations necessarily generates revenue. The person is not yet a customer, and may never be one. If the only comfortable places to stop belong to businesses, then every pause arrives with a small question attached: what do I owe for being here?

The public bench offers one answer: nothing, beyond the ordinary obligations of sharing a public place.

This is not a right to monopolize a seat indefinitely or prevent others from using it. It is a modest and enormously useful freedom: being able to remain in the city without becoming a purchaser, a worker, an applicant, or a patient.

New York City provides an instructive case through its privately owned public spaces. Since a zoning program begun in nineteen sixty-one, some developments have received building-related benefits in exchange for providing areas intended for public use. The areas are privately owned, but their public use is part of the bargain. Rules and signage determine access, required amenities, and other conditions.

That arrangement produces a question more interesting than whether the property is technically private or public. What does a promised public benefit actually consist of? A plaza may be legally accessible while giving people no reason, or even no comfortable means, to spend time there. It may contain a public space on paper and a space that feels private to its visitors.

To its credit, New York's current zoning standards for qualifying public plazas address seating directly. They require a mix of seating types and distinguish solitary from social use; they even prohibit some devices intended to prevent people from sitting on otherwise usable surfaces. A policy document that specifies where a seat should face may look comically mundane next to the moral vocabulary of public life. Yet the mundane rule is one way a public promise becomes something a person can use.

The words open to the public mean more when somebody has somewhere to be.

## The politics of the armrest

There is a complication that cannot be hidden behind the warm image of the bench.

Consider a wooden bench divided by a metal armrest in the middle. Some people see a helpful design. An older adult, or somebody recovering from an injury, may need the support of an armrest to stand up. A bench without armrests can be almost impossible for certain users to rise from safely. The city of Seattle's own accessibility guidance explicitly recommends armrests and backrests for this reason.

Other people see a deliberate barrier. The armrest prevents somebody from lying across the bench to sleep. It can be part of a design strategy intended to make certain uses of public space physically difficult, especially for people who have nowhere else to sleep.

Both observations can be accurate at once.

This is where the easy arguments fail. If I declare every divided bench malicious, I ignore the people who need a place to push themselves upright. If I declare every divider an accessibility feature, I can overlook the deliberate exclusion that some designs are meant to accomplish. The identical object may support one use and obstruct another. The ethical question depends on the consequences, the available alternatives, the surrounding conditions, and sometimes the documented purpose of the design.

Researchers call some of these strategies hostile, defensive, or exclusionary design. Those terms are not perfectly interchangeable, and scholars have argued about how to define them. The basic examples are easy to recognize: an otherwise usable ledge covered with studs, a bench shaped so that lying down is impossible, a public area equipped with devices intended to discourage particular people from lingering.

A qualitative study published in two thousand twenty-five interviewed fifteen unhoused people about their experiences of exclusionary design. The participants described rejection, stress, and disruption. This was a small interview study, not a representative survey of every person experiencing homelessness or a causal measurement of any city's health. But it supplies a perspective often missing when a design problem is discussed solely by people responsible for managing the property.

A question that sounds like engineering may conceal a decision about whose discomfort counts. The property manager may be concerned about complaints. A passerby may feel unsafe. The person without shelter may be trying to survive a cold night. These concerns are not automatically of equal urgency, nor can they all be satisfied by a clever piece of metal.

A particularly troubling move is to treat the removal of a visible problem as the solution to the underlying problem. Making a bench impossible to sleep on does not create housing. It can move a person to a doorway, a more exposed location, or somewhere the public no longer sees them. Whether some specific intervention improves safety is an empirical question that must be evaluated, but concealment by itself is not an achievement.

There are legitimate questions about safety, maintenance, access, and the use of scarce space. A bus shelter cannot be permanently occupied in ways that exclude people waiting for the bus. No city can promise that every public surface is always available for every purpose. Nor do people sleeping outdoors deserve to be treated as the solution to municipal housing failures. A bench is not a bed, and insisting that it remain sleepable is not an adequate housing policy.

The conclusion is not that objects are morally pure or impure. It is that design decisions cannot relieve us of responsibility for the problems they rearrange.

A practical response may include several types of seating, some with supportive armrests, others with different arrangements; seating in locations where people actually need to rest; accessible shelters and toilets; reasonable rules applied fairly; and, quite separately, serious efforts to provide safe housing and appropriate services. The design must be considered in the context of its users rather than praised or condemned by the shape of one armrest in isolation.

## Being together without becoming friends

I want to return to the quieter thing a bench permits.

Two people sit beside one another and do not speak.

We have many words for social life that involve active relationships. Community, friendship, cooperation, deliberation. These are important. But we also need a word for the civil, low-pressure condition of occupying the same place without demanding anything from one another.

A person can sit on a bench and watch children play in a nearby square. Someone else can sit a few feet away and read. A musician might rehearse softly where local rules allow it. A pedestrian asks whether the seat is taken; the first person shakes their head. That may be the entire social transaction.

Nothing dramatic has occurred. And yet the two people have practiced a minimal kind of coexistence. Each has allowed the other a little room.

I don't want to exaggerate this into the claim that public benches create democracy. A person does not become tolerant merely by sitting near a stranger. Sharing space can produce anxiety, conflict, intimidation, or indifference, as well as curiosity and goodwill. It depends on the situation and the people involved.

What I find worth preserving is the possibility of presence without compulsory intimacy. Public life should not require everyone to participate in a carefully managed community-building exercise. Sometimes a person wants a place to be alone among others. That is not a contradiction. It is a recognizable kind of freedom.

Movable chairs are interesting in this regard. A fixed row tells everyone how to arrange themselves. A chair that can be shifted allows a person to move closer, turn away, sit beside a friend, or keep a little distance. Whyte noticed this flexibility when watching people use successful plazas. The apparently small ability to control one's position can make shared space easier to inhabit.

The design of public seating therefore includes more than the engineering of a comfortable surface. It includes degrees of choice: sun or shade, conversation or solitude, a view of the street or some quiet greenery, a backrest or a ledge, proximity or separation. The capacity to choose is part of the comfort.

There is an aesthetic dimension, too. A good street is not simply a corridor connecting important destinations. It can itself be a place where life is encountered. If every moment spent on the street must be justified by moving toward some task, then people are allowed to inhabit the city only while doing something else.

I think that is too small a picture of a city.

## The bench that somebody has to care for

A bench also gets wet. Its fastenings loosen. Snow piles around it. Someone spills a drink. Someone scratches an insult into the wood. On a hot day the seat may become too warm; on a cold one, a metal back can be unpleasant to touch. A badly positioned bench may obstruct a narrow footpath. A beautiful bench placed too far from any activity can remain empty for years.

This is not a reason to give up on benches. It is a reminder that public hospitality has a maintenance budget.

When a city installs a seat, it is making a promise that lasts beyond the photograph of the ribbon cutting. It must think about surfaces, drainage, cleaning, visibility, access for wheelchairs and mobility devices, and the work required to keep the area usable. The workers who repair and clean the place are participants in its design, whether or not their names appear on the architectural drawing.

New York City's transportation department describes public seating as a service for pedestrians and transit riders and provides ways to suggest locations and report damage. Those unglamorous mechanisms matter. A citizen who can tell the city that a seat is broken, and reasonably expect somebody to respond, has more than a bench. They have a small functioning relationship with an institution.

Even so, public money is finite. A poorly placed bench can be a bad use of it. A seat alone cannot overcome a dangerous crossing, the absence of a sidewalk, or harassment that makes a place frightening. Public design involves comparison with other needs, not merely affection for its most photogenic objects.

And sometimes people disagree sincerely. Residents may want fewer obstructions. Older pedestrians may request more resting places. Merchants may worry about blocked entrances. People living outdoors may point out that rules intended to preserve comfort often deny them any dignified place to exist. Each claim needs to be examined rather than dismissed with a label.

The practical work is to notice actual use, create ways for affected people to contribute, and make the decisions revisable. A public-space design that cannot learn from its users is an odd form of public service.

## What a city chooses to count

Suppose a city conducts an audit of two streets.

On the first street, nearly everybody moves quickly through. On the second, people stop, sit, talk, and sometimes cross the pedestrian flow. A traffic engineer concerned with throughput may give the first a higher score. A shopkeeper concerned with customers may prefer the second. A person trying to get through a narrow space using a wheelchair may have a different assessment again.

Nobody has to be lying. They may simply be measuring different goals.

The point is not that speed is bad. If a crossing is blocked or pedestrians cannot pass safely, the problem is real. The point is that an urban design optimized entirely for circulation will tend to regard the act of staying as a cost. And a city that treats staying as a cost should not be surprised if its public life becomes thin.

Whyte's method is useful again here: look at how the space is actually used, not only at what the plan says it permits. Ask who is missing, not just how many are present. A plaza full of people who can comfortably stand may be a disappointing place for someone who needs to sit. A sheltered bus stop used only by healthy commuters may tell us less about universal access than the sight of an older person who can now travel there independently.

We should be cautious about claiming that the number of occupied seats directly measures flourishing. A crowded bench may mean the place is beloved. It may also mean there are far too few places to sit. An empty bench may be unnecessary, or it may be a vital option used intermittently by people who need it most.

The goal is not to maximize the bench occupancy rate. The goal is to make the place more usable. The number helps only when we remember what it was intended to illuminate.

## What a bench does not promise

I should be clear about the limitations of my argument, because there is a tempting sentimental version of it that I don't believe.

A bench does not abolish poverty. It does not make a city safe by virtue of being present. It does not automatically reconcile conflicting needs. Its presence does not make every visitor welcome, any more than a publicly funded building automatically treats everybody inside with dignity.

In some locations, building a bench is simply the wrong priority. A wider sidewalk, a safer crossing, an accessible toilet, shade, trees, or reliable transport may matter more. A hostile street with no protection from speeding vehicles will not become friendly because a seat has been added a few feet from the traffic.

I am also not claiming that strangers have unlimited obligations to accommodate every action in shared space. A public place exists because people must coexist under rules. Rules may be necessary to prevent harassment, protect children, preserve routes for emergency access, or keep seating available for those who need it. The question is whether the rules respond proportionately to actual harms, and whether they are enforced fairly, rather than treating the mere presence of an inconvenient person as a harm.

There is another objection. Perhaps a public bench is too small a subject for a moral argument. Surely housing, transit, employment, and political representation matter much more. Of course they matter. They are not competitors in a contest to see which issue can claim all our attention.

In fact the larger problems become difficult to discuss coherently if we refuse to look at their smaller expressions. A right that exists only at the highest level of abstraction can fail when someone tries to exercise it. A promise of mobility may fail at the curb. A promise of inclusion may fail at a doorway. A promise of public space may fail at the first place a tired person tries to sit.

The scale of the object does not determine the scale of the principle.

## A place to stop

Return to the bench outside the station.

Its maker may have thought primarily about weather resistance and the cost of replacement parts. The planner may have thought about the bus stop and pedestrian traffic. A council member may have argued about its budget. A maintenance worker may have had the most accurate understanding of how people actually treat it. A resident may simply have wanted somewhere to rest on the way home.

All of these perspectives belong in the story of the object.

And the people who use it are not merely entries in a usage statistic. Their needs do not always announce themselves. Someone sits because a knee hurts. Someone sits because the day is too much. Someone sits because there is nothing urgent to do, and the sky is pleasant, and they would rather remain a little longer in the world than hurry to the next obligation.

I think a good city should allow for that last person, too.

The most generous things we build are not always expensive, or spectacular, or innovative. Sometimes they are decisions not to demand an explanation. A patch of shade. A drinking fountain. A door that opens without a purchase. A safe place to cross the road. A seat from which a person may watch the afternoon go by.

There is a difference between making a place people are allowed to enter and making a place people are allowed to inhabit. A bench does not settle that difference. But it is one modest, material way of taking the second promise seriously.

The city says: you may stop here.

That is not everything a society owes its members. It is also not nothing.

## Companion notes (not spoken)

**Thesis.** Public seating is not merely decorative street furniture. Its design, distribution, permission structure, and maintenance reveal how a community understands access, economic participation, civic coexistence, and the tension between hospitality and exclusion.

**Constructed illustrations.** The railway-station bench, the two pedestrians with different walking endurance, the two-street audit, and the imagined users of a street seat are illustrative thought experiments, not eyewitness accounts, studies, or descriptions of named people. This essay is normative synthesis grounded in cited urban-planning and accessibility sources; it does not purport to measure the causal effects of one bench.

**Qualifications.** Whyte's field observations do not establish universal behavioral laws. The 2025 interview study with 15 unhoused participants is qualitative evidence of reported experience, not a representative prevalence estimate or a causal measure. Some armrests genuinely provide accessibility benefits while other designs may be intended to prevent sleep, and both effects can occur together. Urban access is jointly shaped by pedestrian safety, sanitation, transport, climate, shelter, and local operations; benches alone are not an intervention on homelessness or a replacement for those conditions.
