# The Moon Was On Time

*How a late eclipse of Jupiter's moon turned light into something that could be timed.*

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

On November 9, 1676, an astronomer at the Paris Observatory had a reason to watch Jupiter very carefully. A small moon called Io was due to reappear from Jupiter's shadow. According to the prediction being tested, the event would be late.

That sounds like a problem with an astronomical timetable. The moon had been observed for decades. Its orbit was regular enough to use as a clock. An eclipse coming ten minutes after its expected time ought to mean something had gone wrong with the calculation.

Ole Rømer, a Danish astronomer working in Paris, had suggested another explanation. The moon would emerge from the shadow when it always did. The delay would occur between the moon and the observer. Light from a more distant Jupiter took longer to arrive at Earth. Astronomers had been reading the sky as though every event could be seen the instant it happened. Rømer proposed that they were reading old news.

The November observation supported the proposed delay. The estimate was rough, the subsequent arguments were contentious, and the history of who first recognized the explanation is more complicated than the shortest versions of the story admit. Still, something substantial had happened. A celestial clock had seemed to run late, and that failure had given astronomers a way to measure how long a beam of light spent on the road.

To see why, we need to understand what that clock was built to do.

## A clock hanging beside Jupiter

In 1610, Galileo Galilei turned a telescope toward Jupiter and noticed small points of light that changed position from night to night. Four of them proved to be moons revolving around the planet. For Europe, the discovery challenged the picture in which everything in the heavens had to revolve around Earth. It also supplied an intriguing practical possibility.

A navigator can find latitude by examining the height of the Sun or stars. Longitude is harder. If you know the local time where you are and the time at a reference place, the difference tells you how far east or west you have traveled. Earth rotates through fifteen degrees of longitude in one hour. A four-minute difference corresponds to one degree.

That arithmetic is easy to say. During the seventeenth century, keeping a reference clock accurate through a long sea voyage was very difficult. Pendulum clocks were good at keeping time on stable ground. Ships rolled, pitched, warmed, cooled, and battered their instruments. A sea captain needed a clock that would keep the time of a faraway port without being able to check that port's clock.

Galileo saw a possible answer beside Jupiter. If one of its moons disappeared into the planet's shadow at predictable intervals, observers around the world could consult the same astronomical event. Make a table of eclipse times for a reference meridian. Observe an eclipse locally. Compare the time printed in the table with the local solar time. The difference gives longitude.

In theory, the moons supplied a common clock. In practice, finding Jupiter and monitoring its small moons from a moving ship could be extremely awkward. Weather, the planet's visibility and the need for a telescope limited the scheme. On land, where the observer and instrument could be stable, the method was much more promising.

The difficulty was that the timetable had to be right. A few minutes of error would translate into a substantial mistake on a map. If the table said an eclipse was occurring at noon in Paris and you saw it when the Sun at your location indicated a time ten minutes different, you would infer a longitude difference of two and a half degrees. Near the equator that is a distance of roughly two hundred and eighty kilometers. A ten-minute mistake in the eclipse prediction could therefore become a large error on the ground. So a seemingly specialized astronomical calculation belonged to the business of finding the position of places on Earth.

It was also a particularly strange way to build an international clock. The reference time was not transmitted by a government or carried on a ship. It was made visible from space. Observatories could collaborate without possessing identical local clocks, provided they observed a common event and knew its predicted time in a shared reference system. The difficulty of synchronizing distant people by astronomy made the quality of the astronomical predictions a practical concern.

The innermost of Galileo's four large moons, Io, completes an orbit in about one day and eighteen hours: roughly forty-two and a half hours. The same kind of event happened often enough to build up a long record. On many passes, observers could watch the moon enter or emerge from Jupiter's shadow. With repeated observations, they could refine the period and predict future appearances.

There is a seductive assumption hidden in such a table. If the satellite goes around Jupiter with a regular period, perhaps the appearances should also come at perfectly regular intervals. But the telescope does not show the event itself. It collects light that left the event behind.

A missing distinction in the clock's design was going to become an experimental opportunity.

## Two cities, one sky

Before Rømer arrived in Paris, a French astronomer named Jean Picard had been making precise measurements of positions on Earth. He traveled to the island of Hven, between modern Denmark and Sweden, to compare observations from the site of Tycho Brahe's former observatory with observations made in Paris. Timing events associated with Jupiter's moons helped determine the longitude difference.

The young Rømer assisted Picard with the work. He later joined the observing program at the new Paris Observatory, founded in 1667. There he worked among astronomers who were accumulating and comparing predictions for Jupiter's satellites. Giovanni Domenico Cassini, one of the observatory's leading figures, made eclipse tables and had already encountered discrepancies between the predicted and observed times.

The discrepancies had a pattern. As Earth moved along the part of its orbit that increased its distance from Jupiter, Io's appearances tended to fall progressively behind the predicted schedule. Months later, when Earth was approaching Jupiter, they came increasingly early. The pattern returned as Earth went around the Sun.

Imagine a wall clock that loses a fraction of a second every day for months, then gradually gains time for the next several months. You would suspect something about its environment or mechanism. The puzzling feature here was that the moon itself had no obvious reason to change its orbit according to where observers happened to be on Earth.

One possibility was that the satellite's motion was less regular than the tables assumed. Planetary orbits are not completely isolated mechanical systems. Other moons exert gravitational pulls. Even today, a careful ephemeris has to account for genuine orbital variations. It was reasonable to demand more than a pleasing geometrical explanation before treating the discrepancy as a discovery about light.

The historical record leaves room for disagreement about the division of credit. Cassini was involved in identifying irregularities in the tables and at least considered a light-travel-time explanation. The extent of his contribution to the decisive claim has been debated by historians examining the academy's records. Rømer developed the argument and publicly defended it with a prediction that exposed the hypothesis to a test. The work depended on Picard's measurements and on years of repeated observations by several people.

There was no laboratory in Paris capable of sending a flash of light to Jupiter and asking it to return. The experiment had already been running in the sky. The astronomers had to understand what their instruments were measuring.

## The distance between an event and its report

We can reconstruct the main reasoning with nothing more complicated than a moving observer and a repeating signal.

Suppose there is a beacon that flashes at exactly the same interval, over and over. If you stand at a fixed distance from it, each flash reaches you after the same travel time. You might be far away, but that constant delay cancels when you calculate the interval between successive arrivals. The arrivals retain the beacon's rhythm.

Now start walking away from the beacon. After one flash, you have moved farther away before the next is emitted. The second flash has a longer route to travel. It arrives later than you would predict from the interval between emissions. If you keep moving away, each interval you measure is slightly stretched. Walk toward the beacon and the opposite happens: the next flash has a shorter distance to cover, and the intervals shrink.

Io's eclipse was the beacon. Earth was the moving observer. The light from a reappearance had to cross the changing gap between Jupiter and Earth. Io could maintain essentially the same orbit while observers recorded an apparent irregularity. A small increase in the travel time from eclipse to eclipse accumulated into minutes over a season.

This detail is important. Rømer was not asking seventeenth-century clocks to measure the few milliseconds it takes light to cross a city. He was looking for a slow drift built up while Earth traveled millions of kilometers through space. Nature had provided both a very long measuring baseline and a repeating signal.

Try the arithmetic with modern values. Light travels about three hundred thousand kilometers in one second. In a single minute it can travel approximately eighteen million kilometers. The diameter of Earth's orbit is roughly three hundred million kilometers. Light needs around sixteen minutes and forty seconds to traverse that extra distance.

That is large enough to appear in months of astronomical observations, provided the timetable is reliable. Nobody needs to see a ray of light crawling across space. The observer only needs to distinguish an event from the delayed signal announcing the event.

There are complications. Earth and Jupiter both move, so the distance between them changes in a more elaborate way than a simple picture of Earth sitting at the two opposite ends of a straight line. Io's shadow events cannot all be observed at every point in the planets' orbits. The moon's motion has perturbations of its own. Real predictions must handle these details. But the central travel-time effect changes sign according to whether the observer is approaching or receding, and that reversal is a powerful clue.

Instruments commonly reveal things because a signal is changed while traveling. Here, the signal's travel time was the thing that needed to be measured.

## The ten-minute appointment

In 1676 Rømer made his proposed explanation answerable to a specific forecast. The eclipse schedule constructed from earlier observations did not merely have to fit events that had already been seen. A later reappearance of Io would be delayed by about ten minutes compared with a calculation that ignored the growing light-travel time.

Accounts of the observation place the test in November. A contemporary report associated with Rømer's presentation to the French Royal Academy of Sciences described the anticipated delay, and the observation on November 9 corresponded to it. On November 21 he presented the argument to the academy. A short report appeared in the *Journal des Sçavans* in December.

The event was not a ten-minute pause in the moon's orbit. Nor was the claimed delay the time that light took to travel the whole distance from Jupiter. It was the accumulated difference between one observation geometry and another: an extra journey imposed by Earth's changed position.

The distinction saved the prediction from an obvious objection. People already knew that light from Jupiter had to cross a great distance if its speed was finite. The crucial experimental question was whether a changing distance would alter the observed times in a systematic and forecastable way.

Rømer estimated that light would take roughly twenty-two minutes to cross a distance equal to the diameter of Earth's orbit. The modern value for that distance is about sixteen minutes and forty seconds. His figure was too large, and our distance estimates, instruments and orbital models are vastly better. But the result was of the right order and came from observations of a tiny moon hundreds of millions of kilometers away.

Notice what Rømer actually had: a travel duration. To turn a travel duration into a speed measured in kilometers per second, one also needs the length of the route in kilometers. Seventeenth-century estimates of the dimensions of the solar system were uncertain. Later accounts sometimes attribute to Rømer a crisp numerical value for the speed of light that he did not publish in that form. Christiaan Huygens used the astronomical result and a distance estimate to attempt a numerical speed calculation. The familiar modern number is the result of much later measurement and standardization.

This makes the original experiment, in one way, more interesting. Rømer could establish the essential claim before possessing a sufficiently accurate terrestrial ruler for the solar system. The regularity in the eclipse times carried information about how light traveled even when the distance scale was imperfect.

Skepticism did not evaporate in November. A successful prediction could be interpreted within disputes about the satellite tables, the precision of observations and the nature of light itself. Cassini remained unconvinced by the full claim, and others defended instantaneous propagation. Rømer had supplied an argument that could be improved and tested, not an authority that ended every argument on the spot.

## A second kind of delay

A few decades later, astronomers found another way that finite light speed makes itself visible. This time the clue was not an eclipse arriving late. It was a star appearing in a slightly different position during different seasons.

In the 1720s, James Bradley and Samuel Molyneux studied the apparent position of stars, partly in hopes of detecting the small geometric shifts caused by Earth's movement around the Sun. One star, Gamma Draconis, behaved in a way the expected annual parallax could not explain. The apparent direction shifted over the year, but the timing was wrong for the explanation they had been looking for.

Bradley eventually recognized an effect now called the aberration of light. The direction from which starlight appears to arrive depends slightly on Earth's motion while the light enters the telescope. A common analogy is walking through vertical rain. Even if the rain falls straight down with respect to the ground, someone moving sideways will experience it as slanting toward them. The apparent slant depends on the ratio of the sideways speed to the downward speed.

For starlight, the sideways motion is Earth's orbital speed. The much larger speed is light's. The apparent stellar shift is tiny, but measurable. Its yearly pattern gives an independent way to estimate the ratio between the speed of Earth and the speed of light.

The analogy with rain is only an introductory picture of the geometry; a modern relativistic account is more precise. Bradley's result nevertheless gave astronomers evidence of finite propagation without relying on Jupiter's moons. Two very different astronomical puzzles now pointed in the same direction.

Then experimenters began shortening the distance. In 1849, Hippolyte Fizeau measured light travel time along a terrestrial route with a rapidly rotating toothed wheel and a distant reflector. A beam passed through a gap in the wheel, traveled to a mirror and back, and could be blocked by a tooth that rotated into the gap while the light was away. The wheel became a fast shutter. Its rotation rate and the path length together supplied the timing information.

It was an extraordinary change of scale. Rømer had needed the solar system to make the delay measurable. Fizeau used an engineered instrument to make the delay observable over a few kilometers. The underlying question was the same: how long does a signal take to cross a known distance?

Galileo had once contemplated testing whether light took time by uncovering lanterns on distant hills. The difficulty was that human reactions and crude timing instruments were vastly too slow for such a short journey. A beam of light crosses one kilometer in roughly three millionths of a second. If the observer waits to see it, decides to respond and uncovers another lantern, the human actions overwhelm the travel time. Fizeau's wheel did not need a person to react at that speed. Its motion supplied the timing mechanism.

Over the following century, the experiments became steadily more precise. The measured number approached three hundred thousand kilometers per second in vacuum. As rotating mirrors, interferometers and eventually lasers replaced the older devices, the problem of measuring a very short travel time became a problem of constructing instruments stable enough to reveal tiny differences. A laboratory that could not produce a sufficiently brief flash could instead compare the phase or frequency of waves with immense precision. The light itself had not changed. Engineers had learned to use its regularity in a different way.

After a while, we grew so confident in the number that the way we used it reversed.

## A speed that became a ruler

In 1983, the international body responsible for the metric system redefined the meter in terms of the distance light travels in vacuum during a specified fraction of a second. The numerical value of the speed of light was fixed at exactly 299,792,458 meters per second. Today's wording of the International System of Units expresses the same relationship through defining constants.

That can sound circular if we forget the history. Scientists originally compared distances and times to determine the speed. As clocks and optical-frequency measurements became much more reproducible than the older physical length standards, it became practical to fix the speed as a constant in the definition and use measurements of time to establish distances.

The number is exact by definition. That does not mean every practical measurement is exact; instruments still have uncertainty, and the speed quoted is specifically the speed of light in vacuum. In glass, water and air, light's propagation behaves differently. The unit's definition supplies a stable reference while the actual work of measuring a laboratory object still requires care.

Rømer would have recognized something familiar in the idea of extracting distance from timing. His eclipse observations needed an independent idea of how large Earth's orbit was. Modern metrology uses precise timing and the defined speed of light to realize lengths. The tools have changed almost beyond comparison. The relation between travel time and distance remains at the center.

A modern satellite navigation receiver encounters a related problem whenever it calculates your location. A GPS satellite broadcasts a radio signal that includes information about when the signal was sent and where the satellite was. A receiver measures arrival times from several satellites, accounts for clock errors and the journey through Earth's atmosphere, and infers its position from the signal travel times. The signals are electromagnetic waves, so the vacuum speed of light is fundamental to the calculation. From an orbit more than twenty thousand kilometers above Earth, a signal typically takes over sixty milliseconds to reach the ground. That is long enough to ruin accurate navigation if ignored.

The GPS receiver is also a lesson in everything that has become more complicated since Rømer's day. Its clocks must be synchronized; it must use multiple signals to solve for its own clock offset; the satellites and Earth are moving; and relativistic corrections are required. The principle is recognizable: an arrival time is partly a report about an event and partly a report about the distance the message traveled. What an astronomer once regarded as an irritating disturbance of the eclipse timetable has become a basic ingredient in finding one's way across Earth.

There is a tempting final historical symmetry here. Rømer returned to Copenhagen in 1681, helped improve astronomical instruments, worked on standards of weights and measures and took on substantial public duties. A man celebrated for timing light also spent part of his career helping a state agree on quantities such as distance. Much of his unpublished observational material was lost in Copenhagen's great fire of 1728, after his death. The historical record we use to reconstruct his astronomical reasoning is thinner than the work itself once was.

That loss is a warning against imagining that the four-page account from 1676 contained a complete modern theory. It was a compact public report of an argument made in a larger working culture: observations, tables, corrections, correspondence, disagreement, instruments and calculations. We can admire its result while remembering how much of the work is hard to recover.

I have spent this lecture discussing a tiny moon mostly as a celestial timepiece. In reality, Io is a violent and fascinating world: a body reshaped by tidal heating, with volcanoes erupting across its surface. The astronomers of the seventeenth century knew almost nothing about that geology. For their purposes, the useful property was that the moon repeatedly vanished into Jupiter's shadow and came back into view.

Imagine watching that reappearance through a telescope today. Io is where the orbit says it should be. Jupiter is where the ephemeris places it. Your clock can be excellent, and still the observed event is older than the instant shown on the clock. Light needed time to cross the distance between the moon and the glass.

The moon was on time. The news had to travel.

### Technical companion (not spoken)

If an astronomical event occurs at time `t_event`, its observed arrival (in a simplified fixed-distance approximation) is `t_obs = t_event + D/c`. When the Earth–Jupiter distance changes from `D1` to `D2`, the resulting difference in propagation delay is approximately `(D2 - D1)/c`. The modern diameter of Earth's orbit is approximately `2 AU = 299,195,742 km`; dividing by `c = 299,792.458 km/s` gives approximately 998 seconds, or 16 minutes 38 seconds. The commonly quoted comparison is rounded to 16 minutes 40 seconds. Real ephemerides require dynamical corrections and accurately calculated changing geometry.
