# The Pond That Needs to Dry

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

A woodland pond can be crowded in April and gone by August.

On a mild spring night, when rain has softened the last crusts of snow, wood frogs travel through leaf litter toward a shallow depression in the forest. Their calls are abrupt and nasal. A crowd of them can sound, improbably, like ducks arguing in a drainage ditch. Spotted salamanders may arrive at the same basin from the surrounding woods. They move slowly enough to be overlooked, but the journey is urgent. The warm, wet interval that lets them cross exposed ground is brief, and there is breeding to be done before the water retreats.

The pond is not grand. It may have no stream feeding it, no stream draining it, and no name on a map. Fallen leaves lie underwater. A stick points out of the surface. From above, there is little to distinguish it from a low spot that happens to be flooded.

By late summer, that same hollow can be dry. Ferns press in at the edges. The leaves have settled into the soil. A visitor who never saw the spring water might reasonably doubt that there had been a pond there at all.

The trouble begins when that visitor decides the dry basin is an unsuccessful pond.

In the northeastern United States, many such places are called vernal pools: seasonal wetlands that fill with rain, snowmelt, or rising groundwater and later lose their standing water. Some fill in autumn and last into summer. Others gather water in spring. Not all dry every single year. Their timing varies with soils, weather, topography, and the size of the basin.

The water's disappearance is part of the habitat. The animals that breed there have arranged their lives around it. A permanent pond would support a different community, and a pond that dried too early would fail another way. A calendar is hidden in the mud. Ecology has written its appointments into the weather.

I want to argue for a habit of thought that this small, vanishing place rewards: when something changes, disappears, or sits apparently unused, the appropriate question is not always how to make it more permanent. Sometimes the task is to understand the cycle well enough to avoid interrupting it.

## The clock in the dirt

A pool's hydroperiod is the pattern of its flooding and drying: when water arrives, how deep it becomes, how long it remains, and when it recedes. The term sounds technical, but it describes a practical bargain made by the animals in the basin.

Fish are excellent at eating the eggs and young of many amphibians. A permanent pond can sustain fish from one year to the next. A small seasonal wetland that dries completely cannot support a resident fish population through the dry interval. In spring, that gives certain frogs and salamanders breeding space with fewer of their most formidable aquatic predators.

Fewer predators does not mean no predators. Newts, aquatic insects, turtles, and other animals still take eggs and larvae. A vernal pool is not a nursery behind locked gates. It has simply excluded one class of resident that can make life especially difficult for eggs and tadpoles.

There is a price for that advantage. Every egg laid in the water is a wager on the date when the water will leave.

A frog egg develops into a tadpole. The tadpole feeds, grows legs, changes its breathing, and eventually becomes a small frog capable of leaving the pool. A salamander's path is different in detail but similar in its dependence on water during its early development. For many breeding species, the young must reach a terrestrial stage before the basin dries. If the water vanishes too soon, larvae may be stranded before they are ready. A summer that looks pleasantly dry to the walker can mean a reproductive failure to the creatures that started their year there.

The balance is therefore stranger than a simple preference for wet or dry conditions. A pool that holds water for several months may support animals that cannot finish their development in a pool that dries after a few weeks. A basin that stays wet through almost every summer has other possibilities, including predators that shorter-lived pools can exclude. Different hydroperiods create different combinations of inhabitants.

This is one reason an ecological map with only two categories, water and land, throws away so much of the story. The pond may belong to both categories, at different times, and its inhabitants depend on the sequence.

The idea is almost embarrassingly simple once stated. Yet much of our everyday language for landscapes describes what is present in a particular instant. A pond is a blue shape. A forest is a green one. A field is open ground. The words are nouns, settled and solid. The places themselves are doing things: filling, drying, sprouting, migrating, burning, recovering, eroding, and rebuilding.

The mistake is to treat the noun as more fundamental than the activity.

## A city whose citizens live elsewhere

The pool may be the most visible part of a wood frog's annual itinerary, but it is not the animal's whole world.

Adult wood frogs spend much of the year away from their breeding water. They live among the leaf litter and damp cover of the surrounding landscape. Spotted salamanders are even easier to forget. Outside their breeding season, they spend much of their lives concealed in the forest, using underground refuges and the shelter of logs and soil. A person can own a piece of woodland for years without meeting one.

Then a rainy spring night brings the landscape into motion.

In some places, the amphibians cross a road between upland shelter and the pools where they breed. From the perspective of a driver, the asphalt is the ordinary route and the frogs are temporary obstructions. From the perspective of the frogs, the road is a recently imposed barrier across an older annual journey. Those two routes occupy the same surface for an hour or two, and the encounter can be lethal for the slower travelers.

Local groups sometimes monitor these migrations, temporarily restrict traffic, or build passage structures where conditions permit. A useful road crossing is an ingenious piece of conservation engineering, but it points to something larger. Protecting the water alone does not protect the animal.

A development plan might preserve the basin exactly where it is and clear the trees around it. The footprint of the pond would remain untouched. Yet its shade, leaf litter, water balance, and the terrestrial habitat used by adults could all change. A narrow ring of protection might save the blue patch on a map while leaving the amphibians nowhere safe to spend the rest of their year.

Even the term *isolated wetland* can mislead. A pool may have no permanent stream connecting it to another pond while still being connected to the surrounding forest by animals, water moving through soil, fallen leaves, and seasonal movements. The phrase tells us something about the visible surface water. It does not describe every ecological connection.

A woodland pool, in this sense, resembles a town square more than a self-contained apartment building. Many of its visitors live elsewhere. They converge for a particular part of life, then disperse. The square can be almost empty without losing its purpose. Its continued usefulness depends partly on the streets and homes beyond it.

There is no need to make the analogy perfect. Salamanders have no town council, and a forest is not a human city in miniature. The comparison simply corrects the picture of a breeding pool as an isolated container of water. Its boundaries are larger and more mobile than its shoreline.

## The animals that wait in the dirt

The woodland pools of the Northeast are only one version of this seasonal world. California has a different and extraordinary family of vernal pools.

Across parts of California, winter rain collects in shallow depressions where an underlying layer of clay or hardened soil keeps the water from draining freely. Through winter and spring the basins hold water. As evaporation and plant growth draw it down, the basins emerge into sun. By summer many appear to be patches of hard, cracked earth in the middle of grassland.

Some of these places are inhabited by fairy shrimp. The name is extravagant for an animal so small. Fairy shrimp are freshwater crustaceans with many pairs of feathery swimming legs. They often swim upside down, moving their legs in waves that carry them through temporary pools. They do not look like the shrimp on a seafood plate. They look like something a pond dreamed up after being given a limited budget for appendages.

A number of fairy shrimp species are adapted to seasonal water. The California vernal pool fairy shrimp, for example, lays resting eggs called cysts. The cysts can persist in the dry basin after the adults have died and the water is gone. Rain and suitable conditions can later trigger hatching. Some may remain dormant through more than one cycle, an insurance policy written into the life history of a creature that has no way to call the weather office.

When the pool is dry, its animal population has not simply vanished. Part of it is waiting in another form.

The plants have their own calendar. California vernal pools can develop bands of flowers as the water retreats. Different species occupy zones that experience different amounts and durations of flooding. The rings can be bright enough to make the outline of a disappearing pond more visible after the water has begun to leave than while it was full.

This is an almost perfect answer to the question of whether a landscape has to stay visually the same to be preserved. What appears in winter as reflective water may become, in spring, concentric bands of blooming plants and, in summer, a dry basin sheltering dormant seeds and eggs. The continuity lies in the sequence and in the organisms adapted to it.

It is tempting to call the dry period a pause. From the perspective of a fairy shrimp cyst, however, survival during the pause is active in a different sense. Its protective structures, the chemistry of dormancy, and the timing of hatching are the means by which the species makes use of a place that cannot promise water all year.

One qualification is worth keeping. The California grassland pools and the northeastern woodland pools are not interchangeable scenery. They differ in climate, soils, plants, and inhabitants. Even within either region, one pool can behave differently from its neighbor. Their common lesson is about seasonal timing; their biological details deserve their own names.

## A well-meant improvement

Suppose a landowner finds a shallow seasonal pool and decides to help it.

The water disappears every summer. Surely deeper would be better. A little excavation could make a permanent pond. Then frogs would have more time to develop, waterfowl would have a reliable place to land, and the landscape would hold water even in a dry spell. It sounds, at first, like generosity expressed with a shovel.

For some management goals, a longer-lasting water body can indeed be valuable. Certain amphibians need more time to complete their development, and climate change may make longer-holding pools important refuges. A new pond created in the right place for the right purpose may serve wildlife well. There is no universal rule that shorter hydroperiods are better.

But deepening an existing vernal pool without understanding its ecology may change the conditions that support the community already there. Fish might become established if the new basin can sustain them and they are introduced or reach it by a suitable connection. Changes in depth, sunlight, temperature, soil disturbance, and vegetation may favor different species. Excavating the bottom can damage dormant eggs and seeds. A small intervention can replace the original habitat with another kind of water body while leaving the word *pond* unchanged.

There is a related temptation in the other direction: a wet patch of ground is inconvenient, so it is drained, filled, graded, or connected to a ditch. The owner acquires reliable dry land. The seasonal ecosystem loses the hydrology on which it depends. Depending on the site, the loss may be invisible from a distance. There is no grand lake to remember, no river to rename, and no dramatic shoreline retreat. Someone has merely made the property a little more predictable for a different use.

Predictability is a real human good. Houses should not fill with water. Roads need foundations. Farmers and builders make difficult decisions about drainage, safety, and livelihoods. Conservation is not an instruction to keep every muddy depression untouched regardless of its circumstances. The relevant question is what the place does, what would change, who would bear the costs, and whether the same human goal can be achieved with less damage.

The little pond offers a sharp warning about apparently generous improvements. A project can supply more of the thing we notice—more water, more permanence, a cleaner edge—while taking away the conditions that made the original place productive. The intended benefit and the ecological loss are both real. We need enough information to compare them, rather than treating a longer-lived pool as an automatic upgrade.

## The surveyor arrives in August

Imagine the annual inspection of a property falling on an August afternoon. This requires no villain. The people conducting the survey are conscientious, trained, and working under a deadline. They record what they can observe.

The woodland hollow is dry. The soil is dark and leaf-strewn, and perhaps there are marks on tree trunks showing where water once stood. Without the right experience, or without evidence from a wetter season, the place may look like ordinary forest floor. A map made from a summer image may miss the spring water. A map made from a spring image may show the water without revealing how quickly it dries or which animals depend on it.

The error is not that one map lies. Each tells the truth about its moment. The error comes from asking a single moment to testify about a cycle.

Massachusetts has an instructive approach to part of this problem. Its Natural Heritage and Endangered Species Program maintains records of certified vernal pools. Biological evidence, including breeding activity and characteristic species, can establish that a basin functions as this kind of habitat. Certification can inform land-use decisions and contribute to legal protections under applicable regulations. Certification by itself is not a universal promise that every square foot around a pool is protected; jurisdiction and other conditions still have to be considered.

That distinction is less dramatic than a slogan about saving wetlands, but it is practically valuable. There is a difference between observing a pool, documenting its ecological function, identifying applicable law, and designing effective protection. Each step can succeed or fail independently.

Community observations can help. A pool visited in April, June, and September reveals more than one photograph taken on the driest day. Egg masses, calling frogs, signs of earlier high water, and the pattern of drying all add evidence. So do repeated observations over several years, because one dry summer may be quite different from another.

The same principle applies when the creatures are absent. A salamander that spends most of the year underground is not evidence against the pool's importance when it cannot be found. A fairy shrimp that survives the summer as a resting egg does not announce itself to the land survey. The absence of an adult animal at the wrong season is not the absence of a population.

This poses a problem for institutions that must make decisions on schedules. Permits are issued on dates. Surveys are commissioned for particular months. Development plans have deadlines. Wildlife has life cycles. Good procedure cannot abolish that mismatch, but it can acknowledge it and collect the evidence the decision actually needs.

A place that is difficult to recognize for ten months of the year can be easiest to destroy during those ten months. The time when it looks least like habitat may be the time when its protection needs the most imagination.

## Climate writes in pencil

There is another complication. The calendar is changing.

In a warming climate, the fate of a seasonal pool cannot be predicted from annual rainfall alone. The timing of rain, the amount of snow, the date of spring thaw, the rate of evaporation, the shade provided by surrounding trees, and the demands of plants all influence how long water remains. A region can receive more precipitation overall while particular pools lose their water earlier during the breeding season. Other pools may persist longer, at least in some years. The outcomes are local and uneven.

A 2021 study by Jennifer Cartwright, Toni Lyn Morelli, and Evan Campbell Grant used thousands of field observations from hundreds of northeastern vernal pools to investigate which ones were likely to remain wet later into the season. The motivation was not to find the single perfect pond. It was to identify pools that might continue to provide breeding habitat in dry conditions—places that could serve as refuges as climate patterns shift.

This is an elegant kind of ecological forecasting because it begins by admitting that the most valuable unit may be a *set of different pools*. A shallow basin that dries early can favor one group of organisms. A larger, deeper basin that holds water longer can support others and may buffer a drought year. The distances between basins, and the forest through which animals move, affect whether populations can recover from local failures.

The year-to-year variability is part of the difficulty. One breeding season might succeed spectacularly. Another may end with the water gone before the tadpoles have grown legs. That does not mean every unsuccessful season marks a permanently ruined habitat, nor does it mean that repeated early drying is harmless. Ecologists have to distinguish normal variation from sustained deterioration, and a land manager has to make decisions before the future sequence of wet and dry years can be known.

Human planning often prefers a target that can be checked once: plant this many trees, preserve this many acres, maintain this much water. Those measures can be useful. They are poor substitutes for understanding a dynamic process. A pool cannot be judged solely by the amount of water visible on inspection day, any more than a breeding population can be judged by the number of adults seen on one afternoon.

The climate problem is particularly sobering because the schedule cannot be protected by a fence. A boundary around a pond does not keep spring temperatures, rainstorms, or summer droughts outside. Conserving a seasonal habitat therefore involves protecting a range of conditions and possibilities, not simply maintaining a picturesque appearance.

## Keeping a place that disappears

I am drawn to these pools because they defeat a familiar assumption about care: that to care for something is to make it last longer in the form in which we first admired it.

There are times when this instinct is exactly right. A roof should keep out rain. A bridge should remain standing. A patient should receive reliable treatment. People have good reasons to want stable things. We have learned the costs of systems that fail unexpectedly, and it would be foolish to romanticize breakdown as an ecological virtue.

A vernal pool presents a different kind of continuity. Its water can vanish while its ecological function persists. The adults have gone back to the woods. The young that survived have left the pool. Some eggs and seeds remain in the basin. The forest canopy shades the hollow; the soils still collect water. When the season turns, the cycle may begin again.

This does not guarantee that the pool is healthy. A basin can become too dry to support successful breeding. It can be degraded by salt, runoff, excavation, or the loss of surrounding forest. A vanished pond is not automatically a thriving one. The ecological claim is narrower and more useful: a seasonal absence of water is compatible with a functioning habitat, and sometimes essential to it.

The idea reaches beyond wetlands. A meadow cut too often may never flower or set seed. A seasonal river's pulse can shape the life downstream. Migratory animals depend on places that may be occupied only briefly. The timing of an event can be a property of an ecosystem every bit as consequential as its location. The examples differ, and each needs its own evidence, but the conceptual mistake is common. We protect the object we can see and overlook the schedule that makes it work.

There is something quietly challenging here for a culture that likes permanent upgrades. A dried pond seems to invite correction. A lawn seems improved when every patch is equally green. A landscape seems easier to manage when nothing surprising happens. But sometimes the variation is the design, and our intervention is the surprise.

I do not want a world in which people stand helplessly before every patch of mud, afraid to move a stone. I want decisions informed by the possibility that the stone is part of a larger arrangement. The point of ecological knowledge is to improve our choices, not paralyze them.

An ordinary wooded hollow, flooded in April and dry in August, deserves at least the dignity of being understood before it is improved. It has no permanent shoreline and little chance of appearing in a tourist brochure. Its annual work is performed by bodies small enough to fit beneath a fallen leaf, by rains that arrive on no reliable timetable, and by soil that remembers the water after the water has gone.

In summer, nothing may be swimming there. That is one of the ways the pond survives.

## Author's argument and its limits (not spoken)

The essay advances a normative argument about treating ecological processes and temporal patterns as objects of conservation. This is the author's synthesis, not a scientific theorem or a position attributed to any cited agency. The analogies to maps, cities, permanent upgrades and human infrastructure are explanatory analogies, not claims of ecological equivalence. Terms such as *purpose* and *calendar* are metaphors; the pools do not have intentions or goals.
