# Make a Place for the Bad News

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

A team is preparing to release a new piece of software. The tests pass. The demo works. The customer has seen the schedule. At the last review, somebody near the end of the table says, "I think there may be a problem."

The room has a choice to make before anybody knows whether the warning is correct.

The team can ask for evidence. It can identify the consequences if the warning turns out to be right. It can set aside enough time to investigate. Or it can make the person wish they had remained quiet.

The second response need not be dramatic. Nobody has to shout. An exhausted manager might say, "We've already been through this." A colleague might ask why the objection wasn't raised earlier. Someone else might point out that reopening the decision would disappoint the customer. The speaker may be permitted to finish the sentence, but the room has already taught everyone a lesson about what happens to unwelcome information.

This scene is invented. I suspect its structure will be familiar to people who have worked on projects of almost any kind.

What matters is that the room has not yet established whether the warning was right. It has already established something else: the price of being the person who raises it.

I want to argue that our ability to learn is limited not only by our intelligence, but by the social arrangements through which information reaches us. An individual can be careful, intellectually honest, and willing to revise a belief, and still belong to an organization that makes those virtues costly to exercise.

We often talk about humility as a trait of the individual: being willing to admit error, to say "I don't know," or to accept criticism. All of that matters. But the next question is harder. What happens after somebody admits the error? Is there a process that can use the admission? Does the warning have a route to a person who can act? Does the person who brought it receive an answer? If the warning is mistaken, can the organization explain why without treating the question itself as an offense?

An institution that depends on good news is not merely optimistic. It is cutting itself off from some of the information it most needs.

## What a report is for

In aviation, the people closest to a hazard often encounter it before anyone else knows it exists. A confusing instruction, an ambiguous display, a misleading phrase, a procedure that encourages an unsafe shortcut: any of these may nearly cause an accident without producing one. If the event leaves no injury and no damaged aircraft, a conventional record of accidents will never contain it.

Yet the people involved have learned something. The question is whether that learning remains private.

Since 1976, NASA has operated the Aviation Safety Reporting System, usually called ASRS. Pilots, air traffic controllers, maintenance personnel, cabin crews, and other participants can voluntarily report incidents, near misses, hazards, and practices worth sharing. NASA receives the reports, removes identifying information, analyzes what was submitted, and makes de-identified information available to the aviation community. The system can also issue safety alerts to organizations able to investigate or correct a problem.

There is a reason NASA, rather than the federal aviation regulator, receives the information. A person who has made an error has reasons to fear giving a regulator a signed confession. The design separates the collection of safety information from ordinary enforcement. The Federal Aviation Administration offers limited protections in qualifying circumstances. They are not blanket immunity: eligibility has conditions, and reports of accidents or criminal activity are outside important parts of the protection.

I am interested in the design choice more than the reassuring word "confidential." The system treats information about human fallibility as a resource that cannot be obtained reliably if the people who possess it believe disclosure will automatically injure them.

Nor does the system pretend that every report is an established fact. NASA warns that these accounts are voluntary and not independently verified. Reporting is selective. The existence of many accounts of a hazard does not, by itself, tell us how frequently that hazard occurs in the entire aviation system. A reporter may misremember an event, misunderstand what another person did, or see only one part of a larger sequence.

That limitation doesn't make the reports worthless. It defines their job. They are clues: material for investigation, training, research, and possible alerts. They reveal situations that might otherwise remain invisible. You would not use a pile of voluntarily submitted near-miss stories as a statistically representative census of all flights. But you might discover a recurring ambiguity that deserves a carefully designed study.

This is a broader lesson. An institution should not demand that every concern arrive already proven. If it does, the institution is asking the person with the concern to perform the institution's investigative work before they are allowed to be heard. At the same time, the institution must not confuse a report with a verdict. The point is to turn one person's observation into something that can be checked.

A successful reporting channel moves information from private unease to public examination without pretending that the journey is complete at the moment of submission.

## Why a worse-looking number can be good news

There is a measurement problem hiding in this story.

Suppose one workplace has one hundred errors in a year, but people report only twenty of them. A second workplace has eighty errors and people report sixty. If management counts the reports and calls the higher number a worse safety record, it will announce that the second workplace is failing, even though our imaginary numbers say the opposite about the underlying errors.

The arithmetic is simple. The information problem is not.

A report count depends on at least two things: how many reportable events occurred, and how likely people were to report them. Changes in either quantity can change the count. If people begin trusting the reporting process, the number of recorded incidents may rise even while the actual number of incidents falls. If a frightened workforce stops reporting, the dashboard may improve while the organization becomes less informed.

This is a constructed example, not an estimate of actual aviation or hospital performance. It illustrates why a measure of observed events is not automatically a measure of events that occurred.

Amy Edmondson's research gives the issue a human dimension. In work on error detection and correction in hospital patient-care teams, she investigated how group conditions affect whether errors are noticed and discussed. In a later 1999 study of fifty-one manufacturing teams, she examined what she called psychological safety: the shared belief that a team is safe for interpersonal risk-taking. She found an association between that sense of safety and learning behavior in the teams she studied.

The phrase can sound as if it belongs to an office poster. But think about what interpersonal risk includes. It includes asking a question whose answer everyone else seems to know. It includes saying a senior colleague may be mistaken. It includes acknowledging that your own work contains a defect. These actions can expose incompetence, or at least the appearance of incompetence, to people whose judgments affect your standing.

If a team learns that raising errors is dangerous, its members may learn to hide them. And if outsiders later count only errors that have been reported, they may reward the team that has learned least about its own problems.

None of this means that more reports prove a healthier organization. An actual increase in hazards can also produce more reports. A reporting system can be flooded with low-quality accounts or manipulated for personal reasons. The right response is to interpret the evidence, not to reverse the scoreboard and declare the highest report count the winner.

But we should notice how easily a superficially sensible demand can become destructive. "We want fewer incidents" is a defensible aspiration. "We want fewer incident reports" may be a demand that people stop telling us what is happening.

A truthful institution must be able to tolerate numbers that look worse before it understands whether reality has become worse.

## The warning that cannot get upstairs

A reporting channel is useful only if someone can act on what travels through it. The failure to transmit important information has sometimes had catastrophic consequences.

On February 1, 2003, the space shuttle Columbia broke apart during reentry, killing all seven members of its crew. During launch, insulating foam from the external tank had struck the orbiter's left wing. The investigation later concluded that the strike damaged the thermal protection system, allowing superheated gas to enter during reentry. But the physical breach was not the whole explanation of the disaster.

The Columbia Accident Investigation Board devoted extensive attention to organizational causes: the status of engineering concerns, the treatment of risk, the routing of requests for imagery, the authority of safety functions, and the influence of schedules and past success on decision-making.

One disturbing feature of the report is how much information existed without acquiring the institutional force it needed. Engineers had concerns about the debris impact. Some wanted imagery that might clarify the damage. Different groups possessed pieces of the situation. The process by which questions, requests, and assessments traveled upward did not reliably preserve their meaning or urgency.

I want to be careful here. An investigator looking backward has evidence that the people making decisions did not possess at the time. It would be a mistake to tell the story as if one person had a complete and correct diagnosis and everyone else simply refused to listen. The board's account is more complicated. Engineering uncertainty was not resolved adequately; organizational barriers kept dissenting technical views from receiving an effective hearing; and the resulting decisions were made inside a system that had grown accustomed to explaining away departures from expected performance.

The board identified cultural traits that included reliance on earlier success instead of sufficient testing, barriers to the communication of critical safety information, and the suppression of professional disagreement. It also examined how hierarchy affected whose information counted.

One of the lessons is that technical organizations can be excellent at solving many engineering problems while remaining poor at governing disagreement about which engineering problems exist.

No matter how sophisticated the instruments are, information eventually passes through meetings, briefings, management chains, and human judgment. During that passage, uncertainty can become certainty without any new measurement. A cautious technical statement can be shortened into a reassurance. A request for more evidence can be treated as an objection already answered. A question about a possible hazard can be reframed as a challenge to the team's competence.

When that happens, the system has performed a kind of destructive compression. It has reduced the amount of uncertainty in the message by deleting the uncertainty, not by resolving it.

An organization can make impeccable charts and still be operating with an impoverished picture of reality.

## Who must prove what?

Suppose an engineer has evidence that a component has behaved outside its expected conditions. There are two questions a review meeting might ask.

The first is: "Can you prove that this will cause a failure?"

The second is: "What would we need to know before we could reasonably regard this as safe?"

These questions are not interchangeable. Neither guarantees a good answer. But they distribute the burden of uncertainty differently.

The first can be appropriate when assessing a proposed change that would impose a cost or restriction. We should not halt all activity whenever somebody imagines a remote possibility. Yet if a system is operating outside the conditions under which its safety has been established, demanding proof of imminent catastrophe may be an impossible standard. Lack of such proof is then converted into reassurance, though nothing has been learned about the unknown condition.

The second question does not presume disaster. It asks whether the evidence supports the confidence being claimed. It makes room for an answer such as, "We have not tested this regime," or, "Our model is being used beyond the range where it was validated." Those are meaningful statements, even if the system turns out to be safe.

The Columbia board criticized a pattern in which management's preconceived conclusions and organizational structure limited serious engagement with contrary technical information. That is not a general rule that all dissenters are right. It is a warning about a decision procedure that cannot discover when a dissenter is right.

Sometimes the dissenter has misunderstood the data. Sometimes the majority has. The point of a technical review is to distinguish these possibilities while decisions can still change.

I think this is where humility becomes an engineering property. We cannot guarantee that the best judgment will always win. We can design a process in which a contrary observation has a fair chance to reach someone with the competence, authority, and time to investigate it.

## A room where disagreement is allowed to matter

The phrase "psychological safety" is sometimes used so loosely that it can seem to mean never having an uncomfortable conversation. That would be a poor theory of how people learn.

Imagine a laboratory in which everyone speaks warmly, nobody interrupts, and every proposed explanation receives praise. It might be pleasant. It might also be a terrible laboratory if nobody ever says that an experiment failed to distinguish two hypotheses, or that a result cannot be reproduced, or that a colleague has made an error in the analysis.

Now imagine another laboratory in which criticism is sharp, evidence is expected, and weak arguments are challenged. This second laboratory might be a better place to learn, provided that criticism of a claim does not become a permanent stain on the person who offered it. The standard can be demanding without making intellectual risk professionally ruinous.

There is a difference between a harsh standard and a hostile environment. In a harsh standard, a mistaken proof fails, a defective analysis is rejected, and an unsupported claim is not published. In a hostile environment, the person who noticed a defect is punished for bringing it up, or the person who made the mistake learns that concealment is safer than correction. The first protects the work. The second protects appearances.

Of course, people can sincerely disagree about which environment they are in. A manager may believe the team is free to raise concerns because nobody has been explicitly forbidden to speak. An employee may know from experience that raising a concern means becoming the person asked to explain every delay. A public invitation is not the same as a credible guarantee.

An especially revealing test is what happens when the correction is expensive. Anyone can welcome a suggestion that improves a result at no cost. Will the same people welcome the finding that the result is invalid and the experiment must be repeated? Will the team let a junior engineer show that a celebrated demonstration concealed a failure? Will a manager let the evidence overturn a schedule that management already promised?

This is not an argument for letting every objection hold a project hostage. A team needs a procedure for deciding when the evidence justifies further investigation and when it can reasonably proceed. But the procedure should make its reasons inspectable. If a concern is rejected, the reporter should be able to learn what evidence or reasoning supported that rejection. "We've decided to move on" may be a decision; it is not, by itself, an answer.

People do not need a guarantee that they will always persuade the room. They need a fair opportunity to persuade it with evidence.

## Fairness is not the absence of consequences

There is an immediate objection to all of this. If people can report their own mistakes without facing consequences, won't some of them become careless? Might a clever employee describe deliberate misconduct as a harmless error? Could a policy intended to encourage honesty become a shield for people who should be held responsible?

Yes, those are real design problems. A system that cannot distinguish a slip from reckless behavior is not a serious accountability system.

In patient safety, an approach often called a "just culture" tries to make that distinction explicit. Guidance from the United States Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality separates ordinary human error, at-risk behavior, and reckless behavior. A slip or lapse may call for better system design, training, or support. A shortcut whose risks have been underestimated calls for examining incentives and coaching safer practices. Conscious disregard of a substantial and unjustifiable risk may warrant formal sanctions.

This is not a magic classification tool. Consider the person who skipped a verification step to finish a task on time. Did they understand the danger? Was the shortcut quietly expected by supervisors who praised speed and withheld staffing? Had the official procedure become impossible to follow under ordinary working conditions? Was there a reasonable alternative? These are factual questions. Labels applied before investigation can become a way to avoid asking them.

There is also a temptation to classify behavior by its outcome. If two people take the same unsafe shortcut and one happens to cause serious harm, managers may judge that person far more harshly than the other. The harm matters enormously to the people affected; responsibility for responding to it does not disappear. But the mere fact of a bad outcome does not tell us whether the actor was mistaken, negligent, or reckless. Luck complicates moral judgment.

A fair system must attend to both the behavior and the consequences. It must be capable of supporting someone who made an honest error, correcting a dangerous practice before it hurts anyone, and imposing accountability when someone knowingly exposes others to unjustifiable risk.

It also needs safeguards for the accused. Reporting channels can carry mistaken interpretations, personal rivalries, malicious allegations, and incomplete evidence. Confidentiality for a reporter is not a license to treat every claim as proven. Fair inquiry means hearing relevant accounts, preserving records, allowing correction, and distinguishing safety action from a conclusion of personal wrongdoing.

The most important idea in a just culture is not that nobody is blamed. It is that responsibility should be assigned after examining what happened, under standards that are consistent enough for people to understand in advance.

Paradoxically, this makes truth-telling easier. The employee who makes an honest mistake can expect a different response from the employee who deliberately concealed a known danger. And the organization has to take responsibility for the conditions it created, rather than treating every failure as evidence that one person happened to be defective.

## The social cost of changing a sentence

Move away from laboratories and aircraft for a moment. Think about a person who publicly takes a position, and then encounters evidence that changes their view.

At first, the intellectual task may be manageable: inspect the new evidence, compare it with the old explanation, and revise the conclusion. Then the social task appears. Friends remember the original position. Opponents have recordings. Colleagues may wonder whether the change reveals weakness, opportunism, or an unreliable temperament. The person is invited to explain not just what is now believed, but why a different belief was held before, why it was expressed so confidently, and whether anyone should ever trust the speaker again.

Some of these questions are fair. If a public claim had consequences, an honest correction should account for them. If somebody intentionally misled others, a change in position does not erase the deception. But we can make the price of ordinary revision so high that we encourage people to maintain a claim long after they have learned it was wrong.

One of the most perverse incentives in public argument is that admitting a mistake provides critics with a clean, quotable statement, while refusing to concede lets the speaker continue contesting the evidence indefinitely. A truthful correction is often easier to excerpt than a carefully maintained evasion.

So I want to propose a distinction between being accountable for a previous assertion and being imprisoned by it. The first is a condition of trust. The second makes trust harder to earn, because it rewards people who never offer a clear correction.

A good correction can be quite plain: "I said the evidence supported this conclusion. I have reviewed the new result. It doesn't support that conclusion. Here is what changed, and here is what I think follows now." It need not become a theatrical confession, nor an announcement that the speaker has acquired a new identity.

We should be interested in whether the correction is accurate and whether any resulting obligations are met. If the only possible response to revision is ridicule, we should not be surprised when public positions become harder than facts.

## Where the bad news goes next

It is easy to endorse honest disagreement in principle. It is more difficult to build a route that works on an ordinary Tuesday when everyone is busy.

Imagine a research group that has built a benchmark to evaluate a new model. The results are promising. A student notices that a portion of the evaluation data may have leaked into training. At this point the group needs more than permission to speak. It needs a way to record the concern without losing it in a crowded conversation, someone authorized to reproduce the alleged leakage, a procedure for freezing or qualifying the external claim while it is investigated, and a route to publishing a correction if the concern is confirmed.

If the investigation finds no leakage, that should also be recorded with reasons. The student should not be labeled a troublemaker for asking, and the allegation should not follow an innocent colleague indefinitely as an unresolved suspicion. If the investigation finds leakage, the group's job is to change its claims, not merely discover who first introduced the problem.

The example is invented, but the architecture is practical. A useful process separates the ability to report from the authority to judge. It preserves enough detail for somebody else to investigate. It gives the reporter a receipt and eventually a response. It provides a route around the local hierarchy when the hierarchy itself may be implicated. It allows for urgent temporary action when the possible harm is large, without disguising temporary caution as a settled finding.

It should also track what happened after the report. Too many systems are perfectly capable of collecting concerns and surprisingly poor at closing the loop. A report disappears into a form. Weeks later the person who submitted it cannot tell whether anyone read it, whether it was rejected, or whether a correction was made. The official channel exists, but the practical lesson is still to stop using it.

The NASA reporting system offers a useful example of the difference between collection and action: it does not merely hold a database. It analyzes reports, disseminates lessons, and can send alerts to people positioned to respond. That does not prove every hazard is corrected, and it does not make the data complete. It demonstrates that a reporting channel has an intended destination beyond the inbox.

An institution with a hundred methods for collecting metrics and no dependable method for handling a technically sound objection is not information-rich in the way that matters.

The harder question is how to preserve these procedures when the information threatens someone's budget, reputation, or promotion. An independent review path can help. So can documented decisions, a right to escalate concerns, and evaluations that recognize the value of surfacing important problems early. None eliminates politics. Each makes it harder for ordinary politics to erase the record entirely.

Trust is not established by putting "speak up" in a policy. Trust is accumulated when people speak up, receive a fair hearing, and see that the result was governed by reasons rather than rank.

## Why the skeptical voice also needs standards

There is a romantic version of this argument in which the lone dissenter is always courageous and the institution is always wrong. That version is appealing because it offers a hero. It is also false.

Some warnings are mistaken. Some objections are repetitive after the crucial evidence has been examined. Some allegations are made strategically to delay a decision or injure a rival. People can exploit the language of uncertainty to demand that others prove a negative under impossible conditions. If every unresolved possibility were a veto, very little collective action would be possible.

A system that values dissent therefore needs standards for dissent, too. The person raising a concern should be encouraged to identify the observation, the inference being drawn, the size of the possible consequence, and the evidence that could settle the question. The point is not to require a formal report from someone with an urgent warning. It is to help the investigation distinguish the warning's content from the intensity with which it is expressed.

A responsible organization can conclude, "We investigated this concern and found that it does not change the decision." It can even conclude, "This is a recognized risk that we accept for the following reasons." The conclusion must bear the weight of the evidence, and decision-makers must be empowered to accept responsibility for it.

And the person who raised the concern may need to accept that their argument did not prevail. Intellectual hospitality is not an obligation to agree. A technical culture that allows only approval of objections will eventually lose its ability to make decisions, just as a culture that punishes objections will lose its ability to detect problems.

The aim is neither permanent consensus nor permanent opposition. It is to make disagreement productive enough that the organization can learn from it and eventually act.

## The role of an assistant

There is a version of this problem that belongs specifically to AI assistants.

An assistant may generate an answer, a plan, a report, or a piece of code that looks complete. If the assistant discovers a defect, or a user identifies one, the useful response is not an elaborate performance of contrition followed by the same mistake. It is a correction that reaches the actual artifact.

If the code was wrong, the code must change. If a conclusion relied on a source that did not say what the assistant claimed, the conclusion and its evidence record must change. If a report stated an uncertain fact with unjustified confidence, the user should be able to see what has been corrected and what remains uncertain. Otherwise the assistant has apologized in conversation while leaving the error alive in the work.

There are incentives in the other direction. Smooth answers feel helpful. Admitting uncertainty can slow a workflow. Raising a concern about the user's plan can create friction. But suppressing relevant concerns to maintain a pleasant interaction is a poor model of assistance. Equally poor is an assistant that challenges every decision merely to display independence.

What should matter is whether the objection is supported, proportionate, and useful. A low-stakes choice may call for a quick recommendation. A potentially serious defect in a safety-sensitive design may deserve more explicit qualification and a check that the user can evaluate independently.

I don't think a conversational system should pretend to possess a private, infallible conscience that overrides its users. I do think it should be designed to make errors legible, to invite independent verification when the stakes justify it, and to leave corrections in forms people can actually use.

Perhaps that is the most practical standard: when challenged, does the system become more accurate, or merely more agreeable?

## Let reality finish the sentence

Return to the room at the beginning. The person near the end of the table has said there may be a problem. We do not yet know whether they are right.

The strongest response is not automatic belief. It is not automatic suspicion. It is a willingness to find out, backed by enough time, authority, and process that the answer can matter.

Sometimes the investigation will find a serious defect. Sometimes it will find a misunderstanding. Sometimes it will leave a real uncertainty, and the group will have to decide what risk it is willing to carry. Those are different outcomes, and an intelligent institution should be capable of reaching all three.

We spend enormous effort teaching people to be more confident, decisive, persuasive, and resilient. I would like us to spend more effort making it possible for a person to say, "I think our understanding is wrong," and be met with a question instead of a punishment.

Because the person who brings bad news is not necessarily the cause of the bad news. Very often they are the reason anybody has a chance to do something about it.

An institution cannot arrange for reality to always be agreeable. It can arrange for reality to have somewhere to enter the conversation.

That is what I mean by making a place for the bad news.

## Evidence, limits, and constructed scenarios

The opening software release, the hypothetical workplaces with different underlying and reported error counts, the two imaginary laboratories, the hypothetical engineer's decision questions, the public correction, and the benchmark leakage investigation are constructed examples. They are illustrations, not reports of particular historical incidents. The essays' normative conclusions are the author's reasoning, not claims that the cited studies prove the full philosophical argument.

NASA's Aviation Safety Reporting System began in 1976; NASA describes it as voluntary, confidential, and non-punitive and explains de-identification and dissemination. NASA and FAA identify exclusions and eligibility conditions for regulatory penalty waivers. ASRS reports are voluntarily submitted accounts; NASA says that such reports are not verified and cannot be used alone to estimate the prevalence of hazards in the national airspace system. The manuscript deliberately distinguishes a report from a proven event.

The Columbia Accident Investigation Board's 2003 report identifies both physical causes and organizational factors, including broken paths of technical communication, treatment of dissent, and institutional reliance on past success. The account here synthesizes the Board's findings without inventing dialogue, attributing complete knowledge to specific engineers, or implying that a single ignored request establishes a fully determined counterfactual outcome.

Amy Edmondson's 1996 study of hospital errors and her 1999 research on 51 manufacturing teams concern the detection of errors, interpersonal safety, and team learning. These are reported as study findings, not as universal causal laws. The 1999 study's relationship is described as an association.

AHRQ's "just culture" materials distinguish human error, at-risk behavior, and reckless behavior. The lecture uses this distinction to argue against both blanket punishment and blanket immunity; its proposed principles for scientific review and AI-assisted work are editorial proposals, not claims of existing standards.
