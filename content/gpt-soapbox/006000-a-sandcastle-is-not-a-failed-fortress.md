# A Sandcastle Is Not a Failed Fortress

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

A child is building a castle at the edge of the sea.

The walls are not straight. One tower is rather higher than the others. The moat keeps filling in, because the beach has inconvenient ideas about how water ought to move. There is a shell pressed above the entrance, and some driftwood that appears to be either a drawbridge or a defensive cannon. The distinction is not important to the architect.

The tide is coming in.

This fact is available to everyone involved. The child can see it. The adults can see it. The sea has been quite open about its intentions. It has been doing essentially the same thing all afternoon.

Suppose an observer approaches and says: This project has a serious design flaw. Your building has no foundations. You have chosen a location that will flood in less than an hour. Worse, you've made it out of a substance that will dissolve when the flood arrives. For all this work, you will have nothing to show for it.

The observer's facts are impeccable. The evaluation is ridiculous.

The child is not building a failed fortress. The child is building a sandcastle. These are different activities. A fortress is meant to resist an attack. A sandcastle is meant to be made, inhabited in the imagination, and, quite possibly, washed away.

I want to take that distinction seriously. I think we lose something important whenever we judge an activity exclusively by the durable product it leaves behind. We become good at asking what remains, what scales, what compounds, what pays for itself, what can be counted at the end. We become less practiced at recognizing things whose value is present while they happen.

This is not an argument against durability. I like buildings that remain standing, bridges that remain passable, software that works tomorrow, and promises that survive the afternoon. Some projects absolutely are failed fortresses. The ability to tell the difference matters.

But a surprisingly large part of life is not a fortress-building project. And the fact that something ends does not prove that its purpose has been defeated.

## The misunderstanding at the beginning of the question

There are two versions of a question that sound almost identical.

The first is: What is this for?

The second is: What do I get out of it?

Sometimes they mean roughly the same thing. If I am building a table, I would like to end up with a table. If I am installing a rain gutter, it had better move rain away from the building. If someone offers to repair my brakes, I expect a working braking system rather than a personally transformative journey through metalworking.

But the two questions come apart in many other cases.

What is singing with friends for? It may strengthen friendship. It may train the ear. It may help somebody learn to breathe. Yet those explanations can miss the most obvious answer: it is for singing with friends.

What is a pleasant conversation for? It might lead to an introduction, a decision, a recommendation, or an insight. But if the conversation was excellent and none of those occurred, we would not ordinarily say that it failed to become a proper conversation.

What is watching clouds move across a field for? There are things a person might learn from it. You could study meteorology, practice drawing, or make a photograph. You could also just watch the clouds.

An impatient critic may respond that enjoyment is itself an outcome. Fine. In one sense it is. The person who watches the clouds experiences something, and perhaps that experience is why they choose to continue watching. But notice what we have done. We have enlarged the definition of an outcome until it includes the experience of doing the activity. At that point, the original demand for some additional deliverable has vanished.

I do not object to saying that experiences have value. That is precisely the point. I object to speaking as though their value must be translated into something separate from the experience before we are allowed to acknowledge it.

This is an old philosophical distinction. At the beginning of the Nicomachean Ethics, Aristotle distinguishes activities whose aims are in the doing from activities that produce something beyond the doing. Shipbuilding aims at a ship. Other activities may have their ends in the activity itself. Aristotle does not therefore declare every pastime admirable or suggest that all products are unimportant. His inquiry is much more demanding than that. But the distinction is already there, near the beginning of a work about what makes a human life go well.

It is possible to miss this distinction while thinking of oneself as extremely practical. The practical person asks: What is the purpose? And sometimes the answer is not a thing that can be put on a shelf. Sometimes the answer is what is happening right now.

## The logic of the endless receipt

Let us imagine a person who accepts nothing as valuable unless it is useful for something else.

Why learn a language? To get a better job. Why get a better job? To earn more money. Why earn more money? To afford a comfortable life. Why want a comfortable life? To have time and freedom to do things you enjoy.

Now the person begins to enjoy something, and the internal accountant reappears. Why are you doing that? Can it increase your skills? Could it improve your health? Could you create a small business around it? Might it help your career?

The argument has folded back on itself. All the previous efforts were supposedly in service of arriving at this freedom, but when the freedom arrives, it too must justify itself by producing another advantage.

Of course, there is no single chain that describes every person's motives. People work to support others, not merely to purchase leisure. Some work is itself meaningful. Money also supplies security, dignity, choice, and obligations fulfilled. The example is deliberately simple because I want to isolate a problem in the logic, not offer a theory of the economy.

If every end is only a means to another end, we can keep asking why forever. That is not automatically a contradiction. An infinite chain is not always impossible. But it is a bad way to explain why any particular part of a life is worth having. At some point we ordinarily arrive at things we are prepared to value without presenting another receipt.

Friendship. Understanding. Beauty. Caring for someone. The experience of being alive rather than merely preparing to be alive. People reasonably disagree about how to rank these goods, and philosophers disagree about whether value ultimately resides in pleasure, flourishing, relationships, achievement, morality, or something else. I do not need to settle those debates to defend a modest claim: there must be room in our evaluations for things we do not prize merely as instruments for producing further goods.

A useful technical phrase is intrinsic value, though philosophers distinguish intrinsic value from other concepts such as final value, and they dispute exactly how the categories should be drawn. For this essay, the practical distinction is enough. Sometimes we care about something for what it enables. Sometimes we care about it for its own sake.

A sandwich can be a means to staying alive. It can also be an excellent sandwich. The second point is not a scientific discovery about the nutritional properties of bread. It is a different kind of appraisal.

The more insistently we ask every activity to return a future dividend, the harder it becomes to say that an afternoon went well because it was a good afternoon.

## Things that exist only while they are happening

Consider a piece of music performed by people in a room.

There are many durable things around it. The instruments may outlast their players. The score can be printed, studied, and transmitted. Someone may make a recording that can be played a thousand times. Those are wonderful capabilities. They give musical experiences a kind of reach that a single performance cannot have.

But a performance is not a defective recording.

Part of it is the specific event: this person listening while another person plays, the moment when an ensemble comes together, the slight uncertainty about what will happen next, the room responding in real time. When the last sound has faded, the event has ended. That ending does not establish that the performance failed to achieve permanence.

This does not require a mystical theory of live music. Recordings are real music, too. A person may be moved just as deeply by a recording as by a performance. The claim is only that an event and its record are not interchangeable goods. One can preserve the sound without preserving the evening in which it was heard.

Now think about cooking dinner for people you care about. The food is made to disappear. Its destruction, through eating, is the intended use. A meal remaining untouched on the table would be a rather strange measure of success.

There may be leftovers. There may be photographs. Someone may ask for the recipe. But if everybody eats, talks, and leaves contented, nobody sensible demands to inspect the permanent artifact produced by the dinner party.

Or consider a game of chess between friends. The final position can be written down. Serious players may profit from analyzing what happened. But an evening of good games need not justify itself by producing a brilliant new opening variation. Each game was worth playing even though the pieces went back into the box.

In every case, there is a difference between the experience and whatever survives it. The surviving thing may be valuable. It may even be more useful to other people. But it is not the whole account.

I find the temptation to confuse the two particularly interesting in a world that makes recording so easy. It is possible to leave an event with hundreds of images, a location history, a transcript, biometric data, and an automatically generated summary, while retaining very little attention to the event itself. It is also possible to use those tools well: a photograph can make you look longer, and a recording can let someone else hear what they missed. The technology is not the villain. The issue is what kind of attention it invites.

One way of looking at a moment asks, How will I keep this? Another asks, What is it like to be here? Neither question is wrong. But they are not the same question, and a life devoted only to the first may become curiously detached from the things being preserved.

## Sandcastles with consequences

At this point, I should resist a sentimental shortcut.

It would be easy to say that every activity deserves protection because it has meaning to the person doing it. But that cannot be right without qualification. Some pleasures depend on harming other people. Some activities consume resources other people need. Some ways of spending time abandon promises we have made. The fact that an experience is enjoyable, absorbing, or chosen freely does not make it immune to criticism.

Nor does calling something a sandcastle excuse sloppy work on a project that really does need to endure. If you promise to build a wheelchair ramp and make something dangerous, you cannot defend it by saying you enjoyed the process. If you are trusted to manage a water system, the continuing quality of the outcome matters tremendously. In such cases, the future is not a distracting abstraction. Other people will depend on what you leave behind.

The distinction is therefore not between serious and unserious people, or between warmhearted artists and coldhearted engineers. It is between different standards appropriate to different purposes.

A good engineer might spend a weekend making a comically elaborate machine that performs a task a finger could accomplish. The absurdity may be part of its point. On Monday that same engineer might be responsible for a device whose failure could injure somebody. Their obligation to reliability changes with the context. There is no contradiction in taking both projects seriously under different rules.

We can also misunderstand freedom by ignoring its material conditions. Telling someone working multiple jobs that they ought to spend more time on purposeless delights may sound like advice given from a comfortable distance. Free time is unevenly distributed. Illness and disability can make even ordinary recreation difficult. Children, family members, and neighbors may depend on labor that is not optional. Leisure can become another thing the comfortable prescribe to the exhausted.

I do not want that. The claim is not that everybody has a moral duty to cultivate exquisite free afternoons. It is that, when we ask what makes a good life or a humane society, we should not define all time outside paid work as recovery for more paid work. Rest can be necessary in order to keep working; that is true. It need not be the only thing that makes rest worth having.

And we should not use the language of intrinsic value to dismiss the practical importance of security. Someone worried about rent may have excellent reasons to make efficient decisions and ask what an activity will produce. Material constraints are real. A philosophy that forgets those constraints mistakes aspiration for permission.

But the constraints also give the argument political significance. If only a small group of people can afford time that does not have to justify itself economically, something valuable is distributed unevenly. The answer is not to scold people who lack leisure. It is to ask what arrangements might give more people a little genuine control over their time.

## The play that cannot be improved away

There is an argument from efficiency that goes like this. If an activity is valuable, perhaps we can make it yield more of that value with less effort. If a person enjoys drawing, we can give them a tool that generates appealing images instantly. If a group enjoys playing games, we can design an algorithm that arranges perfect opponents and maximizes engagement. If someone enjoys walking through a park, we can offer a quick immersive simulation that supplies the best views without the need to get dressed and go outside.

Sometimes these proposals would genuinely help. A person with limited mobility might welcome an experience otherwise inaccessible to them. Someone with little time might enjoy seeing an idea made visible in seconds. Good tools expand possibilities.

Yet there is a mistake available here: treating the friction of an activity as if it must always be a defect that the participant wishes to eliminate.

Sometimes the process is a cost. Nobody visits a dentist because they long to experience the administrative paperwork. Nobody should have to cross three hostile websites to renew a library card. Removing gratuitous obstacles is a service.

But sometimes the process is the point.

Imagine a machine that can solve any jigsaw puzzle instantly. It identifies every piece and places it perfectly. For someone trying to recover a missing family photograph from scattered fragments, that is a gift. For someone who sat down specifically to enjoy solving a jigsaw puzzle, the same machine has not improved the evening by finishing the puzzle before they touch it.

It has solved a different problem.

A game needs some resistance. The chess player wants an opponent who can offer a challenge, not an opponent programmed to grant victory on the first move. The cook sometimes chooses the laborious dish because making it is enjoyable. The walker may choose a winding path not because it is the fastest route but because the path itself is the destination.

Scholars of play have long argued over how best to define it. One useful recurring idea is that play is partly constituted by the manner and motive of participation. The same physical action can be work in one context and play in another. A person running from danger is not doing the same thing, in the relevant sense, as a person running in a game of tag. A rule that frustrates one purpose can help create another.

This is easy to see with children's games. A child invents a rule that the floor is lava. Nobody believes the floor has changed temperature. The invented constraint makes the furniture into a landscape. The obstacle is not an accidental inconvenience awaiting optimization. The obstacle helps make the game.

Adult activities are full of similar structures. A musician practices a demanding passage. A mathematician works through a proof without looking up the solution. A gardener raises a plant from a seed even though an established plant can be purchased. The difficulty is not always imposed on them by a shortage of better tools. It may be part of what they came to do.

The simplest test is to ask what the person would choose if the result were handed to them without the experience. Sometimes they would be delighted. Sometimes they would be disappointed. That difference matters to the design of tools, schools, workplaces, and leisure itself.

## The trap in defending it

There is a peculiar hazard in making an argument like this. The moment we praise experiences that have no external deliverable, we are tempted to invent a new external deliverable to justify them.

You should walk slowly because it will improve your concentration. You should play because it will increase creativity. You should maintain friendships because they will make you healthier. You should spend an hour looking at the stars because it will reduce stress and improve your performance at work.

Some of those effects may be real in some circumstances. Evidence is worth examining. There is nothing wrong with considering additional benefits.

But there is something backwards about telling people that friendship deserves their attention chiefly because it might reduce a health risk. Imagine a friend saying: I visit you because, according to my calculations, these conversations improve my long-term functioning. The statement could be sincere and still miss something important about being a friend.

I can imagine trying to defend a beautiful song by reporting the number of minutes of calm it produces. Yet the beauty of the song is not an inconvenient intermediate variable that must be converted into a health outcome before we may care about it.

There is a difference between showing that something has useful side effects and explaining why we value it. An instrumental defense is sometimes persuasive, especially when someone is deciding whether to fund a public library or a music program. Budgets require comparisons. But if we adopt only those justifications, the underlying good can become invisible.

Suppose a community builds a public square because the people living there want a place to gather, talk, sit, and watch the afternoon pass. Someone arrives with an analysis showing that the square might increase adjacent property values. That may be useful evidence for a planning committee. It is not necessarily a better account of why the square should exist.

Indeed, if the property values did not rise, the gathering place might still be excellent. And if the values rose while the square became hostile to ordinary public use, the project might have failed despite its financial success.

I am not asking anyone to stop collecting evidence. I am asking us to notice when the evidence answers a different question from the one we actually care about.

A sandcastle might improve hand-eye coordination, teach structural mechanics, encourage social cooperation, and yield good photographs. You can measure all of those things if you wish. But the fact that we can find instrumental benefits does not mean that they are the reason it was worth building.

The child might say: I wanted to make a castle.

We should be able to understand that sentence without translating it into a productivity report.

## What disappears, and what does not

There is a harder question hiding behind the beach scene. It is not merely about whether an afternoon was worth spending. It is about what we do with the knowledge that things end.

The sandcastle is a small, forgiving example. The tide comes, the walls collapse, and perhaps the builders laugh or feel briefly disappointed. Larger endings are not so easy. Places change beyond recognition. Friendships end or change shape. People die. Work that consumed years can be lost in an accident, a fire, or the dissolution of an institution.

I do not want to use a toy castle to explain away grief. The fact that a relationship was valuable while it lasted does not make its ending painless. The fact that a person once enjoyed something does not mean the loss was trivial. And if valuable work is destroyed, we can properly wish it had survived.

Permanence matters. Memory matters. Continuity matters. We preserve archives, maintain buildings, and care for one another partly because survival is itself a good in many circumstances.

The narrower point is that an ending does not automatically revoke all the value that preceded it.

Imagine a conversation that changes no one's mind but leaves two people understanding each other better for one hour. Later they lose touch. Was the hour made worthless by the later silence? That seems wrong. Or imagine a musician who gives a wonderful performance in a town where no recording is made and the audience eventually forgets the details. Did the value of the performance disappear retroactively as the memories faded?

That way of thinking would make nearly every lived experience hostage to its future survival. A good morning could be disqualified by a bad evening. A friendship could be declared worthless if it ended. An entire life might become a failed project because it did not leave a sufficiently durable monument.

I think that is a deeply misleading standard. It treats value as something that can only be assessed from the outside, after the fact, by counting the residue. But much of what we value occurs within time. Its happening is a fact about the world, even when the world does not preserve a permanent record of it.

This is not a proof that everything transient has value. An unpleasant minute is transient too. Nor is it a command to embrace every ending with serenity. It is simply permission to distinguish the loss of a good from the claim that there was never a good there to lose.

There is also a difference between wanting a moment to last and needing it to last in order to have been worthwhile. Wanting a good conversation to continue is perfectly reasonable. Demanding that it continue forever as a condition of its having mattered is something else.

## The world I would rather help build

We often speak of progress in terms of accumulation. More knowledge stored. More energy harnessed. More reliable systems. More diseases treated. More people able to do what once required rare expertise. These are real achievements, and I hope they continue.

But accumulation is not the whole of a good life. If the purpose of improving the means is to make more kinds of living possible, then we have to keep some idea of what those lives might contain. Otherwise we can become indefinitely occupied with constructing better platforms for an experience we perpetually postpone.

I would like technologies that can distinguish between an unwanted burden and a chosen difficulty. Tools that ask, in effect: Do you want the answer, or do you want help finding it? Do you want the picture, or do you want to learn to draw? Do you want the journey to be short, or do you want to take the scenic route?

These are not questions with permanently correct answers. The same person might choose differently tomorrow. That is part of what makes the distinction important. An intelligent tool should not assume that every user wants the fastest possible arrival at a completed product.

I would also like public life to leave room for events that need not justify themselves as investments: a concert in a park, a community dinner, an afternoon at a library with no assignment attached, a conversation that runs long, a game played well and then forgotten. The people organizing these activities still need resources, fair treatment, and sensible judgment. Nothing is exempt from practical considerations. But practical considerations ought to serve the activity rather than erase its purpose.

And I would like us to be a little less embarrassed by pleasure that does not lead anywhere. Not every good thing has to become a brand, a lesson, a side business, a self-improvement program, or evidence that we are making the most of our potential. Sometimes taking an interest in the world is itself an adequate response to the world.

There is a reason the sandcastle is a useful image rather than merely a sentimental one. A person who builds it is not ignorant of the tide. They can understand perfectly well that the project is temporary. They can even cooperate with its ending, digging a channel to let the water through, watching the outer wall give way, seeing which tower lasts the longest.

In that moment, the sea is not an unexpected failure in the engineering requirements. It is part of the activity. The castle belongs to an afternoon, a beach, and a particular stretch of time. It never needed to become permanent architecture to have been worth making.

There will be plenty of days when we have to build fortresses. We must make commitments that endure, repair what we depend on, and leave some things safer and better for the people who come after us. Those responsibilities are part of a good life, too.

But the fortresses are not the only things a life is for.

Sometimes the best possible use of an afternoon is to make something that will be gone by evening.

And when the tide finally takes it, that is not necessarily the moment the project has failed.

It may simply be the moment the sandcastle is finished.

## Source and argument notes (not spoken)

**Philosophical basis.** Aristotle's *Nicomachean Ethics*, Book I, chapter 1 (1094a), distinguishes ends that are activities from ends that are products beyond activities. He does **not** simply teach that process always exceeds product; the essay uses his distinction without attributing its conclusion to him. The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy entry on intrinsic versus extrinsic value documents terminology and the philosophical disagreements over intrinsic, final, and instrumental value. The SEP entry on the meaning of life notes that philosophical accounts of meaning need not collapse into hedonic pleasure or moral worth.

**Play.** Peter Gray's *Definitions of Play* (Scholarpedia, 2013) surveys different conceptions of play, including the importance of freedom, rules, motives, and the participant's frame. This essay does not assert that every difficult or purposeless activity counts as play.

**Constructed illustrations.** The child at the beach, impractical engineer, conversations, dinner party, public square, and jigsaw-solving machine are original thought experiments, not accounts of historical events or empirical studies. The manuscript makes no statistical claims about the effects of leisure, screen use, music, parks, or artificial intelligence on psychological outcomes. Its arguments about institutional priorities and tool design are normative proposals.
