# The Day It Rained Stones

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

On the afternoon of April 26, 1803, people near the town of L'Aigle in Normandy heard a violent noise overhead. Some saw a bright object crossing the sky. Then small dark stones began falling across the countryside.

Not one stone. Thousands of them, scattered over fields and villages. Some had an unfamiliar black exterior. When broken open, their interiors looked different from the rocks people ordinarily found in that part of France.

The noise was heard over a large area, so accounts of the event did not all sound identical. People who had seen the light were not necessarily the same people who had heard stones strike the ground. Others arrived afterward and picked up fragments. It was possible to doubt one witness or misunderstand one rock. It was harder to explain the entire pattern as a collection of unrelated mistakes.

The French government sent a young physicist, Jean-Baptiste Biot, to investigate. He did something that sounds pedestrian now and was decisive then. He went to the villages. He talked with witnesses. He compared what they said with the stones they had collected, and with the places where those stones had been found. He made a map.

In the years after his report, it became difficult for European scientists to deny that rocks sometimes reached Earth's surface from beyond the atmosphere. A problem that had attracted eyewitnesses, collectors, chemists, astronomers, and some very skeptical learned societies was becoming a science of its own.

There had been rocks falling from the sky long before 1803, of course. The change took place among the people trying to explain them.

## A stone with a reputation

In November 1492, a large stone landed near Ensisheim, a town in Alsace. People recovered it from a field. Pieces were chipped off. The stone became a public object: something to display, interpret, protect, and, for some visitors, take home in fragments.

The future emperor Maximilian passed through the town and treated the fall as a portent. The stone was kept under protection. Centuries later, enough of it survived to be studied as a meteorite.

The physical event was real. The interpretation belonged to the political and religious world in which it happened. A stone might fall, but no one was obliged to regard it as a sample from another body in the solar system. The very idea of small rocky bodies orbiting the Sun was not yet available in anything like its modern form.

There were older accounts from many parts of the world of stones, fireballs, and other phenomena in the sky. These reports were not all equally reliable, and different societies gave them different meanings. Some were carefully described. Some were embellished in retelling. Some concerned events that cannot now be reconstructed. The problem for later investigators was to distinguish a recurrent physical phenomenon from the enormous variety of things people said about it.

A lump of iron does not arrive with an address label. If a traveler hands you one and says it fell from the heavens, you have a question about the lump and another question about the traveler. A remarkable claim can be truthful even when the witness has no theory that can explain it. It can also be a mistake that has hardened into local tradition.

At the end of the eighteenth century, natural philosophers had reasons to be suspicious. Rocks were already familiar products of the Earth. Volcanoes threw matter into the air. Lightning and thunderstorms were invoked to explain stranger reports. Accounts of celestial wonders sometimes had a long history of exaggeration. And scholars lacked a convincing picture of where masses of iron and rock could be coming from if they really did begin above the atmosphere.

One tempting reaction was to classify the whole subject with superstitions. That position could pass for rigor. It had a serious weakness, though: refusing to investigate an unlikely event does not make the event stop happening.

The stone at Ensisheim had survived for more than three centuries by the time of the Normandy fall. Its meaning was about to change again.

## A musician searches the library

Ernst Chladni is remembered in physics for a beautiful demonstration involving sound. Sprinkle fine sand on a metal plate, vibrate the plate in an appropriate way, and the grains migrate toward lines that remain relatively still. A hidden pattern of vibration appears as a visible pattern in sand.

Chladni was good at making an effect that could only be heard into an arrangement that could also be seen. His interest in meteorites began somewhere else: in reports collected from the past.

He had trained in law before pursuing physical research. In 1794, after searching through eyewitness accounts, he published an argument that masses of stone and iron really do fall from the sky. He proposed that their origins were cosmic. The Earth was being struck by material moving through space.

This was a bold claim, but it did not come from finding an oddly shaped rock and deciding it looked extraterrestrial. Chladni assembled accounts separated by geography and time. Fireballs and loud reports often appeared in the same narratives as stones that landed on the ground. Some recovered masses contained iron. Some looked as though their outer surfaces had been heated. A series of independent reports could be compared for recurring physical features.

There were holes in the evidence. Witness descriptions were often collected long after an event. The reports were written in different languages and for different audiences. People disagreed about distances and times. There was also the question of what could travel through space in the first place. The first asteroid, Ceres, would not be discovered until 1801. Chladni was arguing for a population of bodies that astronomers had scarcely begun to recognize.

His claim was dismissed by many contemporaries. He kept working, because the same stubborn details kept reappearing. If rocks were produced by storms, why did reports include masses of iron with such peculiar properties? If an unseen volcano had thrown the stones across half of Europe, where was it? If witnesses simply invented the fall, why were there stones that looked so much alike?

A book can establish that an explanation is possible. A recovered stone, demonstrably connected to a witnessed fall, gives it a different kind of footing.

## The field beside Wold Cottage

On December 13, 1795, a stone fell in East Yorkshire, near Wold Newton. A farmworker named James Shipley was close enough to the impact to be struck by dirt thrown up from the ground. The object was large, roughly twenty-five kilograms, and blackened on the outside. The noise had been heard well beyond the field.

The landowner, Edward Topham, took the event seriously. He was a journalist and former military officer, and he was not content to leave the matter to rumor. He collected statements from people who had been nearby. He compared their descriptions of a dark body moving through the air and striking the earth. He eventually put up a monument at the spot.

The inscription did not declare that the rock had come from an asteroid. It said that an extraordinary stone had fallen from the atmosphere.

That wording is worth noticing. Someone could acknowledge the fall without yet knowing where the stone had traveled before it entered the sky above Yorkshire.

Topham also understood publicity. The rock was exhibited in London, and its story spread. It went on to enter the collection of the mineralogist James Sowerby and eventually the British Museum. It survives today in the Natural History Museum's meteorite collection.

We should be careful not to turn the witnesses into props in a tidy fable about educated skeptics and wiser peasants. The evidence did not settle itself simply because people had seen something. Topham's effort to record the accounts was part of what made them useful. The recovered object mattered because it could be examined by other people. The local claim acquired an independent physical witness.

There is a distinct pleasure in the Wold Cottage monument. Its maker spent money and effort commemorating an event that established scientists still had trouble explaining. The inscription stands at a very particular place in the history of knowledge: precise about what people had witnessed, modest about what they could prove.

Other falls were reported and studied in the years around the Yorkshire event. Evidence was accumulating, but it did not all look the same. There were reports, recovered stones, measurements, rumors, and samples passed from hand to hand. It needed a method for making these pieces speak to one another.

## What the chemists found

In London, the chemist Edward Howard investigated alleged meteorites with the mineralogist Jacques-Louis de Bournon. Their work appeared in 1802, the year before the stones fell at L'Aigle.

Chemistry offered a line of inquiry that eyewitness testimony could not. A witness could report a bright object and a noise. Chemistry could ask whether stones supposedly collected after different falls shared something that distinguished them from common local rocks.

Some of the specimens had thin dark crusts. Inside were small rounded structures, sometimes called globules at the time. Modern mineralogists call such structures chondrules. Many are droplets of silicate material that solidified very early in the solar system, long before there were people or geological laboratories.

The investigators also found iron associated with nickel. This was particularly interesting in samples believed to have fallen as separate stones in distant places. A shared composition was emerging from rocks that had different owners, different histories, and different locations of discovery.

None of these observations, taken alone, amounted to an infallible stamp reading 'made in space.' Terrestrial rocks can be dark. They can contain iron. Many can attract a magnet. Some industrial slags look convincingly alien. A classification is built from a combination of mineralogical features and chemical measurements, checked against other explanations. Even today, meteorite museums receive large numbers of ordinary rocks submitted by hopeful finders.

But early investigators were no longer confined to asking whether witnesses were honest. They could compare material independently. If several stones shared uncommon physical properties and each had a credible account of falling from the sky, a hypothesis about their common origin became harder to dismiss.

The chemical evidence and the reports did different work. Chemistry did not tell Howard exactly when each fragment fell. Eyewitnesses could not determine the precise minerals inside the stone. The arguments strengthened one another where they overlapped.

It matters that these developments preceded Biot's investigation. The Normandy fall did not suddenly reveal an idea that no one had previously considered. The idea had been argued, challenged, and tested against new observations for years. In 1803 a particularly abundant fall finally provided a field investigation large enough to make the existing evidence cohere.

## Biot walks the fields

Jean-Baptiste Biot traveled from Paris to the country around L'Aigle in the summer of 1803. He was twenty-nine years old. The French Institute had charged him with investigating what had happened on April 26.

The countryside did not present him with a laboratory experiment prepared for his arrival. The ground contained stones, and residents had collected some already. Memories were a couple of months old. A bright object had been seen from different directions. Loud sounds had been heard at different distances. If Biot wanted a physical explanation, he would have to reconstruct the event from fragments of information.

He asked witnesses what they had seen or heard. He looked at the stones people said had fallen. He compared them with ordinary stones from the region and with samples from earlier reported falls. He wanted to know whether the object had passed across the sky in a particular direction, and whether the fallen stones were distributed in a way that fitted that movement.

His map became central. The recovered fragments occupied an elongated region, about ten kilometers in length and four across in the reconstruction commonly cited today. Its longer axis followed the path inferred for the fireball.

That shape was important. A pile of curious rocks in one farmyard might have arrived in a cart. A string of locations crossing several villages, accompanied by consistent reports of an overhead event, would require a much more elaborate story to explain away. The rocks had not merely been found near one another. They described a geographically connected event.

Biot investigated the witnesses as people, too. Where had each observer been? How could that person know what they claimed to know? Did a statement describe a directly perceived event or repeat a neighbor's account? Did the recovered rock resemble other fragments? The answers were imperfect, but their agreement could be assessed.

He presented his report in July. It was a persuasive account of a particular shower of stones, not a direct observation of an asteroid leaving its orbit. The explanation of their celestial origin still relied on the combined evidence assembled by Chladni, Howard, de Bournon, and many observers before them.

Afterward, the fall at L'Aigle became a standard reference in the new study of meteorites. Museum collections acquired specimens. Specialists studied their mineralogy. The stones had become objects for science instead of merely objects of dispute.

The word 'meteorite' was acquiring a stable place in the vocabulary. Its journey to that place had passed through a Yorkshire field, a library in Germany, chemical apparatus in London, and a long walk through French villages.

## Three names for one arrival

There is a small vocabulary problem that becomes useful once you begin studying falling stones.

A meteoroid is a natural piece of rock or metal traveling through space. When it enters the atmosphere and produces a visible streak of light, the luminous phenomenon is a meteor. If some of the material survives passage through the atmosphere and reaches the ground, that surviving material is a meteorite.

These words distinguish the object, the visible event, and the recovered remainder. A meteor is not a rock lying in your hand. And a meteorite is not necessarily the whole body that first approached Earth.

At orbital speeds, the atmosphere is formidable. Air compresses ahead of a fast incoming object, heat flows into its outer layers, and material can melt and be stripped away. That removal is called ablation. A thin outer surface can resolidify into a dark fusion crust. The body may fragment under aerodynamic stresses. Much of the original mass can disappear before anything reaches the ground.

This also explains why a large flash does not necessarily mean a large surviving rock. The light records an energetic encounter between an incoming body and the atmosphere. The objects that finally reach the ground have already endured that encounter. Once a surviving fragment has slowed sufficiently, it can continue falling at far lower speed through the atmosphere. It is not generally a red-hot bullet throughout its final descent, as popular images sometimes suggest.

The fragments of L'Aigle are classified today as an ordinary chondrite, type L6. The technical label carries two pieces of information. The L refers to comparatively low total iron content within the ordinary chondrite families. The 6 indicates substantial thermal metamorphism in the parent body, meaning the rock experienced enough heat to modify its original texture without simply melting into a new igneous rock.

The classification does not provide the name of the specific asteroid from which every piece broke away. It describes a kind of material and part of its history. Chondrites can preserve minerals and chemical ratios from the formation of the solar system, even while their parent bodies have suffered collisions and heating.

There is a striking difference between the age of the L'Aigle material and the time for which it has sat in a museum. The Paris museum's specimen record dates it to roughly 4.567 billion years. Human institutions have handled the object for a little more than two centuries. The stone was already ancient before Earth had acquired the familiar surface we now walk upon.

That is one reason meteorites became so valuable. They were no longer merely proof that stones fall from the sky. They were physical evidence of places and times the Earth itself could not preserve for us in the same way.

## The bad geography of a meteorite

If an object comes from space, the place where it falls may tell you very little about where it formed.

A meteorite found in France is not a French rock in the geological sense. The name tells collectors where the fragment was recovered, because the original parent body may never be identified. The Wold Cottage meteorite is named after a house. The L'Aigle meteorite is named for a town. These are excellent addresses for curators. They are poor statements of birthplace.

Most stony meteorites are thought to derive from asteroids, often bodies whose histories involve collisions. Some meteorites have come from the Moon or Mars. We identify those rare arrivals by matching their mineralogy, chemistry, isotopic signatures, and other properties to what is known about those worlds. The path is not always recoverable. The material sometimes speaks more clearly about its chemical past than about its precise route to Earth.

Scientists therefore distinguish a meteorite 'fall' from a meteorite 'find.' A fall has a witnessed event that can be connected with recovered material. A find was recognized only after the stone had been lying on Earth for an unknown time. A weathered fragment in a desert can contain precious information, but its clock has started picking up earthly weather and chemistry. Water, oxygen, and soil organisms may have altered it. Its surroundings may supply clues or may merely confuse its history.

There are places where the geography of Earth makes the search much easier. Dark meteorites stand out on pale ice in Antarctica, where the movements of glaciers can concentrate them in particular areas. Dry desert surfaces can also preserve and expose them. This is why meteorite collections include stones recovered far from where people live, as well as spectacular documented falls near settlements.

The work is surprisingly patient. Someone has to see a suspicious speck, retrieve it without ruining the evidence, record its location, and determine whether it belongs in a meteorite collection at all. Finding a rock is the beginning of classification.

Biot had one advantage over those investigators: the event was witnessed. He knew roughly when to look and where. In exchange, he had to depend on people who had seen the sky under frightening, confusing circumstances. Every kind of evidence brings its own limitations.

## A driveway in Winchcombe

In February 2021, a bright fireball crossed the sky over the United Kingdom. This time cameras were ready. Dedicated meteor-monitoring instruments recorded the path, along with doorbell cameras and other ordinary recording devices. Scientists could reconstruct a probable pre-atmospheric orbit.

The following morning, residents of Winchcombe in Gloucestershire found dark fragments on their driveway. They collected the material promptly and cleanly, before rain had a chance to soak it. The specimen was small, but it was unusually valuable because so little time had passed between its fall and its recovery.

Researchers classified the rock as a carbonaceous chondrite. This type of meteorite contains compounds and minerals that can reveal the role of water and organic chemistry in the early solar system. Scientists could combine images of the fireball, an orbit reconstruction, laboratory analysis, and exact collection circumstances.

Compare the two investigations. At L'Aigle, Biot interviewed witnesses and reconstructed a path from statements and recovered stones. At Winchcombe, scientists had camera tracks and a carefully handled specimen. The basic questions were recognizable: What crossed the sky? Where did it go? Did this rock truly belong to that event? What could its composition tell us about its history?

The tools had changed dramatically. The dependence on clear chains of evidence had not.

Winchcombe also offers a practical correction to the romance of finding a mysterious space rock. A stranger might have swept the driveway clean. A specimen might have been soaked, washed, handled with greasy hands, or discarded with the grit. The family who found the fragments preserved scientific value simply by responding carefully to something they could not yet identify.

A rock from space can be reduced to a curiosity in seconds. Preserving the details of its arrival turns it into a scientific specimen.

## Going to the source

Today, some investigators have decided that waiting for meteorites to arrive on Earth is not quite enough.

A spacecraft called OSIRIS-REx visited the asteroid Bennu. It gathered material from Bennu's surface in October 2020 and returned a capsule to Earth in September 2023. The collected grains reached the laboratory without having fallen naturally through Earth's atmosphere or spent years in its soils.

This was an enormous change in the investigator's control over the sample. Researchers knew which asteroid had been visited. They could record the sampling operation and the journey home. The sample was handled with contamination in mind from the beginning. It did not have to be identified by its resemblance to other stones.

Analyses have found minerals altered by water and a diversity of organic molecules. In 2025, studies reported amino acids and the five nucleobases used in terrestrial genetic material. These are ingredients of chemistry, not evidence that life existed on Bennu. Their presence in asteroid material tells us that some compounds important to biology can arise in places where no living organism is known to have existed.

It is possible to make this sound more conclusive than the evidence allows. Finding molecular ingredients in a rock does not reconstruct the origin of life on Earth. It does not establish that life began elsewhere and arrived here. The results enlarge the list of reactions and materials that existed in the young solar system, leaving many questions about how chemistry became biology.

Yet the continuity with the work of 1803 is remarkable. The first challenge was persuading scholars to accept that the stones really had come down from above. The next was understanding their composition. Today we can send an instrument to a known asteroid, return material, and compare its chemistry with meteorites collected on Earth.

We have learned to visit the place from which some such fragments originate. It has taken more than two centuries of questions about stones that fell without warning into fields.

## Back in Normandy

A fragment of the L'Aigle meteorite is kept at the Muséum national d'Histoire naturelle in Paris. Its catalog entry identifies it as an ordinary chondrite. It records the fall date, the locality, and the name of Jean-Baptiste Biot as collector. Under classification and age, it carries information the people who first found the stones could not have read from the rock's exterior.

If you look at a fragment now, there is little theatrical about it. The outside is dark. The interior is stony. It does not glow. It has no memory that can be played back as a movie of its fall. A museum label, a thin dark crust, and a record of testimony are what connect a small piece of matter with the noise that frightened the French countryside on an April afternoon.

Biot could not interview the asteroid. He questioned the people who had been there, recorded the pattern of fragments across the ground, and compared their rocks with others already being studied.

Two centuries later, the little fragment remains in its collection. The label gives the date: April 26, 1803.
