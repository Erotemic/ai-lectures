# The Small Agreements That Make a World

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

Suppose you walk into a grocery store and buy a packet of tea. It is a modest errand. There is no reason to congratulate yourself on successfully participating in a global civilization. You take the tea, perhaps some bread, perhaps something you did not intend to buy, and go home.

But consider the amount of agreement that this ordinary action requires.

Somebody assigned an identity to the product. Somebody else made a machine capable of reading that identity. A manufacturer and a retailer arranged for the same identity to mean the same thing in two different information systems. A warehouse received goods that had been labeled and counted according to conventions its workers and software could understand. Vehicles used roads laid out according to shared rules. If the tea crossed an ocean, it might have traveled inside a standardized freight container that fit machinery owned by several companies in several countries. If you paid electronically, your bank and the merchant's bank communicated through systems built on still more agreements.

You did not negotiate any of this. You could not have negotiated it. Neither could the cashier, the ship's crew, or the people who cultivated the tea. The transaction works precisely because almost nobody involved needs to know the entire chain.

This is the feature of civilization I want to talk about: the quiet power of agreements between strangers.

I don't mean that everybody agrees on politics, morals, manners, or the purpose of life. We emphatically do not. I mean the narrower, practical agreements that let people with different beliefs, languages, businesses, and plans coordinate their actions. The agreement about where to put a label, how to name a measurement, what a message means, or which part of a machine is allowed to touch another part.

It is tempting to regard these matters as dull preliminaries to the interesting work. I think that gets the hierarchy almost exactly backward. For a great many human achievements, the agreement is what makes the interesting work possible.

There is a catch, and it matters enough that I want to state it at the beginning. A standard can enable cooperation. It can also impose somebody's preferences on everybody else. A convention can remove needless barriers, or it can preserve a barrier so thoroughly that people stop recognizing it as a choice. What I want to defend is not uniformity for its own sake. It is a particular kind of cooperation: agreeing on enough of the boundary that we can remain different on either side of it.

## The identifier that does not know the price

Let me start with one of the least romantic objects in modern life: a barcode.

On June twenty-sixth, nineteen seventy-four, a pack of Wrigley's Juicy Fruit gum was scanned at a Marsh supermarket in Troy, Ohio. The cashier was Sharon Buchanan. The buyer was Clyde Dawson, who worked for Marsh. It was a public milestone for the Universal Product Code, usually called the U P C. The important part of the story had begun earlier, when competing businesses agreed to adopt a common way to identify products.

It would have been possible to build several functioning barcode systems. In fact, earlier inventors and engineers had experimented with different symbols and scanning methods. The remarkable decision was that an industry would settle on one identification convention that a great many participants could implement.

There is a small technical detail here that I find revealing. The usual retail barcode does not contain the price of the item. It identifies the trade item. The price can be stored and changed elsewhere, in a retailer's system. This distinction is the point.

A product maker can assign the identifier. A printer can put a corresponding pattern on a package. A scanner can turn the pattern back into digits. A store's computer can look up those digits and decide what to charge. Those parties need not all agree on the price, the scanner manufacturer, the accounting software, or the store layout. They need to agree on what the identifier means and how it is represented.

That is an elegant division of responsibility. The shared convention is deliberately less ambitious than the complete transaction. It carries just enough meaning across a boundary to allow the other work to happen independently.

Imagine the alternative. Every manufacturer publishes product codes that only its own machines understand. Every store maintains a private translation system. A new supplier must negotiate not just shelf space and delivery dates but the vocabulary of the checkout counter. Some large firms might cope. Small firms would pay a larger fraction of their resources merely to participate. An ordinary purchase would involve an invisible accumulation of conversion work.

The barcode did not eliminate conflict or competition. It removed one particular conflict that nobody needed to keep having.

That distinction is worth remembering. In many settings, cooperation is not the opposite of competition. Cooperation on an interface is the thing that makes a broader and more varied field of competition possible.

Of course, once an identifier becomes important, questions of governance follow. Who assigns codes? Who pays to register them? Can a small producer obtain them on fair terms? Who gets to change the specification? The act of choosing a common language does not abolish politics. It relocates some of the politics to the institution that maintains the language.

Still, there is a lesson in that first packet of gum: a tiny symbol on a wrapper can support an enormous amount of independent action, provided enough people agree to read it the same way.

## Where the telegram had to stop

Now move from a supermarket to nineteenth-century Europe.

The electric telegraph made it possible to send information faster than a person, horse, or ship could travel. That sounds like an invention that would naturally shrink the distances between nations. But a fast signal is not the same thing as a working communications system.

Different states and operators had different arrangements for telegraph equipment, procedures, charges, and the handling of messages. A telegram crossing a border could encounter another set of rules. Countries negotiated bilateral and regional arrangements, but an expanding network of separate agreements became difficult to navigate.

In Paris in eighteen sixty-five, representatives of twenty states met to establish a more general framework. They signed the first International Telegraph Convention and created the International Telegraph Union, predecessor of today's International Telecommunication Union. The convention and its regulations addressed practical matters such as operating procedures and tariffs, not merely lofty declarations that international communication would be nice.

The historical record is especially useful because it shows how unglamorous the work was. People had to argue about charges and accounting. They had to decide which procedures would be used when a message crossed a jurisdiction. They had to work out how a service could be legible to people who did not share a government.

The initial agreement was not a meeting of the whole world. It reflected the states and interests represented at that conference, overwhelmingly European ones. We should not turn an account of successful coordination into a fairy tale of universal inclusion. Still, it illustrates something fundamental. An invention produces a new possibility. A working system often requires institutions willing to negotiate the ordinary details of making that possibility usable.

I sometimes think our stories of innovation contain too few conferences. Not because conferences are inherently noble, or because every meeting improves the world. Many accomplish very little. But an agreement reached after a tedious argument can change the practical value of an invention more than a spectacular demonstration does.

The demonstration says, "We can send a message down this wire." The agreement says, "A person who has never seen this wire can send a message through a network of wires owned by strangers, and reasonably expect it to arrive."

That second claim is harder to make. It is also, for most people, the one that matters.

## How long is the same length?

Consider measurement.

If a carpenter tells another carpenter that a board is two meters long, the statement is only useful if the people involved can connect the word "meter" to sufficiently consistent measurements. In a small workshop, they can compare their measuring sticks. For a machine assembled from parts manufactured on different continents, personal comparison is not a practical strategy.

And so, in eighteen seventy-five, seventeen states signed the Metre Convention, establishing an international framework for coordinating measurement standards. The institution created by that treaty, the International Bureau of Weights and Measures, still supports international measurement coordination.

A unit of length is not a philosophical view about what houses should look like. It is a way for two people who may disagree about houses to describe dimensions consistently. Once they can do that, they can order parts, inspect a bridge, compare experiments, and identify disagreements that would otherwise hide behind inconsistent units.

Notice the peculiar relationship between precision and trust. Standardized measurement does not require me to trust the skill or honesty of every supplier without checking. It allows me to specify what a check would mean. If a part is too large, we can disagree about the result of a measurement rather than discovering that we were never using the same length in the first place.

There are exceptions and complications. Real measurements have tolerances. Instruments need calibration. A nominal dimension does not guarantee a physical part will fit. Conformity to a standard can be claimed falsely. But these are not arguments against standards. They are reasons to make them testable, with a way to trace measurements back to shared references.

The important social invention is not that every ruler in the world is magically perfect. It is that people can have public procedures for checking whether their rulers agree closely enough for the task.

We sometimes speak of trust as though it were a pleasant feeling about other people. There is another form of trust that I think deserves admiration: confidence supported by a procedure that makes blind faith unnecessary.

If I can specify a dimension, inspect the delivered part, and appeal to a publicly understood measurement system when something is wrong, I can collaborate with a stranger without pretending to know their character.

That is not an impoverished kind of cooperation. It is one of the ways very large societies make cooperation possible at all.

## The box with the important corners

A freight container is, in appearance, a big metal box. As an idea, it is a promise about how a box will meet the rest of the world.

The ability to pack goods into a box was not invented with modern container shipping. What mattered was developing arrangements under which a loaded container could move between trucks, ships, railway wagons, terminals, and owners without its contents being unloaded and repacked at every transfer.

That takes more than a common description of "big." The dimensions and weight ratings have to be specified. The places where lifting and securing equipment engage the box have to be predictable. A crane or a ship's fittings cannot rely on every manufacturer having made approximately the same decision about where the corners ought to be.

International standards including I S O six six eight specify classifications, dimensions, and ratings for important freight-container families. Another standard, I S O one one six one, defines requirements for the fittings that let handling equipment connect to those containers. A freight terminal can then be designed around something more reliable than a collection of individual guesses.

Here is the interesting part. Standardizing the container did not require standardizing the cargo.

One box might carry machinery. Another carries fabric. Another carries books. They can belong to different companies, follow different routes, and involve people who would not recognize one another's names. The agreement concerns the external geometry and handling interface. The contents remain somebody else's business, subject of course to transport rules and physical constraints.

That is a recurring pattern I want to draw attention to. Sometimes a small amount of sameness around the edges makes a very large amount of difference on the inside possible.

If everything must be custom-built to connect to everything else, every new relationship creates a new engineering project. If the relevant boundary is shared, the interior can change without requiring the entire network to change along with it.

This is not to deny the costs. Standard dimensions favor some kinds of cargo and not others. Equipment and ports require investment. Once a system is widespread, adopting an alternative becomes expensive even when the alternative has merits. Standardization can produce tremendous efficiency and also a difficult path dependency.

But that is a reason to ask good questions about the standard, not a reason to overlook what it achieves. A box whose corners are predictable can travel through a world of unpredictable ambitions.

## The trick the internet performs

In computing, the same idea appears in a more abstract form.

Imagine two computers sending information to one another across several networks. One network might use fiber-optic cables. Another might use radio. Different manufacturers made the devices. Different organizations operate the links. Their internal machinery need not be identical. The computers require common rules for how information will be addressed, packaged, transmitted, and interpreted across the boundaries between those systems.

The Internet Protocol is part of the answer. Its widely used version was documented in a nineteen eighty-one specification edited by Jon Postel, with earlier work behind it and many subsequent revisions and related protocols. Transmission Control Protocol, or T C P, supplies another set of rules for applications that need a reliable ordered stream of data between endpoints, so long as the connection is working. These protocols are not the whole internet; they are parts of the shared vocabulary that lets otherwise different networks communicate.

A protocol is an agreement about behavior. It tells participants what information to send, how to interpret responses, and what to do under certain conditions of failure. It does not require every participant to use the same processor, programming language, operating system, or business model.

I find this idea more intellectually exciting than a great deal of talk about making everything seamless. The point of an interface is often that two sides can remain different. A protocol lets a piece of software say, in effect, "You do not need to understand how I work internally. Here is what I promise to send and receive."

That creates a boundary around ignorance. Not ignorance in the pejorative sense of refusing to learn, but the ordinary and unavoidable fact that nobody understands every component of a large system. Each party can specialize because the interfaces are comprehensible enough to make specialization useful.

There are limits. A specification can leave ambiguity that causes systems to behave differently. Implementations can have bugs. Networks can fail. A vendor can claim to support a protocol while relying on proprietary extensions that defeat its purpose. A readable document is not enough: people need implementations that actually work together.

The internet engineering community has spent decades thinking about this distinction. An influential description of its approach is "rough consensus and running code." The phrase was associated with Dave Clark's nineteen ninety-two presentation and discussed in a later Internet Engineering Task Force document on consensus. It is a reminder that standards have to be argued through and tested, not just declared from a podium.

I would not make that slogan the constitution of every society. Technical standards bodies have their own governance problems. But there is wisdom in refusing to treat a formal description as proof of interoperability until independent implementations have tried to communicate.

A treaty between machines is only as good as the behavior of the machines that signed it.

## What we should agree about, and what we shouldn't

At this point, the praise of standards can become dangerous.

It is easy to slide from "shared conventions make cooperation possible" to "greater uniformity is always better." That conclusion does not follow.

Suppose a city requires every shop to use the same shape of front door. Perhaps that makes the architect's paperwork easy. It does not follow that it makes the city better. A standard should earn its reach. The fact that multiple choices make administrative work harder is not enough to justify abolishing the choices.

The crucial question is: where does agreement create a shared capability, and where does it merely require people to conform to someone else's preferences?

The answer depends on who benefits, who bears the costs, and who had a voice when the rule was made. A convention adopted by powerful firms may look like a neutral technical decision to the firms and like an entry fee to everyone else. A design built around a supposed average user may make that user comfortable while making other people unable to participate at all.

Accessibility provides a clear example. A curb is a simple, familiar piece of street design until you encounter it with a wheelchair, a stroller, a rolling cart, or limited mobility. Requirements for accessible routes and curb ramps are not decorative details. They change who can use public space. Standards can encode exclusion, but standards can also require a public environment to accommodate people who would otherwise be excluded.

The question is not whether standards have politics. They do. The question is whether their authors and users are willing to examine those politics rather than hiding them behind the apparent neutrality of a technical drawing.

There is a second distinction. Interoperability is not the same as centralization.

A shared protocol may let many independent organizations cooperate. A single dominant provider may instead offer a convenient service in which every participant must join the same private system. Both can feel simple to the user. But in the first arrangement, the shared boundary can make it possible to choose among providers. In the second, the apparent simplicity may depend on one provider controlling the entire route.

I don't think the first arrangement is automatically superior in every circumstance. Operating a single integrated system can make certain tasks easier or safer. But we should not confuse a smooth experience with an open one. A proprietary system can hide the complexity of coordination by forcing all coordination to happen on its own terms.

A good standard can make dependence more distributed. A badly governed one can make dependence harder to escape.

## How the rules are made

Whenever people praise common standards, I want to ask a slightly uncomfortable question: who was in the room?

The eighteen sixty-five telegraph conference was consequential. It was not representative of humanity. The product identification standards that emerged from grocery-industry coordination reflected businesses with particular costs and priorities. Internet standards are often developed in public technical forums, but participation still requires time, expertise, and resources. An invitation to comment is not the same thing as equal practical ability to influence the outcome.

This is not an argument that agreements made by imperfect institutions have no value. It is an argument for treating legitimacy as part of the engineering problem.

A rule that everyone must follow, even informally, can impose costs far beyond the committee that wrote it. A small difference in a technical specification can determine whether a competing product works, whether a small organization can enter a market, or whether an assistive technology can communicate with a service.

A robust standards process should therefore do more than collect signatures. It should make objections legible. It should test consequences, publish enough information for independent implementation where that is the goal, and provide a way to revise decisions that turn out badly.

Even consensus requires care. If everyone must be happy, a determined participant can block change indefinitely. If a majority can ignore a technically serious minority, the resulting standard may fail in the very conditions that minority understood best.

The Internet Engineering Task Force's discussion of rough consensus is informative here. Its account does not simply say to count people in favor. It emphasizes taking technical objections seriously and weighing their consequences before deciding that an objection does not prevent progress.

The precise governance model need not be universal. The general lesson is that standard-making is neither magic nor merely clerical. It is collective decision-making under uncertainty, with real consequences for people who may not have been present.

That suggests a demanding test of the institutions behind our invisible agreements: do they help participants coordinate without demanding that the least powerful participants simply absorb all the cost?

## The agreement must be allowed to age

There is another problem, almost the reverse of the last one. What happens when a successful standard becomes old?

The longer people use an interface, the more expensive changing it becomes. Factories buy equipment. Software is written. Operators learn habits. Documents refer to earlier rules. A proposed improvement has to compete not just against the original design, but against every investment made around that design.

This is why compatibility is a serious engineering constraint. A new barcode format may carry more information, but stores still need to identify the products customers bring to existing checkouts. A new network protocol may solve a limitation of an older one, but computers cannot switch in perfect synchronization. New and old have to coexist, sometimes for years.

Compatibility is also capable of becoming an excuse for paralysis. The cost of changing a standard is real, but so is the cost of keeping one that no longer serves its participants. A reasonable system needs a way to distinguish legitimate compatibility concerns from a veto on all improvement.

Version numbers, transition periods, deprecation notices, interoperable formats, and practical migration tools can sound like small technical details. They are actually part of the social promise. They say that an institution recognizes the people who must live through the change.

A good standard, then, has two apparently conflicting duties. It must be stable enough that others can build on it. And it must be revisable enough that those others are not trapped by the decisions of people who lived before them.

I think this is one of the central difficulties of civilization. Institutions must outlast individuals, but they must not be so tightly bound to their founders that later people cannot correct them.

The answer is not to replace every agreement at the first sign of age. Nor is it to defend every familiar convention as though it were a law of nature. It is to build a way to have the argument again without first destroying everything that depends on the current answer.

## The small promise worth making

Let me return to the ordinary purchase with which I began.

A cashier scans a product. A machine recognizes a number. A store identifies the item. A payment is processed. Perhaps none of the people present could explain the protocols involved, the history of the standards committees, or the calibration procedures that keep the relevant instruments consistent.

That is not necessarily a failure of education. There is more knowledge in the world than any one person can carry. The achievement of a well-coordinated society is not that each citizen becomes competent in every specialty. It is that people can cooperate across differences in what they know, own, value, and intend.

The cooperation depends on people whose work is very real: engineers who write specifications, people who test competing implementations, clerks who update records, translators who work out what a technical term means across languages, administrators who handle disputes, inspectors who check whether a promise has been kept. The paperwork can be dull. It can also be the place where a fragile idea becomes a reliable public capability.

I am not proposing a world in which everybody must agree on everything. I would find such a world both implausible and undesirable. I am proposing that we take more care in deciding what belongs in the common layer.

Agree on the unit of measurement, so we can disagree about the design. Agree on a usable interface, so we can build different machines. Agree on how to exchange a message, so we can send different messages. Agree on the rules of a fair dispute, so disagreements need not end the relationship.

And when we establish those agreements, remember that they are choices made by people, not features of the universe. Keep them understandable. Keep them testable. Make room to repair them. Ask whether someone has been left out. Try not to make a shared vocabulary into a monopoly on speech.

We often imagine civilization as a pile of impressive objects: buildings, ships, satellites, factories, libraries, laboratories. It includes those things, certainly. But you can put two sophisticated machines beside one another and discover that they cannot exchange a single useful message. You can build two excellent railway systems and make travelers cross the border on foot. You can manufacture a precise component and still be unable to say, in the supplier's language, what dimension is wrong.

The visible object is only part of the achievement. The other part is the ability of independent people and systems to understand enough of one another to do something together.

It seems a small ambition: to make a promise about a boundary and keep it.

Multiply that promise across millions of boundaries, and you begin to get a world.

### Qualifications

- The first commercial UPC scan is a milestone of adoption, not the invention of all barcodes. Its importance here is coordination among parties, not crediting one person with the whole invention.
- UPC/GS1 identifiers identify products; pricing information is typically maintained in separate point-of-sale data. There are multiple GS1 code families and changes to this system over time.
- The 1865 Paris agreement was not worldwide representation; it is an example of coordinated state action with historical asymmetries.
- ISO standards for freight containers include multiple complementary specifications. Dimensions and corner fittings are examples, not a comprehensive guide to container safety or intermodal operations.
- TCP's ordered-byte-stream service does not guarantee that a broken connection will deliver data. Internet routing, security, and application behavior depend on other protocols and systems not surveyed here.
- The claims about institutional legitimacy, the value of shared interfaces, and political costs of standardization are the author's evaluative synthesis, not statements made on behalf of GS1, BIPM, ISO, the ITU, or the IETF.
