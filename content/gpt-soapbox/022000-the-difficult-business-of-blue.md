# The Difficult Business of Blue

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

If you visit the National Gallery in London and look at Giovanni Bellini's painting of the Virgin and Child, there is a splendid expanse of blue in the Virgin's cloak. The gallery's conservators have identified the pigment as high-quality ultramarine. Bellini used it generously.

The material began as stone. Lapis lazuli was mined in what is now northeastern Afghanistan, traveled through networks of traders, and reached workshops where someone had to separate the intense blue mineral from the rest of the rock. Grinding the stone was only the beginning. The valuable blue had to be extracted and refined, and even a good batch contained a great deal that a painter did not want.

A painter could spend substantially more on that blue than on many of the other colors in the picture. The buyer of a painting might recognize the expense. The choice of pigment could itself be a statement about devotion, prestige, and what the patron considered worthy of the finest materials.

Now place a modern tube of ultramarine paint beside Bellini's painting. The label may use the same color name. Much of the ultramarine pigment sold today is manufactured rather than obtained from lapis lazuli. It comes from industrial chemistry, and you can buy a small tube without financing a trading expedition across Asia.

I think there is a good story hidden in that price difference. It involves kilns, mines, accidental reactions, woodblock printers, and a crystal that resisted the efforts of electrical engineers for decades. It also involves a confusion that is easy to make because the sky looks blue every clear day.

Blue light was always available to our eyes. A dependable blue that we could put on a wall, a manuscript, a piece of cloth, or a machine was another matter entirely.

## You cannot scrape pigment off the sky

Why should blue have been difficult? There is plenty of it above us.

Stand outdoors on a clear afternoon and look up. Sunlight is entering Earth's atmosphere. Small gas molecules scatter shorter wavelengths of visible light more strongly than longer ones. Light from the blue part of the spectrum is redirected across the sky, and some of it reaches your eyes from directions other than the Sun itself. The exact color depends on the spectrum of sunlight, the atmosphere, and human vision. But a blue sky is not a huge reservoir of blue powder suspended overhead.

Paint has a different job. Shine white light onto a painted surface. Some light is absorbed; some is scattered or reflected back toward your eyes. A blue pigment preferentially returns enough short-wavelength light, relative to the other visible wavelengths, that you see blue. The physical details depend on the pigment. Its crystal structure, the electronic states within it, particle size, and the material holding the particles all matter.

A dye is different again. Dyes generally dissolve or are fixed within a material, often a fiber, whereas pigments consist of small particles dispersed in a binder. That distinction has exceptions and complications, but it explains why a good recipe for blue cloth is not automatically a good recipe for blue paint.

And an electric light that emits blue is doing yet another job. Instead of filtering the light falling on it, it generates its own light. We will get to that.

If you wanted to paint a blue wall in antiquity, you needed a substance that would stay blue while you prepared it, spread it, let it dry, and exposed it to light. You needed a way to acquire enough of that substance and make one batch resemble the next. You needed the pigment to behave reasonably when mixed with the other materials in the paint. And you needed somebody who knew how to do all this.

Earth supplies ochres rich in iron oxides with relative generosity. Yellow, brown, and red earth colors have been used since deep prehistory. Some blue minerals exist, of course. Azurite can make a blue pigment. Lapis lazuli can supply ultramarine. But high-quality sources were limited, and using them was not always simple.

The presence of a color in the natural world tells you little about how easy it will be to manufacture an object of that color. Consider a flame. You can see its light, but you cannot jar the flame and sell it as paint. A rainbow is even less cooperative.

That is where our story begins: with the difference between witnessing a physical phenomenon and controlling it.

## The kiln

In ancient Egypt, people made a blue that did not require a blue stone to begin with. We call it Egyptian blue. It was a manufactured pigment, principally based on a compound now known as cuprorivaite, a calcium copper silicate. The ingredients could include silica-rich sand, a calcium source such as limestone, copper-bearing material, and an alkaline flux. They were heated to temperatures high enough to form the blue material.

An Egyptian craft worker did not need the modern molecular formula to make the product. The practical problem was one of recipes and furnaces: which ingredients to combine, in what proportions, how finely to prepare them, how hot to fire them, and when to stop. The resulting material might then need to be ground to an appropriate particle size. Those decisions could change the shade and usefulness of the pigment.

Archaeologists have identified Egyptian blue in objects from the third millennium before the common era. It became widely used in Egypt and across parts of the ancient Mediterranean world. The pigment survived on sculptures, wall paintings, and decorative objects for thousands of years. It was a triumph of controlled manufacture, achieved long before anyone possessed a modern account of the electrons inside copper compounds.

Imagine trying to reproduce it without a thermometer, a chemical balance, or a written periodic table. You could still learn. Fire enough batches. Keep track of what went into them. Notice the conditions under which the powder is pale, dull, greenish, or satisfactorily blue. Teach the process to someone who comes after you. You need a culture of practical experimentation and enough continuity for good results to be repeated.

That last condition deserves attention. An ingredient list is not the same as a working process. We know this from cooking. A written recipe can specify flour and water and tell you how long to bake, yet an experienced baker notices a dozen things that the recipe omits. A kiln introduces more variables than a kitchen oven. Material technology can live partly in the hands and eyes of its practitioners.

Egyptian blue was also not the only route humans found to a manufactured blue. In China, craftspeople developed copper-bearing pigments known as Han blue and Han purple, chemically distinct from Egyptian blue. In Mesoamerica, artists made what is now called Maya blue by combining an indigo colorant with a particular clay, palygorskite. The combination is strikingly resistant to many conditions that would degrade its organic colorant alone. Different civilizations worked on the problem with different materials, for different objects, and arrived at different solutions.

The word *synthetic* can make these ancient colors sound somehow less authentic than a mineral dug out of the ground. But a compound formed through firing is as much a physical substance as the ore you started with. The artist wanted a blue that could be used. The furnace supplied one.

There is a particularly pleasing detail about Egyptian blue. Modern conservators can detect traces of it through its response to light. Illuminate the pigment with visible light and it can emit radiation in the near infrared, beyond what the eye sees. A sensitive camera can record that emission. On an ancient object whose visible paint has worn thin, the surviving pigment may give itself away through a faint signal in a part of the spectrum invisible to a museum visitor.

The people who manufactured it had no infrared camera. The material they made nonetheless has properties they never needed to know about. Chemistry does not wait for its practitioners to understand every consequence of their work.

## Beyond the sea

Centuries later, European painters working on expensive commissions prized a particular blue from lapis lazuli. Its name, ultramarine, means roughly *from beyond the sea*. The name describes the position of the pigment in a trading world. To many European buyers, it arrived from far away, through merchants and ports.

The best-known deposits were in the region of Badakhshan, in present-day Afghanistan. Lapis lazuli is a rock made of several minerals. The blue comes mainly from lazurite; the rock may also contain white calcite and small glittering flecks of pyrite. To make fine pigment, painters and specialist suppliers could not simply grind the whole rock indiscriminately and expect every grain to be a deep, clear blue. The valuable material needed careful processing and separation.

This helps explain why ultramarine acquired an importance out of proportion to the mass of powder involved. In a painting, a small region of blue might embody distant extraction, difficult transport, laborious preparation, and the money required to secure it. The result could be spectacular. In some religious paintings the Virgin Mary's mantle became an ideal place to use it. The conventions varied, and blue could carry several associations, but a costly pigment could signal the patron's willingness to spend on the image.

Bellini's Virgin and Child is one example; it is not evidence that every blue cloak in every old picture contains natural ultramarine. Painters had alternatives. Azurite could be used. Colors could be built up in layers. Recipes differed across regions and centuries. The most expensive material was not always the best solution to every pictorial problem.

Nor should this become a story in which European painting discovers the value of a color that the rest of the world had somehow overlooked. Lapis lazuli and its colorants circulated through much older networks. Blue appears in Islamic manuscripts and other artistic traditions; different workshops developed their own techniques for mineral and plant colors. Trade routes did not merely carry objects. They carried materials that allowed artists to make new choices.

Still, the price of ultramarine is a useful reminder that aesthetic decisions have a material budget. A painter can imagine any blue. The workshop must obtain the one that can actually be put on the surface.

An interesting thing happened once chemists began making substitutes with properties that artists wanted. The meaning of a lavish blue changed. An intense pigment no longer always announced the same expenditure. Artists could cover larger areas with it, and its relative affordability opened other possibilities. The color was visually similar; its economic life was different.

We will return to that change after a detour through Berlin.

## The Berlin accident, with a small warning

At the beginning of the eighteenth century, a new dark blue appeared in Berlin. The pigment would become known as Prussian blue. Its discovery is usually attributed to the color maker Johann Jacob Diesbach, with the alchemist Johann Konrad Dippel appearing in the best-known account of the accident.

Here is the frequently repeated story. Diesbach was trying to make a red lake pigment from cochineal. He used a batch of potash that had been contaminated during Dippel's preparation of an animal oil. Instead of red, the chemical mixture produced blue. The result was a pigment that could be manufactured from comparatively accessible materials and was much less expensive than fine natural ultramarine.

The warning is that historians do not possess a neat laboratory notebook in which Diesbach recorded the accident as it happened. The detailed account comes from later testimony. Even the precise year is given differently in sources; around seventeen oh six is a reasonable description. The discovery itself, the early trade in the pigment, and the Berlin origin are better established than every colorful detail of the workshop anecdote.

So I would keep the accident in the story, but not build a fictional scene around it. We do not know what the men said when the blue appeared. We do not know whether somebody cursed, laughed, or saw a commercial future in the first few seconds. A surprising chemical result does not need invented dialogue to be interesting.

Prussian blue is an iron-based compound whose structure includes two different oxidation states of iron connected through cyanide groups. Its strong color arises from the way the material absorbs visible light; electron transfer between the iron centers is important to that absorption. The word *cyanide* in the description is a chemical fact, but it does not mean a finished particle of this stable pigment behaves like a bottle of soluble cyanide salt. Chemical behavior depends on how atoms are bonded and organized.

That organization was not understood by the original makers. Their useful knowledge was that certain reagents produced a deep blue precipitate. Early recipes became valuable commercial information. The methods were guarded, argued over, copied, and eventually published. Recipes spread across Europe.

Prussian blue also shows why the words *new and better* require a specific question. The pigment was powerful and often economical. It did not behave identically under every condition. Conservation studies have examined the fading and sometimes greenish changes observed in historical examples. Variations in preparation, impurities, the binder, and exposure to light matter. The chemistry that makes a color possible also determines which environments can damage it.

A modern conservator looking at a picture may therefore need to ask what blue was originally present and what happened to it after the paint dried. The visible object is the result of artistic decisions plus centuries of chemical history. We meet both at once.

## What the printers did to the wave

Now take the Prussian blue pigment east, to Japan in the early nineteenth century.

The picture I want you to consider is Hokusai's *Under the Wave off Kanagawa*, usually called *The Great Wave*. You probably know its shape even if you do not remember the title. A huge curling wave rises over boats, and the snow-covered cone of Mount Fuji is visible in the distance. The hands of the wave seem to be reaching downward, while the human figures in their long boats cling to the water's movement.

The print belongs to Hokusai's *Thirty-six Views of Mount Fuji*, produced around eighteen thirty to eighteen thirty-two. It was made in a medium capable of reproducing an image in quantity. The artist prepared the design; professional carvers cut woodblocks; printers handled ink and pigment and pressed paper against the prepared surfaces. A successful print depended on the coordinated work of several crafts, not on the draft alone.

Prussian blue had recently become a selling point in Japanese printing. The publisher of Hokusai's series advertised the new color. Imported pigment gave printers an additional choice, and they were quick to explore its appearance on paper.

The Metropolitan Museum of Art has investigated the colors of *The Great Wave* with scientific instruments. Its account makes the printing process more interesting than the simple claim that Hokusai used Prussian blue. The printers combined Prussian blue with the more traditional indigo. They used the pigments in different ways, including mixtures for the dark outlines and layered applications that deepened the blue without making it muddy.

I like that finding because it reminds us how far the word *ingredient* can take us, and where it stops. If a print has only one colorant, the result can still depend on how much pigment is laid down, the paper, the binder, the pressure, and the order of printing. With two blues, the possible effects multiply. The printers were working with light as well as with paint: how much it could pass through a layer, how the paper returned it, and how one color appeared over another.

A cheap commercial tube of blue might make some kinds of image easier to paint today. It does not make Hokusai's print automatically reproducible. For that, you would need to understand the blocks and the people who used them.

It is tempting to tell a simple story of a European chemical discovery reaching Japan and revolutionizing its art. But Japanese artists already possessed sophisticated color-printing practices. The imported pigment entered an existing technical culture. The printers took it apart, combined it with familiar materials, and found ways to make it do what they wanted. Those choices are visible in the waves.

There is also a lesson in the survival of the prints. Different impressions of a woodblock image can look different because of printing decisions, the aging of their colors, and the conditions in which they were kept. We sometimes speak of *The Great Wave* as though it were one unchanging picture. There are many physical sheets, each with its own history of light and paper.

The title names the design. The object you stand in front of has lived a life.

## A recipe that changed the price

In the late seventeen twenties, a different route to blue began to succeed: chemists devised ways to manufacture ultramarine itself. Several researchers in Europe worked on the problem. Jean-Baptiste Guimet received a French prize in eighteen twenty-eight for a method of producing artificial ultramarine; Christian Gottlob Gmelin and others developed related methods in the same period.

This was not a synthetic pigment invented by imitating the *appearance* of lapis lazuli alone. The goal was to manufacture a substance with the chemical features responsible for ultramarine's color. Industrial producers could begin with accessible raw materials and control firing conditions to obtain the pigment in bulk. The processes required expertise, but they did not require extracting and refining an expensive blue stone from a distant deposit.

The effect on the artist's palette was substantial. It became possible to use a familiar, saturated blue at a fraction of the old price. That changed where it could be used and how freely it could be used. Artists did not have to reserve it for a few carefully justified passages, and manufacturers could supply blue products on a much larger scale.

There was no single moment when every painter switched to the new material. Price, quality, trust, availability, and habit all affected adoption. A color might also look different in different binders or in combination with other pigments. The fact that chemists can manufacture a molecule or a mineral-like compound does not, by itself, tell a painter how it will behave over a lifetime on canvas.

But imagine the change in the practical question asked at the beginning of a painting. What once required a patron with a budget for imported mineral color could increasingly be considered part of an ordinary palette. An artistic possibility had been released from one of its material constraints.

The history of blue cloth followed a partly different path. Indigo is an organic colorant obtained historically from plants grown in several parts of the world. Cloth dyeing requires its own demanding chemistry, including methods of making indigo soluble long enough for fibers to take it up and then allowing the blue to develop. The movement of indigo through global trade was entwined with plantation agriculture and coercive colonial labor systems. Synthetic indigo, commercialized by BASF in eighteen ninety-seven, changed the economics of the dye, but did not erase the labor history of the blue that preceded it.

If you wear denim, this history is closer to your body than a museum painting. The familiar blue is the result of a production technique and a supply chain. The exact methods vary across the history of jeans. The point is not to make every pair of trousers into a historical allegory. It is to notice that material changes can alter the costs and labor arrangements of an otherwise familiar color.

Painting a wall, printing a picture, and dyeing cloth all require ways to keep blue where you put it. But the twentieth century offered a problem that those solutions could not answer.

How do you make a tiny electrical component emit blue light by itself?

## The lamp

By the late nineteen sixties, engineers could make light-emitting diodes that produced red light and other longer-wavelength colors. A light-emitting diode is a semiconductor device. Electrical energy causes electrons and the positively charged vacancies called holes to recombine, releasing energy as photons. The color of the light depends on how much energy each photon carries. Blue photons have more energy than red ones.

That sentence makes the task sound almost trivial: choose a material with a larger energy gap, send electricity through it, and collect blue light. The real difficulty was making the material good enough. Some semiconductor crystals were hard to grow without defects. Some materials did not respond to the necessary electrical doping as researchers needed. An inefficient blue glow is not the same thing as a reliable, bright light source for everyday use.

Researchers worked for decades on the problem. Isamu Akasaki and Hiroshi Amano developed methods for growing higher-quality gallium nitride on a suitable substrate and for making layers that could conduct positive charge. Shuji Nakamura, working at Nichia, developed other practical approaches to gallium nitride growth, doping, and device structure. There were teams, students, colleagues, and earlier generations of semiconductor researchers behind those achievements. The three named scientists were awarded the Nobel Prize in Physics in twenty fourteen for the invention of efficient blue LEDs.

Their useful devices emerged in the early nineteen nineties. Getting there required many steps that cannot be captured by the phrase *they discovered blue*. The color was not the discovery. The discovery was how to grow and combine materials so that electricity could produce a bright, efficient blue emission under practical conditions.

Once blue LEDs became good enough, they solved a missing part of another engineering problem. Red and green light can be combined with blue light to make many perceived colors. More consequentially for illumination, blue LEDs can excite phosphors that emit other wavelengths; the mixture can appear white to our eyes. This is the principle used in many white LED lamps. A little solid-state device could now contribute to lighting homes and streets with far less electrical energy than a comparable incandescent bulb.

This is where I need to be careful about the story I am telling. A grain of Egyptian blue pigment and a blue gallium-nitride LED are chemically and technologically different things. The pigment is blue because of how it responds to light arriving from elsewhere. The LED is a source of blue light. Both owe their appearance to the interaction of matter and electromagnetic radiation, but their makers were solving different problems.

The LED also did not end the history of lighting. A good lamp has to do more than convert electricity efficiently. It needs to render objects in usable colors, avoid glare where glare is dangerous, remain serviceable, and put the light in the right place. The physics of a blue junction is essential to many modern lamps; it does not decide when the lamp should be switched on.

Still, the achievement is pleasingly concrete. For generations, the artist wanted a blue powder that would remain vivid on paper. Engineers later wanted a small chip that would produce blue light on demand. They each had to work through the stubborn properties of matter. They found different solutions.

## What you are paying for

We began with a costly blue cloak. Let us return to the modern tube of paint.

The tube has a name, a pigment code, a binder, and a price. Somebody has controlled a chemical process tightly enough that one purchase looks roughly like the next. The pigment is dispersed to make it usable, packed to keep it from drying or spilling, and shipped to a shop. There are safety data and manufacturing tolerances, things the painter in Bellini's workshop would not recognize, as well as a familiar practical question: will the blue behave the way I expect on the surface I have chosen?

I am interested in the fact that its cheapness is itself an achievement. We normally notice the effort required to make something expensive. When production becomes dependable, the work can vanish from our picture of the object. A blue that once required long-distance mineral trade and painstaking refinement can become a standard item on a shelf.

That change did not make the old paintings less skillful. Nor does it make a modern artist's choices automatic. The pigments available to an artist establish possibilities; the artist still has to use them. There is plenty to argue about in the social history of colors, including who mined them, who processed them, who could afford them, and who bore the risks of manufacturing. Those arguments become more useful when we keep the materials in view.

Here is a small experiment to finish with. Put a stroke of ultramarine paint on a white card. Look at it in daylight. It is blue because of the light it returns to your eyes. Switch the lights off and cover the windows. The pigment has not gone anywhere. But in the dark you no longer see a blue mark.

Now turn on a blue LED and point it at the card. Blue light reaches the paint, and some of it comes back toward you. The paint and the lamp can agree on a color while doing entirely different physical jobs.

Between them lie several thousand years of humans learning how to make blue available when and where they want it.
