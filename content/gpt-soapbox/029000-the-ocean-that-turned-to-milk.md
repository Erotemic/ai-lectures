# The Ocean That Turned to Milk

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

On the night of August second, 2019, the sailing yacht Ganesha was crossing the Indian Ocean south of Java. Seven people were aboard, including the captain, Johan Lemmens. They had left Lombok and were making for the Cocos Islands. Around nine in the evening the boat entered a stretch of water that looked wrong.

The sea was luminous.

There was no moon to light it. The glow came from the water itself, continued all the way to the horizon, and persisted as the yacht sailed on. The crew's log recorded the whiteness of the sea and an especially odd detail: the bow wave was dark. They had entered a sea that glowed steadily, while water disturbed by the approaching hull appeared to lose its light. The crew compared the experience to sailing across snow.

This was not an isolated patch of sparkling foam. Ganesha remained inside the luminous region until the following morning, about eight hours after entering it. The crew took photographs with a phone and a GoPro. They also dipped up a bucket of seawater. The bucket showed small points of light that dimmed when the water was stirred.

The people aboard did not know what they had encountered. Months later, one of them learned that scientists had found an enormous glowing feature in satellite observations from the same night, in the same part of the ocean. The yacht's GPS track passed through its southern edge.

When atmospheric scientist Steven Miller eventually brought the accounts and satellite data together, he could put numbers to what the crew had seen. The luminous region covered more than one hundred thousand square kilometers. It was larger than many countries. Low-light sensors followed it across weeks of observations. A single sailboat had happened upon an event that an ordinary photograph of Earth by day would have missed entirely.

The phenomenon has a name: a milky sea. Sailors have described them for centuries. They are real, they are enormous, and scientists still have difficulty explaining how they begin.

## A different kind of glow

Anyone who has seen ocean water flash blue around a paddle, a breaking wave, or the bow of a boat has seen one familiar kind of marine bioluminescence. Many of those flashes are produced by tiny plankton, including dinoflagellates. Mechanical disturbance can trigger a brief chemical light emission. A hand sweeps through the water and leaves a sparkling trail. A wave collapses and its crest shines for an instant.

The brilliance depends on movement. The show ends as the water settles.

A milky sea behaves differently. Witnesses have described smooth water glowing evenly without any visible agitation. The light may be white, gray-green, or pale turquoise, depending on the event and the observer. It can persist hour after hour. Sometimes a ship enters it through a distinct boundary between dark and luminous water; sometimes the transition is gradual.

The effect is also easy to misunderstand if you have seen only pictures of ordinary bioluminescent waves. That familiar blue edge of surf can be spectacular, especially in long-exposure photographs. In a milky sea the whole apparent surface becomes the source of light. It can make the horizon difficult to locate. The sea may seem flatter than it is, because variations in reflected moonlight and wave shadows have been replaced by a nearly uniform glow.

It is a difficult sight to describe in familiar terms, which may help explain why some old maritime reports read like supernatural testimony. A ship at night normally sails across a dark surface. The horizon separates that darkness from a sky whose light comes from the moon, stars, clouds, or distant ships. In a milky sea, the brighter region may be beneath the observer. The usual organization of nighttime vision is reversed.

The Ganesha crew's observation about the bow wave deserves attention. One might expect a luminous ocean to shine more vigorously where the bow stirs it. Instead, the wave was darker. That difference tells us there is a physical distinction between the milky-sea light and the flashes triggered by disturbance. It also provides a clue about the location or organization of whatever is glowing. Yet it does not, by itself, reveal the complete mechanism. A dark wake and a dark bow wave are observations to be explained, not experiments with only one possible interpretation.

The yacht's bucket was similarly suggestive. Points of light remained visible in the collected water, and stirring made them dimmer. The crew was not conducting a controlled microbiological survey. There was no prepared series of samples, no direct count of luminous cells, no measurement of how deep the glow extended. Their improvised observations nevertheless gave later researchers something that a satellite could not supply: a description from inside the event.

## How a bacterium makes light

Bioluminescence is sometimes described as cold light. The phrase is useful as long as it is not taken literally: these organisms are not producing light without expending energy. They are performing chemical reactions in which part of the energy appears as visible photons, rather than being dissipated chiefly as heat. They do it with remarkable efficiency compared with an incandescent filament.

Many marine bacteria can make their own light. Their chemistry involves an enzyme called luciferase, oxygen, a reduced flavin molecule, and a long-chain aldehyde. The enzyme helps bring these ingredients through a reaction that releases a photon, typically in the blue-green region of the spectrum. The light is a product of chemistry occurring inside living cells.

The reaction alone cannot explain a milky sea. A glowing bacterial colony in a flask may be visible to someone standing nearby. The ocean is vast, and seawater can disperse cells so widely that their combined light is imperceptible. To light an area stretching beyond the horizon, immense numbers of bacteria must either multiply or be concentrated in favorable conditions. They need energy and organic material to grow. They need the right physical and chemical environment. And they must emit enough light, at the same time, for human eyes or satellites to see it.

Here bacterial communication enters the story.

Some luminous bacteria regulate light production through a process called quorum sensing. A cell releases signaling molecules into its surroundings. When the local population is sparse, the molecules diffuse away and remain at low concentration. As cells accumulate, the signals can build up. Once a regulatory threshold is crossed, many bacteria change their behavior. In some species, this includes switching on the genes involved in light production.

Imagine one light-emitting bacterium growing on a particle of organic material. Alone, its glow is almost useless to a distant observer. When many neighboring bacteria activate light production, the particle can become visible. A sufficiently large number of illuminated particles might make a patch of water glow. That is one leading account of how milky seas work: luminous bacteria associated with organic particles or microorganisms reach high concentrations, and their light production becomes synchronized by chemical signaling.

There is no mysterious decision by the bacteria to illuminate the sea. Quorum sensing is a chemical feedback mechanism, and it occurs in many organisms that never produce visible light. But it is a way in which something unavailable to an isolated cell can emerge from dense populations of cells.

It would be premature to say that we know precisely which organisms generated the light in the 2019 event, how densely they were distributed, or how the glowing particles were arranged through the water column. No microbiological samples were obtained from that event by an equipped research vessel. The bacterial explanation is well motivated by experiments and earlier encounters, but the full population, chemistry, and structure of the 2019 milky sea remain unmeasured.

The lack of that last step is important. Explaining how bacteria can shine is not the same task as explaining how an enormous stretch of the Indian Ocean managed to shine for weeks.

## A laboratory that was already at sea

In July 1985, a United States naval research vessel, the Wilkes, sailed into glowing water in the Arabian Sea east of Socotra. Unlike most ships that have reported milky seas, this one carried scientists and equipment suited to collecting biological samples. Their encounter was accidental. They were not searching for a spectacle that could be scheduled for a convenient hour.

The researchers gathered water and investigated the luminous organisms in it. They found bacteria identified as Vibrio harveyi associated with colonies of the microalga Phaeocystis. The finding provided a concrete biological candidate for at least one milky-sea event. The researchers also observed a boundary between luminous and ordinary water. Such boundaries can arise where different water masses meet and where currents create a front, concentrating material that would otherwise be diluted.

This was a major improvement over guessing from the ship's rail. There was an organism to identify and an ecological association to examine. The result suggested a plausible way to accumulate enough bacterial light: algae and organic particles could provide places for the bacteria to live and material on which to feed. In the right physical conditions, an entire region of water might become a favorable growth environment.

A difficulty remained. The evidence came from one sampled event. A species identified east of Socotra in 1985 cannot automatically be assigned to an event south of Java thirty-four years later. The same visual appearance could emerge from somewhat different microbial communities. Even within one event, the luminous population might change over time. A sample taken near the surface might not describe organisms deeper down.

The scientists had also entertained the possibility of a luminous slick at the sea surface. If an enormous sheet of bacterial light were carried in a thin film of organic matter, a vessel might be able to tear or disperse that film as it moved. But the later Ganesha crew saw light apparently originating from some depth, and a wake that did not behave as a simple disturbed surface slick might predict. Those reports do not prove a particular depth profile. They do mean that a theory developed from the 1985 encounter cannot be carried unchanged into every subsequent milky sea.

This makes the phenomenon an almost absurd challenge for field research. The main object of interest may cover a hundred thousand square kilometers, yet finding a few liters of the right seawater at the right time can be nearly impossible.

And while the event is happening, no one on land necessarily knows where it is.

## The ship and the satellite

In January 1995, the merchant vessel Lima reported an extensive milky sea off the coast of Somalia. The ship's observations included its location and the boundaries of the luminous water it crossed. There was no organized research expedition waiting at that position. Years later, those coordinates became the key to a discovery made from old satellite data.

Scientists including Steven Miller and the marine biologist Steven Haddock examined images from the United States Defense Meteorological Satellite Program. The satellites were designed for weather and other nighttime observations, and their low-light sensors could detect faint sources of illumination on Earth's surface. But the old imagery was noisy. The ocean was dark in the ordinary sense, and weak artifacts could resemble real features.

The researchers went back to the dates and positions in Lima's report. They found a faint patch of light whose boundaries corresponded to the route described by the ship. It covered about fifteen thousand four hundred square kilometers, and related observations showed it persisting across several nights. The patch appeared to move with the water, curving around a feature of the regional ocean circulation.

The result was published in 2005. For the first time a milky sea described by a ship could be matched to a luminous feature detected from orbit.

Consider why the independent observations mattered. A captain's report could be mistaken about the origin of the light. A satellite image could contain a problem with calibration, stray illumination, a thin cloud, or something else that was not biological. But the two records agreed in location and time. Their limitations were different. Each made the other's interpretation more convincing.

The 1995 event had already ended when the matching satellite data were examined. It could not be sampled retroactively. But the discovery changed the search problem. Researchers no longer needed to rely entirely on somebody aboard a ship recognizing the phenomenon and telling a scientist about it later. An instrument in orbit might reveal where a new milky sea was forming while it still existed.

This possibility became more practical after the launch of the Suomi National Polar-orbiting Partnership satellite in 2011. Its Visible Infrared Imaging Radiometer Suite, known as VIIRS, includes a Day/Night Band designed to operate under extraordinarily dim illumination. Related instruments have since flown on other weather satellites.

At night, the sensor can detect city lights, auroras, atmospheric glow, reflected moonlight, and other weak sources. That wide sensitivity is both its strength and a problem. A luminous patch in a nighttime image is a measurement that needs interpretation. Scientists examine repeated passes, neighboring images, infrared cloud information, patterns of motion, and the surrounding conditions. A candidate milky sea should behave differently from a cloud lit by the moon or a fleet of fishing vessels.

The search has become a specialized exercise in discriminating small signals from several kinds of environmental noise.

## A brightness that lasts for weeks

In 2021, Miller and collaborators published a survey of a decade of newer satellite observations and identified twelve candidate milky seas. One of them was the immense 2019 feature south of Java. The bright region changed shape and drifted with the ocean over several weeks; estimates put its area above one hundred thousand square kilometers.

Another satellite instrument could look at the same water in daylight and measure ocean color. Ocean-color measurements are often used to estimate chlorophyll, the pigment that indicates the presence of photosynthetic organisms such as phytoplankton. For the 2019 event, elevated chlorophyll lay near the luminous water, rather than making an exact copy of its outline.

That spatial relationship is intriguing. If phytoplankton blooms grow and then die or break apart, they can supply organic material on which bacteria feed. Currents can collect and separate different parts of the biological community. The luminous bacteria might flourish near the margins of an algal bloom, where nutrients and particles become available. But neighboring patches of high chlorophyll and milky water are evidence for a relationship worth investigating, not direct proof of which microbial processes took place.

The ocean is not a bucket. It moves in layers, and the motion is not uniform. Warm and cool water masses meet. Surface currents shear past each other. Fronts form; eddies rotate; winds alter the upper layers. Certain biological particles can collect in convergence zones while other material is dispersed. A microorganism that can multiply rapidly in an ideal flask may never reach a luminous density in open water unless the circulation holds its food and its neighbors together long enough.

This is part of why the physical ocean matters to a biological event. A widespread bloom may be constrained by the same currents that distribute nutrients and oxygen. The original pocket of favorable conditions may later stretch into a long ribbon. A satellite tracks the resulting light, but the movement of that light is not identical to the movement of every cell that produced it. New organisms may grow at one edge as others die at another.

The scale makes one further point difficult to avoid. A satellite can tell us where the ocean was luminous and how bright it appeared from above. From that alone we cannot infer the total number of luminous bacteria. We would need to know how densely they were packed, the distribution of light-producing cells with depth, how seawater absorbed and scattered the light, and how much light an individual cell emitted under the conditions of the event.

A brighter patch might contain more bacteria. Or its bacteria might be producing more light. Or the luminous population might lie closer to the surface. Different combinations can yield similar images. The remote measurement is powerful because it covers the entire feature, but it does not replace the missing water sample.

That is why the encounter by Ganesha was so valuable. The vessel reached the 2019 event by chance, without knowing that the light had also been recorded from above. Its testimony supplied a ground-level view. The crew's GPS coordinates showed they crossed only the southern portion of the feature, not its brightest region. The images and interviews confirmed that the milky surface was genuinely visible to people aboard a boat.

The photographs were not laboratory-calibrated radiometers. One was adjusted in the published analysis to approximate the brightness the witnesses remembered. That is a reasonable way to illustrate their experience, provided the photograph is not mistaken for an exact measurement of the ocean's radiance. The strongest confirmation came from the independent match between location, time, eyewitness description, and the satellite signal.

## What the old logbooks have to say

A separate problem is knowing where milky seas happen often enough to send scientists to look for them. One old report cannot reveal a distribution. Nor can a small number of events seen from space tell us confidently which seasons and regions are most favorable.

In 2025, Justin Hudson and Steven Miller published a curated record combining centuries of ship reports with modern satellite detections. The sources include journals kept by marine observers, older scientific publications, and the collected notes of specialists who spent years preserving sightings of bioluminescence. Their database contains 393 eyewitness accounts and 22 satellite observations up to the study's cutoff. Some descriptions refer to the same event, so the number of distinct events is smaller.

This is a delightful kind of scientific archive. A ship's log written by someone whose chief obligation was navigation can become a research instrument long after the voyage. An observation made in the ordinary work of moving cargo can outlast several generations of scientific equipment. And the details that once seemed literary—whether the water was disturbed, whether it glowed steadily, where the ship entered and left it—turn out to be clues that permit comparison with modern data.

The archive has serious limitations. Shipping routes are unevenly distributed, and they change with trade and technology. A place where many ships pass at night will produce more reports than a remote patch of ocean with no witnesses. The records are especially plentiful in periods when maritime observers were encouraged to write and publish what they saw. Descriptions vary in precision. Some report the phenomenon accurately but give uncertain coordinates; others describe a spectacular light without enough detail to distinguish a milky sea from ordinary wave-triggered bioluminescence.

Hudson and Miller found a strong concentration of reports in the northwestern Indian Ocean, with additional concentrations south of Java and in the Banda Sea. They examined how the timing of events related to the monsoons and to large patterns of climate variability, including the Indian Ocean Dipole and El Niño's Pacific-wide climate system.

One of the stronger statistical signals emerged south of Java, where positive phases of the Indian Ocean Dipole were associated with a greater likelihood of a reported milky sea during the regional winter. The Indian Ocean Dipole describes a pattern in which sea-surface temperature differences change between the western and eastern tropical Indian Ocean. Those changes affect winds, upwelling, and biological productivity. It would be plausible for them to influence whether the conditions for a milky sea occur.

Plausible does not mean established down to a microbial mechanism. Correlation with a broad oceanographic pattern cannot yet tell us which local eddy will carry the right particles, what bacteria will colonize them, or whether those organisms will reach luminous densities. And historical observation patterns remain entangled with where ships happened to sail.

Still, the records offer something that the first scientists studying milky seas could scarcely have hoped for: a way to decide when and where an expedition might have a reasonable chance of finding one.

## Reaching the water before the light goes out

Imagine a ship equipped for the task. It would need to arrive while the event was in progress. Researchers could take water samples at several depths and locations, measure light directly, identify the living organisms, and examine the chemistry of the water. They might measure currents and the distribution of organic particles, then return to the same region on successive nights to learn whether the bacterial population was growing, moving, or declining.

The reason for sampling across the boundary would be especially compelling. If a ship could cross from dark water into a luminous region and compare otherwise similar samples on the two sides, it might discover what distinguishes the light-producing ecosystem from the surrounding sea. The contrast could involve bacterial density, algal material, nutrients, temperature, or some combination no one has yet measured adequately.

Even such an expedition would face interpretive difficulties. A sample is a local observation of a changing system. The conditions in a container may change as soon as water is brought aboard. Organisms that live together in open water may behave differently when their concentration, pressure, or oxygen supply is altered. The best experiment would need to preserve enough of the original environment to tell whether the organisms glow naturally, and under which conditions the glow persists.

A successful campaign would also need to distinguish two fundamental questions. How can an immense luminous population exist? And why would bacteria emit light on that scale?

The first question has practical hypotheses involving organic matter, physical concentration, and population growth. The second is more speculative. In some marine species, bacterial light helps a host animal, and the bacteria benefit from a protected, nutrient-rich home. A diffuse population glowing through the open ocean may have a different ecological relationship. Perhaps light affects other organisms that eat or transport the bacteria. Perhaps it is a consequence of a regulatory system beneficial in another setting. The evolutionary explanation has not been settled for these vast oceanic events.

There is a temptation to regard the light as if it exists for the benefit of a witness. The crew of Ganesha could see it; so could an unusually sensitive orbiting instrument. But neither the yacht nor the satellite played a role in producing the glow. It is an ecological process happening in water that would behave the same way on an unobserved night.

Scientists now have a better chance of observing those nights. Satellites can identify possible events and follow their movement. Historical reports help indicate when to look. Better biological sampling could finally connect the ocean-wide images to the chemistry happening on individual microscopic particles.

The unanswered questions are specific and approachable: which organisms are present; how dense are they; where in the water column do they live; what do they consume; and what conditions switch their lights on? Until measurements arrive from the glowing water itself, those questions will retain several plausible answers.

## The edge of the white water

Return to the yacht on August second, 2019. The people aboard had sailed into an expanse of light so large that a weather satellite could follow it, yet they knew nothing about its size. The ship's hull was moving through a biological event that had probably begun before they arrived and continued after they departed.

Their observations were wonderfully imperfect. They did not have a laboratory. Their photographs needed explanation. Their initial guess about plankton was provisional. And they wrote down what they saw, including the part that contradicted a familiar picture of luminous seas: the black bow wave.

The ship kept going. In the morning there was ordinary daylight, and the strange contrast between ocean and sky was gone. Far above the water, a sensor continued collecting faint light from the region through subsequent nights. Later, a scientist compared those readings with the yacht's route and found that the two records described the same event.

The ocean had not been waiting for anyone to solve it. It had simply been shining.
