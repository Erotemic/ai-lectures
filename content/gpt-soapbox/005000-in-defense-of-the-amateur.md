# In Defense of the Amateur

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

There is a peculiar thing we do to people who become interested in something.

Suppose a person begins learning to paint. At first we encourage them. What a good hobby, we say. It is healthy to have interests. They buy a few brushes, discover that paint has an alarming number of names for nearly the same shade of green, and begin making pictures. Some are bad. A few are promising. They learn to see shadows differently. They notice the blue in the underside of clouds. Already, something has changed in their relationship with the world.

Then the questions arrive.

Are you going to sell them? Have you considered commissions? What is your social media account? You could make prints. You should open a store. You ought to build a portfolio. Are you going to take it seriously?

That last question interests me. What would count as taking it seriously? Is learning about composition not serious? Is spending a Sunday struggling to paint a convincing hand not serious? Does the activity become serious at the moment somebody else agrees to pay for it?

I want to argue for the amateur. Not for sloppy work. Not for pretending that years of expertise are unnecessary. Not for the fantasy that enthusiasm is a substitute for knowing what you are doing.

I mean the amateur in the older, more generous sense: a person who pursues something because they love it, whether or not it becomes a profession. The word comes through French from the Latin word for a lover. That is a lovely origin for a term that has since acquired the force of an insult.

My argument is that a society needs amateurs for at least three different reasons. They preserve forms of curiosity that professions and markets cannot always accommodate. They make knowledge into a public activity rather than a product administered exclusively by experts. And they defend a simple human freedom: the freedom to become good at something without being obliged to turn that goodness into income, status, or a permanent identity.

The second reason requires evidence and discipline. The third does not. I want to keep those apart.

## What the word is hiding

The trouble starts with a false division. We use "professional" to mean competent and "amateur" to mean incompetent. But those words describe two different questions.

One question is about your relationship to an activity. Do you earn your living from it? Is it your occupation? Are you accountable to clients, patients, employers, audiences, or professional peers?

Another question is about the quality of your work. Are you skilled? Reliable? Careful? Able to recognize your mistakes? Can you meet the standards appropriate to what you are attempting?

These questions interact, but they are not identical. Professional practice often provides training, repeated exposure, criticism, and stakes that make people better. That is a good reason to respect it. Yet a person can earn a living producing careless work, and another can spend twenty years studying something after dinner with extraordinary care.

A professional musician might be an amateur botanist. A retired engineer might build exceptionally fine violins for no particular market. Someone who has never published a paper might know the birds in one valley better than a visiting researcher does. That local knowledge may be incomplete in scientifically important ways, but it is not nothing merely because no one has issued a certificate for it.

The meanings have also changed over time. The boundary between amateurs and professionals in science was not always drawn as we draw it now. The expansion of laboratories, research careers, formal education, and specialized instruments changed who could participate in particular forms of investigation. Historians of science warn against projecting today's occupational categories backward onto eras when paid scientific careers were far less common.

This matters because stories about the heroic amateur can mislead us, too. I do not want to claim that science was somehow purer before it became a profession. It was often restricted by wealth, class, exclusion, and access to education. The figure of the gentleman with an observatory on his estate is hardly a model of universal opportunity.

Professionalization accomplished real things. It made sustained work possible without private fortunes. It built laboratories that outlived their founders. It created disciplines with standards and methods of criticism. It made difficult, cumulative investigation a vocation rather than an expensive pastime for people who could afford leisure.

So my complaint is not that professional expertise exists. It is that we have allowed one valuable form of participation to become the measure of all the others.

## A green thing in the sky

In 2007, a Dutch schoolteacher named Hanny van Arkel was looking at images of galaxies as a participant in Galaxy Zoo, a public astronomy project. The original task was straightforward: help classify galaxies by their shapes. Astronomers had an enormous collection of images, and volunteers could inspect them online.

Van Arkel noticed something in one of the images that did not fit the expected categories. It was a green-looking object near the galaxy IC 2497. She brought it to the attention of the project, and astronomers followed up. The object became known as Hanny's Voorwerp, using the Dutch word for "object."

The important part of this story is not that one amateur humiliated a roomful of experts. That would be the wrong story. The observation became scientifically useful precisely because a public participant and professional astronomers could do different parts of the work together. A curious person noticed something. She reported it. Other people with additional instruments, methods, and expertise investigated it.

This is collaboration, not the defeat of expertise.

It also illustrates something more interesting than the familiar slogan that anybody can contribute. The observation began with a deviation from the assigned task. A person looking for one kind of pattern noticed a different one. Curiosity was not confined to the menu options.

I do not think this means that every unexplained shape in a photograph deserves a scientific paper. Most surprises turn out to be ordinary artifacts, misunderstandings, or things already known to specialists. The ability to ask "what is that?" is valuable, but the answer still has to survive checking.

Galaxy Zoo itself was designed around that reality. Its team received many independent classifications of the same galaxies and used agreement and other quality checks to assess the resulting data. It did not simply declare that each volunteer's first impression was ground truth. People could participate because the project had built a method for incorporating fallible observations.

That is the distinction I want to emphasize. Lowering the barrier to participation does not require lowering the standard for truth. Often it requires a better system of validation, one that does not assume that either credentials or enthusiasm can replace evidence.

There is a second example, nearer to home and much less glamorous than a strange green object in deep space.

The Community Collaborative Rain, Hail and Snow Network, known as CoCoRaHS, asks volunteers to make standardized observations of precipitation. People use approved rain gauges, learn an observation procedure, and report what falls where they live. The National Weather Service describes these reports as useful to forecasters, researchers, and others trying to understand conditions across a landscape.

A weather radar can be sophisticated and still lack a measurement from a particular backyard at a particular time. A person with a rain gauge does not replace the radar. The local observation can help fill in a different part of the picture.

And notice what makes this possible. Enthusiasm gets someone out of bed to check a gauge. A standard procedure makes the number interpretable. A network makes many separate measurements useful together. Professional organizations provide infrastructure and know how to use the reports.

Love of the subject starts the work. Discipline gives the work a chance to matter beyond the person who did it.

## The freedom to be interested in the wrong thing

Consider two students of the same subject.

The first is paid to solve a specified problem. They have a deadline, a budget, a supervisor, and a reason to favor methods likely to produce results. Their constraints are not a moral defect. A hospital needs reliable instruments. A bridge needs to stand up. A research group cannot spend every dollar on questions that simply happen to charm it this week.

The second student has no deadline and no particular audience. They spend an entire month trying to understand an obsolete notation. Or they make a little mechanical device that has no advantage over a store-bought one. Or they memorize the names of all the mosses in a local park. This may never produce a paper, a product, an improved income, or even an entertaining anecdote.

I still think we should be glad that the second kind of activity exists.

The amateur can afford, within the limits of their real life, to follow a question that would be hard to defend in a grant application. They can change subjects without explaining why the investment will pay off. They can become fascinated by a corner of a field that everybody else has moved beyond. They can choose the slower, more beautiful method because the point is to learn how it works.

None of this guarantees discovery. Most personal projects do not become historic breakthroughs. I am suspicious of defending amateur curiosity solely by pointing to the few famous cases that yielded something useful. That turns freedom back into a disguised wager on productivity.

If you tell a child that taking apart a broken radio is valuable because it might make them the next great engineer, you have attached the activity's legitimacy to a future that may never come. The child might become an engineer. Or a gardener. Or a teacher who knows exactly how a radio works. Or an adult who remembers that it was possible to approach a mysterious object with their hands and figure something out.

That last outcome is enough.

I am arguing that not every good thing needs an external justification. A person can learn a fugue because a fugue is worth hearing from the inside. They can study a language because a sentence in that language delights them. They can build a small telescope because they want to see the moons of Jupiter with an instrument they assembled themselves.

The world does not owe every pleasure public funding. Individuals still have responsibilities to one another, and resources are finite. But the absence of a public funding claim is not the absence of value.

There is a quiet tyranny in the idea that everything we become capable of doing should eventually be used for something else. Once you make that demand, the activity is never permitted to be complete on its own terms. Practice must become performance. Performance must become an audience. An audience must become a business. The business must grow, because if it does not grow, perhaps we were never serious after all.

I think we should be allowed to stop anywhere along that chain.

You can learn to play the piano beautifully and never play for strangers. You can write a story and keep it in a drawer. You can be an excellent amateur cook without opening a restaurant. The meal is not an unsuccessful restaurant merely because no one buys a ticket to eat it.

## Seriousness without a credential

Here comes the objection that deserves the greatest respect.

There are real dangers in romanticizing amateurs. A person who is enthusiastic about medicine is not thereby qualified to prescribe treatment. Someone who has read three books about structural engineering does not get to dismiss the building code. A hobbyist who posts an impressive-looking graph can spread an error to millions of people before anyone examines the axes.

There is a reason expert communities develop methods of verification. Knowledge is difficult. Some errors are expensive. Some are fatal. A professional license is not magic, but in fields where other people's lives depend on competent practice, training, supervision, and accountability are not optional accessories.

My defense of the amateur must therefore reject a tempting but foolish claim: "outsiders are more trustworthy because they are outsiders." No. Outsiders can notice what insiders miss, and they can misunderstand what insiders have already spent decades learning. Both are possible.

The real question is how an idea is evaluated.

In mathematics, a proof does not become valid because its author has a celebrated title, nor invalid because its author has no university job. But neither does mathematical enthusiasm excuse a gap in the proof. If someone claims a theorem, they owe the argument. That may require definitions, technical skill, and a willingness to accept correction. The standard is demanding, and it is not a scandal that it is demanding.

In astronomy, a surprising observation can come from a volunteer, but its interpretation must survive further investigation. In local history, an amateur may discover a neglected archive, but they must still distinguish primary documents from family legend. In natural history, a skilled observer can contribute records, but the records may need photographs, locations, dates, and independent checks.

What I want is an open door and an honest standard. Those two things strengthen each other.

An open door without standards makes the room noisy and unreliable. Standards without an open door can make a field needlessly narrow, and can confuse membership with evidence. A competent community should be able to say, "Show us what you found," and then genuinely look.

This is harder than it sounds. Experts are busy. Most unsolicited claims are not revolutionary. Some people do use the language of independent inquiry to avoid criticism rather than invite it. A community cannot examine every submission with unlimited patience. Triage is necessary.

But triage should not become contempt. Good systems make it possible to distinguish a poorly presented but interesting observation from a claim that has been checked and found wanting. They explain the method. They provide ways to learn the standard. They do not require a person to pretend to hold a position they do not have before an observation can be considered.

The amateur needs humility in the presence of expertise. The expert needs humility about the boundaries of their attention. They are complementary disciplines.

## The danger of being useful

Now let me complicate my own case.

I have been praising the ways that amateurs can contribute to public knowledge. It would be convenient to stop there, with an uplifting picture of millions of curious volunteers helping science along.

But there is a trap in praising volunteer work for how useful it is to institutions. If we are not careful, we end up giving a cheerful name to unpaid labor.

A museum may benefit from volunteers describing objects in its collection. A scientific project may benefit from thousands of people labeling images. A local archive may depend on enthusiasts who know how to preserve documents. Some of these arrangements are freely chosen and mutually rewarding. Some provide public access to otherwise neglected materials. They can be excellent projects.

But there are questions we should ask. Do the volunteers know how their work will be used? Are their contributions acknowledged? Do they get access to the results? Are they learning something, or are they merely performing repetitive tasks for an organization that could afford to hire someone? Is a public institution quietly replacing stable jobs with a stream of unpaid people whose enthusiasm makes them easy to exploit?

These questions do not have one answer for every project. The point is that "done for love" should not automatically mean "free to take."

Some institutions are built partly from gifts of time. A community orchestra, a neighborhood archive, or a collaborative software project may exist because participants want it to exist and choose to sustain it. That can be a genuine form of collective ownership. But a commercial company has a different relationship to unpaid effort. If it captures most of the benefits while the contributors carry the costs, celebration of amateur passion can become a convenient excuse.

There is a difference between refusing to monetize your own hobby and having someone else monetize it for you.

Even a worthwhile volunteer program needs to respect people as participants, not just as convenient inputs. An ethical invitation says what the work is for, what will happen to it, what the contributors can reasonably expect, and where the limits are. A useful contribution should not disappear into an institution's success story while its maker becomes invisible.

And there is another, subtler trap. Even well-intentioned projects can leave amateurs believing that their hobby only matters when it yields data for professionals. That is too narrow a conception of participation.

It is perfectly good to look at galaxies because you enjoy looking at galaxies. You do not have to discover a new astronomical object to justify an evening spent under the stars. You may contribute to a research project, or you may simply learn the constellations. The two activities can enrich each other. Neither is the admission price for the other.

## A civilization of spectators

There is something at stake here that extends beyond individual happiness.

Think about the difference between a society in which people make music and a society in which people only consume music. The second society could have extraordinary performers, excellent recordings, and near-perfect access to every style ever produced. It might even be more efficient at delivering a satisfying listening experience.

But it would have lost something if almost nobody learned an instrument, sang with friends, or tried to compose a tune.

Making music changes the way listening works. A person who has struggled to keep time in a small ensemble hears a performance differently. They may appreciate an expert musician more rather than less. They know, in their muscles, how difficult some of the effortless-sounding passages are. They also know that music is not a sealed product delivered from elsewhere. It is something people can do.

That distinction applies to more than music.

A person who repairs a bicycle understands part of the physical world differently from someone who only purchases transportation. Someone who grows tomatoes begins noticing soil, insects, temperatures, and the timing of rain. A reader who tries writing a poem may become a more attentive reader of poetry. Someone who teaches themselves a little mathematics acquires a new way of noticing patterns and a sharper sense of what remains beyond their understanding.

None of these activities guarantees wisdom. It is possible to take apart a bicycle and learn an incorrect theory of how it works. It is possible to write terrible poems for decades and learn surprisingly little about poetry. Experience does not automatically mature into understanding.

But participation creates the opportunity for a different relationship to knowledge. It replaces the simple division between producer and consumer with a much richer set of possibilities. A person can be a beginner, a collaborator, a critic, a caretaker, an enthusiast, a patient practitioner, and sometimes a contributor to work of real public consequence.

I worry about a future in which this middle ground shrinks. We might have extraordinary tools that can compose, draw, code, explain, and design on demand, while fewer people feel that there is any point trying to do those things themselves.

This is not an argument against powerful tools. I would rather have more people able to make things with assistance than fewer people able to make things without it. A synthesizer does not make learning an acoustic instrument pointless; a camera does not eliminate drawing. The value of a tool depends partly on what it lets people do and partly on what it persuades them they no longer need to attempt.

The dangerous message is not that a machine can perform a task very well. It is that performance was the only reason to attempt the task.

Why learn to draw if a generator can render a scene faster? One answer is that drawing can be an act of looking. Why learn to program if software can be generated from a description? One answer is that understanding how a system works gives you the freedom to question and alter it. Why learn another language when translation can be instant? One answer is that a language is not merely a code for transmitting facts; it is also a way of encountering another person's choices of expression.

There are good reasons not to pursue any particular skill. Time is limited. Nobody can learn everything. A person who does not enjoy drawing has no obligation to draw in order to prove they are fully human. What I resist is the claim that proficiency by another agent, human or machine, has made the activity worthless for everyone else.

Watching a skilled climber reach a summit does not give you the experience of climbing the mountain. And if your goal was to learn to climb, that is not a defect in the video. It is a difference in what you wanted.

## The right to be a beginner

I have defended the serious amateur. I should also defend the incompetent amateur, because everybody begins somewhere and because competence is not the only legitimate reason to begin.

One of the crueler demands we make is that adults present only those interests in which they already appear capable. Children are often permitted to be bad at things. A drawing by a five-year-old can be cherished without an art critic explaining its technical shortcomings. Adults, however, can be embarrassed to sing, dance, learn an instrument, or attempt a new sport in front of other people. There is an implicit expectation that a mature person's public activities should be supported by an established identity and a defensible level of skill.

This expectation is absurd. An adult trying a new craft should be allowed to make an ugly bowl. The bowl does not need to become a metaphor for resilience. It can just be an ugly bowl that taught someone how clay behaves.

Some learners will continue, improve, and find a community. Others will lose interest after three sessions. We should not frame every abandoned hobby as a failure of discipline. Sampling the world is one way of finding out what the world contains, and a great many interests are valuable even if they are temporary.

This is not an excuse to be unreliable when other people depend on you. If you join an ensemble and promise to play at a concert, you owe your fellow musicians preparation. If you volunteer to measure rainfall for a research program, you owe that program honest measurements. Freedom from professional identity does not mean freedom from the commitments you voluntarily make.

But it does mean you can make commitments appropriate to the activity. You can tell a group that you are learning. You can choose a community that welcomes beginners. You can stop when an interest no longer brings you anything, without having to transform its ending into a lesson about perseverance.

The ability to begin again is a kind of intellectual wealth. Someone who is sixty and learning their first musical instrument is not a failed musician because a twelve-year-old prodigy can play better. They are doing a different thing. They are adding a dimension to their own life.

There is no competition in which every participant must be ranked against the most accomplished person alive. Or rather, there can be such a competition, but it need not govern every room in which an activity happens.

## The conditions of freedom

There is a serious distributional problem with everything I have said. Leisure is not equally available. A person with a secure income, free evenings, accessible equipment, and supportive friends has many more opportunities to become an amateur mathematician or painter or astronomer than someone working two jobs and caring for children.

Romantic language about doing things purely for love can conceal the material conditions that make such freedom possible. The historic figure of the wealthy amateur scientist is a warning. So is the modern professional who praises the virtue of side projects while enjoying resources that other people do not have.

If we think amateur pursuits matter, we should care about their accessibility. Public libraries, community workshops, parks, shared instruments, affordable classes, local clubs, and spaces where people can practice without buying an identity all help. So do work schedules that leave people with some control over their time.

I am not claiming that any one policy follows automatically from the value of hobbies. Institutions have budgets and competing obligations. But the question belongs in civic life: does a community give ordinary people somewhere to try things, learn from one another, and participate without first demonstrating economic value?

We should also be careful not to turn leisure into an obligation. A person exhausted after work does not need a lecture about optimizing their spare time through productive hobbies. Resting, watching a silly program, or simply doing nothing is not a moral defect. Amateurism is valuable because it is chosen, not because it supplies another standard by which people can be judged inadequate.

And hobby communities themselves have responsibilities. They can become fiercely territorial. An enthusiast who mocks beginners, hoards methods, or treats equipment expense as proof of seriousness may reproduce the worst habits of professional gatekeeping while lacking any of its public duties. A community devoted to love of a subject should be particularly suspicious of turning access into a status competition.

The positive alternative is neither effortless access nor lowered expectations. It is a welcoming path from curiosity to competence. You are allowed to start badly. You can ask a basic question. Someone will tell you when your measurement is wrong. If you care enough, there is more to learn. The door remains open in both directions: you may enter, and you may leave.

## A world with room in it

Let me return to the painter.

Suppose they continue painting for ten years. They never sell anything. Some paintings hang in the hallway; others are stacked in a cupboard. They come to understand why certain colors become muddy when mixed, why the same building looks different in the morning, and how hard it is to paint something that appears simple.

One day a friend asks whether they are ever going to do something with all that practice.

I think the right answer may be: I already have.

Not because the paintings are necessarily excellent. Not because the person has secretly created future economic value. Not because painting is making them more productive at their real job. They have spent ten years developing a relationship with a material, a craft, and a part of the visible world. That relationship is the thing they did.

I do not want a world without professionals. I want skilled nurses and careful engineers, reliable electricians and patient researchers, accomplished artists and people who know the difference between an attractive hypothesis and a demonstrated result. I want their expertise respected, their labor paid, and their standards protected.

But I also want a world in which these activities do not become private property of the people who make their living from them. A world where people are allowed to have interests that do not scale. Where a neighborhood has musicians who have never sought a contract, naturalists who can tell you which birds have returned, and people who understand some small corner of mathematics for no reason other than that they found it beautiful.

Professional excellence and amateur participation are not rival civilizations. At their best, they keep each other alive. Professionals deepen the disciplines that amateurs can enter; amateurs remind a culture that disciplines belong to human curiosity before they belong to institutions.

We can recognize the expertise of the surgeon without demanding that every person who studies anatomy become one. We can admire the virtuoso without treating the rest of the orchestra of human life as an audience that must remain silent.

The amateur, understood properly, is not somebody who has failed to become a professional.

They are somebody who has not agreed that love of a subject must become a job before it counts.

I think there should be more room for that.

## Source and argument notes (not spoken)

**Terminology.** *Amateur* derives via French from Latin *amator*, a lover; the modern contrast with paid professional practice developed historically. The essay distinguishes occupational status from quality rather than claiming that amateurs usually equal experts in proficiency.

**Hanny's Voorwerp.** The NASA Astronomy Picture of the Day entry dated June 25, 2008 identifies Hanny van Arkel as a Dutch schoolteacher, describes her discovery during Galaxy Zoo participation, and distinguishes the initial sighting from subsequent scientific work. Her own retrospective account and the Galaxy Zoo project history establish the 2007 setting. The essay neither claims she determined the object's physical origin alone nor portrays the case as a failure of professional astronomy.

**Galaxy Zoo.** The project's official results page describes the July 2007 start, its crowdsourced galaxy classification task, and its deliberately redundant classifications for assessing agreement. Methodological caveats about bias and quality control are supported by citizen science literature.

**CoCoRaHS.** NOAA's National Weather Service describes volunteer training, standardized precipitation measurements, and the network's use by government services and researchers. The essay makes no claim that every report has the same accuracy or that volunteers replace professional instrumentation.

**Professionalization and access.** A Cambridge History of Science chapter on amateurs and professionals discusses the historical change from the preprofessional scientific world to professional specialization. The essay also acknowledges that unpaid study has depended unevenly on wealth and leisure, and that citizen science projects can raise questions about exploitation and credit. Those ethical discussions are the essay's own arguments, not claims that a cited institution behaved improperly.

**Imagined scenarios.** The painter, musician, beginner potter, retired violin maker, students, and all other unnamed people are constructed illustrations, not reported biographies. Examples concerning AI-generated art and code illustrate a normative argument, not a measured prediction about the effect of AI on participation rates.
