# You Do Not Owe the World an Immediate Opinion

In September 2011, an experiment seemed to have found particles moving faster than light.

The particles were neutrinos, produced at CERN near Geneva and detected about seven hundred and thirty kilometers away beneath the mountains of central Italy. The OPERA collaboration had spent years making a very difficult measurement. Its instruments appeared to show that the neutrinos were arriving roughly sixty nanoseconds earlier than light would have taken to cover the same distance.

Sixty nanoseconds is a very small amount of time. In the context of modern physics, it was an enormous problem.

If the measurement reflected the actual speed of the particles, it would challenge an important part of the framework of relativity. The appropriate response was not to pretend that a result so consequential could be ignored. Nor was it to declare immediately that Einstein had been defeated. The scientists reported an anomaly and invited other researchers to scrutinize it.

They had a result. They did not yet have a settled explanation.

Over the following months, their colleagues tested the timing, the assumptions, and the apparatus. In February 2012, the collaboration identified possible faults involving an oscillator and a fiber-optic connection in the timing system. Independent measurements followed. By June, CERN was reporting results from four experiments consistent with neutrinos traveling at the speed of light, with the original anomaly traced to a fault in the timing system.

It would be tempting to tell this as a story about embarrassment: a group of clever people made a mistake, the world noticed, and eventually the machine was fixed. But that telling misses what I find most valuable about the episode. The scientists did not have to choose between announcing a revolution and concealing the anomaly. They could make a claim about their measurement while declining to make a stronger claim about nature. They could expose their evidence to criticism without adopting a conclusion they could not yet defend.

They could say, in effect, "Here is what we measured. We do not yet know why. Please help us find out."

I want to argue for the intellectual dignity of that position. We often behave as though every question demands an immediate answer and every answer demands a position. I think this expectation distorts thought. It turns conversation into a contest of confidence, makes revising a view feel like defeat, and rewards people who can sound decisive before they understand the subject.

But there is a danger in the opposite direction too. Doubt can be genuine inquiry, or it can become a comfortable excuse for never deciding anything. A person can hide behind open-mindedness while declining to look at evidence that is already decisive. Institutions can demand endless certainty from people who need an answer today.

So the position I want to defend is not "never commit." It is more demanding: **learn the difference between a question you can answer, a question you can investigate, a decision you must make, and a question on which you simply do not need to have a view.**

## The pressure to have a take

Imagine that a colleague sends you an article and asks, "What do you think?" You have not read the paper behind the article. You do not know the methods, the previous literature, or even whether the headline accurately describes the result. But the social occasion offers two familiar performances.

You can produce a confident explanation that fits what you already believe. Or you can produce a confident explanation of why the result is nonsense. Both performances establish that you have a place in the conversation. Neither establishes that you know what happened.

There is also a less theatrical answer: "I don't know enough yet. It looks interesting. The central question is whether the comparison group was appropriate."

That answer does not satisfy every conversational demand. It may be less entertaining. It may make the speaker seem less knowledgeable to someone who mistakes speed for mastery. Yet it contains more usable information than a polished verdict based on a headline.

The urge to answer quickly is not always vanity. Sometimes it is a reasonable response to the situation. In a meeting you have limited time. In a medical emergency you cannot postpone every choice. In an ordinary conversation you may offer a provisional impression because you trust the other person to understand it as provisional. The problem begins when the impression hardens into a commitment merely because it has been spoken aloud.

Now the claim has become yours. Disagreement feels personal. New information threatens not just an idea but the appearance of having been right. The cost of admitting uncertainty goes up after the first declaration, even though the evidence has not improved.

This is one reason I think the order of operations matters. If the initial response to a difficult question is a verdict, all subsequent evidence risks being recruited into its defense. If the initial response is an inquiry, the same evidence has a chance to change the answer.

There is no guarantee that a person who withholds judgment will reason well. They can still select evidence to suit a desired outcome. But they have at least avoided one unnecessary obstacle: the social obligation to preserve a conclusion they never had good grounds to form.

## Suspended judgment is an activity

The philosopher John Dewey treated this as a central feature of reflective thought. In his book *How We Think*, published in 1910, Dewey described the difference between accepting the first plausible suggestion and holding it for examination. His argument was not that thinking consists of refusing to believe things. It was that a suggestion becomes an instrument of reasoning when we can use it as a hypothesis rather than surrendering to it as a conclusion.

That sounds abstract until you try it on an ordinary problem.

Your computer becomes much slower after a software update. One hypothesis is that the update introduced a performance regression. Another is that it changed a configuration and triggered an expensive background job. A third is that the apparent timing is coincidence: an unrelated hardware problem was already developing. You may have a favorite explanation. A favorite explanation is not a diagnosis.

The productive question is not, "Which story feels most plausible?" It is, "What observation would distinguish these stories?"

Perhaps you can reproduce the slowdown on a clean installation. Perhaps the process monitor shows one background service consuming nearly all the available processor time. Perhaps the machine is throttling because of heat. Each result changes what should be believed and what should be tried next.

Notice that the person who says "not yet" has not stopped working. They may be doing more intellectual work than the person who instantly names a culprit. They are keeping several explanations alive long enough to find the evidence that separates them.

A useful uncertainty is specific. It names what is missing. "I don't know" can mean that I have never studied the topic, that experts disagree about a live question, that the current measurement is too noisy, that the relevant terms are ambiguous, or that the answer depends on values rather than facts alone. Those are not interchangeable conditions. They call for different next steps.

Someone who does not know the answer to a settled factual question may need a reference. Someone facing a genuine scientific controversy may need a review of competing models. Someone asked whether a sacrifice is worth making may need to clarify priorities, not commission another measurement. And someone asked an ill-defined question may need to improve the question itself before an answer is possible.

This is why I distrust the habit of using a single phrase like "there are arguments on both sides" as a universal substitute for thinking. It can be a true observation. It can also be a way of hiding how much evidence actually exists, how strong it is, and whether the arguments even address the same question.

You do not become open-minded by giving every hypothesis equal time. You become open-minded by allowing the evidence to determine how much time a hypothesis deserves.

## The map of what you do not know

There is a difference between not having a conclusion and not having a map.

Suppose you have been asked to evaluate a proposal to introduce an automated tutoring system into a school. You do not yet know whether the proposal is good. That is a reasonable starting point, but it is not a complete assessment.

You might identify several separable questions. Does the tool actually improve learning compared with a plausible alternative? For whom does it work? Does it make important errors? What does it cost, including the teacher time needed to supervise it? What happens to a student's work and personal information? Are there benefits that the current tests fail to measure? Which decisions remain with teachers and students?

Those questions do not all have the same status. Some could be tested. Some require inspecting a contract. Some depend on local constraints. Some are choices about what kind of education the school wishes to provide. It is possible to answer a few of them confidently and others not at all.

An honest assessment therefore need not arrive as a single number or a single adjective. It might say: the evidence supports a modest benefit for one use case; the privacy terms are not yet acceptable; there is no credible evaluation for a second use case; and a short pilot could answer the most consequential remaining questions.

That is neither approval nor rejection. It is a structured state of knowledge. If the school must decide this month, the structure helps it choose. If it can postpone the purchase, the same structure tells it what to investigate in the meantime.

The point is not that every problem deserves a grand research program. Most do not. One of the most valuable forms of judgment is deciding when a question is not worth further investigation. But that decision should come from the stakes, the cost of learning, and the likelihood that new information would change what you do. It should not come from a ritual demand that every thought be finished before it can be spoken.

## Why uncertainty is not a single number

It is appealing to imagine that every unanswered question can be assigned a probability. If you are forty percent confident, perhaps you can just say forty percent and move on.

Sometimes you can. But a probability is only useful when the event has been defined clearly and you can explain what justifies the estimate. A precise number attached to a vague sentence can produce an illusion of rigor.

Consider a factory sensor that flags products as defective. Suppose, for the sake of illustration, that one product in a hundred is actually defective. The detector catches nine out of ten defective products, but it also incorrectly flags one out of ten sound products.

In a batch of one thousand, we would expect about ten truly defective products. The detector would flag nine of them. It would also flag about ninety-nine of the nine hundred and ninety good products. So among the hundred and eight alarms, only nine would correspond to real defects. The chance that a flagged product is truly defective, under these assumptions, is about eight percent.

The detector sounds fairly good if you hear only that it catches ninety percent of defects. But that fact alone is not the probability that an alarm is correct. The base rate and false-alarm rate matter too. Changing the factory's failure rate would change the answer, even if the sensor remained identical.

This example is not an argument against quantitative judgment. It is an argument for quantitative judgment that takes its assumptions seriously. The number is useful because we can show how it was obtained, not because it has a decimal point.

Some uncertainties concern a number in a model we otherwise understand. Others concern which model is appropriate in the first place. Still others concern what we should value. A forecast may be uncertain because a parameter could vary, because the system could behave in a way the model omits, or because the goal has never been specified.

Scientific assessment bodies have learned to distinguish some of these dimensions rather than compress them all into a single vague word. The Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change, for example, separates an assessment of confidence in a finding, based on the evidence and agreement, from likelihood, which expresses uncertainty probabilistically where that is justified. It also recognizes situations of deeper uncertainty in which the models, probabilities, or values at issue cannot simply be treated as known.

I am not proposing that every family dinner adopt scientific terminology. I am suggesting that the distinction is worth carrying into everyday life. "We are confident about the direction but uncertain about the size" is informative. "We know the immediate effect but not the long-term consequences" is informative. "People agree on the facts and disagree on their priorities" is informative.

"Nobody knows anything" is not.

## When waiting becomes a choice

Here is the strongest objection to my argument. Sometimes the world does not wait for our conclusions.

Imagine an engineer who discovers evidence of a structural defect in a bridge. They do not yet know precisely how much strength has been lost or which component failed first. People are still using the bridge. The engineer must recommend something before every investigation can be completed.

Saying "we do not know enough yet" may be an accurate description of the engineering analysis. It is not a complete answer to the question of whether traffic should continue.

The decisions have different consequences. Closing the bridge imposes inconvenience and economic cost. Leaving it open could impose a much larger cost if the suspected problem is real. An honest decision has to account for the uncertainty, the possible harm, the reversibility of the options, and the speed with which more evidence can be gathered.

That is the distinction I want to insist on: **you can be uncertain about what is true while taking a definite action in response to that uncertainty.**

Action is not an announcement of perfect knowledge. It is a choice made with the knowledge available, sometimes with a plan to revise the choice. You can evacuate a building while investigating a gas leak. You can preserve data while checking whether a security incident occurred. You can begin a small pilot rather than approve an enormous rollout. You can also decide that the evidence is already strong enough to proceed without further delay.

The right action depends on the stakes. A reversible experiment may be worth trying under substantial uncertainty. An irreversible change may justify stronger evidence first. But reversibility is not a magic formula either: delaying a necessary action can have irreversible consequences of its own.

A society that insists on certainty before acting will eventually become incapable of taking responsibility. A society that prizes decisiveness while ignoring uncertainty will make avoidable mistakes with great confidence. The task is to keep the assessment honest while making the choice explicit.

This also explains why a provisional decision should not be disguised as an eternal belief. If new evidence arrives, a responsible person should be able to revise the decision without pretending the earlier evidence was something other than it was. The earlier choice can have been reasonable under the circumstances even if later evidence shows it was mistaken.

Being wrong about an outcome and having reasoned badly are not the same thing. Conversely, getting lucky does not retroactively make a reckless decision intelligent.

## The dishonest uses of doubt

There is another objection, more troubling than impatience. Calls for caution can be used to delay the recognition of facts that are already well supported.

The tobacco industry's history provides a documented example. A 1969 Brown and Williamson document, preserved in the University of California, San Francisco's Industry Documents Library, framed the cultivation of public doubt as a communications strategy. The document is often remembered for the short phrase, "Doubt is our product."

This was not the productive uncertainty of a laboratory that had found an anomaly and invited attempts to explain it. It was an effort to encourage public controversy around an existing body of evidence, because controversy served a commercial interest. The difference is not that one group contained uncertain people while the other contained confident people. The difference is what each did with uncertainty and what evidence they were prepared to acknowledge.

A plea for open-mindedness can sound virtuous while being used to keep a conclusion permanently out of reach. Whenever the evidence grows stronger, the standard of proof can be raised. Whenever one claim fails, attention can shift to another question. A demand for perfect certainty can become a machine for making almost any conclusion impossible.

The appropriate response is not to abolish doubt. It is to ask whether the doubt has a clear object and a possible resolution. What exactly is uncertain? Which existing evidence is being challenged? What finding would settle the objection? Are the standards of proof being applied consistently? Is the person asking for more evidence also willing to say what sufficient evidence would look like?

Those questions do not prove bad faith. They help distinguish an investigation from a delay tactic.

There is also a subtler failure: false balance. Imagine that a dozen independent methods support a conclusion, while one poorly controlled study claims the opposite. Saying "scientists are divided" would not be a careful expression of uncertainty. It would misdescribe the distribution and quality of the evidence.

A disciplined refusal to jump to conclusions has to include the ability to arrive at conclusions. Otherwise, patience becomes indistinguishable from paralysis. It is perfectly reasonable to say, "This question remains open" when it does. It is not reasonable to make every established result start from zero whenever someone asks a skeptical question.

## Some questions are not owed an answer

Up to this point I have described uncertainty as a stage in inquiry: you lack an answer, then you investigate, then perhaps you reach one. But there is another possibility. You may not need an opinion at all.

Think about how many subjects demand attention in the course of an ordinary week. Someone asks which technological platform will dominate in ten years. Someone asks you to pronounce on a dispute between strangers. A headline presents a scandal whose facts are still emerging. A prediction market offers a number for an event you have never considered. Every question arrives with the subtle invitation to become the sort of person who has a position on it.

You are allowed to decline.

This is not a claim that ignorance is admirable in itself. It is a claim about the finite nature of attention. Learning enough to hold a responsible opinion costs time, and spending that time on one topic means not spending it somewhere else. We should be choosy about what deserves our inquiry.

There is an ethical dimension as well. A quickly assembled verdict about another person's life can affect their reputation even when the speaker has no access to the relevant facts. Sometimes the responsible contribution is to insist that the evidence is insufficient. Sometimes it is to say nothing at all.

I think this right to remain undecided is important in a culture where an expressed view can become a social identity. If you have no obligation to join every argument, you can be more serious about the arguments you do choose to join. Your attention becomes a contribution rather than an admission ticket.

There is an obvious limit. If you are responsible for a decision, your refusal to form a view may transfer the cost to other people. A judge, a safety engineer, or a manager cannot always say, "This is not my problem." The right not to have an opinion is not the right to avoid every obligation. It applies most clearly where you have no duty to decide and no useful knowledge to contribute.

Still, even people with responsibilities benefit from a simple sentence: "I can make the required decision, but I am not going to pretend it settles every question involved."

## What a good assistant should do with a pause

There is a special version of this problem in artificial intelligence. A conversational system is built to respond. A user asks, and an answer appears. The rhythm makes the empty space between question and answer seem like a malfunction.

But immediate completion is not the same thing as useful assistance.

Suppose a user asks whether a new medical finding proves that an established treatment is ineffective. The assistant can make the conversation feel complete by delivering a forceful yes or no. If it has not inspected the study and does not know the relevant context, that confidence would be borrowed from the fluent shape of the answer. It would not be earned by evidence.

A better response would locate the boundary of what can presently be said. It might explain the established treatment, identify the specific claim the new study would have to support, distinguish a preliminary result from a synthesis of evidence, and name what cannot be concluded without examining the paper. The assistant could still provide useful context. It need not substitute an empty disclaimer for an explanation.

The same principle applies to code review, historical research, and personal advice. Sometimes the missing item is a log file. Sometimes it is the user's priorities. Sometimes it is permission to test a hypothesis. Sometimes it is simply the admission that the world has not supplied enough information yet.

A well-designed assistant should not fill every gap with a likely-sounding guess. Nor should it hide behind endless questions to avoid making a useful attempt. It should decide which uncertainty is consequential, make a provisional effort where possible, and expose the assumptions that matter.

There is an important asymmetry here. An AI assistant can produce a paragraph that sounds settled almost instantly. A human listener may take the paragraph as evidence that someone has done the relevant intellectual work. That inference is not warranted by fluency alone. The assistant should try to make the state of the evidence visible, not merely the state of the prose.

I would rather see a response that says, "The evidence favors this explanation, but I have not checked the latest measurements," than one that silently replaces missing evidence with a confident voice. And I would rather see a system say, "This criticism was mistaken; your counterexample changes the answer," than preserve its first response for the sake of appearing consistent.

The standard should not be endless self-doubt. It should be *corrigibility in the ordinary intellectual sense*: the ability to recognize what would change a conclusion and to let that evidence do its work.

## How to finish a thought without pretending it is final

We need conclusions. The remedy for overconfidence cannot be a world in which every sentence ends in a shrug. Science advances by making claims, testing them, and sometimes rejecting them. Engineering advances by choosing designs and learning where they fail. A person cannot spend an entire life waiting to be certain before they act.

So what does a responsible conclusion sound like?

It states what the evidence currently supports. It separates that from the assumptions required to interpret it. It identifies uncertainties that could realistically change the answer. It does not inflate minor possibilities into equal alternatives. And, where a decision is needed, it says what action follows under the circumstances.

This has a kind of strength that theatrical certainty lacks. A conclusion that acknowledges its conditions does not automatically collapse when one condition changes. It can be updated. It gives its reader somewhere to apply pressure.

Return to the OPERA experiment. The striking fact is not that physics was saved from a bad measurement. Physics is a method practiced by people, so mistakes and corrections are part of it. The striking fact is that the collaboration could publish an anomaly without requiring the whole world to accept its most dramatic interpretation. Its reported discrepancy became a question that other investigators could test.

The most intelligent sentence was not "Einstein was wrong." It was not even "Einstein must be right." It was the invitation to find out what the measurement meant.

I suspect we would have better conversations if we made more room for that posture. We could be less afraid of changing our minds, because we would be less likely to mistake our first thought for a public oath. We could recognize the difference between the person who has studied a question and found the evidence incomplete and the person who has not studied it but enjoys expressing doubt. We could act when action is required without inventing confidence we do not possess.

Above all, we could recover a simple freedom: not every question must become an immediate statement about who we are.

You do not owe the world an opinion on demand. You owe the people affected by your decisions an honest account of what you know, what you do not know, and what you are prepared to do about the difference.

And sometimes the most serious thing you can say is not yes, not no, but "not yet."

## Notes on evidence and constructed illustrations

The OPERA episode is historical, not invented. CERN's 23 September 2011 statement reported an apparent early neutrino arrival over approximately 730 km; its 2012 updates documented potential timing defects and independent results consistent with the light-speed limit. The statement carefully distinguished the original measurement from a validated discovery of faster-than-light particles. The essay's description is a synthesis of the dated CERN release and its later updates, not a reconstruction of researchers' private thoughts.

The computer slowdown, school proposal, factory detector, and bridge scenarios are constructed illustrations. The factory example assumes a 1% defect prevalence, 90% sensitivity and 10% false-positive rate; the posterior proportion is 9 / (9 + 99) = 8.33%. These numbers illustrate conditional probability and are not performance claims about a real device.

The Dewey discussion refers to *How We Think* (1910), especially the chapters on reflective thinking and the conditional use of a suggestion as a hypothesis. It paraphrases Dewey's argument rather than quoting at length. Contemporary philosophical accounts distinguish justified suspension of judgment from failure to consider a question at all.

The IPCC passage refers to the calibrated uncertainty framework of Working Group I, Sixth Assessment Report, Chapter 1. The framework distinguishes a qualitative confidence assessment from probabilistic likelihood and discusses 'deep uncertainty' that may involve unknown or contested models, probabilities, or values. The essay does not suggest that all IPCC findings are unsettled or that uncertainty implies an absence of scientific consensus.

The tobacco example is documented by the University of California, San Francisco Industry Documents Library, which preserves the 1969 Brown and Williamson *Smoking and Health Proposal*. It is included to distinguish genuine inquiry from the deliberate promotion of doubt and should not be generalized into an accusation about every person who asks for stronger evidence.
