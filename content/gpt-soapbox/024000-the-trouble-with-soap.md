# The Trouble with Soap

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

A soapbox seems like a reasonable place to deliver a lecture about soap. It has taken me twenty-three attempts to arrive at the obvious subject, but I think it deserves the attention.

Here is the problem. You have grease on your hands. Perhaps you were fixing a bicycle, or washing the pan in which you cooked dinner. Put your hands under a tap. Water pours over them, and much of the grease stays. You can rub harder. You can turn the tap up. At some point you notice that the water is gathering into little beads, slipping past the smudge instead of carrying it away.

Add soap, work your fingers together, and rinse again. The grease goes down the drain.

Most explanations stop here. Soap has one end that likes water and another that likes grease. This is true, as far as it goes. But it is a remarkably abbreviated account of a substance that required people to master aggressive chemistry before they understood molecules, that made a significant business out of washing wool, that helped change public health, and that can actually destroy a painting by Vermeer.

I also want to get the sequence right. Soap's ability to clean did not wait for an explanation of its chemistry. People learned to make and use something effective, then kept modifying the process for centuries. An explanation arrived much later, and the explanation eventually helped us make better cleaners, including products that are not, chemically speaking, soap at all.

One thing at a time. Why doesn't plain water remove that grease?

## The reluctant mixture

Water molecules attract one another. Each contains an oxygen atom and two hydrogen atoms, arranged in a way that gives the molecule an uneven distribution of electrical charge. The whole molecule is electrically neutral, but one end is slightly negative and another is slightly positive. Water molecules can form transient attractions called hydrogen bonds. They are constantly moving, making and breaking those bonds, but the collective effect is strong enough to matter everywhere from the behavior of a raindrop to the energy needed to boil water.

Grease is a different neighborhood. Most of its molecules have long portions made of carbon and hydrogen. These hydrocarbon structures do not engage in the same favorable interactions with water. Put a little oil into a glass of water and stir. For a moment you can make droplets. Stop stirring and, with time, the oil gathers again. A continuous interface between water and oil costs less energy than countless tiny interfaces. The droplets coalesce, helped along by collisions and the tendency of oil to separate from the water around it.

This is not a matter of oil being dirty and water being clean. Both are made of molecules behaving according to their interactions. If the smudge on your fingers is mostly oil, water has little reason to lift and accommodate it. The oil may also cling to your skin through its own contact with the surface. Water can get around the deposit while leaving much of it where it is.

Enter the soap molecule. To make the chemistry concrete, imagine a molecule of sodium stearate, a common example of a soap. Most of the molecule is a long hydrocarbon chain. At one end is a charged carboxylate group, accompanied by a sodium ion. The hydrocarbon chain can associate with oil. The charged end interacts favorably with water. Chemists call this combination amphiphilic: it has an affinity for two different kinds of environment.

The popular story is that the tail dives into grease while the head grabs onto water, and the stain is pulled away. That is a useful cartoon. Real cleaning involves several processes at once. Soap molecules collect at boundaries between oil and water, helping to reduce the energy required to create new surface. Agitation breaks greasy deposits into smaller pieces. Surfactant molecules help keep those pieces dispersed so that they are less likely to stick straight back onto the surface. Above a sufficient concentration, soap molecules can assemble into structures called micelles, with hydrocarbon-rich interiors and charged outer surfaces. Micelles can accommodate small amounts of oily material in their interiors.

Different kinds of dirt require different contributions from those mechanisms. A lump of grease is not necessarily swallowed whole by a single spherical micelle, and a grain of dust is not made of oil. Mechanical rubbing and flowing rinse water do a great deal of work. Soap makes that work more effective.

I like this level of explanation because it allows the familiar demonstration to become a set of questions. What sort of grease? How much soap? What temperature is the water? What are we washing? How are we agitating it? What else is dissolved in the water? The difference between a clean plate and a greasy one is affected by all of them.

Even foam can mislead us. Bubbles are visible evidence that some surface-active material is present, but the height of a mountain of foam is not a direct measurement of how much grease has been removed. A cleaning formulation can be designed to produce an impressive lather. It can also be designed to clean effectively with less visible foam. What matters is the material left on the surface after the rinse.

Before we get too comfortable with our beautifully cooperative soap molecule, we should ask where it comes from.

## A woman rearranges the water

In 1891, a German experimenter named Agnes Pockels appeared in the pages of the scientific journal Nature, though the appearance required an intermediary. She had written to the physicist Lord Rayleigh about experiments she had been performing on the surfaces of liquids. Rayleigh recognized the value of the work and forwarded a translation of her letter for publication.

Pockels did not have a conventional academic career. She had investigated water surfaces with equipment she devised using ordinary materials. The important part of her apparatus was a shallow trough with a movable barrier. She could spread a small amount of oil on a water surface and then slide the barrier to change the area available to the film. She could measure how the surface tension changed as the film became more or less compressed.

That sounds simple until you try to perform it reliably. A water surface can be altered by a minute amount of contamination. Touch it with the wrong object, and the result changes. Pockels learned to manipulate the surface, including sweeping away material before a measurement. She could make small differences visible as changes in a measurable force.

There is a famous image of scientific discovery in which somebody sees a phenomenon in the natural world and immediately grasps its significance. Pockels's example is less theatrical. She built a way to adjust one variable while holding others steady. That is what turned a kitchen observation into something another scientist could investigate.

The experiment is also a useful answer to the question of what soap actually does at a surface. Molecules that collect at an interface can change the forces there even when the amount of material is far too small to notice as a layer. Compress the film into a smaller area, and the molecules have less room. Their arrangement and interactions change. The surface responds.

You can encounter related effects by blowing a bubble. A bubble contains a thin liquid film, stabilized by surface-active molecules. You see colors moving across the film under white light, and it is easy to think the detergent must be tinted. The colors mostly come from interference between light reflected at the front and back of the thin film. Depending on the film's thickness and the angle from which you look, some wavelengths reinforce one another and others cancel. Water drains and the film thins. The colors move as its thickness changes. The very thinnest regions can look nearly black before the film breaks.

The film's beauty is incidental to the washing job. A dishwasher might not care about it at all. But a physicist can use the same phenomenon to investigate the behavior of a surface at scales too small to see directly. A child's bubble wand is also a device for producing visible evidence about the thickness of an invisible layer.

Pockels's method helped open a field of surface science in which the forces at boundaries can be measured and manipulated. I could call that an inspiring lesson about curiosity. It is more satisfying, I think, to picture her moving the strip across a tray of water and seeing the measuring balance respond.

## The workshop's cauldron

The old recipe sounds suspicious. Take fat. Add something harsh enough to burn skin. Heat and stir. The result, under suitable conditions, can be a material used to wash skin.

Fats and many oils contain triglycerides. A triglyceride is built from a glycerol backbone attached to three fatty-acid chains. When an appropriate strong alkali reacts with the triglyceride, the chemical links attaching the chains to glycerol are broken. The products include glycerol and salts of the fatty acids. Those salts are soaps.

The process is called saponification. It is a chemical transformation, not just the mixing of two substances. It has a particular consequence that strikes me as wonderfully practical: the fatty material you wanted to remove from something can be transformed into a material that helps remove fatty material from something else.

Modern recipes may use carefully measured sodium hydroxide or potassium hydroxide. Historically, producers obtained alkaline materials from plant ashes and other sources. An ash-derived mixture is not automatically identical to pure sodium hydroxide, and the older process could be chemically messy. Different oils, quantities of water, alkali concentrations, temperatures, and cooking methods produced different substances. Some were soft, some hard, some useful for textiles, some suitable for personal washing, and some distinctly unsuitable for a delicate surface.

The chemistry also explains why careless soapmaking is dangerous. Concentrated alkalis are caustic. Their ability to react with fats is not a guarantee that they will politely ignore living tissue. A finished, correctly made bar is a different chemical environment from an unfinished batch containing excess lye.

The earliest written evidence gets us back to Mesopotamia in the third millennium before the common era. Researchers Kristine Konkol and Seth Rasmussen have examined ancient soap production through surviving written records, rather than assuming that the oldest imaginable recipe must be the oldest real one. A tablet from around 2200 BCE describes oil and potash in a context of cleaning cloth. Other accounts of early soap-like materials exist, but dating the first human encounter with this chemistry is much harder than dating a surviving document.

It is tempting to write an invention scene: somebody spills grease into a fire pit, rain washes through the ashes, and an observant neighbor discovers soap. Such a thing might have happened, somewhere, more than once. It is also a story for which we do not possess a witness. I would rather keep the documentary evidence and let the first discovery remain unknown.

There is a more interesting historical complication. Washing cloth, preparing wool for dyeing, caring for hair, bathing, and preventing infection are not interchangeable activities. In early records, soap can be a manufacturing aid. It helps remove grease from fibers so that they can be processed. Wool is naturally oily, partly because of the secretions that coat the animal's fleece. Before it can be worked into certain textiles, the fibers need cleaning. The people handling the cloth might have valued a caustic, unpleasant mixture for its ability to do that one job.

We should be wary of projecting a modern bathroom backward into every account of soap. The ancient Roman world had elaborate bathing practices, but they did not simply consist of using our familiar bars of soap. Oil and scraping tools could be part of bodily cleaning. Pliny the Elder discussed a material called soap in connection with hair treatment among the Gauls. A word survives, and recipes survive, but their uses are not identical to ours.

A chemical technology can spend a long time being useful before it becomes the ordinary household object by which we now recognize it.

## A bar with a family name

There is a way to make the history less abstract than a timeline of inventions.

In Aleppo, soapmakers have long made bars using olive oil, laurel oil, water, and alkali. UNESCO's description of the craft names the seasonal operations: gathering laurel berries, obtaining the oil, cooking the mixture in winter, pouring it onto a broad floor, and allowing the mass to cool. Workers wearing large wooden shoes walk across the set material as they cut it into blocks. They stamp the pieces. The bars are stacked to let air circulate and left to dry for months.

I have no reason to suggest that every workshop follows precisely the same schedule or that the craft has been frozen in time. The documented process is already interesting without that claim. Consider the scale of a floor covered with soap, the care needed to produce a workable mixture, the decision about when to cut, and the way a stamp turns a chemically ordinary block into a product traceable to a maker.

In Nablus, Palestinian soapmakers have their own tradition, centered on olive oil, water, and lye. Families cut and stamp blocks and store them for long periods. In 2024, UNESCO listed both Aleppo's ghar-soap craftsmanship and the tradition of Nabulsi soapmaking as intangible cultural heritage.

The word intangible is worth a second look. We are discussing an extremely tangible thing, a bar you can pick up, rub between wet hands, and eventually use until it disappears. The heritage being recognized includes the knowledge of making it: how to judge the mixture, organize the workshop, cut the mass, and pass the process on. The object leaves the workshop; the know-how must remain available for the next batch.

This is also a reminder that a scientific description does not exhaust a craft. A formula can tell you what reaction is possible. It does not tell you whether a particular batch has reached the texture at which it can be divided cleanly, or how to arrange a room so hundreds of bars dry evenly, or how a business can survive a bad harvest. Those questions are not supernatural. They are practical knowledge, much of it susceptible to scientific explanation, embedded in a particular place and way of working.

The point is easy to miss in a modern shop, where one bar sits in a neat wrapper beside another bar that looks nearly identical. Buying soap rarely requires you to know what happened between the olive harvest and the stamp. You might be glad not to have to know; that is part of the attraction of a dependable product. But the steps have not ceased to exist.

There are people who still make and sell that kind of soap, and the history belongs to them as living work. I do not want to treat them as museum actors performing an earlier century for our benefit.

## When water refuses to cooperate

We have reached a danger for any account that gives soap too much credit. Water can defeat it.

In some places, water contains substantial dissolved calcium and magnesium ions. We call it hard water. In a supply pipe, the minerals may be nearly invisible; in a kettle, they can leave scale. At the sink, they can interfere with soap.

The negatively charged fatty-acid part of a soap molecule is usually paired with a sodium or potassium ion. Put that soap into hard water, and calcium or magnesium can form compounds with the fatty-acid ions. Many of those compounds are poorly soluble. They precipitate. The resulting deposit is familiar as soap scum.

This is an excellent example of why the molecular explanation matters. A soap molecule with a water-loving end sounds universally useful until an ion already in the water changes what the molecule can do. The calcium ion has a positive charge of two, so it can associate with two fatty-acid ions. The result can leave the water and deposit on a surface. Some of the cleaning material has been turned into residue.

If you have lived with hard water, you may have noticed the consequences before hearing the words calcium stearate. You use more product. Rinsing can be frustrating. A film builds up on the tub or the sink. This does not mean no cleaning is possible. It means that the water itself has become part of the formulation problem.

A synthetic detergent can address it differently. Chemists can build molecules that still possess a water-compatible end and an oil-compatible end, but whose other chemical features make them less likely to form the same insoluble deposits. The distinction matters: in ordinary speech, we call many detergents soaps. Chemically, a traditional soap is a salt of a fatty acid. A synthetic surfactant need not be one.

Industrial chemists did not invent detergents merely because they were bored of soap. Different water supplies, industrial requirements, and raw-material constraints presented real limitations. In 1933, Procter and Gamble brought a synthetic laundry detergent called Dreft to market. It washed well in hard water but initially struggled with heavily soiled laundry. The company continued developing detergents and introduced Tide in the 1940s with a more capable cleaning formulation.

The problem did not end with designing a molecule that avoided soap scum. Laundry is a system. A detergent might need additives that improve its performance against different soils, with different temperatures and water conditions. Some formulations used phosphate compounds to manage water hardness. Phosphorus, however, can promote excessive plant and algal growth when it reaches susceptible waters. The chemical answer inside a washing machine can become a problem downstream.

Industrial history has further revisions: changing surfactant structures to improve their breakdown in the environment, reducing or replacing certain additives, reformulating for new machines, and arguing about what constitutes an acceptable cleaning result. I do not need to insist that every new formulation was better in every respect. The useful thing is seeing the series of specific constraints. Removing a grease stain is one task. Protecting a river is another. A competent formulation has to contend with the parts of the route between them.

Soap, at this point, has become something you can learn from even when you have no intention of making it. Every ingredient has a job. Every job has conditions. A product used in a house will leave that house, usually diluted in water, and continue behaving like chemicals do.

## Soap becomes a radio program

There is an unrelated chapter of the story that I cannot resist including. In twentieth-century American broadcasting, manufacturers of soap and laundry products paid for radio programs intended to attract the people buying household supplies. Daytime serial dramas became a particularly useful advertising vehicle. Procter and Gamble sponsored programs including Ma Perkins and The Guiding Light, among others. The association was so persistent that serial melodramas acquired the name soap operas.

That phrase has now outlived the specific advertising arrangement that gave it currency. It is a pleasing example of a material product leaving a trace in language. Soap has nothing chemical to do with a complicated fictional family, unless somebody spills detergent at a critical moment in the plot. The connection was commercial. A company wanted an audience's attention, so it paid for stories whose installments would keep that audience tuning in.

I include this detour because it means a history of soap is also a history of what people spent money on, who was expected to do household work, and how advertisers imagined their customers. There are implications worth examining separately. For today I am content with the strange fact that a chemical designed to lift grease helped give a name to a form of popular drama.

## A bar of soap is not a disinfectant label

Soap often appears in the same sentence as disease prevention, which invites another oversimplification. We say it kills germs. Sometimes soap and related surfactants disrupt certain microbes, especially ones with vulnerable lipid membranes. But ordinary handwashing also works by removing microorganisms from the skin, suspending them in water, and carrying them away.

Those are different mechanisms. And the difference changes what we should expect.

Alcohol-based hand sanitizer can inactivate many microbes without a sink. In many healthcare settings, that speed and convenience are important. Soap and water can work better when the hands are visibly greasy or dirty, when contaminants need to be physically removed, and against certain pathogens such as norovirus that alcohol sanitizer does not address as reliably. The United States Centers for Disease Control and Prevention advises handwashing with soap and water whenever practical in community settings, while recommending alcohol-based hand rubs in many healthcare circumstances where hands are not visibly soiled.

Plain soap is generally enough for ordinary handwashing. A bottle advertising antibacterial power is not, merely by having that label, a better choice for everyday household use. The CDC notes that routine use of consumer antibacterial soap has not been shown to provide added health benefits over plain soap and water.

There are several charmingly unglamorous facts here. The water can be cold or warm; it need not scald you. The entire surface of the hands needs rubbing, including the spaces between fingers and around nails. The reason for spending enough time is mechanical and practical: contact, friction, and removal are not instantaneous. Running water can carry the material away. A clean way to dry the hands completes the job.

None of this depends on foam as a magical signal. Nor does it mean that an ordinary bar is a universal sterilizing agent. Treating a bathroom sink as a sterile laboratory would be both impossible and unnecessary for most of the activities we perform there.

But here is something less cheerful. A recommendation is easy to issue from a place where a person can turn a tap and obtain clean water. In 2024, the World Health Organization and UNICEF estimated that about eighty percent of the global population had access to basic handwashing facilities with soap and water at home. That leaves approximately 1.7 billion people without that basic service. The estimate measures access to a facility supplied with soap and water; it is not a count of people who have never washed their hands, nor an assertion that every household without a fixed sink lacks any ability to clean.

A bar of soap has a small radius of action. It cannot clean a hand if there is no reliable water to rinse it. It cannot repair a broken water supply. It does not plan drainage, pay for treatment works, or maintain the tap. It is tempting to make that an enormous statement about public institutions. For the moment, I want to leave it as an engineering fact. The removal of material from skin requires somewhere for the material to go.

This is also what makes soap such a peculiar technology to admire. Unlike a chair or a bridge, it works partly by being used up. The bar diminishes. The washed-off grease goes elsewhere. Success is a controlled transfer of matter, accomplished by molecules that facilitate the transfer and then leave with the water.

Now I want to take you somewhere the soap most certainly should not leave.

## The painting that made its own soap

Johannes Vermeer painted a view of Delft around 1660. It shows the city and its roofs in unusually concentrated sunlight. Look closely at certain red roof areas, and you can find pale spots that were not simply decorative touches made by the painter.

Conservation scientists have connected these protrusions to lead soaps that formed within the paint. The oil in an oil painting is not completely inert after the artist sets down a brush. Its organic components can age, migrate, and react. Fatty-acid components can interact with lead-containing compounds in pigments and other paint ingredients. The reaction produces lead carboxylates, a type of metal soap.

This is the same broad chemical family as the calcium fatty-acid salts that make soap scum in hard water, though the exact substances, structures, and circumstances differ. In the bathroom, a soap residue is an annoyance to be scrubbed away. In a centuries-old painting, a metal soap can accumulate inside a paint layer, form lumps, push through the surface, alter the appearance, or contribute to the separation of layers.

The Metropolitan Museum of Art has documented this problem in paintings from different centuries. In some cases, the protrusions are visible; in others, conservation scientists detect the compounds in microscopic samples. Zinc soaps in certain paints can be linked to brittleness and delamination. That means the paint layer may begin to detach from the surface underneath. The chemistry is not finished simply because the artist has finished painting.

There is a pleasingly perverse twist here. Soaps can be defined by their chemistry without reference to cleaning. Their name carries a domestic association, but their actual formation is a reaction between particular ingredients. Once you understand the reaction, you should not be surprised to meet some members of the family in a place where cleaning would be entirely beside the point.

Of course, I would not recommend solving the problem by taking the painting to the sink. Conservators face a more delicate set of choices. They need to determine which changes are happening, whether an intervention can stabilize the paint, what new damage an intervention could cause, and how much of an object's aged material state should be left intact. It is difficult to reverse a change occurring inside the paint without harming the paint itself.

I began with grease on a pair of hands. The water had trouble taking it away, so a clever molecule helped. In an oil painting, the fatty substances can take part in a different process, producing compounds that refuse to remain politely hidden. I had assumed, at the start, that soap was a useful member of the household. It turns out the word names a chemical possibility that the world is quite capable of producing without consulting our wishes.

Imagine an expert examining a tiny cross-section of damaged paint. Under the microscope, a pale mass sits in a layer that once had a more continuous color. The people who made the paint did not set out to manufacture soap. The painting did it anyway, gradually, where no one could rinse it away.

## Additional technical note (not spoken)

Saponification of a triglyceride with strong alkali yields glycerol and three fatty-acid carboxylate salts. The precise products depend on the fatty-acid chains and alkali. Many commercial cleaners use synthetic surfactants that are chemically distinct from soaps; not all products marketed as 'soap' are conventional sodium or potassium fatty-acid salts. In hard water, calcium and magnesium can displace sodium/potassium and produce insoluble fatty-acid salts. The art-conservation compounds involve lead or zinc carboxylates rather than the soluble alkali-metal soaps of household washing.
