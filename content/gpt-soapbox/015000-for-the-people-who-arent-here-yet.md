# For the People Who Aren't Here Yet

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

On a Norwegian island in the Arctic, seeds from many countries are stored inside a mountain. The place is called the Svalbard Global Seed Vault. It is a backup for collections maintained by other institutions: packets of agricultural possibility kept in reserve against the day when an ordinary seed bank loses something it cannot easily replace.

In southeastern New Mexico, another long-term project has a very different message to send. At the Waste Isolation Pilot Plant, a deep geological repository for certain defense-related radioactive wastes, planners have studied how to warn people who might encounter the site thousands of years from now. The United States Department of Energy has developed a conceptual system of markers, records, and warnings intended to communicate across a regulatory period of ten thousand years.

One project says, in effect: here is something you may need. The other says: here is something you must be careful not to disturb.

Both are addressed to strangers who cannot answer us. We do not know their languages, their governments, their technologies, or what they will regard as obvious. We cannot know whether they will have preserved our records, or whether they will be puzzled by the things we thought we were preserving. And the people to whom these messages are addressed have no opportunity to inspect our designs, request changes, or refuse the inheritance.

This is the subject I want to defend today: people who do not yet exist can still matter in decisions we make now. But taking them seriously is harder than merely declaring that we care about the future.

It requires us to make judgments under deep uncertainty. It requires us to consider people who have no political representation in the ordinary sense. It requires us to distinguish preserving opportunity from imposing our preferences. And it requires an answer to an especially uncomfortable question: how much may we ask people who are alive now to sacrifice for people who are not?

I do not think there is one formula that settles these questions. I do think there are better and worse ways of facing them. And I think a civilization can reveal a great deal about itself by the way it treats people who are not yet able to complain.

## A gift, and a warning

The seed vault opened in two thousand eight. Its purpose is often described dramatically, as if it were a pantry stocked for the end of the world. That is not quite right. Its ordinary work is more concrete. Crop diversity is stored in gene banks around the world, where seeds support research, breeding, and conservation. Such collections can be lost through conflict, disaster, equipment failure, poor funding, or a hundred less cinematic events. Svalbard keeps backup samples of some of those collections. The institutions depositing seeds retain ownership and the right to retrieve them.

A seed vault cannot preserve agriculture on its own. Seeds need records, trained people, viable storage conditions, institutions able to request them, and places where they can be grown. The vault is not a replacement for living agricultural knowledge. It is one additional layer of protection.

And this is not merely a story about an imagined catastrophe centuries away. The International Center for Agricultural Research in the Dry Areas had kept important seed collections in Syria. As war disrupted its operations, the institute drew on material it had deposited in Svalbard. The first withdrawal occurred in two thousand fifteen. Returned seeds helped establish renewed collections in Lebanon and Morocco.

Notice what makes this achievement important. Nobody in two thousand eight needed to predict the precise events that would make a withdrawal necessary. The architects of the system only needed to understand that the world contains failure modes they could not schedule. They made a future recovery possible without deciding in advance exactly who would need it, or why.

The waste repository asks a harsher version of the same question. The Waste Isolation Pilot Plant, usually called WIPP, receives a specific class of radioactive waste associated with United States defense activities. It does not dispose of spent fuel from commercial nuclear reactors. The distinction is important: different waste streams pose different technical and regulatory problems.

The WIPP problem is not to invite future people to recover something useful. It is to reduce the chance that somebody in a distant future inadvertently enters a hazardous site. Its proposed markers have to communicate that this ground is not ordinary ground and that the warning is worth taking seriously. The Department of Energy has considered more than a single sign: physical markers, maps, archived descriptions, and multiple kinds of redundant information. There are sound reasons for redundancy when any one channel might fail.

But consider the difficulty. A warning symbol that is obvious to us might be unfamiliar to somebody with a different visual culture. An imposing monument might signal danger. It might also signal that something valuable has been buried. A written inscription could be precise today and unreadable in a few centuries. An electronic archive might hold enormous detail and become unusable when its readers, formats, or power supplies disappear.

It is tempting to turn this into a science-fiction exercise about what future people will be like. I prefer the more severe interpretation. We are not trying to predict them. We are trying to avoid making their safety depend entirely on the accuracy of our predictions.

The gift and the warning have something in common. They give future people a chance to respond to circumstances we cannot foresee. One protects a resource they may wish to use. The other tries to protect them from a danger they did not choose. Neither project succeeds by knowing the future in advance.

## The missing seat at the table

A person affected by a decision can usually do something about it, at least in principle. They can argue, vote, sue, protest, negotiate, walk away, or make a competing proposal. These possibilities are unevenly distributed, and many people have far too little power. But the possibilities exist.

Future people have none of them.

Imagine a town choosing between two designs for a bridge. One costs less to construct but will probably require a disruptive replacement in thirty years. The other costs more today, lasts longer, and is easier to inspect and repair. You cannot select the better choice from these facts alone. Perhaps the town urgently needs the money for a hospital. Perhaps the longer-lived bridge is wildly overpriced. Perhaps the supposedly cheap bridge will become expensive when its maintenance is counted honestly.

But there is a recognizable political imbalance. The people who pay today can attend the meeting. Many of the people who will pay to replace the bridge have not been born. The decision that seems inexpensive to present voters may merely have shifted the bill into a period when the voters cannot be held accountable for it.

This is one reason I distrust claims about efficiency that omit time. When someone announces that a project is economical, I want to know whose costs have been counted, who bears the risks, and how many years the account remains open. Efficiency is a relationship between ends and resources, not a property of a number floating free of its assumptions.

Democracy has particular difficulty with this. Elections happen on human schedules. Political careers happen on shorter ones. Annual budgets create their own horizons. Yet the systems governed by these institutions can last decades, centuries, or longer. Their consequences do not disappear when a government loses office.

Wales tried to acknowledge this institutional blind spot in its Well-being of Future Generations Act, passed in two thousand fifteen. The law requires specified public bodies to consider sustainable development and the long-term effects of their work. It established a Future Generations Commissioner with responsibilities that include advice, review, and advocacy for the ability of future generations to meet their needs.

I do not want to pretend that appointing a commissioner solves the philosophical problem. A commissioner is a present-day person with present-day assumptions. The office does not receive votes from future citizens. It cannot know what they will want. Its authority and effectiveness depend on the law and the institutions around it.

Nevertheless, there is a serious idea here: if the people most affected by some consequences cannot show up, the procedure should force us to examine those consequences rather than quietly treat their absence as consent.

We cannot give future generations a seat in a literal town meeting. We can make it harder to close the meeting without discussing their interests.

## What does a hundred years cost?

Economists have a technique called discounting. The basic idea is familiar even if the name is not. A dollar available today is not the same financial resource as a dollar available many years from now. Money can be invested. Risks change over time. Opportunities differ. If you must choose between one hundred dollars today and one hundred dollars in the distant future, there are perfectly rational reasons to prefer the money now.

This becomes difficult when we turn from private finance to public decisions whose effects outlive the people making them.

Imagine a pollution-control system that costs money to install now and prevents injury a century from now. How should its future benefits enter today's calculations? Should we turn them into a present monetary value? If so, at what rate? What should we assume about the future population's wealth, health, technology, and exposure to risk?

There are sound reasons not to treat every future payment as financially interchangeable with a present one. The opportunity to invest resources is real. A future society may, under some assumptions, be wealthier than ours. Spending a fixed sum today may help a poorer present household more than the same resources would help a wealthier future household. And a project that ties up resources may crowd out a better way of helping both groups.

But these arguments are not the same as saying a future person's pain is morally worth less merely because it occurs later.

That distinction is fundamental. One question asks how resources can be converted across time. Another asks whose well-being matters. The answers interact, but the questions are not identical. A discount rate can embody assumptions about investment, uncertainty, economic growth, risk, and ethical concern. Concealing those assumptions behind a single precise figure does not make them disappear.

Here is a simple way to see the stakes. If you discount a future sum at a positive rate every year, its present value shrinks as the date moves away. Over a few years this may be manageable. Over a century, the choice of rate can dominate the answer. A small adjustment to a percentage can change how attractive a long-term prevention project appears.

That is not a defect in arithmetic. It is a warning about the importance of the assumptions being put into the arithmetic.

The problem gets harder when the future is dangerous or unpredictable. A society may be richer in some respects and poorer in others. It may have extraordinary computing power and degraded ecosystems. It may be able to treat diseases that terrify us while facing risks we have not imagined. We should resist both the cheerful certainty that progress will solve everything and the equally unwarranted certainty that decline is inevitable.

The climate-economics debate is one of the places where this disagreement has been worked out most carefully. Scholars distinguish among a pure preference for the present, expectations of future growth, and the way additional consumption contributes to welfare at different levels of wealth. There are serious disagreements about how these should be combined. I do not propose to settle those disagreements with a slogan.

I do want the moral assumption to be visible. If we decide against preventing an injury that may occur in the distant future, we should be able to explain why. It should not be enough to point to a calculation while declining to discuss the reasons the calculation treats that injury as comparatively small.

## The people who would not have existed

There is another difficulty, one that is stranger than money.

Philosophers call it the non-identity problem. Derek Parfit helped make it central to modern discussions of ethics and future generations. The puzzle is that decisions today do more than alter the conditions in which future people will live. Over long periods, they can alter which people will exist at all.

Suppose a country chooses between two energy policies. One is cleaner and produces lower long-term risks, but it also changes employment, migration, marriages, and the timing of births. Decades later, the two possible histories contain substantially different people.

Now imagine a person born under the more damaging policy. Their life is worth living, though it is burdened by avoidable environmental problems. If the country had chosen the cleaner policy, that same person might never have existed. Someone else might have been born instead.

Can we say that choosing the damaging policy made this particular person worse off? Not in the familiar sense of comparing their actual life with the better life they personally would otherwise have enjoyed. There may be no such alternative life for them. The cleaner policy could have led to a different person.

This is a real philosophical complication. It is not a clever excuse for carelessness.

You can respond in several ways. Perhaps we should assess the overall quality of possible futures rather than comparing the fortunes of particular individuals across them. Perhaps there are duties not to create lives under certain avoidable conditions. Perhaps we should emphasize rights, institutions, or the quality of the world we pass on, rather than limit all obligations to whether one identifiable person would have been better off. Philosophers disagree about the details of these responses.

I find the problem valuable because it reveals a hidden assumption in a lot of ordinary moral language. We often imagine that the future consists of today's people, only older, plus a fixed collection of people waiting backstage to be born. In reality, the collection itself is shaped by what we do.

Does that mean our choices do not matter? I think it means we need a broader account of why they matter. We can make one future more dangerous, deprived, or unjust than another even when there is no single person who appears in both. An obligation to avoid building a terrible world need not depend entirely on identifying the particular people who would have inhabited a better one.

Of course, that principle requires limits. If you begin adding hypothetical people without restraint, you can construct calculations in which any present life is overwhelmed by an imagined population of unimaginable size. I do not think our responsibility is to maximize an unbounded tally of possible future lives. That is one way to lose sight of actual human conditions while claiming to value humanity.

My claim is more modest and, to my mind, harder to evade: when our actions predictably shape the conditions under which future people must live, we have reasons to care about those conditions, even when the identities of the people remain unknown.

## Do not write their entire script

There is a danger on the other side of this argument. If we become too certain about what future people will value, concern for them can turn into a kind of domination.

Imagine inheriting a house whose previous owner has made every decision permanent because they believed they knew exactly what you would need. The walls cannot move. The appliances require a proprietary service that no longer exists. The garden has been planted with species you do not like and cannot easily remove. Every room contains a note telling you why the arrangement is for your own good.

The previous owner might genuinely have tried to help. But they have confused making something available with making it compulsory.

A future generation deserves more than a carefully designed cage.

This is why I am attracted to the idea of preserving options. A seed vault does not dictate what a future farmer must plant. It keeps possibilities available. An archive does not instruct future scholars what conclusion they must reach. If it is maintained intelligently, it lets them investigate evidence we would otherwise have lost. A bridge designed to be inspected and repaired does not impose a complete transport plan for the next two hundred years. It allows people to use, adapt, or replace the structure with knowledge of what they are dealing with.

But option preservation is not a universal rule. Some decisions have to be made, and they foreclose alternatives. Building a hospital means not building something else with the same money. Preventing a disease may change which people are born. A society cannot hold every path open forever. Refusing to act can also destroy options.

And preservation is not automatically benign. A landscape may be an ancestral home, a working environment, a source of livelihood, or a site of political conflict. Declaring it preserved for hypothetical future beneficiaries while ignoring the people living there now can be unjust. The language of posterity does not override present rights.

So I would propose a more careful standard. Where uncertainty is large, consequences are durable, and future preferences cannot be known, favor decisions that remain understandable, repairable, and revisable. Where a hazard is serious and cannot be removed, make its existence legible and reduce dependence on the survival of any one warning system. Where present action is necessary, make the reasons and costs visible enough for future people to judge what we did.

This is less romantic than promising to bequeath a perfect world. I consider it a more respectful promise. We can leave them tools, records, protected resources, and opportunities without pretending to possess the authority to determine their entire way of life.

## The strongest objection is standing in front of us

Now for the objection that I think any serious argument about future generations has to face.

There are people alive today who need food, medicine, shelter, safety, education, and political freedom. Their needs are not hypothetical. We can see them, hear them, and sometimes help them immediately. A proposal that requires present suffering for the sake of a distant, speculative benefit cannot demand automatic deference merely by invoking the future.

Imagine two projects competing for the same budget. One would provide clean drinking water to communities currently suffering from contaminated supplies. The other would build a sophisticated archive intended to last several centuries. I can easily imagine circumstances where the water project is the better use of resources. Indeed, I would want an exceptionally strong explanation for choosing the archive at the expense of urgent, preventable illness.

The example is intentionally stark, but it makes a point. Future people are not the only people whose interests are easy for institutions to overlook. The poor, the displaced, children, people under authoritarian governments, and people far from centers of power may be excluded from present decisions. An ethics that counts imagined future populations while failing to count actual marginalized people has failed a basic test of seriousness.

There is another possibility worth recognizing, however. Sometimes the conflict between present and future benefits is exaggerated. Clean water infrastructure can help people now and later. Better disease surveillance can protect the current population and future ones. Maintaining the integrity of public records can serve today's citizens and their descendants. A more reliable electricity system can reduce present hardship and future risks.

In such cases, talking about a battle between the living and the unborn may obscure how competent long-term planning serves both.

Even when there is a genuine tradeoff, we need not choose between absolute presentism and an abstract worship of posterity. We can give strong priority to severe present deprivation while refusing to treat distant harms as worthless. We can distinguish necessities from luxuries, reversible losses from irreversible ones, and secure benefits from speculative promises. We can ask whether a project is a gift to future people or merely an attractive way of avoiding difficult obligations to people alive now.

And we should be wary of the seductive phrase, history will thank us. History is not a person. Future people may condemn our choices, appreciate them, or find them incomprehensible. They may not know we existed. The hoped-for gratitude of people we will never meet is not evidence that a decision is just.

In fact, I think the strongest reason to act responsibly toward the future has very little to do with earning gratitude. It has to do with not leaving strangers a problem that we could reasonably have prevented, simply because those strangers were not present to object.

## An institution with a long memory

If you accept any part of this argument, the practical question becomes unavoidable. What would a decision process look like if it took future people seriously?

I would begin not with a forecast, but with a record of consequences. Which benefits arrive now, and which much later? Which burdens are transferred to people who did not choose them? Which assumptions would have to remain true for the plan to work? What happens if the promised maintenance budget is cut? What will still be hazardous when the original experts are gone?

Then I would ask about reversibility. Can the decision be corrected if future evidence changes? If not, why must it be made irreversible? A project that can be repaired, discontinued, or adapted may deserve more confidence than one that depends on being exactly right for a century. But this is a judgment, not a mechanical law; some irreversible decisions protect people precisely because they cannot be easily undone.

I would ask about intelligibility. Will our successors know what we built, why we built it, which parts are dangerous, and how to inspect them? Do we leave records in forms they can plausibly interpret? Do several institutions hold copies? Are the explanations attached to the artifact, or hidden in a database that will vanish with a vendor?

And I would ask who represents the future in the room. Not who claims to speak with perfect knowledge on behalf of unborn people. Who has the assignment to question the time horizon? Who can say that an appealing cost saving is merely deferred maintenance? Who can require a response rather than being thanked politely and ignored?

These are ordinary institutional design questions. They do not require us to possess a science of predicting future preferences. They require us to be explicit about the consequences we are tempted to leave off the balance sheet.

The Welsh law is one attempt to make such questions part of public decision-making. The seed vault is an example of a specialized institution built to preserve options. The WIPP marker effort is an example of trying to communicate an enduring hazard. They operate in different domains and have different limits. I do not regard any of them as a universal model.

Their common lesson is that an aspiration becomes more credible when it has a budget, a maintenance plan, records, responsibilities, and a procedure for correction. Good intentions without a mechanism can expire at the end of a meeting. A system that keeps asking the right question may survive the people who first asked it.

## What we cannot promise

There is a strange arrogance in imagining that the future will admire the exact shape of our civilization. We cannot be certain that our descendants will share our politics, values, aesthetics, technologies, or even our categories for understanding the world.

But there is an equal arrogance in assuming that because we cannot predict their preferences, nothing we do now can benefit them. We do not know the names of the people who may need a seed collection. We can still see the value of keeping it from being destroyed. We do not know who may be endangered by a buried repository. We can still try to preserve a reliable warning. We do not know the future users of a bridge, a water system, or a scientific archive. We can still make those systems more durable and easier to understand.

The future is not a distant abstraction that arrives all at once. It arrives a day at a time, into the lives of people who find themselves living with decisions made long before they could influence them. Someday the people who are alive now will be the absent names on a drawing, the obsolete authors of an instruction manual, the people who wrote a law that no longer seems adequate, or the people who had the chance to prevent a loss and did not.

I do not think our obligation is to be remembered fondly. Nor is it to sacrifice every current good for the sake of a future we cannot calculate. It is to acknowledge that our freedom to act includes the power to constrain people who cannot yet defend themselves, and then to exercise that power with care.

There will be failures. The archive will need a migration. The marker will require interpretation. The seed collection will need living institutions. The bridge will eventually be judged obsolete. Future people will discover errors in our assumptions, including assumptions we considered obvious.

That possibility is not an argument against making gifts to them. It is an argument for making gifts that can be examined, questioned, repaired, and, when necessary, refused.

I began with two projects beneath the ground. One keeps seeds for somebody who may need them. The other tries to warn somebody who may be in danger. Neither can guarantee that its message will survive the distance in time. Both nevertheless recognize that the person at the other end of that distance is real enough to deserve an attempt.

We cannot give the future instructions for how to live. We can try to leave it more than our debris.

This is an original argumentative essay. The sources establish the historical projects, legislation, and philosophical/economic concepts; they do not endorse the essay's normative position. The bridge, house, energy-policy, budget, and pollution-control examples are hypothetical and are not descriptions of particular decisions. No dialogue or imagined thoughts are attributed to historical individuals. The WIPP markers discussed are part of a documented conceptual passive-control system; the essay does not claim that every proposed element has already been constructed or that a warning can be guaranteed to function for ten thousand years.

The role of future generations is treated alongside, not in place of, duties to people living now.
