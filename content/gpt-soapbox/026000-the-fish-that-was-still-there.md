# The Fish That Was Still There

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

A cod can be preserved with salt, dried until much of its water is gone, carried across an ocean, and made edible again in a kitchen hundreds or thousands of miles from the boat that caught it. This is an astonishing convenience for a world without refrigeration. It also helps explain why people were willing to cross the North Atlantic to look for cod in the first place.

Salt fish is not a glamorous invention. You catch the fish, clean it, add enough salt to discourage the organisms that spoil it, and let moisture leave the flesh. The procedure depends on skill and weather and on getting the details right. A badly cured fish is a wasted fish. A well-cured one can feed somebody who has never seen the ocean, much less sailed on it.

Before we get to the catastrophe in Newfoundland, it is worth remembering that the fish itself was a success. Atlantic cod is a cold-water species, capable of growing large and living for many years. Its flesh cured well. It was plentiful enough in the waters around Newfoundland and Labrador to support centuries of fishing and to make the Grand Banks a name known far beyond the North Atlantic. The arrangements for finding, catching, curing, transporting, trading, and cooking cod became part of the economic geography of Europe and then of Newfoundland itself.

In July 1992, Canada halted the directed commercial fishery for northern cod off eastern Newfoundland and Labrador. About thirty thousand fishers and processing workers in Newfoundland and Labrador lost their work, according to the Newfoundland and Labrador Heritage account. The number does not include every ripple through households, shops, or towns. Some people who had spent their lives fishing were told that there were too few fish left to support the trade. An industry with a history reaching back nearly five centuries had to reckon with a population that had been reduced to a fraction of its former size.

The explanation isn't contained in the word overfishing, though overfishing was central. It involves a changing ocean, boats that were getting better at finding fish, imperfect observations, political choices, and a peculiar property of schooling animals: they can remain easy to catch in the last places where they are still gathered, even as they disappear from much of their range.

That last property is the one that drew me into this story. It means that a fisher can do a successful day's work in a fishery that is failing.

## A fishery that crossed the Atlantic

The European fishery around Newfoundland did not begin with permanent towns. For much of the sixteenth century it was seasonal. Ships crossed from France and Portugal, and later from Spain and England, worked the banks or coastal waters, and brought preserved fish home. A crew might catch and salt its fish on board, returning directly to Europe. Other crews used shore stations where fish could be split, salted, and laid out to dry.

There were several reasons to bother. Cod was desirable food; salting made it transportable. European markets needed preserved protein. Religious patterns of fasting helped shape demand in some countries, but the commerce was broader than any single custom. To understand the fishery, you have to picture a market in which an ordinary dinner might depend on labor performed on the other side of an ocean months earlier.

The history also cautions against imagining one nation discovering a vast, unused cupboard and opening it for everyone. Indigenous peoples had lived and fished in these regions long before European fleets arrived. Commercial European expansion brought competition over shores, harvesting grounds, settlement, and power. The cod trade helped direct where Europeans made homes and how much coastal land they claimed. The supply chain had consequences for people who were already there.

It took ships, navigational knowledge, salt, barrels, drying space, credit, buyers, and workers to make cod an international product. The physical fish was only one part of the transaction. In some places the processing happened at sea; in others it shaped the buildings and working days of communities on shore. Newfoundland's later settlements were intimately connected to the availability of fishing grounds and usable places to land and dry catch.

A fishery that looks durable over centuries can become a dangerous guide to what is biologically possible in any particular decade. Historical catches depend on the number of vessels, their equipment, weather, markets, and rules. People living through a season see the catches that reach their wharves. They do not see all the fish in the sea, nor all the fish that might have been there if earlier catches had been smaller.

That sounds obvious when stated plainly. In practice, the hidden quantity was the subject of fierce technical argument.

## What a boat can and cannot count

Imagine two fishing trips separated by twenty years. On the first, a crew catches a good load of cod in an ordinary day's work. On the second, a better-equipped crew catches a good load in an ordinary day's work. The records show comparable success. That does not establish that the ocean holds the same number of fish.

Perhaps the second boat has better navigation. Perhaps its gear can fish deeper, or its skipper has learned a more reliable route to a seasonal aggregation. Perhaps the boat can stay at sea in weather that once forced it home. Or perhaps the fish are concentrated in one remaining productive area, so the catch remains good there while the total population has fallen elsewhere.

Fisheries scientists use catch per unit effort, often shortened to catch rate, as one source of information. It might mean tonnes per trawling hour or fish per thousand hooks, depending on the fishery. A catch rate can help reveal trends. It can also deceive when the effort being counted is not comparable through time. A modern trawler hour is not the same biological experiment as a trawler hour twenty years earlier.

There is an even subtler problem. Some animals gather together in schools or along migration routes. When the population declines, fish may still be densely packed in the places they prefer. A fishing boat that finds one of those concentrations can keep returning with a large catch, while empty water spreads outside the productive pocket. The catch rate looks steady, even as the population shrinks. Fisheries biologists call this *hyperstability*.

A study by George Rose and David Kulka of northern cod described a remarkable example. In the late 1980s and early 1990s, fish were concentrated around a migration corridor near Bonavista. The researchers described local density and catch rates that stayed high, or rose, while abundance declined sharply. To a working crew in the right location, the fish could seem present. To a survey across the wider region, the loss was becoming unmistakable.

This is an important insight, but it has sometimes been turned into a story that is too simple: scientists trusted commercial catch rates, and therefore failed to notice that the fish were disappearing. A later methodological analysis examined the stock-assessment models actually used and challenged that precise account of how catch-rate data caused overestimation. Researchers also had data from scientific survey vessels, which did not always tell a reassuring story. The warnings weren't absent. Decisions did not always change sufficiently in response to them.

A measurement problem and a decision problem demand different repairs. If your instrument is biased, you need a better instrument or a better model. If your institution receives evidence of danger and postpones the necessary response, more data will not necessarily solve it.

In the late twentieth century, northern cod encountered both problems. Different measures of its population did not agree as cleanly as anyone would have wished. Fishing activity continued under quotas and assumptions that proved unsustainable.

## A difficult animal to manage

Cod are not simply units of meat waiting to be counted. They spawn. Eggs and larvae drift in the sea, and many do not survive. Fish grow at rates that depend on temperature and food. Their chances of reaching reproductive age depend on predators, fishing, disease, and conditions that vary over years.

In the northwest Atlantic, capelin, a small schooling fish, has been especially important as food for northern cod. Capelin themselves fluctuate and were devastated by a collapse around 1991. Removing cod from a fishery does not automatically recreate the system that once supported them. If their prey is scarce, the remaining population may grow slowly even when fishing pressure is substantially reduced.

To manage a stock, scientists try to estimate how many mature fish are present, how many young fish are being added, and how many die from fishing and from other causes. The phrase *spawning stock biomass* means the estimated total weight of reproductively mature fish. It's a useful quantity, but it isn't something that can be lowered into the sea on a scale and weighed. A model combines surveys, landings, ages and sizes, assumptions about catch reporting, and information about ecosystem conditions.

The model's output is an estimate with uncertainty, and a stock assessment is more than a single number. It is a way of integrating evidence that is sampled unevenly over an enormous area.

Northern cod catches reached an extraordinary peak in 1968: roughly eight hundred and ten thousand metric tonnes, according to Fisheries and Oceans Canada's historical stock assessment. The fishing fleets and technologies that made such catches possible were far removed from the seasonal vessels that crossed the Atlantic four centuries earlier. Mechanized trawlers could work fishing grounds on a scale that earlier crews could scarcely have matched.

Catches fell during the 1970s. In 1977, Canada's declaration of a two-hundred-mile fishing jurisdiction changed which fleets could operate over much of the stock's range. The reduction of some foreign fishing effort was followed by a partial recovery during the 1980s. That recovery was real enough to encourage optimism, but it did not mean that management had found a permanent safe level of extraction.

A scientist writing with the benefit of later data can see the pattern: too much fishing pressure, population estimates that were too optimistic, slow or inadequate management responses, and unfavorable ocean conditions in the early 1990s. George Lilly's retrospective scientific account identifies those factors together. He also emphasizes how hard it was to explain the persistent failure to recover afterward. A reduced fishing fleet does not change the ocean back to its previous state.

One mistake in public retellings is to blame foreign boats and then stop. Foreign trawlers contributed heavily to the earlier decline; that fact doesn't absolve Canada's own fisheries management during the later collapse. Another is to describe the ocean as an innocent abundance spoiled solely by technology. A cod population is variable, and ocean conditions genuinely matter. Recognizing natural variability does not make excessive harvest harmless. Both influences operate in the same water.

When the last decades before 1992 are compressed into a few sentences, the catastrophe starts to feel inevitable. It wasn't inevitable. There were choices about quotas, allowable risk, enforcement, how to use surveys, and whose warnings to heed. But there wasn't one lever marked *save the cod* that somebody simply refused to pull.

## July second

On July 2, 1992, the Canadian government announced a moratorium on northern cod fishing. The decision was devastating to coastal communities. About thirty thousand people in Newfoundland and Labrador's fishing and fish-processing economy lost their work. Boats that had been bought and maintained for a particular livelihood no longer had that livelihood. Fish plants could not run on memories of the previous season. Workers who knew how to handle fish had to find other ways to support themselves.

The government offered income support and retraining assistance, including the Northern Cod Adjustment and Rehabilitation Program, followed by the Atlantic Groundfish Strategy. Those programs helped some households and left others facing long transitions. A policy designed to rescue a stock does not erase the human cost of having reached the point where rescue demands a closure.

One should be careful about saying that the ban ended a five-hundred-year fishery overnight. The total fishery wasn't an unbroken institution with the same people, vessels, or rules throughout that period, and limited or differently managed cod fishing continued during parts of the moratorium years. But the scale of the rupture was real. The expectation that northern cod could reliably support commercial fishing had been shattered.

People who had warned about the condition of the stock and people who depended on landing it were sometimes placed rhetorically on opposite sides. That is a poor way of understanding what had happened. The same person could need cod fishing to continue, fear the stock was collapsing, and resent a government that seemed late in acknowledging the danger. The immediate cost of a closure falls on identifiable people; the cost of delaying it is distributed through an uncertain future. Neither cost disappears because someone has made a strong speech about the other.

There is a terrible timing problem in resource management. Closing a fishery early enough to prevent disaster can look unnecessary because the disaster never arrives. Closing after the collapse offers visible justification for a decision that may no longer be sufficient. The manager who waits for every argument to be settled risks discovering that the evidence has become undeniable only after the choices have narrowed.

That does not mean there is a scientifically correct single number for every quota. A fishing limit contains a biological forecast, an estimate of uncertainty, and a social decision about acceptable risk. An error in any of those components can matter. It would be comforting if the biology could dictate the social decision, but the biology does not tell us whose income should fall this season or how much chance of another collapse society is willing to accept.

The moratorium lasted far longer than many people expected. Recovery was slow. Commercial access returned in different limited forms at different times. The offshore population, in particular, did not promptly return to its earlier productivity. That delay is a central fact about the disaster, because it defeated the idea that the sea would refill quickly once the boats stood still.

## The fishery opens again

In June 2024, Canada reopened a directed commercial fishery for northern cod with a total allowable catch of eighteen thousand nine hundred forty-seven tonnes. The decision followed a revised stock assessment that classified the population as being above the critical zone, in what managers called the cautious zone. The reopening was significant to people whose families had lived through the moratorium. There were catches to land, jobs to do, and a market ready to buy fish. For the first time in more than three decades, the word *commercial* applied to a newly reopened directed northern cod fishery.

The numbers had changed too. A government science assessment published in 2025 estimated about five hundred twenty-four thousand tonnes of spawning stock biomass. That was substantial growth compared with the depressed years after the collapse. Yet the assessment also said that biomass had changed little since around 2017. It warned that capelin availability was limiting future growth, and that even without fishing the short-term probability of sustained recovery was not high under the ecological conditions considered. The same report could support relief that the stock was no longer at its lowest point and caution that its recovery had stalled.

In June 2026, Canada increased the annual northern cod total allowable catch to fifty-nine thousand tonnes, from thirty-eight thousand the previous year. Fisheries and Oceans Canada said that its latest science placed the stock in the healthy zone of its management framework for the first time since the moratorium. It described a harvest rate of about twelve percent and a decision intended to support coastal employment while staying within its scientific advice.

If you read only that announcement, the story might appear finished: cod crashed, people stopped fishing, cod recovered, fishing resumed. It would make a clean ending for a schoolbook. The actual arguments are happening now.

Earlier in 2026, Oceana Canada had warned that the label *healthy* would be misleading if heard as a return to historical abundance. Its fisheries scientist Rebecca Schijns pointed to changes in the stock assessment's reference points and to uncertainty about the stock's capacity for renewed growth. In January 2026, fisheries researcher George Rose published a further analysis arguing that recent growth had stalled and that the levels now described by management thresholds were far below the cod population's historical scale.

There is a real technical issue here. Stock classifications depend on reference points: estimates of population levels at which reproduction, recruitment, and production become dangerously impaired, as well as targets for management. A stock can cross a management boundary because the fish have multiplied, because the estimate of the fish has changed, because the reference point has changed, or because some combination of those things occurred. None of that makes the assessment fraudulent. Scientists revise models when better evidence or better methods become available. But the public label doesn't disclose what proportion of a change came from fish and what proportion came from a new way of estimating them.

This is why it matters that *healthy under the present classification* and *restored to the abundance people remember* are different claims. The first is about an explicit framework and its thresholds. The second is about a history, an ecosystem, and perhaps a political aspiration. The two may point in different directions.

The government is not making its decision out of pure wishfulness. The current stock is much larger than it was after the collapse. In its public account, the government points to scientific review, a harvest rate intended to remain sustainable, and allocations that give most of the catch to inshore fleets. The 2026 plan allocates seventy percent to the inshore fleet, with specific shares for the Nunatsiavut Government, Innu Nation, and NunatuKavut Community Council. Those allocations are not incidental to the policy. There are people who have waited decades for cod fishing to become a viable livelihood again, and deciding who benefits from an increased harvest is part of the management decision.

But the counterargument is formidable. A quota is an authorization to remove actual fish, while a population estimate is an inference about how many fish are available. The consequences of a mistake are asymmetric. The fishing income from a higher quota is immediate; the damage from removing too many reproducing fish may not show up fully for years. After 1992, the people of Newfoundland do not need to be taught that kind of asymmetry from a hypothetical example.

I do not have a new stock assessment to contribute here. What I can say is that the disagreement is about an identifiable management choice, with reasons and risks on both sides. It deserves to be treated as that choice, with transparent reporting of catches, surveys, reference-point changes, and ecological indicators. The word *healthy* cannot do all the work.

## What is enough cod?

Consider two possible ambitions for the northern cod fishery. One is to sustain a smaller, stable population that supports a reliable but limited commercial catch. Another is to rebuild a population much closer to the historical size and productivity that once supported a vast fishery. Both ambitions may be compatible with a functioning economy, but they will not always recommend the same quota this year. They may assign different importance to the small fish cod eat and to the people who want to catch cod now.

A management agency can make these goals explicit. Then it can ask what level of catch gives a reasonable prospect of meeting them, under different assumptions about mortality, recruitment, and prey. An agency can update the targets when its evidence changes. What it cannot reasonably do is let a single adjective settle the entire question and expect the public to accept that a technical classification has answered a political choice.

Recovery also raises a question about who gets to define the baseline. To a worker who lost a job in 1992, thirty years of closures might make any substantial commercial season feel like restoration. To a biologist comparing today's biomass with estimates from the 1960s, the same season might be a small fraction of what the ecosystem once supported. Neither comparison tells the whole story of how to proceed. One looks through the experience of a life. The other looks through the history of a population. Good policy has to know which one it is speaking about.

I keep thinking about the fish that was still there when the rest had gone. The point is not that a fisher should have doubted what was in the net. A fish in the net is real. The problem was asking that successful catch to testify about miles of sea the boat never visited and years of reproduction that had not yet happened.

The ocean provides no daily balance sheet. Fish move, breed, starve, gather, and disappear from places that were once reliable. We collect samples, compare them with older samples, and decide what to do. There will always be missing information. After a collapse, there is an additional burden: a justified reluctance to place too much faith in an encouraging trend.

Somewhere off Newfoundland this season, cod can again be hauled aboard under a commercial license. Their flesh is still good for salting. The markets are still there. People can still make a living handling the fish. Those are welcome facts.

A good catch says that fish were present where the gear went down. The question for the next year's quota is how many will be present elsewhere, and how many young fish will survive to replace them. There is no way to answer that question by looking at a single full hold.
