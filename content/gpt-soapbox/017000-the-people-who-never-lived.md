# The People Who Never Lived

Imagine a theater with two hundred seats. The lights go down. An actor enters, carrying an old envelope. She opens it, reads silently, and sits. Nothing much happens for several seconds. Then she tears the paper in half, not angrily but very carefully, as though she might hurt someone by doing it.

There is no envelope with a meaningful history. It was made for this production. The person whose letter it supposedly contains was invented by a playwright. The actor may have been rehearsing the scene all afternoon. None of this is a secret. The audience bought tickets precisely because it knows what sort of event this is.

And yet, people in the audience may find themselves leaning forward. They want to know what was in the letter. They want the actor to change her mind. A few may feel an ache of recognition, remembering a real letter they once threw away or kept too long.

Something remarkable has happened. A person who never existed has made two hundred real people care about a decision that never occurred.

I think this is among the most extraordinary ordinary capacities of the human mind. We do it so readily that we forget to marvel at it. We choose to spend hours with imaginary people, feel grief when they die, argue about what they should have done, and sometimes return to them years later as though visiting an old acquaintance.

The philosopher can reasonably ask what, exactly, we care about. There was no actual person in danger. The historian can ask what this scene has taught us about anything that really happened. The psychologist can ask what processes make a fictional situation feel urgent. All are good questions.

But I want to ask another one. What have we built, collectively, by making it possible to care about lives that never happened?

My argument is that fiction is not merely a diversion from reality, and it is not a substitute for reality either. It is a way of exploring possibilities without first requiring them to become facts. That capacity deserves to be treated as an intellectual achievement, an artistic achievement, and a source of danger. I think it matters especially in a world increasingly able to manufacture plausible stories at great speed.

## The strange bargain of the curtain

The curtain makes a peculiar bargain with us. On this side of it, we know that the castle is plywood, the poison is harmless, and the king has an ordinary name printed on a payroll somewhere. On the other side, we agree to attend to a castle, a poison, and a king.

This is not quite the same as being deceived. If a stage magician secretly hides a card in a sleeve, the concealment is part of the performance. But if a theater audience learns that the swords are props, the tragedy is not ruined. Their being props is what allows the tragedy to be performed repeatedly.

Our capacity to respond to fiction has puzzled philosophers for decades. Colin Radford famously asked how we can be moved by the fate of Anna Karenina when we know Anna is fictional. The puzzle is sometimes called the paradox of fiction. We feel something that resembles genuine concern. We know there is no person there to be helped. Some theories of emotion seem to require belief in a real object of concern. Put all three claims together and they are difficult to reconcile.

Different philosophers resolve the tension differently. Some argue that our emotions are directed at imagined situations or the content of thoughts. Some distinguish ordinary emotion from a form of imaginative participation. Some think the supposed requirement that emotions need a real object was mistaken in the first place. I don't think we need to settle the philosophical dispute to recognize its importance.

The interesting feature is that we can maintain two attitudes at once. We can know a character is fictional and still respond as if the character's choices matter within the world the story has established. We can feel afraid without believing a creature is actually about to enter the room. We can be moved without mistaking a performance for a news report.

That is not simple confusion. It is an ability to keep a boundary while temporarily looking across it.

And fiction can do more than evoke emotion. It can organize attention. A camera can show us a face for a few seconds longer than we expected. A novelist can let us hear a character's private justification for a decision that looks unforgivable from the outside. A playwright can arrange for a character to misunderstand the one sentence that would have saved a relationship. In ordinary life, we rarely get the complete set of perspectives. In a story, somebody can construct them for us.

The danger begins when we forget the word construct. But so does the creative opportunity.

## An experiment without a laboratory

Suppose I ask a question in the abstract. Does a creator have any responsibility for the suffering of a being the creator brings into existence? That is a philosophical problem. We could define responsibility, distinguish intention from foresight, and argue about competing obligations.

Now imagine a scientist who makes a living creature, is horrified by its appearance, and abandons it. Imagine that the creature can speak, remember, learn, desire companionship, and make terrible choices. Imagine that we hear from both creator and creature.

This is not a hypothetical invented for a contemporary ethics seminar. It is the central conflict of Mary Shelley's Frankenstein, first published anonymously in eighteen eighteen.

The familiar shorthand says that Frankenstein is a warning about a scientist going too far. It can certainly be read in that direction. But reducing the novel to that slogan throws away much of what makes it worth reading. The creature is not merely an accident with sharp teeth. He becomes a speaker in the story. He has grievances, makes arguments, and commits acts for which he is responsible. Victor Frankenstein is not simply wrong to discover something forbidden; he is confronted with the consequences of creating and rejecting a being capable of suffering.

Shelley does not solve this by handing the reader a tidy code of conduct. There are several claims on our attention at once. There is ambition, discovery, neglect, fear, vengeance, and the question of whether understanding somebody's suffering changes how we judge that person's violence. We are not required to excuse the violence in order to take the suffering seriously.

A philosophical example can state an assumption and hold it fixed. A novel can make us experience how difficult it is to hold an assumption fixed when other details become vivid. The imagined being does not leave the argument alone. He talks back.

This is why I resist calling fiction a mere illustration of ideas. An illustration helps us understand a conclusion we already possess. A powerful story can make us discover that our original question was too small.

We began by asking whether the scientist should have made the creature. We may end by asking what the scientist owed him after he existed. We may ask how responsibility changes when the created being becomes an independent agent. We may ask whether society's treatment of a feared outsider can become part of the causal history of the harm that outsider inflicts. These are different questions, and the novel allows them to collide.

Of course, the novel does not establish a fact about laboratory biology. It offers no reliable prediction of what would happen if anyone really assembled a living being from dead tissue. That is not the sort of evidence it provides. Its contribution lies in arranging a situation that makes certain relations visible to the imagination.

We can use the situation to think. We cannot use it as an experiment that proves what real creatures, real scientists, or real societies will do.

That distinction is not a weakness in fiction. It is how we keep its powers intelligible.

## A city built on one impossible bargain

Let me give you a quite different example. In nineteen seventy-three, Ursula K. Le Guin published a short story called The Ones Who Walk Away from Omelas. It describes a prosperous, joyful city, and then turns the reader toward a terrible condition on which that happiness is said to depend: the suffering of a child kept in misery.

Some inhabitants learn the secret and remain. Some cannot accept the arrangement and leave. The story does not give us a complete political program for what happens next.

That is part of its design. The moral bargain is arranged with almost mathematical cruelty. We are asked to consider happiness that depends upon a victim who does not consent. What would count as complicity? Does walking away help the child? Is refusing to participate enough? Can the rules of the imaginary city be challenged, or does doing so spoil the thought experiment by refusing its premise?

The story has been read in many ways, and I am not going to claim a single authorized answer. What I find powerful is that its inhabitants occupy positions that abstract ethical vocabulary usually keeps separate. The beneficiaries can enjoy the city. They can know the cost. They can feel troubled. They can leave. They can remain. The reader has to consider what those acts actually mean.

This is not a survey of real cities. It does not show that every society has exactly one hidden victim or that social prosperity always rests on an equivalent arrangement. One could misuse the story that way and emerge with a very dubious theory of politics.

The story is more demanding when treated as a question than when turned into a slogan.

What does it take to make a hidden cost morally visible? What do we owe to somebody who pays a price for benefits they never chose? Are there circumstances in which refusing the benefits is a meaningful act, and others in which changing the arrangement is the more serious obligation?

Notice that we can disagree about these questions without first agreeing that Omelas is a possible municipality. The fiction establishes a shared object of attention. It is a place we can point to while we argue.

There is a reason moral philosophers invent runaway trolleys, divided islands, rings of invisibility, and impossible choices. The invented situations let us separate questions that are entangled in daily life. But literary fiction can also restore the messiness that stripped-down thought experiments remove. It can ask what it is like to inhabit the very case that philosophy has made clean.

Neither approach is automatically better. The stripped-down example offers control. The story offers texture. We need to know which one we are using, and what its conclusions can support.

## The mind that does not look like yours

Fiction provides an especially tempting promise: perhaps by spending time in an invented person's mind, we become better at understanding real people.

I find the possibility plausible enough to take seriously, but the evidence deserves more care than the slogan usually receives.

In two thousand thirteen, David Kidd and Emanuele Castano published experiments suggesting that reading literary fiction could produce short-term improvements on tests of understanding other people's mental states. The result was exciting. It seemed to offer a measurable benefit of the kind people like to attach to cultural activities: read this kind of book, become more empathetic.

Follow-up studies complicated the picture. A large replication reported by Maria Eugenia Panero and colleagues, involving seven hundred and ninety-two participants across three research groups, did not find the predicted advantage from a brief reading assignment. Four further experiments by Dalya Samur and colleagues, involving a total of just over one thousand participants, also failed to reproduce the immediate effect in their comparisons.

That does not establish that reading fiction never changes anyone. It challenges a specific, convenient causal claim: that a short exposure to a literary passage reliably and immediately improves a measurable social skill.

A preregistered meta-analysis published in two thousand twenty-four brought together seventy experiments and found small average cognitive benefits from reading fiction, with results dependent on what fiction was compared against and which outcomes were measured. Its experimental estimate was small, not a transformation of personality. A later review of empathy-related studies likewise described modest, context-sensitive effects rather than a universal moral upgrade.

I like this result, in a way. Not because I want fiction to be ineffective, but because it frees fiction from a suspiciously easy sales pitch. A novel is not a vitamin. A reader does not become more ethical by consuming a prescribed quantity of ambiguity.

A person can read hundreds of novels and remain incurious about the people living next door. A person who rarely opens a novel can be generous, attentive, and perceptive. The relationship between art and character is much too complicated for a vending-machine theory of virtue.

Still, fiction creates an opportunity. A reader can practice keeping track of several motives at once. A character may be both afraid and proud, both honest and self-serving, both deserving of sympathy and responsible for a bad decision. A good story can make it difficult to flatten that person into one adjective.

Whether the practice carries into real behavior depends on the reader, the work, the situation, and what the reader chooses to do with it. We should be able to defend the opportunity without promising a guaranteed outcome.

There is another complication. Understanding a mind is not the same as caring about it. Skilled manipulators can be exceptionally attentive to other people's fears and desires. Empathic perception can serve cruelty as well as kindness. What we do with a clearer picture of another person is a further moral question.

A story can invite sympathy. It cannot compel decency.

## A story can tell the truth without being a report

There is a question I want to ask without getting trapped by a false choice. If the events in a story never happened, in what sense could the story be true?

One answer is that it can't. If someone says that Victor Frankenstein assembled a creature, and means that an actual scientist performed that experiment in the nineteenth century, the claim is false. Accuracy matters. A false history does not become accurate because it is moving.

But ordinary conversation already distinguishes more kinds of truth than that. We say a joke captures something true about an institution. We say a painting is honest about grief. We say a mathematical example reveals a property of an entire class of objects, even when the example uses imaginary numbers of sheep or a train that always leaves on time.

These are not identical uses of the word truth, and they should not be collapsed into one mysterious category. But neither should we pretend that direct reportage is the only way to communicate an insight.

A novel may show how one apparently minor misunderstanding accumulates consequences across years. It may make a social role visible by placing someone in a position where every available choice is compromised. It may offer an anatomy of temptation, or embarrassment, or loyalty. Those are proposed descriptions of possibilities in human life. We can recognize a pattern and then ask whether the pattern is really present outside the work.

The work gives us something to consider, not an exemption from checking.

I think this distinction is especially important because stories are so efficient at creating conviction. Melanie Green and Timothy Brock studied what they called transportation into a narrative: the attention, imagery, and emotion involved in becoming absorbed in a story. In experiments published in two thousand, they found that greater transportation was associated with stronger story-consistent beliefs and more favorable evaluations of protagonists.

Their findings do not license a simple rule that any novel can rewrite anyone's politics. Context, audiences, and later research matter. But the underlying concern is real. If a story draws us in, its claims may acquire an apparent naturalness. We have spent so long inside the world that its arrangement begins to feel self-evident.

That is wonderful when the arrangement helps us notice a neglected possibility. It is dangerous when the arrangement has been designed to smuggle in a false conclusion.

Suppose a thriller repeatedly shows a particular profession as corrupt. The story might be gripping, and some members of the profession might in fact be corrupt. Neither fact tells us how common the corruption is. The novelist chooses the cases. A hundred invented examples remain a hundred invented examples. Their emotional force cannot supply the missing denominator.

Or consider a story that portrays one invented stranger as terrifying and another as heroic. We may become attached to the hero and learn to fear the stranger. A powerful narrative does not require the reader to say, consciously, I believe this happened. It may simply make some possibilities easy to imagine and others hard to imagine. The result can influence how a real claim feels before we have examined evidence for it.

This is why media literacy cannot end with learning to spot factual errors. An individually accurate fact can be placed in a narrative that supplies a misleading impression of its prevalence or significance. And a completely invented character can be made to stand in for a population of actual human beings without anyone explicitly asserting the equivalence.

We need two skills at once: the capacity to enter a fictional world generously, and the capacity to leave it with our judgment intact.

## The art of not solving the person

There is a temptation, when defending literature, to praise only those stories that deliver a clearly articulable lesson. The novel teaches us not to be prejudiced. The tragedy teaches us not to be arrogant. The adventure teaches us courage. If the work can be reduced to a sentence that would fit on a motivational poster, we say the work has value.

I find that a rather impoverished standard.

Sometimes the best thing a story does is make a familiar moral sentence harder to use carelessly. It takes a category we already possess, such as hero, victim, coward, or traitor, and introduces a person who refuses to fit cleanly inside it.

Imagine someone who keeps a promise and thereby does something cruel. Imagine someone who breaks a promise in order to save the very person to whom it was made. We can begin with the general principle that promises matter, and I would not want to lose it. But now we have to ask what, specifically, the promise was for. The imagined exception makes the principle more precise.

The point is not that every ethical judgment dissolves into ambiguity. Sometimes the right answer is quite clear. Stories can also reveal moral cowardice by showing somebody manufacture complexity to avoid an obvious obligation. Ambiguity can be an instrument of evasion as easily as a sign of depth.

But there is a difference between the absence of a verdict and the absence of thought. A story that does not tell us whom to admire may still have done something demanding: it may have required us to hold several incompatible perceptions in view without discarding any prematurely.

A character is often most convincing when the story allows that character to remain partially opaque. Real people are not dissertations with their theses printed on the cover. Even those we know well possess motives we misunderstand, histories we have not heard, and combinations of habit and chance that do not fit a single explanation. Fiction can represent this opacity rather than simply solving each invented person for the reader.

This makes room for a form of attention that is less like classification and more like acquaintance. We learn how someone speaks when frightened, what they notice about a room, which subjects they avoid, what they find funny, which mistake they keep repeating. We might not end with a complete theory of the character. We may end with a more disciplined sense of how little one theory can contain.

And the same restraint can be valuable outside literature. The person in front of us does not become less real when our first explanation of them fails. The failure may be the beginning of paying attention.

I am not claiming that reading complicated characters automatically makes this practice habitual. I am saying the art form can display the practice and make it worth attempting.

## Why invented worlds matter to the real one

Consider another use of fiction: the ability to describe a social arrangement that does not exist, at least not yet.

We can argue about a political or technical institution that has never been built. We can imagine a society where an ordinary feature of our lives is absent, or where some current policy has been carried to its logical extreme. We can remove a customary assumption and ask what changes. We can take an apparently absurd possibility seriously long enough to discover what it exposes about the present.

This is one reason speculative fiction has enduring appeal. Its fantastic machinery may be less important than the relationships it rearranges. A world with impossible technology can still contain recognizable conflicts over power, status, family, labor, and responsibility. The impossibility is sometimes the device that lets the writer isolate the conflict.

The same is true of comedy. An impossible misunderstanding can reveal a very real convention. A character who follows a rule literally, without the tacit exceptions everyone else understands, can make the rule appear ridiculous. The laughter need not be mere decoration; it can be a form of analysis.

But imaginative freedom cuts both ways. Fiction can widen our sense of possible arrangements. It can also narrow it, by presenting a particular cruelty as inevitable or a particular hierarchy as natural. A dystopia may warn us against a danger, yet it can also teach helplessness if every conceivable alternative collapses into the same disaster. A utopia may make neglected goods visible, yet conceal the labor or disagreement required to maintain them.

The responsibility of the reader is not to refuse the story whenever its worldview is suspect. It is to recognize that a beautifully made world can still be built on questionable premises. Artistic excellence does not confer political authority.

This is also why censorship and compulsory moral instruction have such a poor fit with the art. A society that only permits stories whose conclusions have already been approved sacrifices one of fiction's great functions: showing what the approving authorities have failed to imagine. And a classroom that treats every novel as a puzzle with a single correct moral answer trains students to hunt for an official solution rather than engage with a work.

I would rather have readers who can say, this story moved me, and I think one of its assumptions is false. Or, I dislike this character, but the writer has shown me something important through the character's mistake. Or simply, this was beautiful, and I do not yet know what to make of it.

The point is not to protect every work from criticism. It is to preserve room for criticism that is more serious than assigning a score to the message.

## When every story can be generated

There is a modern complication I cannot avoid. We are entering an era in which software can produce competent-sounding stories on demand. Give a system a setting, a genre, a conflict, a preferred ending, and a list of names, and it can assemble a narrative remarkably quickly. More computation, better editing, and more elaborate systems can improve the result. None of this requires us to pretend that the software has lived through the events it describes.

This capability is exciting in some ways. It lowers the cost of trying an idea. A person can explore variations on a scene, turn an abstract concept into a dialogue, or make a first draft of an imagined world that would otherwise remain a rough note. For some people, it makes storytelling more accessible. For others, it is simply another instrument in an already elaborate craft.

But abundance changes the problem. When stories are scarce, the challenge is often to obtain one that speaks to our situation. When stories become cheap, the challenge is to decide which ones deserve attention, revision, trust, and remembrance.

I do not think this is solved by asking whether machine-generated fiction counts as real fiction. The more interesting questions are about what the work accomplishes and the circumstances of its production. Is it good? Does it have an interesting structure? Has someone made meaningful choices about its shape? Does the work distinguish between invention and reporting? Is it borrowing without appropriate acknowledgment? Does it merely imitate familiar rewards, or does it make room for an idea we did not arrive with?

The last question is hard for any writer, human or machine. It is easy to produce a story that confirms every expectation the audience brings to it. A hero is admirable in exactly the prescribed way. Every objection is defeated on schedule. Every character speaks to advance the approved conclusion. The audience receives the pleasure of recognition without the discomfort of discovery.

Sometimes that is exactly what a reader wants, and I don't see a reason to outlaw the pleasure of a familiar tale. Comfort has a place. But we should not confuse a story being perfectly suited to our tastes with a story having exhausted what there is to say.

One danger of recommendation systems and generative tools is that they can make the most immediately agreeable version of everything exceptionally easy to obtain. We can ask to have every uncomfortable passage softened, every ambiguous ending clarified, every disliked character vindicated, every moral conflict settled in our favor.

That degree of control may serve entertainment. It may also remove an important reason to encounter fiction at all: the possibility that another arrangement of events, thoughts, and sympathies will resist us.

I would like tools for making stories that leave room for artistic resistance. Tools that help writers revise without automatically smoothing away strangeness. Tools that help readers explore a work without insisting that the work agree with them. Tools that can explain a difficult passage without pretending that difficulty was a defect.

The machine's ability to provide more text does not answer the question of which text is worth living with.

## Please do not make literature apply for a job

There is a final temptation in everything I have said. I have defended fiction as a way to explore ethical possibilities, attend to other minds, examine institutions, and practice judgment. I have cited psychology and philosophy. I have explained its uses.

Now I want to resist the conclusion that fiction must earn its place by supplying those uses.

A comic scene can be worthwhile because it is funny. A sentence can be beautiful without repairing a public institution. An invented landscape can offer delight even if no one learns a transferable skill from visiting it. A story can be formally daring, sensuous, strange, or exhilarating. Not every value must eventually be translated into an improvement in productivity, civic competence, or measurable mental health.

Aristotle, writing about poetry more than two thousand years ago, connected our pleasure in representations with learning and recognition. It is an illuminating observation, but the pleasure matters too. The fact that we can learn through art does not mean that each work owes us a lesson plan.

Think about a child who insists on hearing a story again, even though the child knows perfectly well what happens. If obtaining information were the whole purpose, the second telling would be unnecessary. Yet the child may want precisely the familiar words, the timing of a surprise already known, the small suspense of waiting for a favorite turn. Adults do this too. We return to books and plays not because we have forgotten their plots but because knowing what happens changes what we can notice.

A fictional person can become part of a reader's memory without having become part of the world's factual history. We know where the character will make the mistake. We hope, absurdly and sincerely, for a different choice. The text does not change. Our relation to it does.

This is not a defect in rationality. It is one of the ways art lets a finite life contain more than one path. We are not actually living all those lives. No one gets to borrow another person's suffering by reading about it. But we can devote real attention to imagined arrangements of experience and then return to our own world with our imagination enlarged, challenged, or simply entertained.

That return matters. The invented person is not owed the same obligations as the living neighbor. The neighbor can be helped or harmed by what we do. The character cannot. One of the proper ends of reading is to avoid confusing these different kinds of concern, even when both can feel intense.

Yet I don't want to dismiss the fictional concern as counterfeit. The attention, the recognition, the laughter, and the grief are events in actual human minds. The work may be invented, but the encounter with it is real.

## After the curtain

So let us return to the theater.

The actor with the envelope has finished the scene. The curtain rises and falls. The audience applauds. In the dressing room, someone will hang the costume on a rack. The letter will go back into a prop drawer. Nothing happened to the person the audience imagined, because that person was never there in the ordinary sense.

But the people in the seats have been somewhere. Not literally to another place, and not in the sense of having acquired a complete education in grief or loyalty. They spent part of an evening attending to a possibility together. They watched a gesture that might have meant regret, or mercy, or the decision not to make a call. They can argue about it on the way home. They may remember it when a real decision arrives, or they may never think of it again.

I think that is enough to make fiction worthy of our respect. We need not pretend that stories automatically make us better people. We need not confuse imagined events with historical evidence. We need not claim that a good work has a measurable use in order to defend its existence.

What we have is stranger and, to me, more interesting: a species capable of building shared worlds out of things that never happened, entering those worlds knowingly, and discovering real questions there.

Perhaps the deepest reason to keep making stories is not that they tell us what the world is. History and science, with their different disciplines, are better equipped to establish many of those facts. It is that stories keep alive our capacity to ask what a world could be like, who we might be within it, and why a possibility can matter even before anyone has lived it.

The people in the stories never lived.

Our attention to them does.

## Editorial and research notes (not spoken)

**Thesis.** Fiction permits committed attention to invented persons and worlds while preserving the distinction between imaginative inquiry and empirical evidence. It can clarify possibilities, complicate judgments and create aesthetic pleasure, but need not be justified by measurable behavioral improvement.

**Scope and care.** *Frankenstein* and *The Ones Who Walk Away from Omelas* are interpreted, not offered as evidence about historical scientific events or real social structures. The essay deliberately avoids claims that short fiction exposures reliably cause substantial empathy gains; replication attempts and more recent meta-analytic evidence are discussed. The opening envelope scene is invented as an illustration, not presented as an actual performance. Discussion of generated fiction is an argument about affordances and risks, not an empirical claim that particular AI tools invariably behave that way.
