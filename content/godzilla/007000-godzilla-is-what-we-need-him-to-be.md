# Godzilla Is What We Need Him to Be

There is a moment in *Godzilla vs. Hedorah*, released in 1971, when Godzilla decides that walking is no longer a sufficient way to travel. He points his atomic breath toward the ground, launches himself through the air, and flies backward across the landscape like an enormous reptilian rocket.

One might reasonably ask how the monster who once made Tokyo look like the aftermath of an atomic attack arrived at this particular solution to a transportation problem.

And then one might watch the scene again, because it is wonderful.

The movie around it has grimy industrial pollution, a toxic adversary, young people trying to make sense of a poisoned environment, and stretches of imagery that are genuinely strange and disturbing. The airborne Godzilla does not fit neatly into a category labelled tragedy or a category labelled children's comedy. The film has room for both. It is not equally successful at every moment, and the flight is not an invitation to revise the laws of aerodynamics. It is an image of a monster whose body has become available for extravagant play.

Set it beside another image. In the 1954 original, Dr. Serizawa enters the sea with the Oxygen Destroyer, a device whose use against Godzilla threatens to create an even more terrible human weapon. Serizawa elects to die with the creature rather than allow his discovery to circulate. The ending is grave, precise, and fearful about what people will invent next.

Now set both images beside a modern Godzilla who rises to confront another gigantic creature while an audience cheers. What is the same across these encounters? The dorsal spines, the tail, the roar, the name, and the recognition that the screen is holding a body far larger than anything in our ordinary lives. But what are we supposed to feel? Grief, amusement, relief, excitement, anxiety, affection, wonder. The answers differ so dramatically that the series can seem like a long argument with itself.

That argument is the reason I think Godzilla has endured.

It would be easy to say that Godzilla stands for nuclear weapons. The original film makes that connection indispensable. It would also be easy to say he stands for nature's revenge, human arrogance, childhood imagination, national identity, or the desire to watch an impossible fight. All of those descriptions catch something. None tells us why a viewer can watch one Godzilla film with tears, another with laughter, and a third with the impatience of somebody waiting for their favorite monster to finally enter the ring.

The real achievement is not that Godzilla has one stable meaning. It is that this unlikely body has become a place where cinema can keep asking new questions. We should celebrate that freedom without pretending every answer is equally good, and without forgetting that the first question arose from a particular human catastrophe.

## A symbol that can look back

Consider the word symbol. It is useful because a giant fictional creature can make history visible in a way ordinary dramatic realism cannot. The creature can embody a fear too large, dispersed, or technically complicated to photograph directly. A filmmaker can let something enormous rise from the sea and have it carry the dread of weapons testing, industrial contamination, war, biological change, or governmental failure.

But describing Godzilla as a symbol can also become a way of avoiding the work of watching the films.

A symbol on a page is often treated as a translation problem. Here is the image; what does it stand for? Solve the correspondence and you possess the meaning. Yet Godzilla movies are full of things that resist that treatment. Godzilla is sometimes wounded. He reacts to another monster. He looks toward an enemy, turns away from a city, moves in anger, pauses as though considering something. In some films he has offspring. In others he is a near-incomprehensible biological disaster. He can die. He can return. He can be horrifying and pitiable within a few minutes.

An audience responds not only to the historical idea attached to him but to his dramatic behavior. We watch him as a character, an animal, an event, and a kind of impossible actor. His apparent intention can be ambiguous, but his presence is never merely a diagram of an argument.

This matters especially in the original film. Godzilla is linked to hydrogen-bomb testing, and his destruction recalls the violence Japanese people had recently experienced. The Lucky Dragon Number Five fishing vessel was contaminated by fallout from the Castle Bravo test in 1954, the year the film was released. The continuing fear of nuclear attack and radioactive contamination was neither ancient memory nor abstract science fiction. It was a lived part of Japan's public life.

But Honda's film does not turn every character into a mouthpiece for one uncomplicated position. Dr. Yamane, the paleontologist, wants to learn from the creature and is horrified by the prospect of its destruction. Other people reasonably fear that he may not understand the immediate human cost of leaving Godzilla alive. Serizawa can end that danger, but the method itself may be even more dangerous if repeated. Emiko and Ogata become caught between personal loyalties and the obligation to prevent further death. The film forces questions into conflict instead of allowing a single slogan to resolve them.

Godzilla is both a product of human destructive power and a creature who destroys people who did not choose that power. He is at once an injured thing and a terrible injury to others. That double position helps explain why the movie remains more disturbing than a simple story in which science creates an evil monster and brave people defeat it.

The body on the screen can look back at us. We might be responsible for what has happened to him. We may still be justified in trying to save ourselves. Those statements coexist uneasily, and the film knows it.

## The trouble with a clean national story

When talking about the 1954 film, there is a temptation to give it a clean political meaning. Japan experienced atomic devastation, and Godzilla appears as the fearful return of atomic violence. That connection is real and essential. It is not the entirety of the history that the film inherited.

Japan had endured horrific bombing, defeat, occupation, and nuclear testing in the Pacific. It had also fought an imperial war in which Japanese forces inflicted enormous suffering on people throughout Asia. The original Godzilla's emphasis on Japanese suffering can illuminate the first history while leaving much of the second outside the frame. The film's human urgency does not relieve a critic of the responsibility to notice what the film chooses not to depict.

This is not an accusation that every disaster picture must recount the whole twentieth century. No work could do that. It is an invitation to resist the comforting notion that historical memory becomes complete when it is emotionally persuasive. A national image of victimhood can be honest about injury and selective about responsibility at the same time.

Film historian William Tsutsui has argued that the Godzilla movies' politics are often more contradictory than the shorthand descriptions applied to them. He observes that even the original's pacifist elements exist alongside displays of military equipment and patriotic feeling. In his discussion of *Godzilla Minus One*, he finds anti-authoritarian elements entangled with nostalgia and historically selective images of postwar Japan. Those are scholarly judgments worth engaging, not universal verdicts we must repeat without thought. The value of that analysis is in refusing an easy choice between calling Godzilla politically innocent and calling the films politically pure.

The 2001 film *Godzilla, Mothra and King Ghidorah: Giant Monsters All-Out Attack*, usually shortened to *GMK*, makes one absence especially vivid. Its Godzilla is associated with the restless dead of Japan's wartime aggression. The creature returns not simply as nature's revenge or an old atomic terror, but as a demand that a country remember people it has reason to prefer forgetting. What is ordinarily outside the triumphant memory of rebuilding becomes part of the monster's interior force.

That is a tremendous transformation of the same visual figure. A creature originally connected with Japanese suffering becomes an instrument through which Japanese wrongdoing can be recalled. The film's supernatural explanation may be fantastical, but its moral challenge is concrete: whose dead receive public attention, and whose are made difficult to see?

The shift does not mean that *GMK* finally supplies the one correct meaning of Godzilla. It means that a monster capable of returning from the sea can also return to places a national story has tried to close. Films can reopen a memory rather than merely preserve it.

At the other extreme, the international monster battles sometimes look as if they could occur anywhere at all. A creature meets another creature; scale and force take over; the immediate national history recedes. There is a pleasure in that distance, and there may even be an ethical freedom in refusing to force every fantasy to become a history lesson. Still, it is worth asking what an international film gains by reducing specific context and what it loses.

If Godzilla can mean anything, the danger is that he will mean nothing in particular. The counter to that danger is not to demand that every film reproduce Honda's politics. It is to ask each film to make a specific choice about the world its monster enters.

## When we want the monster to win

Here is a less solemn fact about Godzilla: people love him.

Not merely the idea of him. Not merely the cultural meaning. People love his strange face, his silhouette, the slow movement of his shoulders, the roaring, the spines glowing before a blast, the moment an adversary realizes who has arrived. They enjoy knowing that a particular movement of the tail will knock something over. Children imitate him. Adults buy figures and build collections. Fans can recognize a film from the shape of the head or the musical phrase announcing an entrance. That affection is not an error that interferes with proper interpretation. It is part of the historical life of the character.

The films began cultivating it long ago. In the Shōwa cycle Godzilla confronts other monsters, sometimes as an uneasy ally and eventually as a protector. *Ghidorah, the Three-Headed Monster*, released in 1964, makes a central encounter into a dispute among gigantic creatures. In later films, Godzilla fights for the defense of the Earth or particular people, and children can cheer him without needing to suspend their sense that he is powerful and dangerous.

We could call this commercialization, and we should not ignore commercial incentives. A monster that can defend the world, carry toys, and headline repeat adventures offers a film studio many opportunities. But children are not merely passive recipients of a studio's strategy. They invent games and loyalties. They recognize personalities in bodies that adults may see only as effects. A movie in which Godzilla saves someone can give a child an imaginative relation to power that the 1954 tragedy was never designed to provide.

One way to describe the transformation is that Godzilla moves from being the danger in the story to being the means by which danger can be faced. The atomic breath that once reduced buildings to catastrophe becomes the very capability an audience hopes he will use against Hedorah, Mechagodzilla, or Ghidorah. The weapon has not disappeared. Its position within the drama has changed.

That change can be exhilarating. It can also make the character difficult to handle when filmmakers want to recover fear. How do you make a creature terrifying after generations have cheered every roar? You can redesign the body, surround it with more sophisticated effects, or give it a new origin. But the audience still knows the name. The audience brings affection into the theater. That familiarity is not simply a problem to solve. It is a force the film must incorporate.

Consider *Godzilla vs. Hedorah* again. The movie can be funny, ungainly, psychedelic, and concerned with pollution within the same running time. Its environmental enemy attacks the world through industrial waste and poisonous contamination. Godzilla becomes an absurd but welcome opponent of that threat. The atomic monster is temporarily recast as the instrument of an ecological rescue.

The logic may not withstand an engineer's inspection, and the imagery may alternate between visual invention and clumsy effect. We should be free to say so. But the movie's peculiar mixture helps explain the franchise's emotional range. The same body that could terrify one generation can give another a reason to cheer, without becoming an ordinary domesticated hero.

A monster that can be loved is not automatically a better monster. But a monster that can only be feared would have led a much shorter career.

## The work of making him real

It is easy to praise the old films for their practical effects and the recent ones for their digital sophistication. Both statements can conceal a great deal of artistry.

In 1954, Haruo Nakajima performed inside the Godzilla costume, with Eiji Tsuburaya's effects unit constructing the miniature environment in which that performance would take place. A model building is not simply a reduced building. For the audience to feel its collapse as weighty, the camera speed, model materials, lighting, framing, sound, and timing all need to cooperate. What looks like a single effortless gesture from a monster is the work of many people, often under difficult physical conditions.

There is a wonderfully productive contradiction in this craft. The viewer knows, at some level, that the creature is not real. The viewer may know quite a lot about the mechanics. Yet knowledge of the trick does not necessarily make the achievement smaller. Sometimes it makes it more moving. Someone had to design that street, make its windows, arrange the debris, construct a costume, and decide precisely when the performer should put a foot down. The creature's overwhelming force depends on astonishing human patience.

That is true of digital effects too, though the labor is less visible to viewers. Computer graphics do not summon a finished Godzilla from a word. Artists shape anatomy, movement, lighting, textures, water, dust, smoke, and the relationships between the creature and photographed environments. The effects become convincing when the impossible body has consequences for the ordinary world around it. In *Godzilla Minus One*, released in 2023, a small boat being pursued across the water gives the eye a scale far more immediate than any number printed in a creature guide. The boat turns; the wake moves; the people aboard know they have almost no room to escape. The size of the monster is measured in the failure of a familiar human machine to outrun him.

The 2014 American *Godzilla*, directed by Gareth Edwards, offers a different but related lesson. The film often refuses a clean, detached view of the fight. A creature appears beyond smoke, through a window, or across a street that suddenly feels far too narrow. Edwards has described his preference for a human-level camera that preserves the impression of massive scale. He is pursuing a sensation as much as an image. An enormous animal filmed at its own eye level may become just another actor in a scene; viewed from the ground, it is an event that reorganizes the space around the audience.

We should distinguish fidelity of image from fidelity of feeling. A creature can have a perfect anatomy and still feel weightless if the film cuts too quickly or forgets the human world. A visibly artificial suit can feel enormous if the surrounding shots and sounds make the weight intelligible. No technology owns authenticity. It is constructed by decisions about time, scale, and attention.

Akira Ifukube's music and Godzilla's unmistakable voice are part of this construction. The roar is more than a loud effect: it identifies a being before the image has fully arrived. The music can lend ritual gravity to a creature who, in another film, is about to perform an act of impossible slapstick. Sound allows the character to be recognizable even when his body changes shape.

That is a remarkable achievement of collaborative cinema. The identity of Godzilla does not reside in a single actor, costume, animator, score, or sound cue. It exists across the work of many craftspeople and the memories of the people who have watched their work.

A historian of the series can document who built the monster. An admirer can enjoy the moment when the whole enterprise convinces the body that something immense has arrived. Those two responses do not need to compete.

## A creature that can die and still return

The original *Godzilla* has a clear ending. The creature is destroyed. Serizawa dies. The decision is not presented as an ordinary victory that clears the way for another adventure. It is a terrible measure taken to prevent greater horror. The film leaves us with the possibility that continued nuclear testing could bring another catastrophe, but it does not wink at the audience and invite everyone back for a larger monster fight.

A long-running franchise asks a question that this ending did not need to answer: what if the monster comes back anyway?

Godzilla's return can be a cynical act of commercial repetition. Make a successful movie, then sell the next one. But it can also become a dramatic resource. The ending of one film closes its argument, while the next uses the known body to begin a different one. The series does not have to pretend that all its histories fit together.

The 1984 *Return of Godzilla* re-establishes the creature as a frightening danger, treating the 1954 film as a foundational event and moving past much of the intervening Shōwa continuity. The connected films of the Heisei cycle develop an unfolding story, culminating in the dying, overheating Godzilla of *Godzilla vs. Destoroyah* in 1995. That farewell asks the audience to grieve a creature who has frightened and entertained it. We have seen him survive missiles and other monsters. Now the dangerous body itself becomes fragile.

The Millennium films beginning in 1999 take another approach. Many establish separate histories rather than continuing one uninterrupted saga. *GMK* recasts the creature in a supernatural register. *Godzilla Against Mechagodzilla*, released in 2002, builds the mechanical guardian Kiryu from the skeleton of the original Godzilla. The new weapon carries the remains of an old catastrophe inside the machine designed to defend Japan. The premise is melodramatic and bizarre, but the metaphor is unusually concise: attempts to master an inherited danger can give that danger a second life.

Then *Shin Godzilla* arrives in 2016 with a creature whose evolving anatomy defeats the government's categories. *Godzilla Minus One* returns to an imagined late-1940s Japan to examine the survival of people who believe they have no right to happiness. Neither film needs every previous Godzilla event to have occurred. Both benefit from our accumulated recognition of the monster. They use that recognition in radically different ways.

Reboots are sometimes described as evidence of creative exhaustion, especially by people who prefer the security of a single canonical history. There is a real weakness in endlessly restarting without developing the consequences of a story. But Godzilla also demonstrates the opposite possibility. A recurring image can make different historical problems comparable without pretending that the plots belong to one continuous fictional timeline.

A monster can be killed in one film and alive in the next because the next film has another question to ask.

That freedom demands judgment. We should not excuse a lazy story simply because it belongs to a flexible franchise. A new Godzilla needs an intelligible dramatic purpose. The films that succeed usually know what sort of arrival the creature represents: punishment, invasion, ecological crisis, ancient sovereignty, opportunity for courage, or the terrible return of a past people hoped had ended.

It may be that the most important rule of Godzilla continuity is not continuity at all. It is the obligation to make the familiar creature strange again, even while allowing us the pleasure of recognizing him.

## Nature is not a kindly judge

One of the most enduring explanations for Godzilla is that he represents nature taking revenge on humanity. In certain films, particularly those that emphasize ecological destruction, the description is apt. But it becomes less helpful if we imagine that nature has the clear moral intentions of a courtroom judge.

The original Godzilla is not simply the Earth punishing humanity for making a bomb. He is also a creature displaced and transformed by destructive technology, a victim of forces he did not choose. His actions kill people who bear no direct responsibility for the nuclear tests. A story in which nature simply distributes deserved punishment would be easier to understand and, I think, less frightening. Real ecological disasters do not assign suffering with such precision. The people least able to prepare may be the most exposed. A poisoned coastline does not ask who voted for the factory. A radioactive cloud does not distinguish an official from a child.

*Godzilla vs. Hedorah* makes industrial contamination visible as a grotesque living adversary. Its tone is volatile. An environmental warning shares space with pop imagery, music, bodily disgust, and that flying Godzilla. The film is not trying to make pollution seem gentle or natural. The monster Hedorah is an image of a human-altered environment that becomes hostile to the living. Yet the solution is not a simple return to an untouched natural world; it requires a battle between creatures and human technological ingenuity. Even the fantasy of ecological revenge must confront the fact that people remain responsible for what happens next.

The American MonsterVerse pushes in another direction. Its Titans are ancient beings associated with the planet's hidden ecosystems and a balance beyond human understanding. Godzilla's intervention can save humanity from monsters more destructive than himself. That mythology is attractive because it corrects a familiar human arrogance. We are not the only important inhabitants of Earth, and our instruments for controlling the world may be laughably inadequate.

But this mythology also risks an easy consolation. If a giant protector restores balance, perhaps humanity need only stand back and recognize the rightful ruler. Environmental repair becomes a battle among magnificent creatures, conveniently staged far above the work of policy, science, local knowledge, and human responsibility. In a film built around giant monsters, it is perfectly reasonable to make the monster fight the main attraction. It is less persuasive to call that spectacle a comprehensive environmental philosophy.

There is a related danger in treating Godzilla as a pure embodiment of nature. A creature that breathes radioactive energy, appears through human industrial and military history, and can be represented by a performer in an elaborate costume is hardly a convenient symbol of nature untouched by humanity. Godzilla is compelling partly because the boundary between the human and the nonhuman is already damaged. He may be a beast, a bomb, a memory, a mutant, or a living weapon. Those categories no longer stay politely separate.

The most fruitful environmental question is therefore not whether Godzilla is on nature's side or ours. It is what kind of responsibility remains when our actions have changed a world that was never fully under our control.

The earliest film's painful answer is that some harms cannot be made harmless by a better weapon. *Hedorah* offers an angrier, more anarchic answer: the contamination itself may become an enemy. The MonsterVerse offers the exhilaration of ancient powers that exceed our institutions. Each invites the audience to reconsider how small humanity is. Only some of them make that smallness politically or emotionally productive.

An enormous monster need not prove that people are insignificant. Often the size difference reveals exactly how much ordinary human conduct still counts.

## A film about a city must remember its people

Godzilla movies spend enormous amounts of time destroying cities. That sentence can sound like an indictment. Destruction is also their spectacular medium. A building falling in a giant-monster film is a visual event, the culmination of design and effects work, and often the source of a pleased exclamation from the audience.

A filmmaker has to decide what lies underneath that pleasure.

The Tokyo of the 1954 film has recognizable civilians, emergency services, doctors, and people trying to recover bodies. We are given time to see destruction becoming a problem for institutions and families. The film's grief does not come simply from showing more rubble than another film. It comes from reminding us that the rubble was once a city inhabited by people whose lives had recognizable continuities.

In *Shin Godzilla*, released in 2016, the human unit of measurement shifts toward a whole administrative order. A creature changes form and capability faster than the government can agree on its nature. The film's meetings are sometimes comic, but they become the necessary labor by which a response can eventually occur. A city is a set of dependencies. Water, transport, evacuation, expertise, authority, information and public trust all have to work together, and a single monster can expose their limits.

That is why the film's most exhilarating human action can be a team making a decision that it has finally gathered enough knowledge to make. An emergency committee becomes a protagonist, which sounds like a joke until the movie makes it work. The film knows that scientific intelligence is not valuable if it cannot become coordinated practice.

*Godzilla Minus One*, released in 2023, moves the center of experience from the state to a survivor. Kōichi Shikishima is a former kamikaze pilot who believes he failed in his wartime duty. He is alive, but survival has become an accusation he turns against himself. He forms an improvised family with Noriko and the orphaned child Akiko, and then Godzilla threatens the possibility of ordinary happiness that he had barely allowed himself to accept.

The film's final plan to defeat the creature involves former military personnel, engineers and civilians acting without an uncomplicated faith in the institutions that conducted the war. The drama is not satisfied with another heroic order to sacrifice oneself. It asks whether people can confront danger without repeating the demand that a useful life must end in death. The resolution is designed to be emotionally liberating. Whether every part of that liberation is earned, and whether its historical setting cleanses the national past too easily, are fair questions. But the film knows that the creature's size is secondary to the size of the life Shikishima might still be able to live.

This contrast is more revealing than an argument about which Godzilla looks more realistic. The original shows a community facing catastrophe. *Shin Godzilla* shows institutions struggling to become capable of collective action. *Minus One* shows an individual and a chosen family trying to escape the shadow of a previous catastrophe. They operate at different scales of human organization. Each film must make us believe that Godzilla can alter something precious at that scale.

The recently released *Godzilla Minus Zero*, set after *Minus One* and returning to its surviving family, tests another version of that proposition. A sequel inherits relationships the audience already knows, so its task is not just to create new danger. It must give the survival achieved in the earlier film some continuing dramatic weight. Reviews published in 2026 disagree over how successfully the sequel balances its larger monster spectacle against renewed emotional wounds. That disagreement is unsurprising. Once a story gives people a hard-won ending, threatening that ending again can either deepen their lives or make the preceding victory seem provisional by commercial necessity.

In all these films, the monster is the shared problem, not the whole story. A creature capable of tearing down a neighborhood is also capable of exposing what that neighborhood was made of. People know one another there. Somebody cares for a child. Somebody decides whether to flee or stay. An engineer has a plan that may fail. A doctor must treat a wound without enough supplies. The public world begins to reveal its private and collective structure.

When a film attends to that structure, the monster's enormousness feels earned. When it forgets, the city may become a beautiful pile of expendable pixels. The spectacle can still entertain, but it carries less beyond the immediate impact.

## The temptation to explain everything

A recurring figure in Godzilla cinema is the scientist who wants to understand the creature. Sometimes that scientist is morally serious. Sometimes the character is a convenient source of improbable exposition. Science can illuminate the monster, but it rarely settles what the monster means.

In the 1954 film, Yamane's desire to study Godzilla and Serizawa's horror at the consequences of his own research turn knowledge into a source of moral tension. The threat is not merely that humanity knows too little. It is that knowledge can become a means of doing harm. An ability to kill Godzilla is also an ability to create a new weapon. The discovery and its consequences cannot be separated.

Later films introduce radiation patterns, prehistoric biology, genetic experiments, extraterrestrial invaders, psychic communication, hidden ecosystems and elaborate maps of monster ancestry. Some of these premises are inventive enough to become a film's whole attraction. The plant and human genetic material in *Godzilla vs. Biollante*, released in 1989, make the creature itself into an expression of grief and biotechnology. The original Godzilla's skeleton inside the machine Kiryu is another striking physical explanation with a dramatic afterlife. The Heisei series' continuity can make one scientific development carry consequences across several films.

But the more completely a franchise explains its creatures, the more it risks reducing them to units in a technical manual. We learn powers, ranks, weaknesses, family relationships, taxonomy and rules of battle. Fans may enjoy all of this, and there is real pleasure in a well-constructed fictional natural history. Yet a catalog can never supply the experience of the first arrival. A chart that tells us a monster is hundreds of feet tall does not reproduce the moment a small person sees its shadow covering a street.

An audience also needs permission not to know everything. The unexplained suggests a world larger than the story; the overexplained can shrink the world until every corner seems accounted for. That balance is not specific to giant-monster cinema, but Godzilla makes it particularly visible. The creature is too large for the frame and sometimes too large for the ideas that try to contain him.

*Shin Godzilla* turns that mismatch into the plot. Officials propose a description and then encounter new evidence that makes it inadequate. The creature's successive forms mock the government's confidence in its categories. The eventual victory is a feat of collective intelligence, but the last image refuses to suggest that the monster has become completely understood.

The MonsterVerse offers a nearly opposite pleasure: the discovery of a large, organized hidden world of Titans, underground geography and ancient conflict. It turns mystery into an expanding map. When that map supports fresh adventures, it is exhilarating. When each revelation mainly prepares the next franchise installment, the world can begin to feel like an advertisement for information we have not been given yet.

The difference is not that one approach is artistic and the other commercial. Both are commercial films with aesthetic intentions. The difference is what each story does with what remains unknown.

A monster that we can explain may still frighten us. A monster that we can name may still surprise us. But we should resist the assumption that complete knowledge is the natural destination of every story worth telling.

## The monster travels; the work does not become one thing

Godzilla belongs to a worldwide audience in the everyday sense that people around the world watch, discuss, criticize, collect and imagine stories about him. He does not belong to every national tradition in precisely the same way.

The 1956 American recut of the original film added Raymond Burr as reporter Steve Martin. That choice increased accessibility for some audiences and displaced aspects of the Japanese film's own perspective. The 1998 American film turned Godzilla into a swiftly moving giant reptile pursued through New York. Legendary's films beginning in 2014 restored many recognizable features while creating an interconnected world in which Godzilla could function as an overwhelming power among other Titans.

These are not different stages in a simple process of becoming more universally authentic. Each is an act of selection and translation. Each emphasizes particular pleasures and leaves others behind. The 1956 film can be a viewer's beloved childhood introduction and an act of adaptation that obscures part of the original. The 1998 film can be an entertaining creature thriller and a Godzilla picture that fails to satisfy what many people reasonably mean by the character. The MonsterVerse can produce extraordinary moments of awe and also sometimes give its human cast little more to do than explain the spectacle.

It is worth noticing that the franchise's Japanese line of production did not vanish during this international expansion. Toho continued to authorize and make films with their own agendas, including the radical catastrophe of *Shin Godzilla* and the postwar melodrama of *Minus One*. A Japanese Godzilla and an American Godzilla can be in theaters or on screens within a few years of each other without one having to be the official future of the other.

There are artistic advantages to this plurality. A film about an emergency government need not solve the same problem as a film about Kong's attachment to a child. A film built around a returning family need not set up a battle with every other creature in the franchise. The audience can prefer one version, even deeply, without demanding that other versions cease to exist.

At the same time, commercial abundance is not automatically artistic health. Familiar intellectual property can become a substitute for taking chances. Reappearances can be designed to reward recognition rather than tell a story; a monster can be deployed as a branded climax because the franchise schedule calls for one. When the audience knows exactly what kind of roar, glow, and fight will arrive, anticipation can shade into routine.

The answer is not to forbid familiarity. Familiarity is part of the pleasure. The challenge is to do something with it: give a beloved gesture a new consequence, place a familiar face in a different kind of world, or allow the monster to refuse a role the audience had comfortably assigned him.

Godzilla's history suggests that renewal does not come from finding a single pure national or tonal identity. It comes from filmmakers choosing a relation to the past that makes the new film worth experiencing now.

## We should be allowed to dislike a Godzilla film

The fact that this character can sustain contradictions does not mean every movie in the series is equally accomplished. There is no need to pretend a poorly structured screenplay becomes profound because a giant lizard is standing in the middle of it.

Some Godzilla films have thin characters, hurried stories, awkward pacing, or special effects that fail to persuade. A few use children as audience surrogates without taking their experiences seriously. Others pile on mythology that the drama has not earned. Even in films with striking visual ideas, the human story can feel perfunctory, and a long monster battle can eventually lose its sense of force.

At the same time, complaints about the most extravagant Shōwa scenes can be lazy. Calling something silly is often a description, not a criticism. A flying Godzilla may be delightful precisely because the film embraces the gesture. A monster wrestling match may have the rhythm and clarity of a well-performed physical comedy. A child may see courage and loyalty in an image that an adult dismisses because its rubber surfaces are plainly visible.

A serious film can also be bad, or serious in ways that conceal weaknesses. Moral earnestness does not automatically make dialogue convincing. A film invoking nuclear history may simplify the people who lived through it. An impressive technical production may have no meaningful place for the characters beneath the giant feet. Critical respect should be earned by what happens on screen, not by the solemnity of the topic.

The 1998 American *Godzilla* provides a useful test. If one begins by declaring it an insult, there is nothing left to examine. If one begins by declaring that all adaptations are equally valid, the very real differences in its dramatic design disappear. We can notice its speed, the ingenuity of some pursuit sequences, the attempt to treat a gigantic creature as an animal with survival instincts, and the difficulty of relating that animal to the Godzilla audiences thought they had come to see. We can admire parts of the experiment and still conclude that it fails as a convincing reinvention of Godzilla.

The 2019 Hollywood *King of the Monsters* offers almost the opposite problem. It knows the history of the classic creatures, restores recognizable designs, and gives the monster battles grand religious and ecological imagery. For admirers, those elements can be intensely satisfying. Yet fidelity to familiar names and shapes is not the same as a strong human drama. A film may understand how an audience wants its monsters to look and still struggle to make its people interesting.

Nor should a critic settle the whole franchise by ranking films from best to worst. Rankings can be fun, and they can provoke useful argument. But a list can hide the diversity of experiences being compared. Is a 1954 tragedy about mass destruction competing on the same terms with a 1970s children's adventure or a twenty-first-century crossover? We can have preferences, sometimes strong ones, without pretending the works all entered the same contest.

This freedom to judge is an expression of affection, not a betrayal of it. The movies are sturdy enough to survive a viewer saying that a joke is bad, a scene is cruel, a performance is moving, or a certain fight is worth the price of admission all by itself. Love of Godzilla is not a demand that we surrender ordinary artistic judgment.

## The generations watching

Think about the people who have encountered Godzilla over more than seventy years. A Japanese viewer in 1954 might recognize the terror of a city under attack. An American viewer might first meet Raymond Burr narrating the aftermath. A child in the 1960s or 1970s might watch Godzilla fight an impossible enemy and decide that the creature is a hero. Another viewer might know Godzilla from television broadcasts, a battered rental tape, a toy, a dubbed film, a new digital restoration, a game, or a huge modern theater screen.

These audiences do not arrive with identical expectations. They also do not keep the same expectations throughout their lives. The child who loved a monster's triumphant entrance can become an adult interested in Honda's wartime experience or Tsuburaya's painstaking production work. Someone who first admired the historical allegory may later find genuine pleasure in the most openly ridiculous films. A viewer who dismissed the old costumes may discover, after seeing the craft behind them, how much expressive movement the performers achieved.

The films offer opportunities to revise ourselves because Godzilla has never settled into only one kind of work. There is no embarrassment in enjoying the battles while taking the history seriously, or in studying the history without losing the ability to laugh at a giant creature propelled through the air by his own atomic breath.

The international community surrounding the monster can also keep its history in view. Restorations and translations allow audiences to distinguish the 1954 Japanese original from the 1956 American recut. Interviews preserve the testimony of performers and effects workers. Critics and scholars ask questions that the promotional material does not. Viewers compare cultural contexts, argue over choices, and defend films that a larger consensus has ignored.

Fans can make the franchise intellectually richer. They can also make it narrower when loyalty becomes a set of prohibited interpretations. If the person who loves the first Godzilla is told that later films must be despised, or the admirer of a modern crossover is told that the 1954 tragedy has nothing to offer, the argument has become a border checkpoint rather than a conversation. Godzilla does not need such protection.

The character has passed through so many hands that fidelity can only mean something thoughtful and specific: fidelity to a film's own choices, to the world it evokes, and to the people whose labor made it. Sometimes fidelity means retaining a familiar silhouette. Sometimes it means preserving the ability to frighten an audience who already knows the silhouette by heart.

Godzilla is not merely the sum of his appearances. He is also the work viewers do when they see those appearances together and ask what each version has made possible.

## The limit of what a monster can do

We have come a long way from a bomb-struck city to an enormous creature flying backward on his breath. The distance is real. I do not want to solve it by announcing that every Godzilla is secretly the same moral lesson, nor by saying the films have no relation beyond a mascot.

There is a continuity of sensation and a discontinuity of purpose. Across many productions, Godzilla enters an ordinary-looking world with a body that does not fit it. The familiar scale of streets, ships, buildings, crowds, and families becomes inadequate. People have to respond to a force they did not authorize and cannot command. Sometimes the force is the threat. Sometimes it is the only available ally against a greater threat. Sometimes it is the thing a film wants us to cheer for before it has even appeared.

But the monster cannot decide for us what to do with that experience. He cannot replace historical understanding with a roar. He cannot guarantee environmental justice by fighting another monster. He cannot make a film profound merely by carrying the shadow of nuclear weapons. He cannot rescue a thin screenplay just because we recognize the spines along his back.

Those limits are important because they place responsibility where it belongs: with the artists making a particular film and with the people who watch it. The creature supplies an extraordinary possibility. A story has to decide what to do with it.

The 1954 ending takes that responsibility with terrible seriousness. Serizawa possesses a weapon capable of destroying Godzilla. He understands the danger of leaving it to the world. He chooses a sacrifice that denies anyone the chance to turn his discovery into a routine instrument of power. The scene does not suggest that knowledge is wicked or humanity helpless. It asks what a person owes others when their own ingenuity has created the means of another catastrophe.

That is not the answer every Godzilla film must give. The later films are entitled to different questions, and some of the happiest moments in the series occur when the monster simply does something gloriously impossible. But the original's seriousness demonstrates an enduring standard. The destruction on screen is only the beginning of the story's responsibility. What do the people who survive believe? What choices do they face? What world remains after Godzilla has passed?

That is why the most convincing Godzilla films do not rely on size alone. They make the audience feel the difference between a world that people thought they knew and a world altered by the monster's arrival. The alteration can be tragic or liberating, terrifying or joyous. It has to be real within the film.

## After the roar

The franchise has given Godzilla dozens of origins, adversaries, allies, abilities, and endings. Some are moving. Some are preposterous. Several are both. I see no need to choose one final authorized version and declare the rest misunderstandings. Nor do I think limitless reinterpretation excuses a film from knowing why it has brought the monster back.

The question that seems most useful is surprisingly ordinary: when Godzilla arrives, what becomes visible?

In one film, a history of nuclear terror that polite language had difficulty containing. In another, the defiant imagination of a child. In another, the fragility of public institutions during an emergency. In another, the possibility that somebody burdened by guilt might still deserve a life. In another, the joy of watching a creature vast enough to make everything else seem temporarily small.

That last pleasure should not be denied. We are allowed to love the spectacle. A movie theater can be a place where people sit together and take uncomplicated delight in a thing no person on Earth could actually survive. The relief of the impossible is part of art. A giant monster is also, sometimes, a giant monster.

Yet even at the height of that delight, the film depends on a small human act of attention. Someone has built a city at a scale a hand can hold. Someone has performed inside a cumbersome body or animated the motion of an imagined one. Someone has decided how long to let the camera rest on a doorway before the creature appears. Someone has arranged a sound that travels through the theater and makes a roomful of people look toward the same point on the screen.

The image is enormous because of innumerable small decisions.

And when the film ends, Godzilla does not have to remain either the hero or the villain. He has served the purpose of that particular story, which may be a dark purpose or a joyous one. We can carry it with us without requiring the next story to agree.

The first Godzilla movie allowed its monster to die. It had the courage to mean that ending. The long history that followed did not make the ending meaningless. It made it possible to discover, again and again, why a filmmaker might need to call such a creature out of the sea—and why an audience would still turn to look.
