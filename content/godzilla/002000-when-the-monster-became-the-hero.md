# When the Monster Became the Hero

Imagine arriving at the ending of the original *Godzilla* in 1954 and being told what the monster would do over the next twenty years. He would fight a giant moth, quarrel with another dinosaur-like creature, acquire a son, perform something resembling a victory dance, become entangled in alien invasions, and propel himself backward through the air by using his atomic breath as a rocket. In one movie, he would be the most terrifying object in Japan. In another, a child might wish he could come to the rescue.

If this sounds like a character losing the plot, that is part of the entertainment. But it is not the whole story. The transformation of Godzilla from a destroyer into a defender was not a single wrong turn after a serious beginning. It was the discovery that a gigantic monster could become a continuing screen personality. The creature who once worked as a figure of uncontrollable catastrophe could now be a rival, a reluctant ally, a parent, a champion, or simply an enormous nuisance whom viewers were delighted to see again.

What changed was not just the subject of the films. It was the audience's relationship to the creature. Fear did not disappear; it acquired company. We could watch Godzilla with curiosity, recognition, impatience, affection, and delight. The films sometimes handled those feelings clumsily. Some are difficult to defend as tight dramas. Yet their willingness to experiment is the reason the character had so many futures available to him.

The most interesting question is not when Godzilla stopped being serious. It is when the act of watching him changed from waiting for a monster to be defeated to hoping that this particular monster would win.

## The problem with a monster who is dead

The first *Godzilla* ends with the destruction of its creature. Dr. Serizawa uses the Oxygen Destroyer and dies to keep the invention from becoming a weapon. It is a surprisingly conclusive ending for a film that would inaugurate a long-running franchise. Within a year, Toho had made *Godzilla Raids Again*, released in 1955 and directed by Motoyoshi Oda.

The solution was both simple and revealing. The Godzilla in the sequel is another member of the kind, rather than a miraculous return of the individual killed in 1954. The film introduces Anguirus, a heavily armored, spiny monster, and sets the two creatures against one another. A threat that had filled the whole dramatic horizon now had an opponent of comparable scale.

That alteration changed the possibilities of the image. In the original film, Godzilla was a disaster moving through human structures. In *Raids Again*, human beings still suffer and try to survive, but the camera can also watch two nonhuman forces collide. We begin to consider how one monster fights, how another resists, and what makes their movements different. The city remains vulnerable, but the destruction has a new cause: not just one creature attacking people, but two creatures whose conflict happens to occupy our world.

A monster-versus-monster fight offers a deliciously unstable kind of identification. Neither combatant needs to be humanly admirable. We may cheer for the one who appears stronger, more expressive, more familiar, or more likely to make the next moment entertaining. Godzilla does not have to be morally good to become a character with an audience on his side. He only needs to become recognizable as someone who makes choices in relation to someone else.

The sequel was not the beginning of an uninterrupted annual series. There was a substantial pause before *King Kong vs. Godzilla* in 1962. That gap also matters. The filmmakers were not simply obliged to continue the story of a single animal. They could rediscover Godzilla as a title attraction whose identity might survive changes in plot, setting, and tone.

And in 1962, the attraction was gloriously explicit. Godzilla would meet King Kong.

## An enormous advertisement for an enormous fight

The original Japanese *King Kong vs. Godzilla*, directed by Ishirō Honda, makes spectacle part of its own joke. Its human world includes commercial promotion and television interests, with corporate enthusiasm for extraordinary creatures extending far beyond any sensible concern for public safety. The monster battle has a whiff of publicity stunt about it. The premise resembles the argument two excited spectators might have in a cinema lobby: who would win if these two famous creatures met?

The picture answers by turning that argument into entertainment. The monsters are not subtle psychological studies, nor are they supposed to be. Their differences are immediate and legible. Kong has an animal's agility and a performer's capacity for gesture. Godzilla is bulkier, more armored in his outline, and equipped with a devastating breath weapon. They belong to different traditions of movie spectacle, and the film makes a show of putting those traditions in the same space.

There is comedy in the fighting, even in the proportions. Watching giants grapple transforms the landscape into something like a wrestling ring. Hills, power lines, water, and buildings are no longer merely locations. They become available objects in a contest. A gesture that would be small in a human fight becomes absurdly consequential when the participants tower over houses.

This is where the expression *monster wrestling* earns its usefulness. The audience is invited to read balance, feints, changes in momentum, and moments of apparent embarrassment. A creature can look as though it is losing face. It can make a mistake. It can give us the pleasure of recognizing that a particular maneuver is ridiculous and entertaining anyway. The film is selling a matchup, not asking us to grieve for a city in the same register as 1954.

There is a historical temptation to call this a descent from art into commerce. But commerce was never absent from the original film. The 1954 production was also a studio picture designed to attract an audience. The difference is what kind of experience the new movie offers. The original asks us to see destruction as a wound. The Kong crossover asks us to enjoy an event so impossibly large that even its foolishness becomes a kind of spectacle. Both are legitimate cinematic projects; their ambitions are simply different.

The picture also reveals how portable Godzilla already was. In eight years he had gone from the threatening center of a specifically Japanese atomic-age story to a participant in an international contest between recognizable screen legends. He had become a figure whose audience could arrive with expectations about *him*, not merely about the plot of the current film.

## Mothra changes the moral geometry

Two films arrived in 1964 that would profoundly reshape Godzilla's screen life. *Mothra vs. Godzilla* and *Ghidorah, the Three-Headed Monster* are sometimes treated as successive steps in a march toward heroic Godzilla. They are more interesting if we notice how different their uses of the monster are.

In *Mothra vs. Godzilla*, Godzilla remains a genuine threat. The human story includes the discovery of a giant egg and attempts to profit from it. Mothra comes with her own mythology, her small singing intermediaries, and an identity tied to protection and obligation. She is not simply another large body for Godzilla to strike. Her presence establishes a moral contrast. The audience has a creature it can regard as a defender, and Godzilla is the dangerous force opposing her.

The film asks us to attach feelings to monsters in different ways. Godzilla's outline and powers are familiar, but familiarity does not yet make him the hero. Mothra's relationship to those she protects gives the fight a meaning that size alone could never supply. A creature can be colossal and still seem vulnerable, especially when the audience understands what it is trying to preserve.

That distinction prepares an important change. Once a monster has motives that viewers can follow, future films can rearrange alliances without rebuilding the entire moral universe each time. The story has acquired a cast of gigantic personalities. A monster's role can be defined not only by what it does to humanity but by how it responds to other monsters.

*Ghidorah, the Three-Headed Monster*, also released in 1964, turns that possibility into an overt dramatic event. King Ghidorah arrives as a threat so destructive that Godzilla, Rodan, and Mothra must reconsider their quarrels. The little Mothra larvae cannot simply overpower every opponent. They have to appeal to creatures who have their own grievances and habits of fighting.

The monster negotiation is one of the strangest and most important scenes in the series. Giant animals seem to discuss whether they have any obligation to defend humans. The tiny Shobijin help mediate or interpret. Godzilla and Rodan do not instantly transform into patriotic guardians. They have reasons for disliking the humans who have attacked them. Their complaints are partly comical, but the comedy makes their earlier treatment part of the new story rather than pretending it never happened.

Imagine a serious wartime alliance drama played by creatures who communicate through bellowing, posturing, and a pair of very small interpreters. The contrast is absurd, and that absurdity is precisely the creative breakthrough. Godzilla is no longer only a terror that humans observe. He is someone who can be persuaded. His decision can alter the fate of others. The audience begins to anticipate not merely what he will destroy, but what side he will choose.

King Ghidorah is ideal for this transformation. His three heads, immense wings, and alien destructive energy make him a spectacle of excess, almost a threat too flamboyant to belong to the earlier film's world. Against him, former enemies can acquire the appeal of an unlikely team. To cheer for Godzilla does not require us to forget the atomic horror. It requires us to enter a different adventure in which there is something worse than the monster we already know.

That is the hinge on which much of the Shōwa era turns. Godzilla becomes a recurring personality because other creatures begin to supply contrasts, threats, and occasions for change.

## Aliens, a dance, and the freedom to be ridiculous

By *Invasion of Astro-Monster*, released in 1965, the monster films can accommodate space travel, alien bargaining, hypnosis or control of creatures, and the pleasures of science-fiction adventure. Godzilla and Rodan are caught up in a conflict with extraterrestrial powers who are not trustworthy partners. King Ghidorah reappears, and the monster battles become part of a larger game of deception.

Here the animals are performers in an increasingly flexible popular genre. They can be used, misused, summoned, and manipulated. Their fights have consequences for human characters, but the creatures themselves also deliver recognizably theatrical moments. Godzilla's celebratory movement after a victory has been remembered as a kind of dance, a gesture that would be almost impossible to fit into the mournful atmosphere of 1954.

It is easy to laugh at the dance, and I think the film expects us to enjoy doing so. We do not need to protect an imaginary dignity of the monster by explaining the gesture away. It is an outrageous bit of performance by a creature whose personality has grown bigger than its original purpose. The screen can now hold both the danger of an alien scheme and the delight of a famous monster acting as though he knows he has won.

Godzilla has, in effect, acquired comic timing. A monster's posture can be funny because the audience understands what triumph might look like in his body. That sounds like a trivial achievement until we ask how strange it is. A character who began as an embodiment of nuclear dread has become legible enough that a small change in gesture can produce laughter.

It was not a permanent replacement of horror by comedy. The franchise would continue to move between modes, sometimes within a single picture. But the comedic possibilities altered what a Godzilla film could promise. Going to see Godzilla now meant watching a familiar being do something we had never quite seen him do before. Recognition and surprise became partners.

## When a monster has someone smaller to look after

The changes become especially visible when the series turns toward younger viewers. *Son of Godzilla*, released in 1967, gives the creature a small companion, usually called Minilla in English. Godzilla's task is no longer simply to defeat an opponent or repel an alien attack. He also has to manage a smaller and less capable version of something like himself.

The relationship is not sentimental in a perfectly ordinary human way. Godzilla is a rough teacher. Minilla struggles, needs protection, makes mistakes, and tries to reproduce a devastating ability that comes far more naturally to the larger monster. The humor rests in the gap between a familiar family scene and the absurd sight of giant reptiles attempting it. A parent trying to teach a child can be impatient; here impatience is measured in atomic fire.

But the film also finds tenderness in the creatures' bodies. An enormous animal can shelter another one. Godzilla's weight and dangerousness, once threats to every human structure, become reassuring when set against something even more vulnerable than himself. The same physical scale that made him terrifying in Tokyo can now make him seem capable of protection. The interpretation changes because the relationship changes.

The ending, with the two creatures exposed to a changing environment, lets the film hold a little softness without ceasing to be a monster picture. The point is not that Godzilla has achieved a coherent human family psychology. He has acquired enough recognizable behavior that audiences can read care in his movements. That is a considerable artistic departure from a creature whose first important act was to make a boat disappear.

The young audience mattered to the series' exhibition culture as well. In the late 1960s Toho increasingly presented monster pictures in programming designed for children and families, including the Toho Champion Festival format. The industrial context did not dictate every story beat, but it changed what kinds of stories could be sold and what forms of familiarity they might reward. A recurring monster could provide the reliability of a beloved performer alongside the promise of a new opponent.

*Destroy All Monsters*, released in 1968, enlarges the scale again. Its pleasure is partly one of abundance: a whole world of creatures gathered in a shared adventure, managed and threatened by humans and extraterrestrials. It offers the sensation of watching a troupe rather than an isolated star. The monsters have become a cast, and the audience recognizes the significance of seeing them appear together.

*All Monsters Attack*, released in 1969, goes further in another direction. It follows a boy whose difficult life and fears find expression in imagined visits to a world of monsters. Minilla becomes a figure through whom childhood anxieties can be staged. The film uses extensive material from earlier movies, and its modest construction is plainly visible. Yet it would be a mistake to judge it only by how much new monster footage it offers. Its purpose is closer to a child's fantasy about courage, loneliness, and bullying than to a new national emergency.

Whether that purpose succeeds for a particular viewer is another question. Some audiences find the film cloying, others retain affection for its frankly juvenile emotional stakes, and both reactions are understandable. What is revealing is that Godzilla now belongs to the vocabulary of childhood imagination. He has traveled from an image of helplessness before destruction to a figure through whom a child can imagine becoming less helpless.

There is something almost mischievous about this reversal. A monster created to show that modern civilization might be powerless has become, for some of his viewers, a source of imagined confidence. The franchise did not plan this transformation as a philosophical exercise. But a popular character can acquire meanings through repeated use that no one installment could have anticipated.

## The smog monster, or how the horror came back sideways

If the Shōwa series had become nothing but harmless children's entertainment, *Godzilla vs. Hedorah*, released in 1971, would be difficult to explain. Directed by Yoshimitsu Banno, it is a movie about industrial pollution in which Godzilla confronts a creature born from or nourished by the waste of modern civilization. It contains cartoon inserts, psychedelic imagery, odd music, youthful rebellion, grotesque deaths, and one of the most spectacularly preposterous maneuvers in the franchise: Godzilla uses his atomic breath to propel himself backward through the sky.

The temptation is to declare that no coherent tone could contain all this. But Banno's movie operates on a particular nightmare logic. Hedorah is not simply a large animal with an unfortunate diet. Its very body seems to be made of the refuse people have thrown away. It shifts shape, spreads toxic substances, and turns things that normally signify industrial growth into sources of immediate danger. Smoke no longer just hides a monster. Smoke may *be* part of the monster.

The setting gives this threat an edge. Japan had experienced rapid economic development after the war, along with severe pollution and political struggles over its effects. Industrial contamination was not an abstract futuristic possibility. Diseases, damaged environments, and public conflict had made it a present concern. Banno's film takes that concern and imagines a being that grows more powerful whenever human society continues its destructive habits.

Godzilla, who had once embodied the consequences of nuclear technology, now appears in opposition to another man-made or man-fed catastrophe. That seems to turn him into a simple defender of nature. The film is odder than that. Godzilla himself remains a radioactive force. His means of resistance is spectacularly destructive. The battle is not a contest between pure nature and bad technology. It is a confrontation among disasters, with human beings desperate for a less terrible outcome.

That is part of why the bizarre tones work. The animated cutaways and surreal nightclub imagery do not merely provide a rest from an ordinary monster fight. They give the film a visual language for a culture that seems to be treating its own degradation as entertainment, commerce, or background noise. The scenes may be funny, frightening, awkward, or exhausting, depending on the viewer. They are not all trying to induce the same emotion.

Banno's later interviews describe the practical constraints and creative freedom surrounding the production, but the screen is our strongest evidence for what the finished movie does. The film keeps changing the scale of the harm. A polluting industrial system is difficult to see as a single object; Hedorah gives it a body. People harmed by toxic environments are often dispersed and ignored; the film brings danger directly into crowds, streets, and homes. A child who loves Godzilla might enjoy the creature's determination while absorbing a much less comforting picture of the modern world.

And then Godzilla flies. The absurdity is not an accident that criticism must politely disregard. It is the kind of moment that makes the movie impossible to reduce to a slogan. The creature bends himself into a shape almost like a projectile and fires his own breath to move through the air. For a few seconds, the movie seems to suspend the expectations that make even its invented physics feel familiar.

One might call the sequence a betrayal of Godzilla's dignity. But a monster that can survive that degree of comedic invention has acquired something remarkable: the audience's willingness to go along with him. The same film can show people suffering from pollution and make us laugh at a giant reptile improvising a jet engine. That combination is not automatically good filmmaking. It is evidence of the character's extraordinary flexibility, and of Banno's refusal to keep the franchise in one approved emotional register.

The film also troubles the reassuring story in which the heroic Godzilla has made catastrophe safe. If nature and industry remain in a destructive relationship, a monster's victory can only interrupt the problem. Human beings must still decide what to do with the world after the fight. Hedorah may be driven off or defeated; the habits that sustain pollution are not so easily destroyed.

## The hero has an increasingly crowded job

In the early 1970s the films repeatedly test how far Godzilla's new screen identity can travel. *Godzilla vs. Gigan* presents an extravagant enemy with an almost comic-book weapon built into its body. *Godzilla vs. Megalon*, released in 1973, gives us Jet Jaguar, a human-built robot whose expanding body allows him to participate in a giant-monster partnership. The films are often discussed for their budget constraints, recycled footage, or theatrical excess, and those features are real parts of their texture. But they also make the central monster readily available for team-ups.

There is a new kind of familiarity in these alliances. Instead of waiting to learn what Godzilla wants, the audience can usually assume that, however irritating or argumentative he may be, he is the one we hope will help when an even worse threat arrives. He is a star whose entrance promises an answer to a crisis. A role that once belonged to the military or to a brave scientist is now partly occupied by a creature who would have terrified both in 1954.

This is not a complete moral conversion. Godzilla does not become a citizen subject to ordinary rules, and the films do not demand consistency from every gesture. He is still stubborn, violent, and occasionally frightening. What has changed is the emotional contract. The audience's memory supplies an attachment strong enough to survive almost any ridiculous premise.

The 1974 *Godzilla vs. Mechagodzilla* uses that attachment cleverly. An apparent Godzilla behaves wrong. The familiar shape becomes an impostor, and the exposure of the mechanical creature beneath the disguise turns recognition into suspense. The movie can only exploit the difference between true Godzilla and false Godzilla because the audience now thinks it knows him. The original monster's silhouette has become a signature, something that can be counterfeited.

Mechagodzilla also supplies an ingenious opposition in terms of movement and design. Godzilla's body has become a recognizable kind of unruly life. The mechanical double is a weaponized copy, an arrangement of metal, artillery, and control. Their conflict is not simply nature against machines, because Godzilla has always carried the contamination of modern technology. But the robot's exact imitation makes the older creature seem oddly individual. A monster once feared for being inhuman now acquires character by comparison with an imitation that is even less human.

## A darker farewell

The final Shōwa-era entry, *Terror of Mechagodzilla* in 1975, returns Ishirō Honda to the director's chair. Its material is still fantastical: aliens, a rebuilt mechanical monster, and Titanosaurus, another gigantic creature drawn into human and extraterrestrial schemes. Yet the human story involving Katsura, a woman transformed through technological interference, brings a distinctly tragic current into the spectacle.

Katsura's body becomes a site of control. She is treated by others as an instrument, a person whose apparent humanity can be overridden by the machinery and ambitions forced upon her. Her relationship with the scientist Ichinose gives the film a human vulnerability that the wrestling spectacle cannot resolve on its own. The monster battles are exciting, but they occur beside a story about the loss of agency and the costs of being turned into a tool.

This is an instructive endpoint. It would be easy to picture the years from 1954 to 1975 as a straight downward line from horror into comedy, with the solemn original forever lost beneath increasingly goofy sequels. Yet the final film is willing to return to tragedy while keeping the full vocabulary of monster battles and science-fiction invention. The series did not forget how to be grim. It had simply discovered that grimness was one possibility among several.

The differences were not always perfectly reconciled. Some pictures are sluggish between their set pieces. Some leave human characters thinly written. Some make their monster conflicts clearer than their political worlds. There are gags that land and others that depend on affection the viewer may not share. Enjoying the Shōwa period does not require insisting that every film is secretly a masterpiece. Its importance lies in the ways the films kept discovering other uses for the same figure.

## What changed when audiences began to cheer

By 1975, Godzilla had gone through a remarkable set of relationships. He had fought an opponent, a rival from another cinema tradition, a protective moth, a space monster, a pollution monster, and a machine designed to look like himself. He had protected a smaller creature. He had been called into alliances, made a victory gesture, and tried a maneuver that turned his atomic breath into propulsion. The character was still recognizable. Yet the feelings a film could reasonably ask of its audience had multiplied.

This was not simply a betrayal of an original message. It was a wager that a popular image could be reinterpreted without becoming meaningless. Godzilla remained large, loud, dangerous, and resistant to human control. The world around him, however, kept changing what those qualities signified. Against a fragile city, his size meant annihilation. Against a worse monster, it meant reassurance. Beside Minilla, his strength could signify protection. Against Hedorah, his radiation-tainted body could become a desperate counterforce to toxic waste. Facing Mechagodzilla, his familiar shape became something worth distinguishing from a copy.

The audience changed too. A person who had seen the earlier films brought memories into the theater. That spectator could recognize an old roar, anticipate an entrance, laugh at a gesture, or feel affection for a creature who had once been an object of dread. Serial filmmaking is not just repetition; it is the accumulation of a relationship between stories and viewers. Even a clumsy sequel participates in that relationship, though it may not reward the time we give it.

There is a final irony worth preserving. The first Godzilla was a warning about the consequences of human power. The later heroic Godzilla increasingly became a fantasy of power that might save humans from threats they could not overcome. Those two impulses can conflict. But they are not foreign to one another. Both begin with the admission that there are forces in the world against which ordinary human action feels terribly small. One version turns that realization into mourning. Another turns it into the thrill of having something immense on our side.

To see Godzilla fly backward through the air is to witness the far edge of that second impulse. It is ridiculous, and it is wonderful that a director could ask an audience to entertain it. The scene does not erase the fishing boat of 1954, just as the fishing boat does not forbid the scene. A popular monster has become large enough to hold both in his history.

So the Shōwa years did not produce one definitive Godzilla. They produced a working language for changing him: new enemies, new relationships, new audiences, new anxieties, new jokes, and the persistent pleasure of watching that immense silhouette appear again. The monster who first made people flee would return, over and over, to find people cheering.
