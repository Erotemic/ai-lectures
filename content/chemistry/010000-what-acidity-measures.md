# What Acidity Measures: Protons, Water, and Chemical Speciation

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

A lemon tastes sour. A solution used to remove mineral deposits may be acidic too. A lake can become more acidic even while remaining nowhere near the acidity of lemon juice. We describe all of these situations using one word, acidity, but the word can hide several different measurements. Does it mean how much acid was added? How readily a molecule donates a proton? How much acid a sample can absorb before its pH changes? Or how the hydrogen-ion activity compares with a standard reference? Chemistry answers these questions separately, and the distinctions explain everything from digestion to ocean water.

One important definition begins with the proton, the positively charged nucleus of ordinary hydrogen. In Brønsted–Lowry acid-base chemistry, an acid is a proton donor and a base is a proton acceptor. Picture an acid molecule called HA transferring a proton to water. The acid becomes A, carrying whatever charge follows from losing that proton, and a water molecule becomes hydronium, the water molecule with an extra proton. In aqueous chemistry, free bare protons are not generally wandering independently through the liquid. They are extensively solvated, and hydronium is our simplest written representation of that protonated water network.

After HA gives up a proton, the resulting A can accept a proton again. The pair is called a conjugate acid and conjugate base. It is a reversible relationship, not two unrelated categories of substance. Water can also participate as either donor or acceptor. When hydrochloric acid transfers a proton to water, the water is a base. When ammonia accepts a proton from water, the water acts as an acid, producing ammonium and hydroxide. A molecule's acid-base role depends on its reaction partner as well as on its own structure.

This leads to a deeper account of acidic strength. A strong acid, in the introductory aqueous sense, transfers its protons to water essentially completely over ordinary dilute-solution conditions. A weak acid establishes an appreciable equilibrium between its protonated and deprotonated forms. Weak does not mean harmless, and strong does not mean highly concentrated. A very dilute solution of a strong acid may contain less acid per liter than a concentrated solution of a weak acid. A concentrated weak acid can still be hazardous, and the chemical effects of any material also depend on other properties, not just its place on an acid strength chart.

Consider acetic acid, the acid characteristic of vinegar. Its molecules are capable of donating a proton to water, but at ordinary dilute concentrations a substantial fraction remain protonated. The mixture contains both acetic acid and acetate ions. If we add acetate, the equilibrium shifts in response to their relative activities. If we dilute the solution, the fraction dissociated may change even while the total acid concentration falls. That is why the phrase weak acid is a statement about an equilibrium tendency, not a direct count of hydronium ions in any particular bottle.

Water itself establishes an acid-base equilibrium. Two water molecules can exchange a proton so that one becomes hydronium and the other hydroxide. In pure water at twenty-five degrees Celsius, the activities of those two ions are approximately equal. The familiar water ion product, commonly written K W, is about ten to the minus fourteen in the standard concentration approximation at that temperature. The corresponding hydronium activity is about ten to the minus seven on the conventional scale. This is where the well-known neutral pH of approximately seven comes from. It is not because seven is a cosmic definition of neutrality.

The pH scale is logarithmic. More precisely, pH is defined using the negative base-ten logarithm of hydrogen-ion activity, represented in aqueous chemistry by solvated proton species. In dilute idealized solutions, concentration can approximate activity, which is why many introductory problems use hydronium molarity directly. A change of one pH unit corresponds to a factor of ten in the relevant activity, not an addition of one fixed concentration. A change of two units corresponds to a factor of one hundred. The scale compresses a large range of chemical conditions into manageable numbers.

Suppose one ideal dilute solution has a hydronium concentration of one thousandth of a mole per liter. Its pH is approximately three. Another has one ten-thousandth of a mole per liter, giving about pH four. The first has ten times the hydronium concentration of the second in that simplified comparison. But it does not necessarily contain ten times the total acid, and it is not necessarily ten times more corrosive. Total acid, buffer capacity, solvent composition, redox chemistry and surfaces all matter. pH reports a particular thermodynamic intensity, not every relevant property of a sample.

The difference between activity and concentration matters most when ions interact strongly. A charged particle in solution does not behave as though surrounded by empty space. Other ions and the solvent affect its chemical potential. The activity coefficient relates the actual thermodynamic activity to a convenient concentration scale. In dilute solutions it may approach the ideal approximation. In salty or concentrated mixtures, the approximation can be less reliable. This is especially important for seawater, concentrated industrial solutions, and carefully calibrated measurements. Chemists use conventions and standards because the activity of an individual ion is not independently measurable without additional assumptions.

Here is a second misconception: neutral water must always have pH seven. Neutrality in this context means equal acidic and basic ion activities under the relevant definition, not a fixed number written on the bottle. The water ion product changes with temperature. At temperatures other than twenty-five Celsius, the pH of neutral pure water changes as well. A neutral warm-water sample can have a pH somewhat below seven without being acidic relative to its own water equilibrium. The actual definition matters more than the memorized landmark.

Acid-base reactions can also involve several protons. Phosphoric acid has multiple ionizable protons, and their removal occurs through distinct equilibria. Carbonate chemistry is another important example. In natural water, dissolved carbon dioxide is connected to carbonic acid, bicarbonate, and carbonate ions. These species have different protonation states and their relative proportions change with pH, dissolved inorganic carbon, gas exchange, and the solution's chemical environment. The chemistry cannot be represented accurately by a single irreversible arrow saying carbon dioxide becomes acid.

Consider rainwater and a limestone landscape. Carbon dioxide in the water can participate in the formation of acidity that promotes dissolution of calcium carbonate. As carbonate dissolves, the water gains calcium and dissolved inorganic carbon species. If the water later loses carbon dioxide or the chemical balance changes, calcium carbonate may precipitate. This is part of the chemistry of caves, groundwater and many marine environments. The acidity and mineral saturation of the water are coupled; neither is determined by one dissolved substance in isolation.

The word alkalinity is also easy to misuse. In water chemistry, alkalinity is an acid-neutralizing capacity measured by titration to a specified endpoint and reported using a stated convention. It often reflects bicarbonate and carbonate species, but other bases may contribute. Two waters with the same pH can have very different alkalinities. One may resist added acid strongly; the other may shift in pH after a small addition. Conversely, two waters can have comparable alkalinity but different pH because their dissolved carbon dioxide and other equilibria differ. To understand an aquatic environment, pH and alkalinity supply complementary information.

This distinction matters when we discuss ocean acidification. When the ocean absorbs additional carbon dioxide, it changes the balance among dissolved carbon species and generally increases hydrogen-ion activity, lowering pH. The ocean remains alkaline on the usual pH scale, but becoming less alkaline in pH is still acidification relative to its earlier state. The same changes can reduce carbonate-ion availability and affect the saturation conditions of calcium carbonate minerals used by many organisms. The phrase does not require the ocean to cross pH seven to name an important chemical change.

Not all acid-base chemistry is proton exchange. In the broader Lewis description, an acid accepts an electron pair and a base donates an electron pair. This framework helps describe metal-ion complexes and reactions where no proton moves. The two definitions are not competitors with one universally wrong and the other right. They answer different organizational questions. A chemist specifies which framework is useful for the reacting system at hand.

How do we measure acidity rather than just describe it? Colored indicators change their molecular form with acid-base conditions and can therefore change color across a useful pH interval. A glass electrode measures an electrochemical response that must be calibrated against reference solutions and interpreted using a convention for pH. We may also titrate a sample with a reagent of known concentration to determine how much acid or base it contains, or how its chemistry responds to added acid. The measurements are related, but they do not produce interchangeable numbers.

One may be tempted to divide chemistry into acid on one side and base on the other. Real chemistry is more interesting. A single molecule can donate or accept protons; a single sample contains multiple protonation forms; a single pH number may conceal substantial stores of weak acids and bases waiting to react. The useful way to think is in terms of speciation: which chemical forms are present, in which proportions, under which temperature and solvent conditions, and how will those proportions change when the surroundings change?

Return to the lake. If its pH declines, we know something consequential about hydrogen-ion activity. To know why it declined and what will happen next, we also need information about dissolved carbon, mineral reactions, biological activity, buffering, and the transport of matter into and out of the water. Acidity is not a simple ingredient poured into a system. It is a property of a network of reversible chemical relationships. Learning to read that network turns a familiar word into an explanatory tool.

## Scientific companion: acid strength, pH and water

Brønsted–Lowry transfer: $\mathrm{HA+H_2O\rightleftharpoons A^-+H_3O^+}$. The conjugate pair is $HA/A^-$. Water autoionization: $2\mathrm{H_2O\rightleftharpoons H_3O^++OH^-}$.

At $25\ ^\circ\mathrm C$ in dilute idealized water,

$$K_w\approx 1.0\times10^{-14},\quad \mathrm{pH}=-\log_{10}a_{\mathrm{H^+}},\quad \mathrm{pH+pOH}\approx14.$$

Thermodynamically pH uses a conventionally assigned individual hydrogen-ion activity, not directly an ion count. Neutrality means equal acidic/basic activities under the appropriate conventions; neutral pure-water pH is temperature-dependent. The familiar pH range zero to fourteen is not a universal limit.

For a weak monoprotic acid in a sufficiently ideal, dilute approximation,

$$K_a\approx\frac{[\mathrm{H_3O^+}][\mathrm{A^-}]}{[\mathrm{HA}]},\qquad pK_a=-\log_{10}K_a.$$

As an illustrative calculation, $K_a=1.8\times10^{-5}$ and analytical concentration $C=0.10\ \mathrm M$ yield $[\mathrm{H_3O^+}]\approx\sqrt{K_a C}=1.34\times10^{-3}\ \mathrm M$, giving pH about $2.87$. Verify $x/C\approx1.3\%$ to justify the small-dissociation approximation. This is an **idealized weak-acid calculation**, not a measurement of vinegar.

In carbonate-bearing waters, $CO_2(aq)$, $H_2CO_3$, $HCO_3^-$ and $CO_3^{2-}$ are linked by equilibria and gas exchange. Common freshwater treatments bundle dissolved $CO_2$ and true carbonic acid into $H_2CO_3^*$; distinguish this lumped convention from molecular $H_2CO_3$. Alkalinity is an operational acid-neutralizing capacity, not another name for pH.
