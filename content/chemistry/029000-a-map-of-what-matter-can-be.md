# A Map of What Matter Can Be: Phase Diagrams and Chemical Equilibrium

At the summit of a mountain, a pot of water boils below the temperature at which the same pot boils beside the sea. In a pressure cooker, the water can become hotter before boiling. And carbon dioxide, the gas we breathe out, can be kept as a liquid in a pressurized cylinder, yet at ordinary atmospheric pressure a piece of dry ice passes directly into gas. These are not exceptions to the chemistry of each substance. They are consequences of the same rule: the stable form of matter depends on the conditions under which its particles are assembled.

A phase diagram is a map of those conditions. Unlike an ordinary map, it is not a picture of where something is located. It tells us which states can coexist, and where one state becomes more stable than another. Learning to read it is a way of connecting microscopic bonding to macroscopic changes we can observe.

First distinguish a phase from a chemical species. Ice and liquid water are made of the same molecules, but they have different structures and different physical properties. They are two phases. A beaker containing liquid water and water vapor has two phases, even if both are pure water. A uniformly mixed solution can be one phase despite containing many chemical species. Conversely, two immiscible liquids, such as water and an oil, can form two phases while both remain liquids. Calling something a solid, liquid, or gas is a useful beginning, not a complete inventory of the possible phases.

Why is there a preferred phase at a given temperature and pressure? Molecules do not choose the most tightly packed arrangement in every situation. In a liquid, interactions favor neighbors close enough to attract one another, but thermal motion permits many changing arrangements. In a solid, there may be a comparatively ordered structure. In a gas, molecules explore a vastly larger range of positions. Each arrangement comes with energetic and entropic consequences. At fixed temperature and pressure, the equilibrium state is the one with the lowest appropriate Gibbs energy. If two phases can coexist, the molar Gibbs energy of the substance, also called its chemical potential for a pure substance, is equal in both.

A phase boundary marks a change in which state is thermodynamically favored. Imagine a sample of pure water whose temperature we change gradually while holding pressure fixed. Below the equilibrium melting temperature, ice is thermodynamically favored over liquid water. Above it, the opposite is true. At the melting temperature, ice and liquid have equal chemical potential. Adding heat can convert ice to liquid without immediately raising the temperature of a two-phase sample. Energy is absorbed as latent heat, reorganizing the substance while the equilibrium temperature stays fixed at that pressure. Once the last ice has melted, continued heating raises the liquid's temperature again.

Boiling is governed by the same idea at the liquid-vapor boundary. A liquid always has some molecules escaping from its surface. In a closed vessel, molecules can also return from the vapor. At equilibrium, the vapor exerts a particular pressure determined by temperature. At a higher temperature, the equilibrium vapor pressure is generally higher. Boiling throughout the liquid begins when the vapor pressure can match the pressure exerted on the liquid, subject to conditions needed to form bubbles. Lower the surrounding pressure and this equality occurs at a lower temperature. Raise the pressure and the required temperature rises. That is why the boiling point is not a property of water alone; the surrounding pressure is part of the question.

Picture a simple graph. Temperature runs horizontally. Pressure runs vertically. Regions indicate where solid, liquid, and vapor are stable. Curves between regions show pairs of phases able to coexist. One curve separates solid and vapor. One separates solid and liquid. One separates liquid and vapor. The three curves meet at a triple point, where all three phases can coexist in equilibrium. For ordinary water the triple point lies near zero point zero one degrees Celsius and a pressure of roughly six hundred and twelve pascals, far below normal atmospheric pressure. That is a well-measured physical condition, not the temperature at which water must freeze in every environment.

There is a subtlety with that number. For many decades the triple point of water helped define the kelvin exactly. Since the 2019 revision of the International System of Units, the kelvin is defined through the Boltzmann constant. The water triple-point temperature is therefore an experimentally determined quantity rather than an exact defining number. You need not memorize the metrological history to read a phase diagram, but it is a reminder that a familiar reference temperature and a formal unit definition are different things.

The liquid-vapor curve ends at a critical point. Near that point, the distinction between ordinary liquid and vapor becomes progressively less pronounced. Above the critical temperature and pressure, there is no liquid-vapor coexistence line to cross. A supercritical fluid can have density and solvent properties that differ greatly from an ordinary dilute gas. Carbon dioxide is a useful example because its critical temperature is only a little above room temperature, around thirty-one degrees Celsius. Under suitable high-pressure conditions it can be used as a solvent in industrial extraction. Calling it a supercritical fluid tells us about its state, not that it is chemically a new substance.

There is also a revealing peculiarity in water's solid-liquid boundary. Most familiar substances become denser when they freeze, so pressure generally favors the solid and the melting temperature tends to rise with pressure. Ordinary ice is less dense than liquid water near its melting point. Pressure therefore tends to favor the denser liquid, and the solid-liquid coexistence curve for ordinary ice slopes the other way. This is one reason ice floats and is one of several consequences of hydrogen-bonded water structures. It does not mean ice skating is explained simply by pressure melting; frictional heating and the properties of the ice surface are also important in understanding sliding.

A phase diagram is a statement of equilibrium, however, and a real sample may not reach equilibrium promptly. A clean liquid can sometimes be cooled below its nominal freezing point without forming crystals. A vapor can temporarily exist above its saturation pressure before droplets form. The missing ingredient is often the formation of a stable nucleus of the new phase. We will return to that when we examine how crystals begin. The map tells you where equilibrium lies; it does not necessarily tell you how quickly you will arrive.

A second kind of phase diagram is more useful for alloys and mixtures. Instead of pressure and temperature, imagine a graph with temperature on the vertical axis and composition on the horizontal axis. At one end is pure metal A, at the other pure metal B. Between them, different compositions may favor a single solid solution, a liquid, or two distinct phases. Draw a horizontal line through a two-phase region at a particular temperature. The line meets the boundaries at the compositions of the two coexisting phases. The overall alloy composition lies somewhere between them. The relative amounts of the phases must account for that overall composition by mass balance.

Here is a numerical thought experiment. Suppose an alloy contains forty percent B overall. At a chosen temperature, equilibrium produces one phase containing twenty percent B and another containing sixty percent B. Half of the material in each phase would make the forty percent mixture: average the two phase compositions, and you obtain forty. If instead the alloy contained thirty percent B, the mass balance would require more of the B-poor phase and less of the B-rich phase. This calculation is the basis of the lever rule. It estimates phase amounts from equilibrium compositions; it is not a license to assume that a rapidly cooled alloy has already attained those amounts.

Mixtures can also have multiple liquid phases. A blend of components may be miscible at one temperature yet separate into two liquids at another. Some substances form compounds with particular stoichiometries or show several crystalline structures. When chemical reactions and multiple components are involved, the map becomes more intricate. Yet the logic survives: equal chemical potentials for exchanging components, mass balance, and a set of conditions that determine what phases can coexist.

Gibbs' phase rule summarizes one constraint on these maps. For an idealized nonreacting system under the standard assumptions, the number of independent intensive variables you may change while maintaining equilibrium is the number of components, minus the number of coexisting phases, plus two. For one component with three phases, that number is zero. A triple point is fixed, not an arbitrary area of temperature and pressure. For one component with two phases, one degree of freedom remains; the pressure and temperature follow a curve. This relationship helps us understand the shape of the diagram rather than merely memorize the terms.

What does a diagram leave out? It does not specify crystal size, dislocations, trapped impurities, porosity, or the cooling history. Two samples can have the same bulk composition, temperature, and pressure while showing different strengths or appearances because they formed their structures at different rates. A phase diagram also does not automatically predict a chemical reaction's speed, much as knowing a reaction is favorable does not tell you how fast a catalyst makes it proceed.

A practical way to read a diagram is to narrate a journey through it. Start with carbon dioxide at ordinary atmospheric pressure and at a temperature well below zero. As you increase temperature at that fixed low pressure, you cross the solid-vapor boundary: dry ice sublimes. You never cross the liquid region. Now instead imagine carbon dioxide confined at sufficiently high pressure. The path may enter a liquid region, and if temperature and pressure are raised beyond the critical point, it can enter the supercritical regime. The material is still carbon dioxide throughout. Which phases appear is determined by the path through pressure and temperature, not by a change in chemical formula.

A binary alloy diagram tells a similar story in a different coordinate system. At some compositions, a cooling alloy first begins to form solid while substantial liquid remains. The early solid may have a different composition from the remaining liquid. As solidification proceeds, the liquid becomes enriched in whatever components the growing solid rejects. If diffusion inside the solid is slow, those early composition differences may be retained as microsegregation. The final solid can therefore depart from a perfectly equilibrated phase-diagram calculation. When an engineer uses an equilibrium diagram to guide casting, the next question is what diffusion and mixing can realistically accomplish during cooling.

When you next hear that a material has a melting point, that a liquid boils at a certain temperature, or that an alloy has a particular phase, add the unspoken question: under what conditions, and at equilibrium or in practice? A phase diagram is powerful because it gives us the possible destinations. Understanding the actual material requires the journey as well: how it nucleated, grew, cooled, was worked, or was exposed to its surroundings.

## Scientific companion: how to read a phase map

**Pure-substance coexistence.** At fixed $T,p$, stable phases minimize $G$. Along a two-phase coexistence curve for a pure substance, $\mu^{(\alpha)}(T,p)=\mu^{(\beta)}(T,p)$. For a simple compressible substance the Clapeyron relation is

$$\frac{dp}{dT}=\frac{\Delta S}{\Delta V}=\frac{\Delta H}{T\,\Delta V}.$$

For melting of ordinary ice, $\Delta V=V_{liquid}-V_{ice}<0$ and $\Delta H_{fusion}>0$, so the local solid-liquid coexistence curve has negative $dp/dT$. Water's multiple ice polymorphs produce more complex behavior outside this region.

**Gibbs phase rule.** For a nonreacting system with $C$ independent components and $P$ phases at equilibrium, $F=C-P+2$. For one component, $P=1$ gives $F=2$, $P=2$ gives $F=1$, and $P=3$ gives $F=0$. Constraints or chemical reactions modify the count.

**Lever-rule example.** An idealized binary two-phase region contains $x_B^{\alpha}=0.20$, $x_B^{\beta}=0.60$, and total $x_B=0.30$. For phase fractions $f_\alpha+f_\beta=1$, $0.20 f_\alpha+0.60 f_\beta=0.30$, giving $f_\beta=0.25$, $f_\alpha=0.75$. Apply consistently to either mass or mole fractions, using the corresponding definition of composition. A cooling process may leave nonequilibrium fractions or segregation.

**Triple-point nuance.** Ordinary water has a triple-point temperature close to $273.16\ \mathrm{K}$ and a pressure near $611.657\ \mathrm{Pa}$ for a specified isotopic composition. Since 2019, its thermodynamic temperature is no longer an exact definition of the kelvin.
