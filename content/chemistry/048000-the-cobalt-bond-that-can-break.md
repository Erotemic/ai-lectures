# The Cobalt Bond That Can Break: Vitamin B12 and Radical Chemistry

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

There is a remarkable fact about a molecule best known as a vitamin. Vitamin B12 contains a cobalt atom bonded to carbon. One form of the cofactor can use that bond to launch an organic radical. Another form can use the cobalt-carbon connection to move a methyl group between molecules. It would be difficult to find a better demonstration of why a biological metal center cannot be understood from the name of the element alone.

B12 is often discussed as a dietary requirement, but here our subject is the chemistry it makes possible. The cobalt sits in an elaborate organic structure called a corrin ring, related in broad architecture to, but chemically different from, the porphyrin ring of heme. The molecule is called cobalamin, and changes at its axial coordination positions produce different active forms. Those forms do not simply store the same vitamin in differently labeled packaging. They participate in genuinely different reaction mechanisms.

In methylcobalamin, cobalt carries a methyl group, a carbon bonded to three hydrogens. The cofactor participates in methyl-transfer reactions, including the reaction catalyzed by methionine synthase that ultimately helps convert homocysteine to methionine. The enzyme coordinates the transfer of a one-carbon group through the B12 cofactor. This chemistry intersects with folate-dependent one-carbon metabolism. It is not enough for a cell to possess a carbon atom; the atom must be carried in a chemically usable form and transferred selectively to a particular acceptor.

In adenosylcobalamin, a different group occupies the cobalt-carbon position: a five-prime deoxyadenosyl group. Under the influence of its enzyme environment, the cobalt-carbon bond can break homolytically, meaning that the two bonding electrons are divided rather than both going to one product. This makes a carbon-centered radical. The radical can remove a hydrogen atom from the substrate, generating a substrate radical that undergoes a rearrangement before hydrogen is returned. The active cofactor is regenerated through a carefully staged series of steps.

Why go through the risk of radical chemistry? Many organic rearrangements are kinetically difficult by ordinary polar mechanisms. A radical intermediate can permit a migration or transformation that is otherwise strongly disfavored at the temperature and composition of a living cell. But radicals are also reactive. A free radical wandering through the cytoplasm can attack many possible targets. The enzyme supplies a cage, placing the radical beside the intended substrate, controlling timing, and limiting unwanted side reactions.

One of the classic examples is methylmalonyl-CoA mutase, important in the metabolism of certain fatty acids and amino acids. It catalyzes rearrangement of methylmalonyl-CoA into succinyl-CoA. The reaction changes the carbon skeleton rather than merely adding an oxygen or swapping a phosphate. Adenosylcobalamin provides radical chemistry that enables the rearrangement. The products enter metabolism through pathways that would otherwise have difficulty carrying out that structural change.

The word radical needs precision. It does not mean a molecule is necessarily explosive or unstable on any practical timescale. It means a species has an unpaired electron. Reactivity depends on where that electron resides, which bonds surround it, and how the environment stabilizes the species. Enzymes can generate and consume radical intermediates repeatedly with enormous selectivity. Radical chemistry is not the exception to ordered biological chemistry; it is one of its tools.

There are different ways to break a covalent bond. If both electrons in a bond go to one fragment, the process is heterolytic; if one goes to each fragment, it is homolytic. In ordinary organic chemistry, we meet heterolytic bond cleavage in many acid-base and nucleophilic reactions, and homolytic cleavage in radical pathways. Cobalamin can support both broad categories of chemical behavior in different derivatives. Its cobalt center is an electronically tunable junction between metal chemistry and carbon reaction mechanisms.

A natural question is why vitamin B12 is so structurally complex. Would a bare cobalt ion not be simpler? It would be simpler to draw, but it would not give the same control. The ring changes the electron density and oxidation-state preferences of cobalt. The upper axial ligand carries the functional group; the lower coordination environment and enzyme contacts tune reactivity. Nearby protein groups control substrate recognition and how the cofactor is handled during turnovers. The molecule's structure is part of the catalytic design, not decorative scaffolding.

B12 also provides a lesson about delivery. Human cells cannot synthesize the cobalamin scaffold from scratch. They depend on obtaining it from dietary and microbial sources, absorbing and transporting it, and converting it into the active forms needed in different compartments. Most animals depend ultimately on microbial production through ecological and food-web pathways. In humans, a disruption can arise from insufficient intake, impaired absorption, transport problems, or defects in intracellular cofactor processing. A molecule can be chemically present but unavailable to the enzyme that needs it.

This distinction matters when interpreting biochemical evidence. If a certain reaction slows, the enzyme might be damaged; its protein could be expressed at a low level; the required cobalamin form might fail to arrive; or one of its reactants might be depleted. Measuring total vitamin content alone does not distinguish those possibilities. Metabolite profiles and enzyme-specific measurements can sometimes reveal which part of the chemical network is affected. Clinical diagnosis involves additional medical evidence and should not be inferred from one reaction in a lecture.

Consider how an isotope experiment could distinguish methyl transfer from radical rearrangement. Put a recognizable carbon isotope into the methyl group of a donor and ask where it appears in the product. If the carbon passes through the cobalt-bound methyl of cobalamin into methionine, the label tracks a transported one-carbon unit. In a mutase reaction, by contrast, label particular carbons or hydrogens of the substrate and ask which atoms exchange positions during rearrangement. The cofactor assists a change within a carbon skeleton. The labels expose two mechanistically different operations that would be easy to blur if both were called vitamin B12 chemistry.

The hydrogen tracing can be particularly revealing. In a radical mutase, a deoxyadenosyl radical can remove a hydrogen atom from a specific substrate position. The substrate radical then rearranges and receives a hydrogen back. A deuterium label, replacing a hydrogen atom with its heavier isotope, can follow this transfer or change a measured reaction rate. But the label must be interpreted alongside the enzyme's overall catalytic cycle. If a slow conformational change precedes the chemical step, the observed rate may not report the hydrogen-transfer barrier alone.

This is also a place to distinguish a catalytic intermediate from a chemical product. The 5'-deoxyadenosyl radical is made and then normally consumed within the enzyme's cycle. Its presence does not mean free radicals are released as a useful final output. A good enzyme regenerates the cofactor for repeated turnover, so the carbon skeleton change occurs without stoichiometric consumption of cobalamin. If radical intermediates escape or irreversibly damage the cofactor, activity can fail; cofactor repair and replacement mechanisms become relevant.

Researchers learned about the cobalamin system through converging methods. Isolation and chemical analysis established its cobalt-containing composition. Structure determination revealed the corrin architecture and cobalt coordination. Spectroscopy followed cobalt states and radical intermediates. Isotope tracing established which hydrogen or carbon moved in a reaction. Enzyme kinetics and mutational work tested which protein contacts were essential. The mechanism was not deduced from a vitamin's biological importance; it was assembled from experiments that followed atoms and electrons.

B12-dependent chemistry is not restricted to the two familiar human enzymes. Microorganisms use cobalamin-related cofactors in a wider range of rearrangements, methyl transfers, and radical reactions. Some microbial enzymes combine B12-dependent chemistry with iron-sulfur clusters and other radical-generating systems. These partnerships reveal how metabolism mixes different catalytic motifs: one metal center may generate a reactive species, another may deliver an electron, and a protein environment aligns everything with a substrate.

There is a useful contrast with heme. Heme in hemoglobin reversibly binds oxygen through a coordinated iron center. B12 in an adenosylcobalamin enzyme can deliberately break a cobalt-carbon bond to launch a radical rearrangement. Both use elaborate tetrapyrrole-family organic frameworks around a transition metal, but the biological reactions differ sharply. The same broad principle produces very different chemical tasks: a protein chooses a metal, supplies ligands, tunes electron structure, and channels a particular reaction.

The next time vitamin B12 appears on a nutrition label, it may look like an ordinary constituent of food. Chemically, it represents a spectacular solution to a problem of molecular rearrangement and carbon transfer. A cell can ask cobalt to carry a methyl group or to release a tightly managed radical. Those tasks depend on the complete cofactor and enzyme, not on cobalt in isolation. The biology works because the chemistry is specific enough to be useful and controlled enough to be safe.

## Scientific companion: two catalytic roles for cobalamin

**Active forms.** Methylcobalamin supports methyl-transfer chemistry in methionine synthase. 5'-deoxyadenosylcobalamin (AdoCbl) supports radical rearrangement in methylmalonyl-CoA mutase. The carbon-cobalt bond has different mechanistic fates in the two pathways; simply naming cobalamin does not determine the reaction.

**Schematic radical steps.** $\mathrm{Co-C\rightarrow Co^{II}+C^\bullet}$ is a shorthand for homolytic cleavage in an adenosylcobalamin-dependent reaction, not a complete oxidation-state balance. The 5'-deoxyadenosyl radical abstracts a substrate hydrogen; a substrate radical rearranges; a hydrogen atom is returned and the cofactor is restored. Actual ligand, charge, and radical notation depend on the enzyme and intermediate.

**Atom-tracing check.** A methyl-transfer reaction moves a carbon group between substrates. A rearrangement shifts bonding within a substrate while retaining its atom inventory. Isotopic labeling can distinguish these possibilities, and kinetic isotope effects may help characterize hydrogen-atom transfer.

**Biological scope.** Cobalamin synthesis is performed by certain microorganisms; humans require external supply and dedicated uptake, processing, and trafficking systems. Human enzymes use methyl- and adenosylcobalamin in different compartments.
