# The Other Roads to Fixed Carbon: C4, CAM, and Microbial Autotrophy

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

The carbon in a tree, a blade of grass, and a deep-sea microbe may all have begun as carbon dioxide. But those organisms do not necessarily assemble organic carbon by the same route. The Calvin cycle is widespread, yet it is not nature's only strategy for fixing inorganic carbon. And even among plants that use the Calvin cycle, evolution has produced different ways of supplying carbon dioxide to its central enzyme. To understand why, we have to examine the tradeoffs between water, oxygen, energy, and the chemical machinery available to a cell.

Start with a challenge facing a leaf on a hot afternoon. The leaf needs carbon dioxide from the air, and carbon dioxide normally enters through pores called stomata. Open pores also allow water vapor to escape. Closing them saves water but restricts carbon dioxide supply. Rubisco, the enzyme fixing carbon in the Calvin cycle, then faces a less favorable ratio of carbon dioxide to oxygen. Its oxygenation reaction becomes more competitive, increasing the costs of photorespiration. A leaf can respond by changing stomatal behavior, internal conductance, metabolism, or several other features, but there is no cost-free solution.

C4 plants solve part of the problem by concentrating carbon dioxide spatially. In a common arrangement, one group of leaf cells initially captures inorganic carbon using an enzyme called PEP carboxylase. Rather than binding carbon dioxide directly to Rubisco's five-carbon acceptor, PEP carboxylase adds bicarbonate-derived carbon to a three-carbon compound, phosphoenolpyruvate, making a four-carbon compound such as oxaloacetate. That four-carbon skeleton may be converted into malate or aspartate and moved to another set of cells, often the bundle sheath, where carbon dioxide is released close to Rubisco.

The Calvin cycle then operates in the carbon dioxide-enriched compartment. Rubisco is not replaced; its working environment is changed. The arrangement can suppress the relative importance of oxygenation and allow water-efficient carbon gain in suitable conditions. But the concentration mechanism requires transport and regeneration of the original three-carbon carbon acceptor. That regeneration uses additional energy. The commonly quoted extra cost of the C4 concentrating cycle is on the order of two ATP equivalents per carbon dioxide delivered, although exact bookkeeping depends on biochemical subtype and conditions. A concentrating system that costs ATP can still be advantageous when it avoids a larger photorespiratory penalty.

This is an engineering tradeoff, not a declaration that C4 chemistry is universally superior. Under cool conditions, relatively high carbon dioxide, or different light and water constraints, the extra energy needed to concentrate carbon dioxide may outweigh the savings. Different groups of plants have evolved C4 photosynthesis independently. Familiar examples include maize and many warm-climate grasses. Their success does not mean that all plants should or can operate that way. The leaf's structure, its enzymes, its transport systems, and its environment must make the whole network work together.

CAM plants—named for Crassulacean acid metabolism—change the timing rather than primarily the location of the first fixation. Many CAM plants open stomata at night, when evaporative water loss can be lower. They use PEP carboxylase to incorporate carbon into four-carbon organic acids, often storing malate in vacuoles. During the day, stomata can remain more closed while stored organic acids are decarboxylated, releasing carbon dioxide for the Calvin cycle. The leaf effectively stores carbon dioxide overnight in chemical form and spends it during daylight.

This too has limitations. Vacuoles have finite storage capacity. The daily schedule of acid accumulation and release must be coordinated with light-driven ATP and NADPH supply. CAM metabolism can support remarkable water-use efficiency in dry environments, but it often trades away some potential rate of carbon gain. Species differ in how strongly they express CAM, and some vary the balance of night and day fixation with environmental conditions. The popular image of a cactus doing photosynthesis only at night is therefore mistaken. In typical CAM plants, initial carbon capture is emphasized at night, while the Calvin cycle draws on released carbon dioxide during daylight.

Now leave the leaf entirely. In many bacteria and archaea, carbon dioxide fixation is linked to chemical energy rather than sunlight. A microbe near an oxygen-poor sediment layer may derive electrons from hydrogen, reduced sulfur, or other donors. Its carbon-fixing pathway has to fit those electron sources, environmental conditions, and available enzymes. Nature has several known autotrophic carbon fixation routes, not just one alternative to the Calvin cycle.

One is the reductive tricarboxylic acid cycle, sometimes called the reverse TCA cycle. Where the familiar oxidative cycle tends to oxidize organic carbon and release carbon dioxide, the reductive version uses different, often specialized reactions to incorporate carbon dioxide while building organic intermediates. We should not imagine that a microbe simply spins an ordinary animal mitochondrial TCA cycle backward by wishing it so. Some oxidative-cycle reactions are too strongly driven under standard cellular operating conditions to reverse without different enzyme machinery and appropriate coupling. Reductive pathways need their own redox donors and energetic support.

A particularly striking alternative is the Wood–Ljungdahl pathway, or reductive acetyl-CoA pathway. In broad outline, two molecules of carbon dioxide contribute to the two-carbon acetyl group of acetyl CoA. One carbon follows a route that reduces carbon dioxide toward a methyl group carried by a one-carbon cofactor system. The other is reduced toward a carbon monoxide intermediate at a metal-containing enzyme center. An enzyme complex combines those carbon units with coenzyme A to make acetyl CoA. This is chemical construction at the scale of individual carbon atoms, using metal centers and cofactors in a way that connects directly to bioinorganic chemistry.

Some acetogenic bacteria use the Wood–Ljungdahl chemistry not only to assimilate carbon but also as part of their energy metabolism. Some methanogenic archaea employ related one-carbon chemistry. The detailed carriers and energetic coupling vary among lineages; it would be misleading to assign a single ATP cost or energy yield to every organism using the pathway. The important chemistry is that inorganic carbon can be reduced and joined through routes entirely different from Rubisco-based carboxylation.

Which route is best? That question has no answer independent of conditions. Oxygen can damage sensitive enzymes used by some anaerobic fixation systems. Available electron donors change the cost of reducing carbon. A plant is constrained by its need to exchange gases without losing too much water. A microbe in a sunless environment cannot simply run a light-powered chloroplast, but it may have access to hydrogen or reduced sulfur that a leaf does not. Different fixation networks reflect different balances among energy, electron availability, catalytic mechanism, and ecology.

How could we find out which route an organism actually uses? Genome sequences can reveal candidate enzymes, but genes only show biochemical potential. Enzyme assays test whether the machinery operates. Stable carbon isotope experiments reveal where newly introduced carbon enters metabolites. Measurements of carbon dioxide uptake, substrate consumption, electron donor oxidation, and product secretion constrain the overall accounting. Disrupting a key enzyme may alter the labeling pattern or growth conditions. Together, these observations can distinguish a proposed carbon fixation route from alternatives.

A tracer provides a particularly clear thought experiment. Imagine giving a microbe labeled carbon dioxide and rapidly measuring labeled carbon in acetyl CoA, sugar phosphates, and organic acids. Different routes place the incoming label into different molecular positions and do so in different sequences. But a single labeled metabolite cannot reveal a unique route, because pathways exchange and recycle carbon. The time course and the positional patterns matter. Good inference requires a network model that predicts those patterns, not just a colored picture of a pathway.

The deepest lesson is that autotrophy—building organic carbon from inorganic sources—is not one reaction or even one universal cycle. It is a chemical problem with multiple solutions. Carbon dioxide must be captured, supplied with electrons, joined into useful structures, and connected to the rest of metabolism. C4 and CAM plants modify the delivery system feeding Rubisco. Other organisms use different fixation chemistries altogether. Every solution must pay its own energetic bill, and every solution works only in an environment where that bill can be paid.

## Scientific companion: comparing carbon fixation strategies

| Strategy | Initial fixation | Key adaptation or condition | Critical caution |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| C3 / Calvin–Benson | Rubisco carboxylates RuBP | Broad photosynthetic carbon assimilation | Rubisco also oxygenates RuBP; photorespiration can be costly |
| C4 | PEP carboxylase captures bicarbonate-derived C; CO₂ later concentrated near Rubisco | Often spatial division among mesophyll and bundle-sheath cells | Additional ATP-equivalent cost; depends on subtype and conditions |
| CAM | PEP carboxylase often captures C at night; organic acids stored | Temporal separation of first fixation and Calvin cycle | Storage capacity and schedule constrain carbon throughput |
| Reductive TCA | Specialized reductive carboxylation reactions | Autotrophic microbes with compatible enzymes and reductants | Not a simple reversal of all canonical oxidative TCA reactions |
| Wood–Ljungdahl | Two CO₂-derived one-carbon units form acetyl-CoA | Many anaerobic bacteria/archaea | Exact carriers and energy conservation vary among organisms |

**C4 carbon count:** a 3-carbon PEP plus bicarbonate-derived carbon produces a 4-carbon oxaloacetate; after delivery and decarboxylation, a 3-carbon carbon skeleton is returned and PEP is regenerated. Net carbon gain is tied to subsequent Calvin-cycle fixation, not the mere transient production of a C4 acid.

**CAM timing:** Nocturnal PEP carboxylation and vacuolar malate storage can be separated from daytime malate decarboxylation and Rubisco fixation. Carbon storage is not the same as final carbohydrate production.

**Wood–Ljungdahl core:** two CO₂-derived carbons are incorporated into one acetyl-CoA carbon skeleton via methyl and carbonyl branches. The net energetics depend on the donor, acceptor and membrane-coupled metabolism; do not treat a gross stoichiometric sketch as a universal ATP yield.
