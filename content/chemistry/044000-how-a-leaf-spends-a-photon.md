# How a Leaf Spends a Photon: The Chemistry of Photosynthesis

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

A leaf looks like a surface that gathers sunlight. At the molecular scale it is a collection of reaction centers, pigments, membranes, catalysts and transport systems that turn photon energy into an organized flow of electrons. Photosynthesis is often reduced to an equation in which carbon dioxide and water become carbohydrate and oxygen. That equation is a useful net description, but it does not explain how light enters the chemistry. We will follow one part of the process in detail: how a series of electronic excitations drives reactions that are energetically difficult in the dark.

A pigment is a molecule that absorbs particular wavelengths of light. Chlorophylls contain extended electronic systems that absorb strongly in parts of the blue and red spectrum, leaving much green light less strongly absorbed by many leaves. The green appearance is not a claim that chlorophyll never absorbs green photons, or that every leaf uses exactly the same pigment. Plants and photosynthetic microorganisms employ varied pigments and structures adapted to their environments. At the level of one photon, the central event is familiar from photochemistry: a pigment molecule reaches an excited electronic state.

What can the pigment do with that excitation? It could relax as heat, emit fluorescence, or transfer energy to a nearby pigment. Photosynthetic organisms organize pigments into light-harvesting assemblies so that excitation energy can move through the system toward a reaction center. Not every transfer is a physical electron crossing from one pigment molecule to the next. Much of the energy-harvesting stage is excitation-energy transfer. That distinction matters because at the reaction center a different process begins: an electron is transferred from an excited donor to an acceptor, initiating a directional sequence of redox reactions.

The reaction center has to win against recombination. If the excited electron immediately returns to the original pigment, light absorption has not produced useful long-lived charge separation. The surrounding protein and pigment arrangement positions acceptors and donors at suitable distances and redox potentials so that successive transfers carry the charge away. Molecular architecture, not merely chlorophyll color, makes this possible. A leaf is doing device engineering with chemistry, protein structure and membranes.

In oxygen-producing photosynthesis, two linked reaction systems operate in series. They are called photosystem two and photosystem one, for historical reasons that do not match the order in which electrons pass through them. Electron flow begins at photosystem two, which uses light to oxidize water and transfers electrons through a membrane-associated chain. The electrons eventually reach photosystem one, where another photochemical excitation raises their energy for further reducing chemistry. This arrangement is often drawn as a zigzag of electron energy changes called the Z scheme. It is not a literal wire shaped like the letter Z; it is an energy diagram for a chemically organized sequence.

Why is extracting electrons from water such a demanding task? Water is a stable molecule. To produce oxygen from water, the oxidation process must remove four electrons from two water molecules and form an oxygen-oxygen bond, along with protons. Photosystem two contains a catalytic center with manganese, calcium and oxygen that accumulates oxidizing equivalents over several light-driven turnovers. The oxygen we breathe from ordinary oxygenic photosynthesis is formed from water at this catalytic process, not by stripping oxygen atoms from carbon dioxide during sugar synthesis.

Water oxidation happens stepwise within a protein environment. A single photon does not simply strike an intact water molecule and split it into a complete oxygen molecule. Photosystem two uses repeated excitations and electron-transfer chemistry. The reaction center regenerates its electron donor by withdrawing electrons, through a redox-active chain, from the water-oxidizing complex. This is a system for storing oxidizing power across several events until the chemistry needed for oxygen formation can occur.

Meanwhile, electrons travel through carriers associated with the thylakoid membrane, an internal membrane of the chloroplast in plants. Plastoquinone, a cytochrome complex and plastocyanin are parts of this transport route. Electron movement is coupled to changes in proton distribution across the membrane. Some protons are released into the thylakoid interior during water oxidation, and other transport steps contribute to building the electrochemical proton gradient. The result is not only a flow of electrons but a difference in proton chemical potential across a biological membrane.

That proton gradient powers ATP synthase, a molecular machine that couples proton movement to synthesis of ATP from ADP and phosphate. ATP is a widely useful energy-carrying molecule in cells. The gradient is a form of stored electrochemical free energy: protons can do work by flowing through an appropriate pathway. The chemistry resembles processes in cellular respiration, though the origin and direction of the driving electron transfers differ. Photosynthesis is not simply using light to 'create ATP'; it converts photon energy through redox steps and a membrane gradient into the chemical state of ATP.

At photosystem one, the electrons undergo another light-driven energetic lift. They ultimately can reduce NADP plus to NADPH through electron-transfer proteins and a reductase enzyme. NADPH is a reducing agent, a carrier of electrons with useful chemical potential for later biosynthesis. ATP and NADPH then support carbon fixation through enzymatic cycles in the chloroplast. The Calvin–Benson cycle incorporates carbon dioxide into organic molecules, but its individual reactions do not require a photon to hit each carbon-fixing enzyme. Calling these reactions dark reactions is historically common and potentially misleading: in living plants they are regulated and typically depend on products of the illuminated phase, so they need not operate freely for hours in darkness.

Now we can interpret the familiar overall photosynthesis equation without hiding its internal labor. Carbon is reduced from carbon dioxide into organic material. Water provides electrons for this reduction and is oxidized to oxygen. Light supplies the energy that makes the coupled process possible. ATP and NADPH help connect the light reactions to carbon metabolism. Many atoms and electrons pass through intermediate molecules before any sugar accumulates. The net formula is a summary of a network, not a single collision between sunlight, water and carbon dioxide.

A photon count adds another correction to oversimplified accounts. Oxidizing two water molecules to one oxygen molecule requires four electron removals. In linear electron flow, each of the four electrons passes through both photosystems, each involving photochemical excitation. A widely taught idealized minimum is therefore eight absorbed reaction-center photons for one molecule of oxygen under the basic linear scheme. Real biological energy budgets require additional excitation because of losses, cyclic electron flow, energy-balancing requirements and other processes. The simple number is a stoichiometric teaching model, not a universal quantum yield measured in every leaf.

What happens under too much light? The input of excitations may exceed what downstream chemistry can use. Long-lived excited states and reactive oxygen species can damage proteins, pigments and membranes. Plants have photoprotective processes that dissipate excess energy, redistribute excitation and repair light-damaged components. Deliberately throwing away some photon energy can protect the larger chemical machine and improve survival. The most effective biological system is not necessarily the one that converts every absorbed photon into stored carbon with no other considerations.

Different organisms solve the light problem differently. Some bacteria perform photosynthesis without producing oxygen, using other electron donors. Some plants alter the timing or spatial arrangement of carbon fixation to cope with water scarcity or high temperature. Pigment composition and leaf architecture change with habitat. These adaptations remind us that there is no single one-step photosynthesis mechanism covering every photosynthetic organism. Our detailed account concerns oxygenic photosynthesis, especially the familiar plant and algal pathway.

How can we investigate a process whose key excited states vanish rapidly? Measure chlorophyll fluorescence and its changes under different conditions. Follow oxygen evolution, electron-transport indicators and carbon exchange. Use spectroscopic methods to observe transient charge-separated states or redox changes. Structural studies can locate pigment and metal centers, and genetic or biochemical interventions can test which protein complexes are necessary. Each measurement answers a different question: fluorescence can report competition among pathways without directly measuring sugar production, and oxygen evolution does not by itself specify the rate of every downstream carbon-fixation step.

A solar cell and a green leaf share a conceptual beginning. Both absorb photons, create excited electronic states and try to separate useful charge before recombination. But a solar cell directs that separation toward an electrical load. A leaf directs it through catalytic redox chemistry, a proton gradient and carbon assimilation. Neither device is magic, and neither can be understood from a single band gap or pigment color. What matters is the whole carefully constructed path by which an excitation becomes work.

The leaf is therefore not merely a light collector. It is a chemical factory with a sophisticated energy supply chain. Photons excite pigments. Energy funnels toward reaction centers. Electrons travel through a sequence of redox partners. Water is oxidized. Protons drive ATP synthesis. Reducing power enters carbon metabolism. We can admire the result without collapsing those distinct steps into the word photosynthesis. The science is in how the steps are coupled, and how fragile excited states are turned into stable biological chemistry.

## Scientific companion: redox accounting in oxygenic photosynthesis

A conventional idealized *net* equation for oxygenic carbohydrate production is $6\mathrm{CO_2}+6\mathrm{H_2O}\rightarrow\mathrm{C_6H_{12}O_6}+6\mathrm{O_2}$, but this conceals water balance and biochemical intermediates. Writing a more explicit water balance yields $6\mathrm{CO_2}+12\mathrm{H_2O}\rightarrow\mathrm{C_6H_{12}O_6}+6\mathrm{O_2}+6\mathrm{H_2O}$. Neither expression describes a one-step mechanism or the actual composition of all photosynthetic products.

Water oxidation half-reaction: $2\mathrm{H_2O}\rightarrow\mathrm{O_2}+4\mathrm{H^+}+4e^-$. Linear electron flow through PSII and PSI uses two excitation events per transferred electron in the idealized accounting, for a teaching minimum of eight photochemical excitations per evolved $\mathrm{O_2}$. Real absorbed-photon demand and carbon fixation efficiency vary with physiological conditions and measurement definitions.

**Functional distinction.** Antenna-pigment excitation transfer is not the same event as reaction-center charge separation. Both are distinct from transport of electrons through the thylakoid chain. ATP synthase converts proton-motive force into ATP; NADP reductase forms NADPH; carbon assimilation consumes reducing equivalents and ATP in a multi-enzyme network.
