# Forcing Chemistry with Electricity: Electrolysis, Batteries, and Selectivity

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

A galvanic cell can let a favorable chemical reaction push electrons through a wire. What happens if we connect an external power source and push the electrons the other way? Chemistry can become the recipient of electrical work. We can deposit a metal from a solution, split water into hydrogen and oxygen, or charge a rechargeable battery. These processes belong to electrolysis: chemical transformations driven by externally supplied electrical energy. The underlying accounting of atoms and electrons has not changed, but the direction of energy transfer has.

Start with water. Under ordinary surface conditions, a container of liquid water does not continuously split itself into large amounts of hydrogen and oxygen gas. The net conversion of water into those gases requires free-energy input. If an electrical circuit supplies work through properly chosen electrodes and an ion-conducting medium, the relevant oxidation and reduction reactions can occur. At one electrode, water or hydrogen-containing species are reduced to hydrogen gas. At the other, oxygen-containing species are oxidized to oxygen gas. The exact half-reactions depend on whether the medium is acidic or alkaline, but the overall balanced change is two water molecules becoming two hydrogen molecules and one oxygen molecule.

We have to keep the names of the electrodes straight. The cathode is always the electrode where reduction takes place. The anode is always where oxidation takes place. In an electrolytic cell operated by an external power supply, the cathode is normally connected to the negative side of the supply and the anode to its positive side. In a spontaneously discharging galvanic cell, the anode is the negative electrode and the cathode the positive electrode. The difference does not reflect a change in the meaning of the words. It reflects whether chemical free energy is producing electrical work or electrical work is being supplied to drive the chemistry.

Why does a connected power source make a difference? At a given composition, the desired nonspontaneous reaction has a positive Gibbs-energy change. Electrical work can compensate for that cost. In the ideal reversible limit, the minimum energy required for a specified chemical transformation is related to its Gibbs-energy change. For water splitting under conventional standard-state conditions near room temperature, the reversible electrical potential associated with the overall reaction is roughly one point two three volts. But that is a thermodynamic reference value, not a promise that a practical device will produce useful quantities of gas at exactly that voltage.

Actual electrolysis requires current, and current introduces losses. Charges encounter resistance in conductors and electrolytes. Reaction rates at electrode surfaces require finite driving forces beyond the reversible potentials, often called overpotentials. Gas bubbles can cover active sites and obstruct transport. Membranes and separators permit certain ions to move while restricting mixing of products, but they also add resistance. Electrode materials can corrode, change state or form unwanted products. Energy efficiency therefore depends on current density, electrode chemistry, cell geometry, temperature, pressure, separation systems and other details.

A metal-plating bath gives a simpler electron ledger. Suppose a solution contains copper ions with charge plus two, and the cathode reaction deposits each ion as a neutral copper atom. Each deposited copper atom has accepted two electrons. The amount of copper that can be deposited is therefore proportional to the total electric charge passed through the cathode, provided the charge is used for that reaction. This is the basic content of Faraday's laws of electrolysis. One mole of electrons corresponds to about ninety-six thousand four hundred eighty-five coulombs of charge. Two moles of electrons can, in the ideal limit, deposit one mole of copper from copper two-plus ions.

Now give the process a clock. Suppose a current of two amperes runs for thirty minutes. An ampere is one coulomb per second, so the delivered charge is three thousand six hundred coulombs. Divide by Faraday's constant to find about thirty-seven thousandths of a mole of electrons. Because each copper ion requires two electrons, the maximum amount of copper is about nineteen thousandths of a mole, or around one point two grams. This number assumes that all the charge contributes to copper deposition. In practice, current may also drive hydrogen evolution or other competing reactions. The fraction of charge producing the desired material is called the Faradaic, or coulombic, efficiency for that product.

There are two distinct efficiencies to keep separate. One measures the fraction of charge making the intended chemical product. Another concerns how much energy we had to supply per amount of useful product. A cell could have high Faradaic efficiency but poor energy efficiency because it needs a large overpotential or has large resistive losses. Conversely a cell might run at relatively low voltage but divert significant current into an unwanted reaction. Describing an electrochemical technology as efficient requires saying efficient in which sense, under what operating conditions, and with what balance of material and energy.

Competition among possible reactions is a recurring feature of electrolysis. A cathode in aqueous solution may reduce dissolved metal ions, but it may also reduce water or solvated protons and evolve hydrogen. Which reaction dominates is influenced by their thermodynamic potentials, concentrations, surface kinetics and electrode material. At an anode, water oxidation may compete with oxidation of dissolved species. Standard potentials provide a useful starting map, but they do not predict the product distribution by themselves. Electrochemical selectivity is a kinetic question superimposed on a thermodynamic one.

This distinction becomes clear when comparing molten salts with aqueous solutions. In a molten salt, there is no water competing for electron transfer. In an aqueous salt solution, water is present at high abundance and can participate at either electrode. The products can therefore be different even when the salt's nominal ions are the same. We must not conclude from a table of reduction potentials alone that every ion present will be reduced or oxidized in the obvious numerical order. Activities, overpotentials, electrode surfaces and side chemistry complicate the actual outcome.

An electrolysis apparatus also has a materials problem. The anode may be subjected to strongly oxidizing conditions. If it dissolves or forms a passivating oxide layer, its behavior changes. A cathode may become covered by a metal deposit or gas bubbles. The composition near an electrode surface can differ sharply from the bulk solution because reactions consume and create species faster than diffusion and mixing replenish them. Local pH can differ from the beaker-average pH. A reaction that looks desirable in bulk equilibrium calculations may encounter conditions at the surface that promote an entirely different pathway.

Recharging a battery is an especially interesting form of driven electrochemistry. During discharge, a cell delivers work as its electrode materials transform. During charge, a power source pushes electrons in the reverse direction and drives reactions that move the battery toward a more energy-rich state. But the reverse of the net equation is not necessarily the exact reverse of every microscopic event. Some structural changes may be only partly reversible; some dissolved species may migrate to undesirable places; some side reactions consume electrolyte or build resistive films. These changes help explain why rechargeability has limits even when the cell's main reaction appears reversible on paper.

Consider a lithium-ion battery in conceptual terms. During operation, lithium ions move through an electrolyte between host materials while electrons travel through the external circuit. Charging reverses the useful direction of lithium-ion movement and electron transfer. The host materials store different chemical potentials of lithium, and that difference contributes to cell voltage. The battery is not a box full of free electrons waiting to escape, and lithium ions do not move through the external wire. Its energy resides in the electrochemical state of the materials and their arrangement. The details vary substantially across electrode chemistries, so one cartoon cannot describe every lithium battery.

The same bookkeeping explains why a battery can be damaged by excessive charging or rapid charging. If the intended intercalation or other storage reaction cannot accept charge fast enough, competing reactions may occur. Temperature, local concentration gradients, electrode potential, and material structure all influence the margin between useful charge storage and unwanted chemistry. Good battery management is therefore partly a problem of predicting the chemical state of a device from its electrical measurements. Voltage by itself does not tell us exactly how much charge remains; current history, temperature and aging complicate the inference.

Electrolysis is sometimes described as the reverse of a battery, but the distinction is operational rather than a complete taxonomy of devices. An electrolyzer is usually designed to make and collect a chemical product using electricity. A rechargeable battery is designed to store energy and later release it electrically. A fuel cell is supplied continuously with reactants from outside, converting chemical energy into electrical work as products leave. These designs can share half-reaction principles while differing fundamentally in material flow, energy storage, and engineering goals.

Historically, the relationship between chemistry and electricity became apparent soon after Volta's pile gave experimenters a sustained source of current. In 1800, William Nicholson and Anthony Carlisle used a voltaic apparatus to investigate the decomposition of water. During the nineteenth century, Faraday's quantitative investigations connected the amount of chemical change at electrodes with the amount of electric charge transferred. The unit called the faraday, and Faraday's constant used today, preserve that connection between particle-scale electron accounting and measurable electrical current.

Our discussion has focused on the theoretical and chemical principles rather than on a particular manufacturing recipe. The same equations nevertheless govern many very different technologies. Electroplating changes a surface. Water splitting makes gaseous chemical fuels and oxidants. Industrial electrolysis produces chemicals that may have no practical non-electrical synthesis route. Electrochemical sensors use potential or current to infer composition. The engineering constraints vary, but each application balances free energy, electron accounting, ion transport, kinetics, and selectivity.

We can now put chemical equilibrium, kinetics and electrochemistry on one map. Thermodynamics tells us the energetic difference between chemical states at given conditions. Kinetics tells us what paths are available and how fast reactions progress. Electrical work lets us alter which paths can be driven in a chosen direction. An electrode and wire do not abolish the rules that govern reactions in a beaker. They give us additional control over the exchange of energy and charge. That is why electrochemistry can both explain a battery lighting a lamp and make it possible to use the lamp's electrical supply to create a different chemical substance.

## Scientific companion: electrical charge versus chemical yield

For constant current $I$ over time $t$, $Q=It$. For a target electrode reaction involving $z$ electrons per molecule of product,

$$n_{\mathrm{product}}=\frac{\eta_F Q}{zF},\qquad F\approx96485\ \mathrm{C\ mol^{-1}},$$

where $\eta_F$ is the Faradaic efficiency for that specific product. For $\mathrm{Cu^{2+}+2e^-\rightarrow Cu(s)}$, $z=2$.

**Worked plating example:** $I=2.00$ A for $t=30.0$ min gives $Q=3600$ C. Assuming $\eta_F=1$, $n_{e^-}=0.03731$ mol, $n_{\rm Cu}=0.01866$ mol, and $m_{\rm Cu}\approx1.19$ g using $M_{\rm Cu}=63.55$ g/mol. This ideal yield is an **upper limit under the stated assumptions**, not a production guarantee.

For water electrolysis, net reaction $2\mathrm{H_2O(l)\rightarrow 2H_2(g)+O_2(g)}$ involves four electrons for two molecules of hydrogen. Under the conventional standard-state assumptions near $298\ \mathrm K$, the minimum *reversible electrical* cell voltage is about $1.23$ V. Operating voltages at useful currents must account for electrode overpotentials, electrolyte and membrane resistances, transport limits, and system-level requirements. The overall thermoneutral voltage and the voltage required under load are different ideas.

### Electrochemical roles

- **Cathode:** reduction, independent of the sign of the terminal.
- **Anode:** oxidation, independent of the sign of the terminal.
- **Galvanic discharge:** spontaneous chemistry produces electrical work; anode generally negative and cathode positive.
- **Electrolysis:** external electrical work drives an otherwise unfavorable direction; anode generally positive and cathode negative.
- **Faradaic efficiency:** fraction of total charge used by the specified electrode product; not identical to total energy efficiency.
