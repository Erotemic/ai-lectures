# A Reaction That Borrows a Photon: Photoredox Chemistry and Catalysis

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

Imagine a reaction mixture containing two molecular compounds that hardly react when combined in the dark. Shine the right color of light onto it, and a new product begins to form. The lamp has not added a new element to the mixture. Nor has it merely warmed the solution as a hot plate might. In many such reactions, a light-absorbing species temporarily changes its ability to give or take electrons, opening a reaction pathway that was inaccessible or very slow in its ordinary ground state.

This is the promise of photoredox chemistry: using photon-driven electron transfer to change chemical reactivity. The word redox refers to oxidation and reduction. Oxidation removes electrons from a species; reduction adds them. In a conventional electrochemical cell, an external voltage or a favorable chemical reaction can drive electron transfer. In a photoredox reaction, the absorbing molecule or material can use excitation energy to become a stronger electron donor or acceptor than it was before illumination. The light changes the energetics of a particular step.

A useful cast of characters contains a photosensitizer or photocatalyst, a substrate, and possible electron donors or acceptors. Suppose a colored catalyst absorbs a photon and enters an excited state. It might transfer an electron to a substrate, creating a reduced substrate radical or radical ion. Or it might accept an electron, producing an oxidized substrate radical ion. Subsequent steps can form bonds, break bonds or rearrange atoms. If later electron-transfer steps regenerate the original catalyst, the catalyst can operate repeatedly rather than being consumed in stoichiometric proportion to product.

What makes a radical special? A radical contains an unpaired electron. That often changes which bonds can form and which reaction barriers matter. In a traditional polar organic mechanism we follow pairs of electrons from a nucleophile toward an electrophile. In radical chemistry, single-electron pathways can lead to intermediate species and product distributions that a simple two-electron curved-arrow picture does not capture. Neither class is inherently more selective. Reaction conditions determine whether radical addition, abstraction, fragmentation or recombination dominates.

A thought experiment clarifies the catalytic cycle. Call the light-absorbing catalyst C. Light excites C into C-star. C-star transfers an electron to molecule A, converting A into a reactive reduced species while C becomes oxidized. A separate donor B then reduces the oxidized catalyst back to its initial state. The overall reaction consumes or transforms chemical reagents, and the light provides energy that enables part of the sequence. C can participate again if it remains intact. Without the donor or a corresponding way to close the redox cycle, the catalyst may become trapped in a different oxidation state or decomposed.

There is an important thermodynamic limit. A photocatalyst cannot make matter or energy from nothing. If the overall reaction stores free energy, that energy must ultimately come from absorbed light or from another source supplied to the system. Sometimes light merely helps cross a barrier while an overall downhill chemical transformation provides the energetic driving force. In other cases a sacrificial donor or acceptor supplies chemical free energy and is consumed. A report that a reaction occurs under visible light does not establish that sunlight alone powers all the chemistry. The net chemical equation and photon balance are essential.

Photocatalysis is broader than molecular photoredox chemistry. A semiconductor particle can absorb a photon and generate an electron-hole pair. The electron may drive a reduction reaction at one surface site and the hole may drive an oxidation at another. For a useful overall transformation, both sides must proceed without rapid recombination and without destructively altering the catalyst. This is related to a solar cell, but with a different destination for the excited carriers. A solar cell tries to extract them through an electrical circuit; a photocatalyst tries to spend them in surface chemistry.

Consider an often-proposed application: splitting water into hydrogen and oxygen using light. Making fuel from water requires an energy input. The overall chemical reaction must supply four electrons' worth of oxidation to produce one oxygen molecule while corresponding reduction chemistry produces hydrogen. Photons alone are not a guarantee of working water splitting. The semiconductor's energy levels, reaction kinetics, catalytic surfaces, ion transport, competing reactions and material stability all have to line up. Some experiments use sacrificial reagents that are consumed; those demonstrate particular half-reactions but should not automatically be described as complete unbiased water splitting.

A different photochemical route uses energy transfer instead of electron transfer. A photosensitizer can absorb light and transfer excitation energy to another species, such as molecular oxygen. Under suitable circumstances, this produces singlet oxygen, a more reactive electronic state of oxygen than the common ground-state triplet. Singlet oxygen can react with organic substrates through pathways that differ from ordinary thermal oxidation. This can be useful for controlled chemical reactions and can also damage biological tissues. Calling a reaction light-driven does not tell us whether electrons, excitation energy, heat or some combination is transferred.

How do we know which mechanism occurred? The most convincing case does not rest on the observation that a colored mixture made product under a lamp. Investigators measure absorption of the catalyst, identify short-lived intermediate species when possible, examine product distributions and change the identities of donors and acceptors. Time-resolved spectroscopy can reveal the excited-state lifetime and whether a substrate quenches it. Electrochemical potentials help test which electron-transfer steps are plausible. Product isotope patterns or radical traps may provide additional clues, although a trap that changes the reaction can itself create interpretive ambiguity.

An action spectrum offers a particularly useful test. Vary illumination wavelength while keeping the absorbed photon count under control. If the reaction is initiated by a particular chromophore, its wavelength-dependent effectiveness should be related to that chromophore's absorption, once the quantum yield and other effects are considered. A reaction proceeding better under a brighter lamp at one wavelength than under a dimmer lamp at another is weak evidence until the photon flux and absorption are accounted for. Illumination geometry, temperature and penetration depth become experimental variables.

Suppose our reactor consumes one mole of substrate every hour. How many photons did it actually absorb during that hour? What fraction reached the catalyst rather than being scattered or absorbed by products? How many molecules transformed per absorbed photon? The photochemical quantum yield and photonic efficiency address different questions, with definitions that must be stated. A chain reaction may transform several substrate molecules after one photo-initiation event, while a reaction with extensive recombination may require many absorbed photons per useful product. Energy efficiency adds still another layer because photons of different wavelengths carry different energies.

Scale matters. In a tiny transparent vial, light may reach nearly the whole solution. In a large dense reactor, photons can be absorbed near the wall, leaving deeper material under-illuminated. Stirring and transport change where reactive intermediates encounter substrate. A reaction that performs beautifully in a thin experimental tube may not work the same way in a deep vessel. Reactor geometry, light distribution, flow and optical path length are as much part of process development as choosing a catalyst molecule.

A good photochemical experiment also includes dark controls, temperature-matched controls, catalyst-free controls and measurements of chemical products rather than color alone. If the reaction continues after the light is switched off, that can indicate a stored intermediate or a radical chain; it does not automatically falsify photochemical initiation. If product appears in the dark as well, the illumination may accelerate an ordinary reaction rather than enable a distinct pathway. Mechanistic claims are strongest when several independent observations support the same account.

What makes photoredox chemistry exciting is not that it invalidates thermodynamics. It offers a way of changing the reaction landscape for a short time. Light prepares a reagent or catalyst in an electronically excited state with different redox properties, and chemists arrange the surrounding molecules so that useful chemistry wins the race against deactivation. The resulting reaction may build a carbon bond, activate a small molecule, or transform a contaminant. In every case, the photon is one participant in a complete chemical economy of electrons, energy, intermediates and products.

## Scientific companion: a photoredox cycle and an energy ledger

Schematic oxidative-quenching cycle: $\mathrm{PC}+h\nu\rightarrow\mathrm{PC}^{*}$; $\mathrm{PC}^{*}+\mathrm{A}\rightarrow\mathrm{PC}^{+}+\mathrm{A}^{-}$; $\mathrm{PC}^{+}+\mathrm{D}\rightarrow\mathrm{PC}+\mathrm{D}^{+}$. Actual states and charge notation depend on the specific catalyst and reagents. The overall net reaction must account for the donor and products. A distinct energy-transfer pathway may occur without transfer of an electron between the sensitizer and substrate.

**Photon accounting.** If monochromatic illumination delivers photon flux $N_{\mathrm{inc}}$ and an absorber takes up a fraction $f_{\mathrm{abs}}$, the absorbed rate is $N_{\mathrm{abs}}=f_{\mathrm{abs}}N_{\mathrm{inc}}$. A measured chemical quantum yield is $\Phi=N_{\mathrm{events}}/N_{\mathrm{abs}}$ using a clearly defined reaction event. A value above one can reflect chain chemistry, not violation of energy conservation; free-energy accounting still includes all reagents and illumination.

**Worked example.** A reactor absorbs $8\times10^{17}$ photons per second and forms $2\times10^{17}$ product molecules per second. Its reaction quantum yield is $0.25$ product molecules per absorbed photon. If the reaction proceeds through a radical chain of length four with initiation yield below one, the net chemical quantum yield can be different from the probability that an excited sensitizer produces a radical.

For proposed complete water splitting, $2\mathrm{H_2O}\rightarrow2\mathrm{H_2}+\mathrm{O_2}$; both gas products, electron balance and input energetics must be tracked. A sacrificial-donor hydrogen-evolution half-reaction is not this full reaction.
