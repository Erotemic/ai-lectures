# Three Questions Before a Reaction: Selectivity in Organic Synthesis

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

A chemist draws an arrow from a starting molecule to a product. The arrow looks singular. It can conceal a hundred alternatives. If a molecule has an alcohol and a ketone, a reagent might react with either, or both. If there are two apparently similar carbon atoms, a new bond might form at either position. If a new bond creates a three-dimensional arrangement, the product may emerge in two mirror-image forms, or in several other configurations. The synthetic challenge is therefore not merely to make a reaction possible. It is to persuade a particular reaction to happen in preference to its competitors.

This is the central problem of selectivity. It is also a useful way to organize almost every choice that follows in a multistep synthesis. The first question is, which *kind* of group reacts? The second is, *where* within the molecule does the event occur? The third is, *which spatial arrangement* does it produce? Chemists call these chemoselectivity, regioselectivity, and stereoselectivity. They are distinct questions, and a synthesis can answer one perfectly while failing badly on another.

Consider a molecule that contains both an aldehyde and an alkene. We want to turn the aldehyde into an alcohol without destroying the carbon-carbon double bond. A reducing agent that delivers hydride to the polarized carbonyl carbon may do that. Sodium borohydride is often useful for reducing aldehydes and ketones while leaving ordinary isolated carbon-carbon double bonds unchanged. Hydrogen gas over a suitable metal catalyst, by contrast, might reduce the alkene as well, depending on catalyst and conditions. A reagent is not a magical instruction that reads the desired product. Its chemistry makes particular reactive sites accessible.

Even a phrase such as “reduce the aldehyde” understates the planning problem. What other groups are present? Will a free acid quench a strongly basic reagent? Does the solvent react with the reagent? Will the reaction stop after one reduction? Can the product be separated from starting material? Chemoselectivity must be judged against an actual molecule, not a memorized list of reagents. A condition that is selective in one substrate can be much less selective in another because of nearby groups, electronic effects, and competing pathways.

Regioselectivity begins when several positions can undergo the same type of transformation. Imagine adding an electrophile to an unsymmetrical alkene. Two constitutional products may be possible, depending on which end receives which part of the reagent. Or imagine substituting another group onto a benzene ring that already bears a substituent. The positions adjacent to it, opposite it, or separated by one ring carbon may have different reactivities. Those positions are called ortho, para, and meta. A reaction can be cleanly selective for *aromatic substitution* yet give an inconvenient mixture of positions.

Regioselectivity is measured by products, not only by our preferred drawing. If one product represents eighty percent of the relevant products and another twenty percent, the reaction favors the first pathway four to one. But if both substances are difficult to separate, four to one may be disappointing. If they crystallize differently, it may be acceptable. The usefulness of a selectivity depends on what follows. A reaction delivering ninety-nine percent of the wrong spatial isomer is exceptionally selective and exceptionally unhelpful.

Stereoselectivity asks which three-dimensional product predominates. In a planar carbonyl group, a nucleophile can often approach from either face. If those two faces are equivalent in an achiral environment, the products may be mirror images formed in equal amounts. A chiral catalyst or chiral group near the site can make the approaches unequal. For cyclic reactions, new substituents may end up on the same or opposite faces of a ring. These are different spatial outcomes even if the connections between atoms are identical.

There is a further distinction between stereoselective and stereospecific. In a stereoselective process, one stereoisomer forms more readily than another from a given starting material. In a stereospecific process, the stereochemical character of the starting material directly constrains which product stereochemistry can arise through the mechanism. An S N two substitution at a defined stereogenic carbon, when it proceeds without competing pathways, involves backside attack and inversion at that center. That mechanistic relationship is not interchangeable with saying a catalyst simply prefers one face.

How do these preferences emerge? They reflect differences between competing transition states, the high-energy arrangements through which bonds change. One pathway may require a nucleophile to squeeze past a bulky group. Another may allow a favorable interaction with a polarized bond. Another may preserve conjugation or avoid a repulsive alignment. The difference in reaction rates can be large even when the competing transition states differ in free energy by only a few kilojoules per mole.

Here is a concrete scale. At room temperature, a difference in activation free energy of about five point seven kilojoules per mole corresponds, roughly, to a tenfold rate preference under a simple kinetic model. Tenfold sounds large, yet it gives an approximately ninety-one to nine product ratio if the products form through parallel, irreversible routes with equal opportunity. That might be easy to purify—or not. Increasing selectivity is often less about overwhelming chemical forces than about creating a modest energy difference at the decisive step.

The temperature matters because selectivity is linked to the competition of rates. The solvent matters because it may stabilize charged intermediates or bind a catalyst. Concentration matters when one pathway involves two molecules meeting and another proceeds within one molecule. Time matters when a first-formed product can rearrange or equilibrate. A laboratory product ratio does not reveal the whole mechanism by itself. The ratio may record a competition during formation, redistribution afterward, or even selective decomposition of one product.

Consider two possible ways to obtain the same product. A direct reaction makes eighty percent desired product in one step and generates a closely related impurity that is hard to remove. A longer route makes the right functional group in one operation and then transforms it selectively in another. If each of those two steps gives ninety percent isolated yield, the simple yield arithmetic is about eighty-one percent overall—nearly the same as the direct reaction. But the longer route could be easier to purify or more reliable at scale. Alternatively, it might cost more time, generate more waste, and expose a sensitive intermediate to failure. Step count alone does not determine what route is better.

Selectivity therefore has an economic and environmental dimension. Every undesired reaction consumes starting material and creates separation work. A protecting group can suppress the wrong reaction, but installing and removing it adds reagents and waste. A catalyst that forms the right product in a direct operation can reduce this burden. Chemists also speak about atom economy, which asks what fraction of reactant atoms appear in the intended product, but atom economy alone does not tell us yield, solvent consumption, purification cost, or safety. Several metrics are needed for a serious comparison.

A useful planning exercise is to draw not one arrow, but a forked diagram. At the center is the starting molecule. Each branch represents a plausible reaction. Label the branches according to what distinguishes them. Does one branch reduce a ketone and another an ester? That is chemoselectivity. Does one put a group next to an existing ring substituent and another put it opposite? Regioselectivity. Does one approach the top face and another the bottom face? Stereoselectivity. Then ask what physical interaction might lower one barrier, and what measurement would reveal the actual product distribution.

The measurements must themselves be chosen carefully. A single broad chromatographic peak may hide isomers. Ordinary mass spectrometry can show that two species have the same mass without telling which atom is bonded where. Nuclear magnetic resonance can distinguish many constitutional isomers; chiral chromatography or another stereochemically sensitive method may be needed for enantiomers. A chemical route and an analytical plan are not separate acts. If we cannot distinguish the proposed products, we cannot demonstrate selectivity.

Now invert the usual question. Suppose a reaction *can* make the bond you need, but it also produces five others. Should we study the catalyst until it makes the desired bond exclusively? Should we alter the order of steps so the competing groups are absent? Should we protect one group, change the substrate, or use an entirely different bond-forming transformation? The answer depends on more than reaction names. It depends on the molecule, the mechanism, and the entire route. Synthetic organic chemistry is the art of arranging those constraints so the molecule has fewer productive ways to disobey the plan.

In that sense selectivity is not a finishing detail after we have learned reactions. It is the reason we learn the mechanisms of reactions in the first place. Mechanisms reveal competing paths; kinetics tells us how strongly those paths compete; structure tells us which interactions distinguish them; and analysis tells us what actually happened. The arrow in a synthesis becomes useful only when we can explain why its many alternatives have been made less likely.

## Scientific companion: measuring selectivity

**Parallel kinetic channels.** For two irreversible pathways starting from the same substrate, under comparable conditions, a simple selectivity estimate is

\[
\frac{k_A}{k_B}=\exp\left(-\frac{\Delta G_A^\ddagger-\Delta G_B^\ddagger}{RT}\right).
\]

At 298 K, \(RT\ln 10\approx5.71\ \mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}\). A 10:1 initial product ratio corresponds to about 90.9:9.1 if downstream redistribution is negligible.

**Yield versus ratio.** A 4:1 product ratio is 80% desired among those products, *not* automatically 80% isolated yield. Unreacted feedstock, side products, workup losses and purification may change mass recovery.

**Vocabulary.** Chemoselectivity chooses the functional-group type; regioselectivity chooses the position/connectivity; stereoselectivity chooses a three-dimensional outcome. Enantioselectivity concerns mirror-image products; diastereoselectivity concerns non-mirror-image stereoisomers. Stereospecific describes a defined substrate-product stereochemical relationship encoded by the mechanism, not simply a high product ratio.

**Route comparison example.** Two successive 90% isolated yields multiply to \(0.9^2=0.81\) (81%). Higher theoretical selectivity does not guarantee less waste when solvents, reagents and separations are considered.
