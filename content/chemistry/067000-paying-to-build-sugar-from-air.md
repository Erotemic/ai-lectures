# Paying to Build Sugar from Air: The Calvin Cycle's Carbon Account

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

A green leaf takes carbon dioxide from the atmosphere and builds molecules that can become wood, starch, protein, and new leaves. That is easy to say and difficult to accomplish. Carbon dioxide is a stable, fully oxidized form of carbon. To make a sugar, the cell must reduce that carbon, supply chemical energy, and arrange its atoms into usable molecules. Light provides the energy indirectly: photosynthetic reactions generate ATP and NADPH, which the carbon-fixing machinery then spends. How many of those chemical payments are required to get useful carbon out?

To answer, we need the Calvin–Benson cycle, a repeating network of reactions in the chloroplast stroma of plants and in related forms in many photosynthetic microorganisms. The cycle is sometimes called the dark reaction of photosynthesis, but that name encourages a mistake. It does not mean the pathway runs freely in darkness. Many of its enzymes are light-regulated, and its principal supplies of ATP and NADPH normally depend on the light reactions. It is better to speak of *carbon fixation and reduction*, distinguishing these chemical steps from the capture of light.

Begin with a five-carbon acceptor called ribulose one, five bisphosphate, or RuBP. An enzyme called Rubisco brings carbon dioxide into reaction with RuBP. The first resulting six-carbon compound is unstable and cleaves into two three-carbon molecules of three-phosphoglycerate. In carbon accounting, we began with five carbons in the acceptor, added one from carbon dioxide, and obtained six carbons in two pieces. Rubisco has not, by itself, made a sugar. It has incorporated inorganic carbon into an organic intermediate.

Rubisco's chemistry is remarkable and imperfect. The active site can react with carbon dioxide, but it can also act on oxygen. These two reactions lead to different consequences. Carboxylation contributes carbon to the productive cycle; oxygenation makes one three-phosphoglycerate and one two-phosphoglycolate, a compound whose carbon must be recovered through photorespiration. That recovery consumes energy and can release carbon dioxide. The competition is affected by carbon dioxide and oxygen levels, temperature, and the organism's cellular arrangements. We will return to its ecological consequences shortly.

For now, assume the productive carboxylation route. Three Rubisco reactions use three molecules of carbon dioxide and three RuBP molecules. The three incoming carbon atoms are now distributed among six molecules of three-phosphoglycerate, holding eighteen carbon atoms in total. But most of those eighteen carbons belonged to the acceptors we started with. The cell's problem is to release a net three-carbon product while restoring the fifteen carbon atoms in three RuBP molecules, so that fixation can continue.

The first payment for the reduction phase is ATP. Each three-phosphoglycerate is phosphorylated to one, three bisphosphoglycerate. Six such molecules consume six ATP. Then six NADPH reduce these intermediates to six glyceraldehyde three phosphate molecules, often abbreviated G3P. The enzymes transfer electrons into the carbon-containing molecules while regenerating NADP plus for the light reactions. The ATP has supplied part of the free-energy cost, and the NADPH has supplied reducing equivalents. No amount of sunlight described merely as brightness substitutes for these actual chemical transactions.

Here is the cycle's cleverest bookkeeping step. Six G3P molecules have eighteen carbon atoms. One three-carbon G3P equivalent can leave as the net gain from the three newly fixed carbon dioxide molecules. The remaining five G3P molecules contain fifteen carbons, just enough to reconstruct three five-carbon RuBP acceptors. But they are in the wrong arrangements. A network of sugar-phosphate rearrangements redistributes carbon skeletons, and the regeneration reactions consume three additional ATP to reform the necessary RuBP. The acceptors are ready for another round.

Add the charges: fixing three carbon dioxide molecules and producing one net G3P equivalent consumes nine ATP and six NADPH in the familiar simplified stoichiometry. Those values are not guesses from the cycle's name. Six ATP are spent in reducing the three-carbon intermediates, three ATP in regenerating acceptor, and six NADPH supply the reducing power. If the net carbon is ultimately incorporated into one six-carbon hexose equivalent, the simplest doubling of this account gives six carbon dioxide, eighteen ATP, and twelve NADPH. Actual biosynthesis and transport may involve additional conversions and costs.

Why does the cell not simply make glucose directly? Because building a hexose from carbon dioxide involves a chain of chemically feasible steps. An enzyme cannot wave away the energetic uphill climb. The cycle couples fixation, reduction, and acceptor regeneration so that a stable carbon dioxide molecule enters a machinery already stocked with activated carbon skeletons. The pathway makes a three-carbon exportable intermediate from which many other compounds can be assembled. Calling it a sugar factory is accurate only if we understand the factory's intermediate chemistry.

Now consider the oxygenation side reaction. Rubisco evolved in environments very different from the air surrounding a modern leaf, and its discrimination between carbon dioxide and oxygen is imperfect. When oxygen wins, photorespiratory metabolism salvages carbon from two-phosphoglycolate through reactions distributed among chloroplasts, peroxisomes, and mitochondria in many plants. The process is not simply a useless mistake that evolution forgot to repair; it is a salvage network with physiological interactions. Nevertheless, in ordinary carbon-gain terms it can consume energy and release carbon dioxide that had been fixed. For many plants, a high rate of oxygenation lowers the net efficiency of photosynthetic carbon assimilation.

Whether that competition becomes severe depends on the setting. In a dry, hot environment, plants may close stomata to conserve water. This reduces inward carbon dioxide diffusion, potentially lowering carbon dioxide availability near Rubisco while oxygen remains abundant. Photorespiration can become more important. Some plants have evolved carbon dioxide concentrating strategies—such as C4 photosynthesis and the nocturnal fixation used in CAM plants—that change where or when carbon dioxide is delivered to Rubisco. Those strategies have their own energy and architectural costs. The environment determines whether the tradeoff is beneficial.

An experiment on carbon fixation therefore needs more than measuring how much oxygen a leaf releases. Oxygen evolution originates from water oxidation in the light reactions. Net carbon gain reflects carbon fixation, photorespiration, mitochondrial respiration, transport, and other processes. Gas exchange under controlled light, temperature, and carbon dioxide conditions helps separate some of these contributions. Stable carbon isotope experiments can trace what newly fixed carbon becomes. Fluorescence measurements and biochemical assays can provide further constraints. The separate measurement channels matter because the light reactions and the Calvin cycle are coupled but not identical.

There's a hidden balance with the rest of metabolism. The Calvin cycle consumes ATP and NADPH; other pathways may compete for these carriers. The G3P it exports may become carbohydrate, supply carbon skeletons for amino acids, or contribute to other synthesis. If the cell removes carbon from Calvin-cycle intermediates at the wrong rate without restoring the acceptor pool, fixation will falter. If the light reactions make ATP and NADPH in the wrong proportions for the demands of carbon fixation and other processes, adjustments in electron flow and metabolism become necessary. The diagram of one ring is embedded in a larger network.

Think of the eighteen carbons in the six G3P molecules just after the reduction phase. Fifteen must remain in the cycle so that the machinery can continue; three are the net carbon gain. We can now see exactly what it means to say a leaf makes organic matter from air. It is not a mysterious conversion from nothing. Each carbon atom enters through an enzyme-catalyzed reaction; electrons arrive on NADPH; energy arrives through ATP; and a regeneration scheme keeps the acceptors available for another turn. The cycle succeeds because every material and energy account can be closed.

## Scientific companion: the Calvin–Benson ledger

**Three carboxylations:** 3 RuBP (5 C each) + 3 CO₂ → 6 three-phosphoglycerate (3 C each). The net carbon gain is 3 C, but 15 C of acceptor must be regenerated.

**Reduction and regeneration:** 6 three-phosphoglycerate + 6 ATP + 6 NADPH → 6 triose phosphate equivalents (with phosphate and cofactor products omitted here); five triose phosphate equivalents (15 C) are rearranged, consuming 3 further ATP, into 3 RuBP (15 C). Thus the conventional tally is

\[
\boxed{3\mathrm{CO_2}+9\mathrm{ATP}+6\mathrm{NADPH}\ \Rightarrow\ 1\ \text{net G3P equivalent}}
\]

where the arrow is a *partial bookkeeping shorthand*, not a fully atom- or charge-balanced single reaction. For one six-carbon hexose equivalent, this doubles to 6 CO₂, 18 ATP and 12 NADPH before accounting for additional synthesis/transport costs.

**Rubisco oxygenation:** Oxygenation of RuBP yields a three-carbon three-phosphoglycerate and a two-carbon phosphoglycolate; photorespiratory salvage recovers carbon but incurs energetic and carbon costs. It should not be described as oxygen gas production or a simple inverse of photosynthesis.

**Worked budget:** If a thought-experiment chloroplast fixes 30 nmol CO₂/min exclusively through the conventional Calvin-cycle stoichiometry, the nominal requirements are 90 nmol ATP/min and 60 nmol NADPH/min, and the net triose-phosphate carbon export capacity is 10 nmol three-carbon equivalents per minute. Measured leaves can deviate owing to photorespiration and other fluxes.
