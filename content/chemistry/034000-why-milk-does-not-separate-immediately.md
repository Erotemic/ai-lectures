# Why Milk Does Not Separate Immediately: Colloids, Emulsions, and Tiny Particles

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

Milk is white even though water is transparent. Mayonnaise can hold a huge amount of oil in a creamy mixture, yet oil and water poured into a glass tend to separate. Paint may remain usable in a can for months, but a small change in its formulation can cause pigment particles to clump. These are problems of colloid chemistry: how small droplets or particles behave when they are dispersed through another substance.

We should begin by separating three ideas that are often blurred together. In a true molecular solution, the dissolved species are molecules or ions distributed through a single phase on the molecular scale. In a coarse suspension, comparatively large particles may settle rapidly under gravity. A colloidal dispersion contains tiny droplets, solid particles or other structures distributed through a continuous medium. Its constituents can be large enough to scatter light and form interfaces, yet small enough to remain suspended for a long time under appropriate conditions. There is not a single magical particle diameter that separates all colloids from other mixtures; behavior depends on composition and the question being asked.

Why does a colloidal particle remain moving in a quiet liquid? The surrounding molecules constantly collide with it. Their forces fluctuate, giving the particle an irregular trajectory called Brownian motion. For sufficiently small particles this motion can compete with sedimentation and help keep the mixture distributed over relevant timescales. The motion does not eliminate gravity. A denser particle still tends to settle on average, and a sufficiently large droplet may cream upward if it is less dense than the medium. The balance depends strongly on particle size, fluid viscosity and density difference.

For an isolated small sphere in a viscous fluid, the ideal Stokes settling relation predicts a speed proportional to the square of particle radius, provided the flow regime and other assumptions apply. Double the radius, and the gravity-driven settling speed can become about four times as large. That size dependence is one reason a dispersion of fine droplets can look stable while the same ingredients separated into large droplets do not. It is also why growth and aggregation matter so much: making particles bigger can fundamentally change what gravity does to them.

But even if tiny particles settle slowly, what prevents them from colliding and sticking together? Attractive forces often favor aggregation. Two oil droplets that touch may merge into one larger droplet. Two solid particles may form a cluster, a process described with terms such as flocculation or coagulation depending on the mechanism and convention. If aggregation makes effectively larger objects, sedimentation or creaming can accelerate. A useful dispersion therefore needs a way to resist sticking, not just a way to make particles small at the beginning.

One route is electrostatic stabilization. Many dispersed particles carry effective surface charge in a liquid. Oppositely charged ions gather nearby, forming an electrical double layer. When two similarly charged surfaces approach, overlapping double layers can create a repulsive contribution. Adding salt often screens the electrostatic interaction, reducing its range and sometimes allowing particles to aggregate more readily. But ionic strength is not the only variable. Surface chemistry, specific ion adsorption, pH, particle shape and polymers in solution can change the result. There are dispersions that destabilize when salt is added and others for which the behavior is more complicated.

Another route is steric stabilization. Molecules or polymers attached to particle surfaces extend into the surrounding liquid. When two such coated particles approach, their layers overlap, and this can be energetically unfavorable. The coatings can keep particles apart even where charge screening would otherwise permit closer contact. The effect depends on solvent quality, polymer length, surface coverage and temperature. A polymer that is a good stabilizer in one solvent may cause bridging flocculation in another if one chain binds multiple particles at once.

Oil-in-water emulsions provide a concrete case. Without a stabilizer, blending oil and water can temporarily create small droplets, but that does not mean they have become a molecular solution. The droplets have oil-water interfaces and therefore interfacial energy. When two droplets merge, their total surface area usually decreases, reducing this energy. The mixture has a thermodynamic incentive to coarsen or separate. Making an emulsion requires mechanical work to create the droplet area, and maintaining one requires slowing the processes that destroy it.

An emulsifier is often amphiphilic, with one part of its molecular structure attracted to water and another part more comfortable in oil or other nonpolar environments. It can concentrate at the oil-water boundary and reduce interfacial tension. Proteins and other complex molecules can also form protective interfacial layers. In mayonnaise, components of egg yolk help stabilize finely distributed oil droplets in a water-based phase. In milk, fat droplets have interfaces containing complex biological materials rather than just naked oil. The stable appearance comes from structure at the interface and processing history, not from the claim that the oil has vanished into the water.

Emulsions can fail in several distinct ways. Droplets may move upward or downward without changing their individual identities: creaming or sedimentation. They may gather into clusters without fully merging: flocculation. Their thin intervening films may rupture, letting droplets merge into bigger ones: coalescence. And smaller droplets may shrink as material diffuses through the continuous phase toward larger droplets: Ostwald ripening. These phenomena often occur together, but diagnosing the mechanism matters because each calls for a different intervention.

Ostwald ripening is especially revealing. Very small droplets have highly curved interfaces. Under many conditions, the chemical potential of material in a tiny droplet is higher than that in a large one, partly because of the surface-energy contribution. Molecules can leave small droplets, diffuse through the surrounding phase and join larger droplets. The total droplet count decreases while average size grows, even without droplets colliding. An experiment that shows average droplet size rising is therefore not sufficient to prove coalescence. We need observations capable of distinguishing diffusion-driven growth from merger events.

A dispersion can also become a gel. If particles or polymer chains form an extended network, the mixture may resist flowing like an ordinary liquid while still containing a large amount of solvent. Some networks are permanent chemical gels with covalent crosslinks. Others are physical gels built from reversible interactions. Temperature, salt, acid, shear and time can alter their connectivity. A gelatin dessert, a colloidal clay slurry and a polymer hydrogel may all seem solid-like while having very different microscopic structures.

The optical behavior of colloids is another clue. When light passes through a dispersion, particles or droplets can scatter it. The scattered intensity depends on size, wavelength, refractive-index contrast, concentration and structure. This helps explain why milk looks white and why certain fine dispersions look bluish or opalescent under particular lighting. But whiteness is not a chemical assay. Many different substances can create similar scattering patterns. To identify ingredients, we need spectroscopy or another selective technique in addition to what our eyes tell us.

Emulsions and colloids also explain why 'homogeneous' can be an observation-scale-dependent adjective. A jar of paint may look uniform to the naked eye yet contain pigment particles and polymer binders arranged as distinct structures under a microscope. A droplet of milk looks smooth, but its properties depend on a distribution of fat-droplet sizes and protein assemblies. Even if a sample appears uniform, it may separate during storage, respond differently to temperature or fail after repeated freezing and thawing.

Engineers measure these systems in several ways: microscopy, scattering, rheology, particle-sizing techniques, zeta-potential measurements and accelerated stability tests. Every measurement has limitations. A technique that reports a hydrodynamic particle size may include adsorbed polymer layers. A vigorous mixing step taken before measurement may break up clusters that existed during storage. Zeta potential can be useful as an indicator of electrokinetic behavior, but it is not a complete quantitative prediction of stability for every formulation. The test must resemble the intended use.

A useful numerical comparison makes particle size tangible. Take two otherwise identical droplets in the same low-Reynolds-number fluid, one with radius half a micrometer and one with radius five micrometers. In the simplest Stokes model, the larger droplet's creaming or settling velocity is one hundred times that of the smaller one, because the radius ratio is ten and velocity scales as radius squared. This is not a precise prediction for milk, which contains interacting droplets and proteins. It is a way to see why controlling droplet size can have a large effect on storage behavior even when the ingredients are unchanged.

Food processing offers another example. Homogenization breaks larger fat droplets into much smaller ones by forcing the liquid through a process that generates strong deformation. Smaller droplets are less likely to cream rapidly in the ideal gravitational model, but creating new surface area also requires sufficient interfacial stabilizers. If processing makes tiny droplets faster than proteins or other emulsifiers can protect the new interfaces, some droplets may aggregate or coalesce again. Mechanical work creates the dispersion; chemical coverage helps keep it. Both steps are needed to explain whether the product remains stable on the shelf.

Return to milk, mayonnaise and paint. Their ingredients are important, but their usefulness depends equally on droplet or particle size, surface chemistry, interactions, and processing. A chemist can stabilize a dispersion by changing a surface layer without changing the core substance. Another chemist can deliberately destabilize a colloid so that unwanted particles settle and can be filtered from water. The same principles allow either outcome. Colloid chemistry is the study of what happens when a material's interfaces become so numerous that the boundary is nearly as important as the bulk.

## Scientific companion: particle stability and coarsening

**Brownian diffusion.** In the ideal dilute Stokes–Einstein approximation for a sphere of hydrodynamic radius $r_h$, $D=k_BT/(6\pi\eta r_h)$. The random-motion scale over one coordinate is $\sqrt{2Dt}$. Applicability depends on continuum hydrodynamics, solvent conditions and other assumptions.

**Settling.** For a small isolated sphere in the creeping-flow regime, $v=2\Delta\rho\,g r^2/(9\eta)$, where $\Delta\rho$ is the particle-medium density contrast. Agglomeration alters the effective size and structure, and concentrated dispersions violate independent-sphere assumptions.

**Curvature and ripening.** For an approximately spherical droplet the Laplace pressure is $\Delta p=2\gamma/r$. The Kelvin-related curvature contribution to chemical potential helps drive Ostwald ripening; it does not alone determine ripening rates, which depend on transport, solubility and formulation.

**Failure modes.** Creaming/sedimentation: movement of droplets or particles. Flocculation: aggregates form without necessarily merging. Coalescence: droplets merge. Ostwald ripening: molecular transport redistributes dispersed phase from small to large droplets. A microscopy time series can help distinguish mechanisms.
