# Growing a Carbon Skeleton: Aldol, Claisen, and Michael Reactions

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

Most of the atoms in a complicated organic molecule are not especially mysterious. The difficulty lies in arranging them. A target may contain twenty carbon atoms, yet the laboratory starts with bottles of compounds containing three, five, or eight. Synthetic chemistry therefore needs dependable ways to join carbon fragments, preferably in patterns that can be predicted before a reaction is attempted. Carbonyl compounds are extraordinarily useful for this job. They can present an electrophilic carbon, generate nucleophiles beside that carbon, and create products that can react again in a controlled sequence.

The aldol reaction is the first major example. One aldehyde or ketone provides an enol or enolate nucleophile; another provides an electrophilic carbonyl. A carbon-carbon bond forms between the alpha carbon of the first partner and the carbonyl carbon of the second. After protonation, the result is a beta-hydroxy carbonyl compound: a molecule with an alcohol group two carbons away from the retained carbonyl. The prefix beta counts from the carbonyl carbon. A new carbon-carbon bond has linked two pieces, and the product still contains useful functional groups.

Picture acetaldehyde, a two-carbon aldehyde. Under conditions that produce a small amount of enolate, one molecule can attack the carbonyl of another. The carbon count doubles. The initial aldol addition product is a four-carbon beta-hydroxy aldehyde. If the reaction conditions permit dehydration, the molecule can lose the elements of water and form an alpha-beta unsaturated aldehyde: a carbon-carbon double bond conjugated with the carbonyl. The product has changed not only in length but in electronic behavior.

The word aldol historically combines aldehyde and alcohol, but the chemistry is not confined to that simple example. Ketones can participate. Some combinations can form rings intramolecularly. Yet the most basic mixed reaction immediately exposes a selectivity problem. If two different aldehydes both have alpha hydrogens, each can form an enolate and each can serve as electrophile. Four constitutional coupling patterns may be possible even before considering stereochemistry, dehydration, or repeated reaction. A convenient arrow that combines two different reactants is not proof that their mixture will give one product.

One route to control is to choose partners with different roles. Benzaldehyde has an aldehyde attached directly to a benzene ring and no alpha hydrogen available for ordinary enolate formation. It can accept an enolate attack without itself becoming a corresponding enolate donor. Acetone has equivalent alpha positions and can supply the nucleophile. Their crossed aldol reaction is therefore more predictable than one between two similar enolizable aldehydes, although acetone self-condensation, overreaction, and product stereochemistry still deserve attention.

This example gives a useful named structure. The dehydrated single-addition product of benzaldehyde and acetone is often called benzalacetone, or four-phenylbut-three-en-two-one. It has a benzene ring connected to a three-carbon enone portion. The new carbon-carbon double bond is conjugated both with the ketone and, through its other end, with the aromatic ring. The more extended system can absorb light differently and can participate in conjugate addition. The transformation has not merely stuck two starting molecules together. It has created a new kind of reactive architecture.

Claisen condensation uses a related logic but changes the electrophile. Instead of one carbonyl partner being an aldehyde or ketone, an enolate derived from an ester attacks the carbonyl carbon of another ester. The tetrahedral intermediate can then expel an alkoxide leaving group, regenerating a carbonyl. The net product is often a beta-keto ester, containing a ketone and ester separated by one carbon. This is nucleophilic acyl substitution joined to carbon-carbon bond formation, rather than the simple carbonyl addition of an aldol reaction.

Ethyl acetate can undergo a classic self-Claisen condensation to form ethyl acetoacetate. Two small ester fragments become a product with a highly useful methylene group between two carbonyls. That central carbon's hydrogens are substantially more acidic than those next to just one ordinary ester, because the conjugate base is stabilized by both carbonyl groups. This acidity helps drive the overall Claisen sequence when the product is deprotonated under the reaction conditions; an acid workup then restores the neutral beta-keto ester. It is not enough to copy an aldol arrow and change a letter. The ester leaving group and final acid-base step change the chemical accounting.

The new beta-keto ester becomes a valuable building block. Its stabilized enolate can be alkylated, or it can add to an alpha-beta unsaturated carbonyl compound. This second process is a Michael reaction. Rather than adding to the carbonyl carbon directly, a nucleophile forms a bond at the beta carbon of the conjugated system. Electrons shift through the conjugated array, and after proton transfer the carbonyl group is retained. Chemists call this conjugate addition or one-four addition, counting the atoms across the connected electrophilic system.

Why would a nucleophile choose the beta carbon instead of the carbonyl carbon? The answer depends on its character, the electrophile, catalyst and reaction environment. Some nucleophiles are more likely to add directly to a carbonyl carbon and form an alcohol after workup; others favor conjugate addition and retain the carbonyl. The distinction is another example of chemoselectivity and regioselectivity arising from the electronic pathways a reagent can access. It is not an invariant rule that all negative carbon nucleophiles always attack one-four.

A Michael reaction can create a product with two carbonyl groups separated by several atoms. That spacing makes new intramolecular reactions possible. Suppose a molecule produced by conjugate addition has a carbonyl group on one end and another carbonyl elsewhere along the chain. Its enolate might attack the second carbonyl internally, closing a ring. A famous sequence called the Robinson annulation combines Michael addition with intramolecular aldol condensation to produce a cyclohexenone framework. Several simple bonds are reorganized into a six-membered ring and a conjugated carbonyl system.

This cascade reveals why synthesis planning often begins by recognizing recurring patterns. A target cyclohexenone ring may look complicated when drawn in isolation. If we recognize that its ring could arise from an intramolecular aldol, and that the precursor might come from Michael addition, the structure becomes a record of possible earlier steps. The strategic insight is not that every ring must be made this way. It is that a certain spacing of carbonyls and electrophilic double bonds suggests a reliable family of transformations.

One must still solve the selective formation of each intermediate. A Michael acceptor might polymerize or undergo another addition. The initial aldol product might dehydrate before it can be isolated. A Claisen condensation may be reversible or complicated by mixed ester partners. An intramolecular reaction can favor one ring size over another because of strain and geometry. A successful carbon skeleton is a necessary condition for a useful synthesis, not a guarantee that one pure product can be collected.

How do we distinguish these outcomes experimentally? Nuclear magnetic resonance can show whether the aldehyde proton has disappeared and whether new alkene protons have appeared. Infrared spectroscopy can give clues about carbonyls and hydroxyl groups, though overlapping absorptions limit certainty. Mass spectrometry can test elemental composition, while chromatographic separation can quantify and purify compounds. In more difficult cases, two-dimensional NMR and stereochemical analysis are needed to determine exactly which bonds formed. The measurement plan is part of the synthetic plan.

There is an elegant common thread across aldol, Claisen and Michael chemistry. We change where an electron pair can attack by activating a carbon next to a carbonyl. The electrophile then decides whether the result is a beta-hydroxy carbonyl, a beta-keto ester, or a conjugate-addition product. Each product stores new reactivity for later operations. Organic synthesis is not a series of disconnected named reactions. It is a strategy for repeatedly converting simple polarities and geometries into useful new carbon-carbon connections.

## Scientific companion: three carbon–carbon bond patterns

**Aldol addition:** \(\mathrm{enolate\ C_\alpha + RCHO\rightarrow RCH(OH)C_\alpha(C{=}O)}\). It generates a beta-hydroxy carbonyl. Dehydration can create an alpha-beta unsaturated carbonyl.

**Crossed case:** \(\mathrm{PhCHO + CH_3COCH_3 \rightarrow PhCH{=}CHCOCH_3 + H_2O}\) is a net representation for benzalacetone after dehydration. Benzaldehyde has no alpha hydrogen; acetone can self-condense, and conditions/product ratio still require attention.

**Claisen:** \(\mathrm{2\ CH_3CO_2Et \rightarrow CH_3COCH_2CO_2Et + EtOH}\) after acid workup, formally. In the basic reaction mixture the beta-keto ester product is substantially deprotonated. The mechanism includes acyl substitution and alkoxide departure.

**Michael:** an enolate nucleophile attacks the beta carbon of \(\mathrm{C{=}C-C{=}O}\), giving conjugate addition with the carbonyl retained. Product structure depends on nucleophile/catalyst and protonation.

**Robinson annulation:** Michael addition followed by intramolecular aldol condensation; a useful motif for construction of cyclohexenones, subject to substrate and ring-size constraints.
