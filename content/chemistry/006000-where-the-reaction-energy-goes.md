# Where the Reaction Energy Goes: Heat, Entropy, and Chemical Direction

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

A flame is easy to recognize. You put fuel in the presence of oxygen, supply a way for the reaction to begin, and light and heat appear. The visible result invites a misleading explanation: the fuel must contain energy in its bonds, and breaking those bonds releases it. But a stable chemical bond is an arrangement with lower energy than its separated atoms. Breaking a bond normally costs energy. So if a flame releases energy, where does the release actually come from?

The answer is a comparison between the entire set of reactants and the entire set of products. To get from one arrangement to another, some interactions must be disrupted and new interactions formed. The net result depends on all the energy changes, including the new bonds and the phases of the materials. A reaction can release heat when the products end up in an energetically more favorable state than the reactants under the stated conditions, even though individual steps along the path require energy inputs.

This is the entry point to chemical thermodynamics: how energy and entropy constrain changes in matter. Thermodynamics does not start by asking how quickly a reaction occurs. It asks what changes of state are possible and what conditions favor one direction over another. The difference between possibility and speed is so important that we will return to it several times.

Begin with a sealed container containing a reacting mixture. The chemical system has internal energy: motion and interactions of its particles, including microscopic kinetic and potential contributions. Energy may pass between the system and its surroundings as heat or work. Heat is energy transferred because of a temperature difference; work is energy transferred through a generalized force and displacement, such as gas pushing a piston. A hot product is not made of a special substance called heat. It has energy, and heat describes a way energy crossed a boundary.

The first law of thermodynamics states that energy is conserved in the accounting. If our system loses energy to the surroundings, the surroundings gain that energy, subject to the stated conventions and other transfers. A cold pack absorbs thermal energy from its surroundings; an exothermic reaction can warm a container and the air around it. The opposite signs assigned to system and surroundings are not contradictory. They are the same transfer viewed across different sides of a boundary.

A chemist often uses enthalpy, a state quantity conventionally written H, to describe processes at constant pressure when only pressure-volume work is relevant. The enthalpy change of a reaction, delta H, is negative when the reacting system releases heat at constant pressure under those conditions. Such a change is called exothermic. A positive delta H describes an endothermic change, where the system absorbs heat in the corresponding process. This terminology is about the reacting system's energy balance, not whether a beaker definitely feels hot to your hand. Apparatus, heat losses, and coupled processes can affect the measured temperature.

Imagine burning methane in oxygen. The net products can be carbon dioxide and water. To reach those products, carbon-hydrogen and oxygen-oxygen bonds in the reactants must be reorganized, while carbon-oxygen and oxygen-hydrogen bonds form in the products. The whole transformation can release a substantial amount of energy. If you ask for one numeric heat of combustion, however, you have to specify conditions. Is the produced water liquid or vapor? Did the fuel and oxygen begin at the same temperature? Do we report a value at a standard reference pressure and temperature? Condensing water vapor releases additional energy, so the numerical heat of combustion differs depending on the product's phase. A bare number without its state labels is incomplete chemistry.

How would we measure a heat change? Calorimetry measures temperature and uses known or calibrated heat capacities to infer energy transfer. In a simple thought experiment, put a reacting sample in a well-insulated vessel surrounded by a known quantity of water. Suppose the water warms by two degrees Celsius. If the water's mass and heat capacity are known, you can estimate how much heat it absorbed. If the apparatus also warms, its heat capacity matters too. The heat gained by the surroundings is related to the heat lost by the reaction system, but the equality requires a careful energy balance over everything that exchanges energy.

For water, a convenient approximate specific heat capacity near room temperature is 4.18 joules per gram per degree Celsius. If one hundred grams of water warms by two degrees, it has absorbed roughly eight hundred thirty-six joules. You cannot conclude that an unknown reaction released exactly that much unless the vessel, thermometer, surroundings, and other energy transfers are accounted for. This is why a calibrated calorimeter and a casual temperature observation are not interchangeable.

A second powerful tool is Hess's law. Suppose the same initial chemicals can be transformed to the same final chemicals through different hypothetical steps. The overall enthalpy change is the same regardless of the chosen steps, provided we compare the same thermodynamic states. Enthalpy is a state function: its difference depends on endpoints, not the route. We can combine known reaction enthalpies algebraically to estimate one that is difficult to measure directly. We can reverse a reaction and reverse the sign of its enthalpy; we can multiply a reaction equation and multiply the stated enthalpy by the same factor. That is a form of chemical bookkeeping built on conservation and the existence of a state function.

Yet heat is not the only criterion for whether a process is favored. Ice melts when warmed above its equilibrium melting temperature at an appropriate pressure. Melting requires an input of heat, yet it can proceed spontaneously under those conditions. A substance may dissolve while the solution cools. How can a process favor a direction that absorbs energy? The answer is that thermodynamics considers entropy as well as enthalpy.

Entropy is often introduced as disorder. The word captures a loose intuition about some examples, but it can mislead. A more useful statistical idea is that macroscopic states differ in the number and distribution of microscopic arrangements compatible with them. The entropy measures aspects of that multiplicity and the constraints on energy distribution. When a gas expands into a larger available volume, many more positional configurations become accessible. When two compatible gases mix, the number of possible arrangements generally increases. The tendency of isolated systems toward higher total entropy is not a moral rule about messiness; it follows from the statistical structure of overwhelmingly many-particle systems and the assumptions used to describe them.

Even this description requires care. Creating a crystal from dissolved material makes the solid more spatially ordered, but it can still be favorable if other entropy and energy changes in the system and surroundings compensate. A snowflake forming is not a violation of the second law simply because the crystals look organized. The second law concerns the total entropy balance for the relevant system and surroundings, not a visual judgment of one part of the process. Local order can grow while the larger environment's entropy increases.

At constant temperature and pressure, a common chemical criterion uses Gibbs energy, G, defined as enthalpy minus temperature times entropy. For a change between states at the same temperature, its change is delta H minus T times delta S. If delta G is negative under the relevant constraints, the proposed change is thermodynamically favored in the forward direction. If it is positive, the reverse direction is favored. At equilibrium, the relevant Gibbs-energy change for an infinitesimal progression is zero. These statements are not magical arrows that guarantee a reaction will occur immediately. They summarize the combined energy and entropy contributions under stated conditions.

Temperature matters. Suppose a reaction has a positive enthalpy change and a positive entropy change. At a low enough temperature, the energy cost can dominate and the reaction may not be favored. At a sufficiently high temperature, the entropy term can outweigh it. Other combinations behave differently. This is one reason phase changes have characteristic equilibrium temperatures and why changing temperature can shift chemical equilibria. The transition is not caused by temperature altering the count of atoms or ignoring conservation; it changes the weighting of energetic and entropic effects in the conditions for equilibrium.

What about reactions that release heat and still do not seem to happen? A mixture of hydrogen and oxygen gas can persist for a time without producing a dramatic amount of water. The overall reaction is strongly favorable under ordinary conditions, but getting it started typically requires overcoming a kinetic barrier. An ignition source or catalyst changes the available reaction path and rate; it does not change the identities of the starting and ending states used to calculate their overall thermodynamic difference. Thermodynamics can tell us the destination is energetically favored, while kinetics tells us why the system may remain where it is.

Graphite and diamond give another example. Diamond is metastable relative to graphite under many ordinary surface conditions, yet a diamond on a ring does not promptly turn into pencil graphite. Transforming one bonding network into another requires coordinated changes through paths with substantial barriers. Long-lived states can be thermodynamically disfavored while remaining kinetically persistent. That is why the word stable in everyday speech can hide two different questions: lower Gibbs energy compared with alternatives, or slow transformation on the timescale we care about.

Chemical equilibrium itself is often misunderstood. If the concentrations in a reacting mixture become steady, we might assume all reaction has stopped. But many reactions are dynamic: forward and reverse processes continue while balancing in a way that leaves the overall composition constant. At a given temperature, an equilibrium constant describes the ratio of chemical activities in a particular mathematical combination. Adding a reactant or changing temperature can alter the direction of net change until a new equilibrium is reached. The direction of the initial response depends on the actual reaction quotient, not a slogan about a reaction wanting to undo what we did.

There is a useful quantitative relation hiding here. For a reaction at given temperature and pressure, the Gibbs-energy change depends on the current activities of reactants and products. It can be written as the standard reaction Gibbs energy plus the gas constant times temperature times the natural logarithm of the reaction quotient. At equilibrium, this change is zero, so the standard Gibbs energy and equilibrium constant are related. In later work, this connects a table of thermodynamic data to predictions of chemical composition. For now the essential insight is that reaction direction depends on what is actually present, not only on the name of the reaction.

The terms standard state and spontaneous deserve respect. Standard-state thermodynamic tables refer to carefully defined reference conditions; the numbers are not necessarily the changes in a real vessel at arbitrary concentration. A spontaneous process in thermodynamics means favored in direction under the given constraints. It does not mean instantaneous, violent, or even visibly detectable over a lifetime. Conversely, a reaction that is thermodynamically uphill can be driven by coupling it to a more favorable process, by applying electrical work, or by changing the environment. Chemistry is full of systems that use one process to power another.

Living cells are expert at such coupling. They maintain concentrations, use membranes, exploit redox gradients, and channel reactions with enzymes. A plant capturing light and building sugar is not violating thermodynamics by making organized molecules. It receives energy from outside and exports energy and entropy through its environment. A cell is not an isolated equilibrium flask. Its chemistry is sustained by ongoing flows of matter and energy. The same physical principles explain a flame, a battery, a mineral dissolving, and the operation of metabolism, though their mechanisms and constraints differ greatly.

Return to the flame. The energy did not appear because bonds are little boxes from which we release stored heat by cutting the strings. The reaction reorganized electrons and nuclei into products whose overall enthalpy is lower under the relevant conditions; the difference could be transferred to the surroundings. Whether the reaction can proceed, how far it proceeds, and how rapidly it proceeds require different layers of explanation. Energy accounting, entropy, equilibrium, and rate are not competing theories. They answer different questions about the same chemical world.

## Scientific companion: thermodynamic accounting

### Quantities and conventions

- First law for a closed system with the usual chemistry sign convention: $\Delta U=q+w$, where $q$ is heat **into** the system and $w$ is work **on** the system.
- Enthalpy $H=U+pV$; at constant pressure with only pressure-volume work, $q_p=\Delta H$. Negative $\Delta H$ is exothermic *for the system*.
- Gibbs energy $G=H-TS$. For a change between states at one temperature, $\Delta G=\Delta H-T\Delta S$. At fixed $T,p$, $\Delta G<0$ indicates a favored forward change under appropriate constraints, not rapid completion.
- For a reaction at arbitrary composition: $\Delta_r G=\Delta_r G^\circ+RT\ln Q$ using dimensionless reaction quotient in terms of activities; at equilibrium $\Delta_r G=0$, hence $\Delta_r G^\circ=-RT\ln K$.
- Hess's law: sum enthalpy changes of component reactions to obtain the enthalpy change between the same initial and final thermodynamic states.

### Worked examples

**Calorimetry:** 100.0 g liquid water warming by 2.00 °C, approximate $c_p=4.18$ J/(g °C): $q_{\rm water}\approx 100.0\times4.18\times2.00=836$ J. If the *only* energy transfer is heat between reaction and water, $q_{\rm reaction}\approx -836$ J. Real calorimeters also require calibration and other terms.

**Phase-sensitive reaction enthalpy:** $\mathrm{CH_4+2O_2\to CO_2+2H_2O}$ is not a fully specified standard thermochemical equation until physical phases and reference conditions are given. Producing liquid water differs energetically from producing water vapor.

**Temperature example:** For hypothetical constant $\Delta H=20$ kJ/mol and $\Delta S=100$ J/(mol K), the approximate crossing $\Delta G=0$ occurs at $T=\Delta H/\Delta S=200$ K. This is a *constructed* pedagogical example; real enthalpy and entropy can vary with temperature, and phase changes may intervene.
