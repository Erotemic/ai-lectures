# Rust Is a Battery You Did Not Ask For: Corrosion and Passivation

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

A steel railing can stand for years in dry indoor air and deteriorate rapidly when water and road salt reach it. A stainless-steel screw can remain shiny on an exposed surface yet develop a deep pit inside a damp crevice. A piece of zinc attached to a steel structure may be consumed while helping preserve the steel. These are not three unrelated kinds of aging. They are examples of electrochemistry occurring in a material's environment.

Corrosion is a chemical or electrochemical interaction between a material and its surroundings that degrades useful properties. For many metals in water-containing environments, the reactions can be understood as parts of a tiny electrochemical cell. Some metal atoms lose electrons and become dissolved ions. Elsewhere, a reduction reaction consumes the electrons. The currents may travel through the metal, while ions move through the electrolyte. The metal does not need a separate battery attached; the energy for the process comes from a favorable overall chemical reaction.

Take iron as the familiar example. At an anodic location, iron can be oxidized, forming iron ions and releasing electrons to the metal. In aerated, near-neutral water, oxygen can be reduced at cathodic locations, often producing hydroxide ions. The dissolved iron may react further with oxygen and water to form a mixture of iron oxides and oxyhydroxides that we call rust. The visible reddish material is not necessarily one pure compound, and the sequence depends on conditions such as pH, oxygen, moisture and other dissolved species.

The crucial point is that the oxidation and reduction sites need not be the same microscopic place. Different locations on a single piece of metal can act as anodes and cathodes. A scratch, an inclusion, a difference in oxygen concentration, a grain boundary, a coating defect or contact with another metal can change where reactions proceed. A moist region under a deposit may become chemically different from the open surface beside it. The resulting gradients can sustain local electrochemical cells.

Why does salt often accelerate corrosion? Dissolved salts can improve the ionic conductivity of a water film, lowering resistance to current. Chloride ions can also influence passive films and support localized corrosion in certain metals and environments. But it would be inaccurate to say all salt merely supplies extra oxygen or acts as a universal catalyst. Different salts, concentrations, temperatures, oxygen levels and alloy compositions produce different outcomes. Corrosion depends on the complete electrochemical environment.

A metal's standard reduction potential helps us estimate possible reaction directions under specified reference conditions, but it cannot predict the service life of a bridge by itself. Standard electrode potentials assume particular activities and temperature. Real corrosion depends on mixed potentials where anodic and cathodic reaction currents balance, on the kinetics of charge transfer, on diffusion of oxygen and ions, and on resistances in films and electrolytes. A thermodynamically possible corrosion reaction can proceed slowly if the necessary steps are strongly inhibited.

Passivation is one such inhibition. Some metals form a thin surface film that drastically slows further dissolution under suitable conditions. Stainless steels owe much of their corrosion resistance to a chromium-rich passive oxide film, not to the absence of chemically reactive iron inside the alloy. Aluminum also develops a protective oxide in many environments. The film can be very thin and still have a large effect because it changes the rate at which ions and electrons cross the interface. Yet passive does not mean invulnerable. Specific environments can dissolve, disrupt or locally destabilize the film.

Consider a stainless-steel fastener under a washer. Outside the washer, the metal may have good access to oxygen and a stable passive film. Inside a narrow wet crevice, oxygen supply can become restricted and dissolved ions can accumulate. Electrochemical and transport processes can shift local chemistry, making conditions more aggressive than the surrounding water. Corrosion can become concentrated in the crevice. A surface that looks excellent from a distance can hide serious localized damage. This is why inspecting average appearance is not enough to assess every corrosion risk.

Pitting corrosion is another localized pattern. A small region of passive film can break down under unfavorable conditions, initiating a pit. Once a pit grows, its internal electrolyte may differ from the external solution because metal ions hydrolyze, local acidity changes and transport in the confined geometry is restricted. Depending on alloy and environment, that chemistry may help maintain dissolution within the pit. Not every surface imperfection will become a self-sustaining pit; initiation, stabilization, propagation and repassivation are separate events.

Galvanic coupling provides a more deliberate example of anodic and cathodic roles. Suppose two different metals touch electrically and share an electrolyte. Their coupled potentials and reaction rates can change. One may corrode faster while the other is protected relative to their isolated behavior. The result depends on both materials, the environment, kinetic polarization and the ratio of exposed cathodic and anodic areas. A small anodic region connected to a large efficient cathode can be particularly troublesome. A simple ranking of metals by noble character is only a first approximation.

Engineers exploit that logic through sacrificial-anode protection. Zinc or magnesium can be connected electrically to a steel structure in a suitable electrolyte. If properly selected and installed, the more readily oxidized metal supplies electrons while the steel is maintained at a potential that suppresses its corrosion. The sacrificial metal is used up and must eventually be inspected or replaced. In other systems, an external power source provides impressed-current cathodic protection. Neither method is independent of geometry, electrolyte resistivity, coatings or the electrochemical reactions available at the surfaces.

Coatings defend in a different way. An intact paint or polymer film can reduce the contact between metal, water, oxygen and aggressive ions. Its performance depends on adhesion, permeability, defects, surface preparation and durability. A tiny breach may concentrate corrosion at the exposed metal, especially when moisture creeps beneath a coating. A coating system should be understood as a barrier with specific transport properties and failure mechanisms, not an impenetrable shield simply because it has a large dry-film thickness.

The behavior of copper helps reveal why not every visible corrosion product means equivalent damage. In some outdoor environments copper develops a patina that can reduce the rate of further attack. Iron's common rust products may be porous and less protective, allowing water and oxygen to reach fresh metal. The color of a surface cannot tell us its protection quality without knowing the film's composition, adherence, transport properties and exposure. A green statue and a rusty railing may both show corrosion products, yet their long-term consequences differ.

There are also microbially influenced corrosion processes. Microorganisms can change chemical gradients, produce metabolites, affect oxygen availability or alter surface films. The organisms do not need to 'eat metal' in the popular sense for their activity to influence electrochemical reactions. The effect is environmentally specific and can be difficult to distinguish from purely chemical contributions without suitable measurements. Biofilms are yet another way interfaces acquire different local chemistry from the bulk fluid.

How would we evaluate corrosion in a laboratory? A mass-loss coupon can report average material lost under a defined exposure, but it can miss the importance of a few dangerous pits. Electrochemical polarization measurements can probe current-potential behavior, yet converting them into long-term predictions requires assumptions about reaction mechanisms and steady conditions. Microscopy can locate pits and cracks; chemical analysis can investigate films and deposits. Field evidence about moisture, salt exposure, galvanic contact and service loading is as important as an elegant electrochemical curve.

There is an important distinction between a metal that corrodes uniformly and one that suffers localized penetration. Imagine two steel panels that each lose the same total mass in a salt-spray test. On the first, the loss is spread rather evenly over the exposed area. On the second, almost all the loss occurs in a narrow pit. If the panels are used as pressure boundaries, the second may be far more dangerous, because one small location can lose enough thickness to leak or rupture. Average mass loss is useful for broad comparison but cannot substitute for a measurement of deepest attack.

Consider too a galvanic joint between stainless steel and a less noble metal. The electrical connection, the area ratio and the local conductivity of moisture can control where current flows. An insulating washer that breaks electrical continuity might reduce the galvanic interaction, but if the joint still traps a salty crevice, localized corrosion can remain. It would be a mistake to call the problem solved simply because the two metals no longer touch electronically. Practical diagnosis asks which electrochemical pathway was operating and whether the intervention actually removed it.

Corrosion is a practical lesson about coupling. A chemical formula alone cannot guarantee resistance, and a battery voltage alone cannot predict material failure. The metal, the film on its surface, the water in contact with it, and the geometry of the exposed region form one active system. When engineers design a bridge or a water pipe, they are designing the interfaces and possible electrochemical cells as surely as they are selecting the bulk alloy.

## Scientific companion: mixed potentials and local cells

**Illustrative half reactions** in a suitable aqueous environment:

$$\mathrm{Fe(s)\rightarrow Fe^{2+}(aq)+2e^-}$$

$$\mathrm{O_2(g)+2H_2O(l)+4e^-\rightarrow 4OH^-(aq)}.$$

These are components of a possible corrosion mechanism, not a full universal description of rust composition or every pH regime.

**Thermodynamics versus corrosion rate.** $\Delta G=-nFE$ relates electrical potential to an electrochemical reaction under specified conditions. Corrosion current emerges from coupled anodic and cathodic kinetics at a *mixed potential* and from transport. The Faraday relation $m=(I t M)/(nF)$ gives metal dissolved for a known purely Faradaic oxidation current, assuming appropriate valence and current efficiency; local attack requires geometric information as well.

**Area effects.** Galvanic corrosion can become severe for a small exposed anodic area coupled to a much larger cathodic area. A ranking from standard potentials is insufficient without solution chemistry and polarization behavior.

**Localized corrosion.** A passive film can coexist with pitting or crevice corrosion; local acidity, chloride enrichment, oxygen gradients and transport limitations may matter. Alloy grades and conditions must be specified before making service-life claims.
