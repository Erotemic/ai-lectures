# Water and the Chemistry of Dissolving

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

Stir a spoonful of salt into a glass of water. The grains disappear. Pour a spoonful of oil into another glass of water, and most of the oil gathers into droplets that eventually separate into a layer. Sprinkle sugar into a third glass and it may disappear just as the salt did. Three everyday substances, three outcomes. Why do some mixtures become uniform while others refuse to stay mixed? The answer involves a competition among molecular attractions, thermal motion, and the number of possible arrangements of the particles.

We need to reject one tempting explanation immediately: water does not dissolve salt because the salt molecules are simply small enough to fit between the water molecules. Salt crystals do not consist of independent sodium chloride molecules in the first place. Nor does small particle size by itself guarantee solubility. The issue is whether the resulting microscopic arrangement is thermodynamically favorable enough, under the particular conditions, for a significant amount of substance to disperse.

Begin with the water molecule. The oxygen-hydrogen bonds have an uneven distribution of electron density, and the molecule is bent. That combination gives water a permanent electric dipole: a more negative region near oxygen and more positive regions near the hydrogens. Water molecules attract one another through several interactions, including hydrogen bonding and electrostatic effects. Those interactions help explain why liquid water is cohesive and why its boiling point is high compared with several similarly small molecules.

A sodium chloride crystal is a lattice of sodium ions with positive charge and chloride ions with negative charge. Opposite charges attract strongly in the crystal. If the ions are to separate into the liquid, the system must give up some of these stabilizing lattice interactions. Water molecules can then surround the separated ions. The oxygen-rich side of water tends to orient toward sodium ions; the hydrogen-rich side tends to orient toward chloride ions. This process is called hydration, a particular form of solvation in which water is the solvent.

There is no single microscopic moment in which the lattice simply gives up. Water interacts with ions at the exposed crystal surface, ions enter solution, hydrated ions move by diffusion, and the surface may gradually retreat. At the same time, ions in solution can return to the crystal. The net process depends on concentration, temperature, and the balance of microscopic possibilities. A solution that appears motionless still contains molecules and ions moving and colliding.

Think of the energetic accounting. Pulling ions apart from an ionic solid costs energy. Reorganizing water and forming ion-water interactions can release energy. The net enthalpy change of dissolution is the difference between these contributions, with additional effects from the system's conditions. Some salts warm water as they dissolve. Others cool it. Sodium chloride dissolves readily even though its dissolution is slightly endothermic under ordinary conditions. That alone should warn us that the sign of heat flow cannot decide solubility.

Why can a slightly endothermic dissolution occur? Because energy is only part of the story. Thermodynamics also cares about how many microscopic arrangements are accessible. When ions spread through a volume of liquid, mixing can favor a much larger family of possible configurations. But dissolving ions can also organize nearby solvent molecules and affect the entropy in more complicated ways. A more complete criterion compares the Gibbs energy of the initial and final states at a specified temperature and pressure. The process can be favorable when the combined enthalpy and entropy contributions support the dispersed state. The simple slogan that disorder always increases is not reliable enough to predict every solution.

Now consider sugar. Ordinary sucrose is a molecular compound. A sugar crystal contains many neutral sucrose molecules held together by intermolecular attractions and a solid structure. When sucrose dissolves, the molecules can become dispersed through the water while remaining largely chemically intact as sucrose. The water interacts with several oxygen-containing groups on the sugar molecule. But dissolving sugar does not produce a large population of sodium-like or chloride-like ions. That difference matters for conductivity. A sodium chloride solution conducts current because mobile ions move in response to an electric field. A relatively pure sugar solution is a poor conductor compared with a similar concentration of salt.

The word electrolyte refers to a substance that produces mobile ions in the relevant medium. Sodium chloride supplies ions by dissociation when it dissolves. Some molecular substances, including strong acids, produce ions through reaction with water. So the label ionic compound does not identify every electrolyte, and the label molecular compound does not guarantee nonconductivity in solution. The chemistry of the solvent can create new species.

Why does oil behave differently? Most cooking oils are dominated by molecules containing long, comparatively nonpolar hydrocarbon regions. Water gains relatively little energetic benefit from surrounding those regions compared with the strong network of water-water interactions and the alternative arrangements available when the phases separate. Calling the oil hydrophobic describes the outcome, not an active force that pushes oil molecules away like magnetic poles. In water, nonpolar surfaces can cluster because that arrangement can reduce the unfavorable consequences of their exposure to water. Molecules with both a water-compatible part and an oil-compatible part, such as many soaps and detergents, can assemble at interfaces and help disperse oil into smaller droplets or structures.

A soap molecule often has a polar or charged head and a long nonpolar tail. In water, suitable molecules can assemble into micelles: small aggregates that shield many nonpolar tails from direct contact with water while exposing heads to the surrounding liquid. Micelles can help solubilize oil-like material, but detergent systems are more diverse than a single uniform cartoon. Emulsions, micelles, and molecular solutions are different kinds of dispersions; they should not be conflated merely because the mixture looks cloudy or stays mixed for a while.

A crucial word is concentration. Suppose you dissolve one gram of salt in a liter of water. The result is dilute. Add more salt and the amount dissolved generally rises until the system approaches a limit set by the temperature, pressure, and chemistry. At saturation, solid salt and dissolved ions can coexist in dynamic equilibrium. Ions continue to leave and return to the crystal, even though the average concentration is steady. This is not a signal that every ion has stopped moving or that water is physically full in the way a box is full of stones.

The amount a substance can dissolve is called its solubility under stated conditions. Solubility is not the same as speed of dissolving. Finely powdered salt may dissolve faster than a large crystal because it presents more surface area to the water, while the final equilibrium solubility remains governed by the same temperature and solution conditions. Stirring speeds transport from a surface into the rest of the liquid; it does not ordinarily rewrite the equilibrium composition. Confusing rate with final amount is one of the most common errors in describing chemical change.

The temperature dependence of solubility also resists a universal slogan. Many solids become more soluble as temperature rises, but not all. Gases dissolved in water commonly become less soluble as temperature rises at fixed gas pressure. For a gas, the relevant quantity is also its partial pressure in the contacting atmosphere. Henry's law describes an approximate relation between dissolved gas concentration and gas partial pressure for suitable dilute conditions. Its proportionality constant depends on the particular gas, liquid, and temperature and has multiple published conventions. The behavior of oxygen and carbon dioxide in natural waters is therefore sensitive to both temperature and atmospheric conditions.

This gives us an environmental example. A warmer lake may hold less dissolved oxygen at saturation, even as the oxygen demands of organisms and decomposition change. That fact matters to aquatic life. It does not by itself calculate oxygen concentration everywhere in the lake: mixing, photosynthesis, respiration, air-water exchange, and stratification also contribute. The chemical equilibrium of one gas is nested inside a changing ecological system.

We can describe dissolved concentration in several ways. Mass per volume, such as milligrams per liter, is natural for monitoring a water sample. Molarity counts moles of solute per liter of finished solution. Molality counts moles per kilogram of solvent. The distinction becomes important when temperature changes volume, when one compares solvents, or when calculating properties that depend on particle number. A ten-gram sample of salt in one hundred grams of water is not a ten-percent-by-mass solution if the percent is defined relative to the total solution: its mass fraction is ten divided by one hundred ten, or about 9.1 percent. Definitions determine arithmetic.

Add some salt to water and its freezing behavior changes. The dissolved ions lower the chemical potential of liquid water relative to pure water under suitable conditions, shifting the temperature at which liquid and solid are in equilibrium. The result is freezing-point depression. It is one of several colligative effects that, in dilute ideal limits, depend mainly on the number of dissolved particles rather than their detailed identities. Because sodium chloride separates into sodium and chloride ions, a mole of dissolved formula units can produce roughly two moles of dissolved ions in the ideal dilute picture. At higher concentrations, interactions and incomplete ideality demand corrections.

A similar counting principle helps explain osmosis. If two solutions are separated by a membrane permeable to water but not to a particular dissolved solute, differences in chemical potential can produce a tendency for solvent movement. The familiar image of water seeking the saltier side is a shorthand, not a universal rule detached from pressure, membrane selectivity, and the identities of the solutes. Osmotic pressure quantifies the pressure difference required to stop net solvent transfer in an idealized setup. Cells, plants, and engineered membranes depend on these consequences of solution chemistry.

Now return to the original glasses. Salt and sugar disappear for different microscopic reasons: one disperses ions, the other disperses largely intact neutral molecules. Oil separates because its interaction with water and the thermodynamics of mixing are different. The chemical formula tells us which atoms are present, but not by itself how the substance behaves in a particular solvent. For that we need bonding, molecular shape, intermolecular forces, and the statistics of many moving particles. Dissolving is the chemistry of relationships: a substance's behavior depends not only on what it is, but what surrounds it.

## Scientific companion: solution formation and measurement

### Models and distinctions

- Dissolution of an ionic solid: $\mathrm{NaCl(s)\rightleftharpoons Na^{+}(aq)+Cl^{-}(aq)}$. The ions are hydrated; the reaction arrow communicates an equilibrium relation, not its exact mechanism or rate.
- For a specified process near constant $T,p$, the Gibbs-energy change $\Delta G=\Delta H-T\Delta S$ determines direction under the usual thermodynamic constraints. A favorable dissolution is not required to be exothermic. The equilibrium solubility also depends on activities, which differ from simple concentrations in nonideal solutions.
- **Molarity:** $c=n_{\mathrm{solute}}/V_{\mathrm{solution}}$ in mol/L. **Molality:** $b=n_{\mathrm{solute}}/m_{\mathrm{solvent}}$ in mol/kg. **Mass fraction:** $w=m_{\mathrm{solute}}/m_{\mathrm{solution}}$.
- Ideal dilute colligative approximation: $\Delta T_f=iK_f b$, where $i$ describes effective solute particle multiplicity; departures from ideal behavior matter at finite ionic strength.

### Worked examples

**Mass fraction.** Dissolve 10.0 g NaCl in 100.0 g water (assuming complete solution at the chosen temperature). Mass fraction $10.0/(110.0)=0.0909$, or about 9.09% by mass.

**Ion counting.** One mole of NaCl formula units gives, under the ideal fully dissociated model, one mole Na⁺ and one mole Cl⁻, not one mole of neutral molecular NaCl dispersed among water molecules. The total ionic charge still sums to zero.

**Rate versus saturation.** Crushing a crystal increases surface area and can speed dissolution. Its equilibrium solubility is a distinct thermodynamic property and is not doubled merely by grinding the crystal.
