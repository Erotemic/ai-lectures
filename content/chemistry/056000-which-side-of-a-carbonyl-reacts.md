# Which Side of a Carbonyl Reacts? Enolates and Competing Sites

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

A carbonyl group usually invites attack at its carbon atom. The oxygen draws electron density away, leaving the carbon electrophilic. But an aldehyde or ketone has another chemical personality. The carbon atom immediately next to the carbonyl—the alpha carbon—can become a nucleophile. Instead of having a new group add to the carbonyl carbon, we can make a carbon-carbon bond beside it. That possibility is one of the most versatile routes for enlarging a molecular skeleton.

The change begins with an alpha hydrogen. A base can remove a hydrogen from a carbon next to a carbonyl group, leaving an anion whose negative charge is stabilized by resonance with the carbonyl oxygen. We call this an enolate. One resonance picture places negative charge on oxygen and a double bond between carbonyl carbon and alpha carbon. Another places negative charge on the alpha carbon and keeps the carbonyl double bond. The actual enolate is not flipping between two separate molecules like a blinking light. These are contributing ways to describe one delocalized electronic system.

An enolate can react through its carbon end or its oxygen end, depending on electrophile, counterion, solvent and conditions. Carbon attack creates a new bond at the alpha carbon. Oxygen attack gives an enol derivative, such as a silyl enol ether under suitable conditions. One reagent may therefore serve carbon-carbon construction while another creates a stable intermediate for a later transformation. A single electronic structure offers different reaction channels.

There is also a neutral relative of the enolate: the enol. An enol has a carbon-carbon double bond adjacent to a hydroxyl group. Ketone and enol forms are tautomers, structures that differ in hydrogen position and double-bond arrangement. For many ordinary ketones, the equilibrium strongly favors the carbonyl form, but a small enol fraction can still react rapidly with an electrophile. This is why an unremarkable bottle of ketone can participate in chemistry that formally occurs at a carbon that seemed saturated in its predominant structure.

Acetone is a simple place to begin. It has a carbonyl in the middle of a three-carbon chain and equivalent methyl groups on either side. Removing an alpha hydrogen from either side leads to the same constitutional enolate. If that enolate attacks a suitable primary alkyl electrophile by an S N two mechanism, we can build a new carbon-carbon bond. But this example conceals a problem because the two sides of acetone are identical. In many other ketones they are not.

Take two-methylcyclohexanone. Around the ring, the carbonyl is adjacent to one carbon that carries a methyl substituent and one that does not. Under suitable conditions, base can remove an alpha hydrogen from either side. These choices create two different enolate connectivities. One is less substituted at its new carbon-carbon double bond; the other is more substituted. Often the more substituted enolate is lower in free energy, but the less substituted enolate can form more rapidly when a strong bulky base encounters a sterically accessible hydrogen.

Here are the two ideas that dominate the conversation. A *kinetic enolate* is the one formed faster under a given regime. A *thermodynamic enolate* is the one favored at equilibrium. The terms are useful summaries, not permanent labels stamped onto a structure. Temperature, solvent, metal counterion, aggregation state, base, and reaction time all affect what mixture forms and whether it equilibrates. A textbook example often uses a strong bulky base at low temperature to favor rapid deprotonation at the less hindered site. Under conditions allowing reversible proton transfer and equilibration, the more stable enolate may predominate. But the details need experimental or well-supported substrate-specific evidence.

Why does this matter in practice? Imagine that we want to put a methyl group at one alpha position of an unsymmetrical ketone. If the wrong enolate is trapped by the electrophile, a new carbon-carbon bond forms in the wrong place. Chemoselectivity might be perfect—the reagent reacts only with the ketone-derived enolate—but regioselectivity is poor. The decision about base and when to introduce the electrophile becomes a decision about final molecular architecture.

A well-controlled alpha alkylation often separates the operations mentally into two stages. First form the desired enolate; then let it react with an appropriate electrophile. The second stage is not unrestricted. Ordinary enolate carbon alkylation with an alkyl halide commonly follows an S N two pathway, so primary or methyl electrophiles are much better candidates than heavily hindered tertiary centers. Secondary substrates may compete through elimination. An electrophile with an acidic proton or a group that strongly reacts with the base introduces still more possibilities.

Now consider what happens if the carbonyl starting material is still present when enolate forms. The enolate can attack the carbonyl of another molecule of the starting material. That is the foundation of an aldol reaction. A chemist attempting alpha alkylation may accidentally allow carbonyl self-condensation. This is one reason a strong base and controlled enolate formation can be used for alkylation, while catalytic quantities of weaker base in the presence of neutral carbonyl are often associated with aldol chemistry. The difference is not a new type of electron pair. It is a different choice of electrophile that the same family of nucleophiles encounters.

A practical example makes the logic clear. Suppose we have cyclohexanone, and the goal is to attach a methyl group at an alpha position. Its enolate can in principle attack a suitable methyl electrophile. If the material is instead mixed under conditions that continually generate a small enolate population in contact with abundant unchanged cyclohexanone, intermolecular carbonyl addition can compete. The actual products depend on concentration and kinetics. To choose conditions intelligently, we must identify the desired electrophile and reduce the enolate's access to competing partners.

The carbonyl itself also influences acid-base behavior. An alpha hydrogen of an ordinary ketone is much more acidic than a hydrogen on an unactivated alkane, because the resulting enolate can delocalize charge. But that does not mean a ketone is a strong acid in water. Complete enolate formation generally calls for a base whose conjugate acid is less acidic than the carbonyl substrate, together with a compatible environment. A weak base may instead make only a small equilibrium population. Percent enolate, reaction rate, and irreversible trapping are different quantities.

We should be wary of a shorthand common in organic drawings. A curved arrow from base to an alpha hydrogen and another from the carbon-hydrogen bond into a carbon-carbon double bond are a useful bookkeeping picture. But in a real system, the base can coordinate to a metal, enolates can aggregate, and the substrate can adopt conformations that change accessible hydrogens. In particular, lithium enolates may behave as coordinated aggregates rather than isolated free anions. That complexity does not invalidate the classroom mechanism. It tells us where a simplified arrow diagram stops being a complete prediction.

How would we find out which enolate formed? One method is to trap it as an enol derivative under conditions that preserve the initially formed distribution, then analyze the products. Another is to react it with an electrophile and characterize positional isomers. Spectroscopic studies can probe aggregated or coordinated forms under carefully chosen conditions. The trapping experiment itself can alter the distribution if it is slower than enolate interconversion. One measurement is not automatic proof of an unobserved intermediate population.

In carbonyl synthesis, the apparent location of reactivity is therefore a controlled choice. The polarized carbonyl carbon is an electrophilic site. The adjacent carbon can be turned into a nucleophilic site. An enolate may exist in more than one regioisomeric form, and each can have competing carbon or oxygen reactions. Knowing the structures and reaction conditions lets us predict a path; measuring products tells us whether the system took it. The carbonyl is more than a functional group. It is a switch that can change the chemical role of the neighboring carbon.

## Scientific companion: enolates and kinetic choices

**Acid-base equilibrium:** \(\mathrm{RCOCH_2R' + B^- \rightleftharpoons RCOCHR'^- + HB}\), with the understanding that the conjugate base is an oxygen/carbon-delocalized enolate. Its equilibrium position depends on relative acidities and medium.

**Enolate resonance shorthand:** \(\mathrm{R-C(=O)-CH_2R' \rightleftharpoons R-C(-O^-)=CHR'}\) represents a conjugate base, *not* a fast reaction between two separate isolated ionic isomers.

**Kinetic versus thermodynamic:** If \(A\) forms faster but \(B\) is lower in free energy, rapid non-equilibrating trapping can emphasize \(A\), while reversible equilibration can favor \(B\). This is not a general rule that low temperature *always* gives one named regioisomer.

**Worked choice:** In 2-methylcyclohexanone, the alpha sites differ: one carries methyl; the other does not. Formation of an enolate at each site leads to distinct alpha-functionalized products. A high preference requires control of base, solvent, temperature and trapping kinetics.

**Electrophile constraint:** Conventional enolate alkylation generally uses primary alkyl halides. Tertiary halides tend toward elimination, not useful backside substitution.
