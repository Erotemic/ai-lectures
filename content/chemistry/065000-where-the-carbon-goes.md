# Where the Carbon Goes: The Citric Acid Cycle as a Working Junction

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

Take one carbon atom in a glucose molecule. You might imagine that if a cell burns glucose, that particular atom travels down a pipe until it leaves as carbon dioxide. The chemistry is less direct. The atom can cross pathways, join new molecular partners, become part of a symmetric intermediate, leave in a later reaction, or be withdrawn to build a different molecule. Following the atom exposes what the familiar circular drawing of the citric acid cycle tends to conceal.

Start where glycolysis leaves us: with pyruvate, a three-carbon molecule. In many aerobic eukaryotic cells, pyruvate enters a mitochondrion and reaches the pyruvate dehydrogenase complex. This machine removes one carbon as carbon dioxide, oxidizes the remaining two-carbon fragment, reduces NAD plus to NADH, and attaches that fragment to coenzyme A. The product, acetyl CoA, carries a two-carbon acetyl group ready for many possible uses. This conversion is not simply a carbon-trimming operation. It is a redox reaction and an activation step performed by a coordinated assembly of enzyme components and cofactors.

The acetyl group can be oxidized in the citric acid cycle, but it can also serve as a building block. Depending on cell type and metabolic state, acetyl CoA may contribute to lipid synthesis, ketone-body metabolism, or other processes. A map that presents acetyl CoA as having only one destination misses its role as a junction between breaking molecules down and making them.

When acetyl CoA enters the cycle, citrate synthase joins its two-carbon acetyl group to a four-carbon acceptor called oxaloacetate. The product, citrate, has six carbons. The reaction releases coenzyme A for reuse. The next enzyme rearranges citrate into isocitrate so that subsequent oxidation and decarboxylation are chemically feasible. Why not simply oxidize a convenient carbon immediately? Because enzymes act on particular molecular geometries and functional groups. The path is a succession of enabled reactions, not a label reading *remove two carbons*.

The cycle then releases two molecules of carbon dioxide during its oxidation steps: first when isocitrate becomes alpha-ketoglutarate, and then when alpha-ketoglutarate becomes succinyl CoA. Along the way NAD plus accepts electrons and becomes NADH. But here is a precise surprise. The two carbon dioxide molecules released during the *first* turn do not ordinarily consist of the two carbon atoms that just arrived in the acetyl group. The incoming acetyl carbon skeleton becomes part of intermediates that persist through the cycle; the first decarboxylations remove carbon positions inherited from the oxaloacetate acceptor. On later turns, the identities of positions become mixed through the cycle, including the symmetry of succinate.

This is exactly the sort of claim that isotope tracers can test. If a scientist labels the two carbons of an incoming acetyl group and follows the earliest carbon dioxide, the pattern does not match the cartoon in which the fresh acetyl carbons are instantly burned away. Over subsequent turns, label can appear in carbon dioxide as the pool is cycled and mixed. There are several details depending on which carbon is labeled and how the metabolite pools are supplied, but the main lesson survives: a cycle's net reaction does not specify the individual history of every atom.

After the two decarboxylations, succinyl CoA is converted to succinate in a reaction that can yield GTP or ATP by substrate-level phosphorylation. Succinate is then oxidized to fumarate, transferring reducing equivalents through FAD, and fumarate is hydrated to malate. Finally, malate is oxidized back to oxaloacetate with another reduction of NAD plus. The four-carbon acceptor is regenerated. That is why the loop can process another acetyl group without having to synthesize an entirely new oxaloacetate molecule for every turn.

The conventional tally for one acetyl group is three NADH, one reduced FAD-associated equivalent, and one GTP or ATP, along with two carbon dioxide molecules. The cycle itself does not make large amounts of ATP directly. It creates reduced electron carriers that can support oxidative phosphorylation when the surrounding respiratory machinery and an appropriate terminal electron acceptor are available. Oxygen is not a reactant in any of the citric acid cycle's individual core steps. Yet the cycle typically depends on oxygen indirectly in aerobic cells because respiratory electron transport must reoxidize NADH and maintain carrier availability.

The language of a cycle can create another misconception. Imagine that citrate, alpha-ketoglutarate, succinate, malate, and oxaloacetate are beads that move together on a rotating wheel. In reality, each is a separate molecular pool. Enzymes transform individual molecules; some intermediates diffuse, some interact with enzyme complexes, and each pool can receive or lose material through other reactions. The order of reactions is cyclic because oxaloacetate is regenerated, not because an identifiable bead is guaranteed to remain on one closed circular track.

Suppose a cell withdraws alpha-ketoglutarate to help make glutamate, or draws oxaloacetate toward aspartate formation. These are *cataplerotic* uses: withdrawing cycle intermediates for biosynthesis. If withdrawal continues without replacement, the cycle loses the pool of intermediates it needs to keep condensing acetyl CoA. A cell must replenish them through *anaplerotic* reactions. A familiar example is pyruvate carboxylase, which uses ATP and bicarbonate-derived carbon to convert pyruvate into oxaloacetate. The replenishment is energetically costly but makes the network able to maintain its functions.

Let's build a simplified numerical case. Suppose one hundred acetyl units arrive per minute and the cycle is otherwise able to process them. Now the cell diverts ten molecules of oxaloacetate equivalents per minute into synthesis. If it does not replace them, the concentrations of the cycle's intermediates cannot remain steady indefinitely. A compensating anaplerotic flux of ten oxaloacetate equivalents per minute can, in the simplest balance, offset that withdrawal. This does not mean that each individual molecule travels once around a perfect loop. It means the net carbon inventories can remain compatible with continued cycling.

The diagram also hides regulation. If electron carriers accumulate in the reduced state because respiration has slowed, several cycle oxidations become less favorable or slower. If ATP demand increases and respiratory function responds, oxidized cofactors become more available and flux may rise. In some tissues calcium signals alter the activity of metabolic enzymes when work demand changes. Substrate supply, cofactor ratios, enzyme activities, transport, and withdrawal all contribute. There is no universal rotation speed built into the cycle.

What if a microbe operates the network in a different ecological setting? Some organisms run variants of central carbon cycles with missing or alternative enzymes. Some use parts of the cycle predominantly to make biomass rather than oxidize incoming fuel. Others can operate a *reductive* citric acid cycle to build organic carbon from carbon dioxide, when they possess the necessary machinery and an appropriate energy and electron supply. The familiar cycle is a powerful central motif, not a single wiring diagram shared identically by every organism.

All of this helps explain why measuring carbon dioxide release alone is not enough to reconstruct metabolism. Carbon dioxide can arise from pyruvate dehydrogenase, steps of the citric acid cycle, the oxidative pentose phosphate pathway, or other decarboxylations. Conversely, carbon fixed by a carboxylase may enter a metabolic pool without immediately becoming biomass. A scientist who wants to assign fluxes must combine gas exchange, substrate consumption, product formation, isotope patterns, and assumptions about the network.

Return to our labeled glucose carbon. We can say that glycolysis redistributes the six carbons into pyruvate without losing any. Pyruvate dehydrogenase can remove a particular pyruvate carbon before the acetyl group enters the cycle. The cycle then rearranges and oxidizes organic carbon, returns an acceptor, and supplies both energy carriers and biosynthetic building blocks. The fate of one carbon atom depends on which path the cell actually takes.

The citric acid cycle is therefore not merely a furnace attached to glycolysis. It is an intersection where energy harvesting and construction compete for the same molecular traffic. Its continued operation depends on regenerating the acceptor, reoxidizing the electron carriers, and replenishing whatever intermediates biosynthesis withdraws. The cycle becomes much more understandable once we stop thinking of it as a circle on paper and start thinking about atoms moving through an active network.

## Scientific companion: carbon, electrons and anaplerosis

**Acetyl entry:** \(\mathrm{pyruvate + CoA + NAD^+ \rightarrow acetyl{-}CoA + CO_2 + NADH + H^+}\), omitting specific protonation details.

**One idealized turn:** acetyl-CoA + 3 NAD⁺ + FAD + GDP + Pᵢ + 2 H₂O → 2 CO₂ + 3 NADH + FADH₂ equivalents + GTP + CoA (plus protons depending on conventions). FAD is tightly associated with succinate dehydrogenase, and its reducing equivalents are passed onward to the respiratory chain.

**Position-specific carbon warning:** Two CO₂ molecules leave during a turn, but the newly introduced acetyl carbons are not the atoms normally decarboxylated on the first turn; labels can be redistributed on later turns. The stoichiometric net equation alone cannot establish positional isotope fates.

**Anaplerosis:** \(\mathrm{pyruvate + HCO_3^- + ATP \rightarrow oxaloacetate + ADP + P_i}\) is an important replenishment reaction (with other substrates and protonation details suppressed). Anaplerotic input is needed when intermediates leave the cycle for biosynthesis or other demands.

**Worked pool balance:** An intermediate pool receives 8 nmol/min by anaplerotic conversion, and 8 nmol/min leaves by biosynthetic withdrawal. Net concentration change from those two processes is zero, even though both fluxes are nonzero. If withdrawal rises to 11 while input stays 8, the pool decreases at 3 nmol/min until conditions change.
