# What a Functional Group Changes: From Alcohols to Amides

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

Two liquids can have nearly the same number of atoms and completely different behavior. One mixes readily with water; another separates into a layer. One can donate a proton under ordinary conditions; another almost never does. One evaporates readily; another remains in the bottle. Instead of learning every substance as a disconnected exception, organic chemists look for arrangements of atoms that repeatedly produce recognizable patterns. These arrangements are called functional groups.

Start with a two-carbon skeleton. Attach a hydroxyl group, oxygen bonded to hydrogen, to one of the carbons, and we obtain ethanol. Arrange the same molecular inventory so the oxygen sits between two carbon groups, and we obtain dimethyl ether. Both have molecular formula C two H six O. Yet ethanol molecules can donate and accept hydrogen bonds to one another, while ordinary dimethyl ether molecules can accept hydrogen bonds but lack an oxygen-bound hydrogen to donate them in the same way. The resulting differences in liquid structure contribute to their very different boiling points. A functional group alters what interactions a molecule can make without changing the fact that it contains carbon, hydrogen, and oxygen.

The word functional does not mean that the rest of the molecule is chemically irrelevant. A hydroxyl group on a tiny carbon skeleton has different solubility consequences from a hydroxyl group attached to a very long hydrocarbon chain. Small alcohols can mix extensively with water because their polar region is a substantial part of the molecule. As the hydrophobic carbon framework grows, that region may no longer dominate. We need to hold two ideas at once: similar groups often react in related ways, and the molecular context can change the actual outcome.

An alcohol has a carbon bearing an O-H group, ordinarily written R-O-H, where R stands for an organic group. An ether has an oxygen connected to two carbon-containing groups, R-O-R prime. A phenol has an O-H group connected directly to an aromatic ring. Phenol is not simply an alcohol with a decorative hexagon. Its conjugate base can distribute negative charge into the aromatic system, changing its acidity. An ordinary aliphatic alcohol is a much weaker acid in water than phenol. The comparison follows from what happens to the whole molecule after proton transfer, not from a rule that oxygen atoms always have the same behavior.

Nitrogen gives another family. Amines can bear one, two, or three carbon substituents on nitrogen in familiar primary, secondary, and tertiary forms. Many amines act as bases because a nitrogen lone pair can bind a proton. An amine that becomes protonated may be far more water-soluble as an ionic salt than its neutral version. This is one reason the pH of a formulation can affect how a compound partitions between water and a nonpolar phase. It is also why charged and uncharged forms of a drug may behave differently in a biological environment, although permeability and distribution depend on much more than charge alone.

A useful warning arrives with the amide. An amide also contains nitrogen, but its nitrogen is bonded to a carbonyl carbon, a carbon that has a double bond to oxygen. The nitrogen lone pair can interact with the carbonyl electronic system. As a result, an ordinary amide nitrogen is far less basic than an ordinary amine nitrogen and the bond between nitrogen and the carbonyl carbon has partial double-bond character. The amide group is approximately planar. It resists free rotation more than a typical carbon-carbon single bond. Proteins contain amide linkages, called peptide bonds, and this constrained geometry contributes to how chains of amino acids can fold. The distinction between amine and amide is not a small naming detail; it is a difference in electron distribution.

The carbonyl group itself is a major landmark. It contains a carbon atom double-bonded to oxygen. Oxygen pulls electron density toward itself, leaving the carbonyl carbon relatively electron-poor and receptive to attack by some electron-rich species. But the atoms attached to that carbonyl carbon matter. If one attached group is hydrogen and the other is carbon, the substance is an aldehyde. If both groups are carbons, it is a ketone. If an oxygen-containing group is attached to the carbonyl carbon, we move toward carboxylic acids and their derivatives. These subclasses react differently because they differ in crowding, electron distribution, and the groups that can leave during a reaction.

Acetone is a small ketone. It has a carbonyl group between two methyl groups. It is a good solvent for many substances and mixes with water in all proportions under ordinary conditions. Its oxygen can accept hydrogen bonds from water, while its small hydrocarbon portions do not overwhelm those interactions. Acetone does not have to form strong hydrogen bonds with itself as a donor to show good water miscibility. Compare that with a much larger ketone: a carbonyl group is still present, but the long nonpolar framework can sharply reduce solubility. Knowing the functional group alone is not enough; knowing it is essential.

A carboxylic acid has a carbonyl carbon bonded to an O-H group. In a structural formula, that same carbon is double-bonded to one oxygen and single-bonded to a second oxygen bearing hydrogen. The proton on its hydroxyl can be donated to water much more readily than the corresponding proton of an ordinary alcohol. The resulting carboxylate has charge shared across two oxygens through its electronic structure. Acetic acid, present in vinegar, illustrates the basic idea. A sufficiently basic solution favors acetate, its negatively charged conjugate base; an acidic solution favors the protonated form. The proportions are set by equilibrium, not by a molecule permanently deciding to be an acid.

Replace the acid's hydroxyl with an oxygen connected to a carbon group, and we obtain an ester. Esters occur in many fragrances, fats, polymers, and synthetic intermediates. Some are volatile and have characteristic odors, but ester is not a synonym for a pleasant smell. The electronic environment around the carbonyl differs from that of a ketone, and the molecule contains a group that can sometimes be displaced in reactions at the carbonyl carbon. Esters can undergo hydrolysis to acid-derived products and alcohol-derived products, although reaction rate and equilibrium depend strongly on conditions.

Acid anhydrides and acid halides are also carboxylic acid derivatives, often more reactive toward suitable nucleophiles than ordinary esters and amides. Why? An incoming nucleophile can add to the electrophilic carbonyl carbon, followed by departure of an attached group. The willingness of that group to leave and the electronic stabilization of the starting material matter. Some groups that are poor leaving groups must first be protonated or otherwise activated. Merely seeing an oxygen atom beside a carbonyl does not tell us that a bond will break in water the moment the bottle is opened.

An alkene, the carbon-carbon double bond, is another functional region. It can take part in electrophilic addition because its pi electrons can interact with electron-poor reactants. A carbon-carbon triple bond, an alkyne, has its own geometry and characteristic reactions. An aromatic ring behaves differently from an isolated alkene. A molecule containing both an aromatic ring and an alkene therefore has two chemically distinct regions that may respond differently to the same reagents. Organic chemistry is often the art of recognizing several potentially reactive sites and asking which one will react first.

Halogen atoms bonded to carbon produce another useful family, often called organohalides or alkyl halides when attached to saturated carbon. A carbon-halogen bond is polarized, and certain halogens can depart as anions during substitution or elimination. But a carbon-bromine bond on a saturated carbon is not equivalent to a bromine bonded directly to an aromatic ring. Bonding environment, electronic stabilization, and possible mechanisms change the behavior. Again, a functional group is a clue, not a complete sentence.

Sulfur provides thiols and thioethers, among many other groups. A thiol contains S-H; two thiols can be oxidized to form a disulfide bond. Disulfide connections can help stabilize extracellular proteins, yet not every protein relies on them. Sulfur differs from oxygen in atomic size, polarizability, and its range of accessible oxidation states. One cannot replace every oxygen with sulfur and expect a slightly heavier copy of the same chemistry.

Many biologically important molecules carry multiple functional groups. An amino acid typically has at least an amine-related group and a carboxylic acid-related group, and often another chemically important side chain. Depending on pH, it can bear both positive and negative charges at once. Sugars carry multiple hydroxyl groups and, in their open-chain forms, a carbonyl group. Their ring structures and stereochemistry change what reactions they can undergo. Functional-group thinking is most useful when combined with a map of the whole molecule.

Imagine we are given a molecule for the first time. We can build an initial hypothesis without memorizing its complete name. Look for O-H donors, nitrogen bases, carbonyl carbons, double bonds, and groups that might leave. Ask whether the skeleton is small or hydrophobic, whether it is rigid or flexible, and whether charges change with pH. Then test the hypothesis against a measurement: solubility, acidity, a spectrum, or a reaction. A functional group is not a magic sticker that assigns a fixed behavior. It is a recurring part of a physical system, and knowing how it shifts electrons and interactions makes unfamiliar chemistry understandable.

## Scientific companion: group identity versus context

| Class | Representative connectivity | Typical structural implication |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Alcohol | $\mathrm{R-OH}$, O-H on saturated-carbon framework | Can donate and accept H bonds; may be oxidized depending on structure |
| Ether | $\mathrm{R-O-R'}$ | H-bond acceptor, not donor in absence of another group |
| Amine | $\mathrm{RNH_2}$, $\mathrm{R_2NH}$ or $\mathrm{R_3N}$ | Often basic; protonation increases ionic character |
| Aldehyde | $\mathrm{R-CH{=}O}$ | Polar carbonyl, often accepts nucleophilic addition |
| Ketone | $\mathrm{R-C(=O)-R'}$ | Polar carbonyl, often accepts nucleophilic addition |
| Carboxylic acid | $\mathrm{R-C(=O)-OH}$ | Weak acid, resonance-stabilized carboxylate |
| Ester | $\mathrm{R-C(=O)-OR'}$ | Acyl substitution possible; hydrolysis can be catalyzed |
| Amide | $\mathrm{R-C(=O)-NR'_2}$ | Delocalization reduces N basicity, restricts rotation |
| Thiol | $\mathrm{R-SH}$ | Oxidation can form disulfides |

**Acid/base example.** For a simple monoprotonic weak acid $\mathrm{HA \rightleftharpoons H^+ + A^-}$ under dilute approximate conditions,

$$\frac{[\mathrm{A^-}]}{[\mathrm{HA}]}\approx 10^{\mathrm{pH}-\mathrm{p}K_a}.$$

At pH two units above an acid's pKa, the deprotonated form can predominate approximately 100:1 in that simplified model. Activity corrections and multiple ionizations can matter outside the basic approximation. An amine's conjugate acid uses an analogous Henderson-Hasselbalch relation, but the charged species is on the opposite side of the amine protonation equilibrium.

**Amide versus amine.** The nitrogen lone pair in a conventional amide is delocalized into the carbonyl system, lowering its availability for accepting a proton and giving partial C-N double-bond character. Do not treat all nitrogen-containing molecules as comparably basic.
