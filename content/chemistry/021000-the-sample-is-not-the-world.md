# The Sample Is Not the World: What Chemical Measurement Actually Represents

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

A laboratory report may say that a river contains five micrograms of a substance per liter. It sounds like a property of the river, as definite as its name. But which river water? Water flowing near the bank or in the fast current? Water taken before a storm or an hour after? Water that passed through a filter or water with suspended particles still in it? And what happened between the river and the instrument that produced the number?

These are not preliminary administrative questions. They are part of the chemistry. Before we can ask how accurately an instrument detects a compound, we need to know what the measurement is intended to describe. A sample is a small, handled piece of a much larger and often changing system. Analytical chemistry begins by negotiating that gap.

Imagine that you are asked to measure the amount of copper in a lake. You could lower a clean bottle from a boat, collect water and deliver it to a laboratory. The instrument might make a very precise measurement of copper in that bottle. Yet the lake has depth, currents, inflowing streams, sediments, living organisms and seasonal turnover. A single bottle cannot automatically represent the average copper concentration of the entire lake. It can represent the water collected at a particular place and time, under a particular procedure. Whether that is the answer to the original question depends on how the question was defined.

Analytical chemists use the term measurand for the quantity intended to be measured. It is more specific than a vague phrase such as 'copper in the lake.' We might mean dissolved copper concentration in surface water at a station at noon; total recoverable copper in a monthly composite; or mass of copper per kilogram of dry sediment in a defined area. Those are different measurands. A result can be accurate for one and irrelevant to another.

This difference is especially visible in the word dissolved. In many water-monitoring methods, dissolved means a fraction that passes through a specified filter under specified handling conditions. A common operational filter pore size is roughly four tenths of a micrometer. That cut does not perfectly separate every individual ion from every colloid, nor is it a universal chemical boundary between soluble and insoluble material. Very fine particles or colloids may pass, while some substances can adhere to the filter. The operational definition is useful precisely because it tells other workers what was actually measured.

Time matters just as much. Consider a stream receiving runoff from a road. Most of a contaminant might arrive in a short pulse at the beginning of a storm. A bottle taken at noon the following day could show low concentration while missing the episode that carried most of the daily contaminant load. A time composite, combining portions at intervals, answers a different question from a single grab sample. A flow-proportional composite can answer another: how much material passed the sampling point as the stream discharge rose and fell. To estimate a transported mass, concentration must be combined with the volume of water that carried it.

Now imagine a soil investigation. Four fifths of a site has two milligrams of a metal per kilogram of soil. The remaining fifth has twenty-two. The mass-weighted average, assuming equal soil mass per area, is six milligrams per kilogram. A single scoop from the contaminated patch reports twenty-two; a single scoop elsewhere reports two. Neither result is chemically wrong. Neither alone estimates the whole-site average. A sampling design has to reflect the spatial pattern and the decision being made. Random locations, systematic grids, or separate samples from different kinds of terrain can all be defensible, but they answer different questions and have different uncertainties.

The most precise balance in the world cannot repair a badly chosen scoop of soil. If variation between locations is larger than variation between repeated instrument readings, taking the same prepared sample to the instrument ten more times may tell us very little about the site. We need repeated primary samples, or another defensible study of spatial variation. This is why uncertainty from sampling can dominate uncertainty from the actual chemical determination.

There is another gap between the world and the detector: preparation. A solid may be ground and mixed before a small mass is taken for analysis. A water sample may be filtered, acidified, extracted into a solvent, or concentrated. A food sample may be dried, digested, or dissolved. Every step has a purpose, and each can change what reaches the instrument. Grinding improves uniformity but can also heat a sensitive material or introduce contamination from the equipment. Extraction may recover most of the target molecule without recovering all of it. A digestion may measure the total amount of an element while losing the information about which chemical compounds originally contained that element.

Suppose a groundwater sample contains iron in a reduced form. If air enters the bottle and the sample stands for hours, some of that iron can be oxidized. Solids can form; iron may leave the water phase. A laboratory measurement the next day might faithfully describe the altered bottle, not the oxidation state that existed underground. Likewise, a volatile organic compound can escape into a headspace if the collection and storage procedure allow it. Light can change photosensitive molecules. Microorganisms can consume or produce chemical species while a sample waits. Sample preservation is therefore not one universal instruction to 'keep it cold.' It is a method-specific strategy informed by which species we intend to measure.

It also matters what preservation does. Adding acid may help keep certain metals in solution for a total or acid-recoverable element measurement. But the same addition can destroy information about the original acidity or the chemical forms of a pH-sensitive compound. We cannot preserve every property of the original water with one treatment. Different measurands may require separate bottles, separate methods and measurements made immediately in the field.

When contamination is possible, chemists analyze blanks. A field blank may be clean material that passes through the field collection and handling process. An equipment blank may reveal residues introduced by a sampler. A laboratory reagent blank tests a later part of the procedure. If a target compound appears in a blank, that observation does not automatically prove every sample result is invalid. It reveals a pathway by which some apparent signal could have originated outside the sampled environment. We have to decide whether the blank contribution is negligible, can be corrected with defensible uncertainty, or requires new sampling.

A second quality-control idea is recovery. We can add a known quantity of a target substance to a suitable sample and see how much the full procedure recovers. Suppose we add one hundred nanograms and the extraction, cleanup and measurement account for eighty-five. The recovery is eighty-five percent in that experiment. That may reveal loss or a response effect, but it is not a universal correction factor. A freshly added spike may behave differently from a contaminant that has spent years bound inside particles. Recovery must be interpreted in the context of the actual sample and the claim being made.

The word matrix describes everything in a sample other than the particular analyte we want to measure. A glass of clean water is one matrix; seawater rich in salts is another; blood plasma, soil extract and burned food are others. The matrix can prevent an analyte from being extracted, change its chemical form, contribute overlapping signals, or alter instrument response. Two samples with the same amount of the target substance can produce different detector signals because the surrounding materials are different. That difference is not necessarily random error. It may be a systematic source of bias.

We have now encountered several meanings of 'repeat.' We can reread the same vial to estimate short-term instrument repeatability. We can prepare separate portions from the same homogenized material to estimate preparation variability. We can collect independent samples from a stream on the same day to estimate environmental sampling variability. We can return on many days to examine temporal change. Calling all these 'replicates' without specifying which steps are repeated hides the reason for doing the work.

There is no perfectly representative sample in the abstract. Representation is always relative to a question. If a contaminant occurs only in a brief hazardous pulse, averaging it over a month might obscure the phenomenon we need to detect. If the goal is annual mass discharged to an estuary, obsessively measuring the peak concentration without measuring water flow can be equally misleading. Sampling is a kind of experimental design: decide what you need to know, identify where variation can enter, and then collect evidence appropriate to that decision.

The U.S. Geological Survey maintains detailed procedures for sampling and processing water because measurements made years apart and by different crews must remain interpretable. The international Eurachem guidance on uncertainty arising from sampling goes further: when the reported value concerns a larger sampling target, the uncertainties of sampling and laboratory analysis belong in the same account. Neither stage automatically deserves all the attention simply because one involves a sophisticated instrument.

Suppose two laboratories report apparently conflicting results for the same river. One analyzes filtered water during low flow; the other measures an unfiltered bottle during a storm. Before accusing either instrument of failure, ask what was collected. Even if the samples had identical labels, their actual measurands might be different. If the measurement questions genuinely match, ask next about storage, transport, extraction, calibration and the uncertainties assigned to each step.

The lesson is that a chemical number is the final link in a long chain of choices. It comes from a particular material, at a particular place and time, with a defined treatment, a detector and a calculation. A strong analytical result does not erase that chain. It makes the chain visible. Once we know what traveled from the world into the instrument, we can ask how the instrument turns that material into evidence.

## Scientific companion: define the target before the assay

**Example of a spatial average.** With 80% of equal-mass area at 2 mg/kg and 20% at 22 mg/kg,

$$\bar c=0.8(2)+0.2(22)=6\ \mathrm{mg/kg}.$$

A single sample from either zone is a valid result for that portion, not an estimator of the whole without an appropriate design. Equal-mass area is an explicit simplifying assumption; real site weighting must use the amount of soil represented.

**Load versus concentration.** An instantaneous transported load rate can be written $\dot m(t)=c(t)Q(t)$ when $c$ is mass per volume and $Q$ is volume per time. Total load is $\int c(t)Q(t)\,dt$. Averaging concentration without considering $Q(t)$ generally does not give the flow-weighted average.

**Operation-dependent definitions.** Filtered, unfiltered, acid-extractable, and total-digested results are different chemical measurements. Methods must specify pore size, storage temperature, preservation, extraction and timing as appropriate. No one filtering protocol universally defines a chemical species.

**Sampling uncertainty.** A simple variance decomposition may distinguish instrument, preparation and sampling contributions, but independence and homogeneity are assumptions to verify. Compare independent primary samples with repeated readings of one vial to learn which variance component matters.
