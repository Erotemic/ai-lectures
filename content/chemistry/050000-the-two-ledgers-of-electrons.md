# The Two Ledgers of Electrons: NADH, NADPH, and Flavins

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

When an animal eats, some electrons that began in the chemical bonds of food eventually reach oxygen. That transfer is central to respiration. But food oxidation is not the whole story of metabolism. Cells must also build molecules, repair damage, and protect themselves against oxidants. The electron carriers that participate in these activities are chemically related, yet they are not all maintained in the same state or assigned the same task.

Take NAD, nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide. The oxidized form is conventionally called NAD plus and the reduced form NADH. One useful way to think about its chemistry is that the nicotinamide ring can accept a hydride equivalent from another molecule: two electrons and one hydrogen nucleus arrive together at a reactive position. Depending on the actual reaction, a further proton may be released to the solution. This differs from an iron-sulfur center that commonly transfers one electron at a time. Electron accounting must pay attention to the carrier's mode of transfer.

In many degradative pathways, a carbon-containing molecule is oxidized as NAD plus is reduced to NADH. A dehydrogenase enzyme supplies the chemistry and specifies the substrate. The resulting NADH can later donate reducing equivalents into a respiratory chain or another metabolic reaction. NAD plus is regenerated and can participate again. It is therefore a recyclable carrier, not a permanent storage tank filled once by digesting food.

Now consider NADP, a closely related molecule that differs from NAD by an additional phosphate group on the adenosine side, not on the reactive nicotinamide carbon. That extra phosphate does not greatly change the fundamental hydride chemistry. Its enormous importance is in molecular recognition. Enzymes that prefer NADP can distinguish it from NAD and route electrons through a different metabolic network. The reduced form NADPH is frequently used to supply electrons for biosynthesis and defense against oxidation.

This is sometimes taught as a rule: NADH is for energy production and NADPH is for building molecules. That rule captures a broad trend but is not universal. Different organisms and compartments use exceptions. Some enzyme pathways couple the pools through transhydrogenases and other mechanisms. What matters is that the cell often maintains contrasting redox ratios, with a relatively oxidized NAD pool suited to accepting electrons in many catabolic reactions and a relatively reduced NADP pool suited to donating electrons in many biosynthetic and protective reactions.

The state of a pool is a thermodynamic variable. Consider the ratio of oxidized to reduced forms for a specified couple. It enters the Nernst equation, affecting the actual reduction potential in that environment. Two copies of the same redox cofactor in different compartments or at different ratios can have different capacities to drive a reaction. Calling NADH a strong reductant without naming its conditions is an abbreviation, not a complete chemical statement.

Where does NADPH come from? Depending on cell type, the oxidative pentose phosphate pathway can supply it by oxidizing sugar-derived intermediates. Other enzyme systems can produce NADPH from other metabolites or transfer reducing equivalents between cofactors. In photosynthetic organisms, light-driven electron transport helps reduce NADP plus. These pathways do not simply create reducing power; they divert electrons from another source and pay for a particular electron distribution.

An excellent application is cellular defense. Some enzymes use NADPH to restore antioxidant systems to a reduced state after those systems have reacted with oxidants. Glutathione is a familiar example. Reduced glutathione can participate in reactions that help remove peroxides, becoming oxidized in the process. Glutathione reductase uses NADPH-derived electrons to regenerate reduced glutathione. The cycle is meaningful only as a network: the oxidant is consumed, the antioxidant is restored, and NADPH must be replenished from metabolism.

Here is a calculation worth doing conceptually. Imagine two hypothetical reaction compartments holding exactly the same chemical NAD couple but maintaining different proportions of NAD plus and NADH. The first contains one hundred oxidized molecules for every reduced molecule. The second contains one oxidized molecule for every one hundred reduced molecules. The chemical structure of the cofactor has not changed, but the relative abundance of the two states changes the reduction potential and the direction in which that pool can most readily participate. Under a simplified two-electron Nernst model, changing the ratio by a factor of ten changes the potential by about thirty millivolts near room temperature, with the sign determined by the ratio convention. Changing the ratio by ten thousand changes it by roughly a tenth of a volt. Those are not negligible differences in a sequence of redox reactions.

The comparison is intentionally schematic. Real cells are not closed bottles containing only two unbound cofactor species. pH, enzyme binding, ionic activity, compartmentation, and transport affect the chemical potentials. NADP and NAD are also kept in separate biochemical pools that interact through regulated reactions. But the calculation shows why a cell can arrange two closely related molecules to serve different energetic purposes. It controls a network's state, not just its molecular inventory.

A useful further distinction is between a two-electron reduction and a hydrogenation. When NAD plus accepts a hydride, a substrate loses a two-electron equivalent, but the net formula of a particular enzymatic reaction may involve water and acid-base changes elsewhere. Simply counting hydrogen atoms on the cofactor will not determine the fate of every proton in the solution. We must balance the full reaction, including the conditions under which the biochemical standard potential is defined.

A redox carrier is not simply an electron bucket. Chemical selectivity matters. A dehydrogenase positions the substrate so that transfer occurs at a particular carbon or bond. Enzymes can discriminate between faces of a planar nicotinamide ring and between competing metabolites. The carrier sets up a broadly reusable chemical transformation, while the protein chooses the particular transaction. A cell avoids indiscriminate reactions in water by using enzymes to mediate donor-acceptor contact.

Flavins offer another kind of versatility. Flavin mononucleotide and flavin adenine dinucleotide contain a chemically active ring derived from riboflavin. Flavins can participate in one-electron as well as two-electron transformations. Their intermediate one-electron states, often called semiquinones, allow a flavin-containing protein to connect a two-electron substrate reaction to one-electron metal-center relays. The enzyme environment strongly influences which states are stabilized and how readily side reactions occur.

Return to respiratory complex one. NADH provides two electrons, and a flavin group helps receive them before they proceed along iron-sulfur centers. Without the flavin step, it would be harder to explain the electronic choreography between the two-electron donor and the successive one-electron carriers. A similar distinction appears in many oxidoreductases, where the cofactor's versatility allows reactions with chemically different substrates and acceptors.

But an electron carrier is only useful when its reduced and oxidized forms can both be regenerated. Suppose one pathway continuously reduces NAD plus, while no other pathway oxidizes NADH. The NAD plus pool would become depleted and the original oxidizing reaction would slow, even if its substrate remained plentiful. This is an important reason fermentation exists: some pathways recycle NADH to NAD plus using internal organic electron acceptors when an external respiratory route is absent or insufficient. Fermentation is not just a primitive version of breathing. It is an electron-balancing strategy compatible with different ecological conditions.

Follow the carbon ledger and the electron ledger together. Glycolysis changes the chemical structures of sugars and produces ATP by substrate-level phosphorylation in certain steps. It also transfers reducing equivalents to NADH. If we account only for ATP, we miss what happens to the electrons. If we account only for NADH, we miss carbon products and phosphate transfer. A complete chemical equation must conserve atoms and charge; a complete metabolic explanation must also specify which cofactors are replenished so the pathway can continue.

The distinction between NADH and NADPH is especially valuable in synthetic biology and biotechnology. Redirecting carbon into a desired product may require more NADPH than the host organism normally supplies. Increasing the amount of an enzyme will not solve a pathway whose electron donor is lacking. Engineers may have to redesign precursor supply, cofactor specificity, transhydrogenase activity, or respiratory demand. This is the practical meaning of metabolic bookkeeping: an apparently carbon-balanced synthesis can still be redox-infeasible.

We have met several kinds of biological electronic currency: one-electron iron-sulfur relays, hydride carriers such as NADH and NADPH, and flavins that can bridge distinct modes of transfer. There is no universal wire running from food to every useful reaction. Cells maintain differentiated pools, direct them with enzymes, and regenerate each state through networked chemistry. To understand metabolism, trace not only where atoms go, but which molecules surrendered the electrons and which molecules will supply the next ones.

## Scientific companion: redox pools as state variables

**Hydride bookkeeping.** A simplified dehydrogenase reaction is $\mathrm{RH_2+NAD^+\rightleftharpoons R+NADH+H^+}$. The precise substrate and proton balance depend on the enzyme. For NADP, the reactive nicotinamide chemistry is analogous, while recognition differs because of the extra phosphate.

**Nernst reasoning.** For $\mathrm{Ox+2e^-+mH^+\rightleftharpoons Red}$, the potential depends on the activities of oxidized and reduced forms and on pH where protons participate. At fixed pH and with the appropriate transformed standard state, $E=E^{\circ\prime}+(RT/2F)\ln([\mathrm{Ox}]/[\mathrm{Red}])$ is a useful simplified relation. Pools can have different potentials at distinct ratios even for related cofactors.

**A stoichiometric exercise.** An oxidation that transfers two electrons reduces one NAD+ to NADH. If 10 such events occur, regenerating all 10 NAD+ molecules requires oxidation of ten NADH molecules through some compatible pathway. It need not be respiration; fermentation and other acceptor routes can provide recycling.

**Flavin chemistry.** FMN and FAD can accommodate two-electron changes through quinone, semiquinone, and hydroquinone-like states. Exact protonation and redox potentials depend strongly on the enzyme pocket.
