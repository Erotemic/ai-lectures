# How Chemistry Produces Voltage: Electrons, Ions, and Galvanic Cells

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

A battery can light a lamp without a flame. It can move energy from one place to another through a wire while its chemicals change inside a container. At first that seems like a different subject from ordinary chemistry. In fact, a battery makes one part of chemical reaction accounting visible. Some reactions transfer electrons from one chemical species to another. If we arrange the reactants so that the electron transfer occurs through an external circuit, the same thermodynamic tendency that would otherwise produce chemical products can do electrical work.

The key word is redox, short for reduction and oxidation. Oxidation means loss of electrons by a species, and reduction means gain of electrons. The two are coupled: electrons cannot disappear from the universe. If a metal atom becomes a positively charged ion by leaving electrons behind, another species must ultimately accept the transferred electrons, or the charges must be stored elsewhere in an explicitly accounted process. In ordinary batteries, the loss and gain occur in coordinated electrode reactions. The electron ledger and charge ledger must both balance.

Consider zinc metal in contact with a solution containing copper ions. In an appropriate chemical arrangement, zinc atoms can become dissolved zinc ions while copper ions accept electrons and become copper metal. The net reaction exchanges the zinc metal for copper metal and transfers two electrons per zinc atom oxidized. If these species are allowed to react directly at the same surface, energy can be dissipated locally and copper may deposit on the zinc. If we separate the oxidation and reduction reactions into two connected regions, the electrons can flow through a wire between them. The reaction's tendency to proceed is now available as a source of electrical work.

This is a galvanic, or voltaic, cell. One electrode is where oxidation occurs: the anode. The other is where reduction occurs: the cathode. In a spontaneously discharging galvanic cell the anode is typically the negative terminal and the cathode the positive terminal. But the names anode and cathode refer to the reactions, not to permanent signs. In an electrolytic cell driven by an external power source, the electrical terminal signs can be reversed while oxidation still occurs at the anode and reduction at the cathode. Memorizing only the signs is an invitation to confusion.

Let's follow the charges carefully. At the zinc electrode, zinc atoms enter the solution as zinc ions, leaving electrons in the conducting electrode. Those electrons travel through the external conductor toward the copper electrode when the circuit is closed. At the copper electrode, copper ions in solution gain electrons and become copper atoms. But the internal solutions must also permit ionic charge transport. Without an ionic path, the separation of charge would rapidly oppose further reaction. A salt bridge, porous separator or ion-conducting electrolyte allows charge balance to be maintained while the chemical transformations proceed.

In an idealized two-beaker zinc–copper example, oxidation makes the zinc-side solution more positively charged unless counteracting ions move. Anions from the salt bridge can migrate toward that side. On the copper side, reduction removes positive copper ions; cations from the salt bridge can help maintain charge balance there. The actual flow pattern depends on which ions are mobile and on the cell's geometry. What matters conceptually is that electrons carry charge through much of the external metallic circuit while ions carry charge through the electrolyte. A conventional diagram that leaves out the ionic return path is physically incomplete.

What determines the electrical potential difference? It comes from the electrochemical potentials of the species participating in the two electrode processes. In a reversible idealization at open circuit, the cell voltage reflects the maximum electrical work available per unit of charge transferred by the net reaction. The unit volt means joule per coulomb. A cell with a potential difference of one volt can, ideally, provide one joule of electrical work for each coulomb of charge transported through the external circuit. That is a statement about energy per charge, not about how much current the cell is actually delivering.

Chemists tabulate standard reduction potentials for half-reactions under specified reference conditions. A half-cell's absolute potential cannot be obtained by connecting a lone probe to nothing. We measure potential differences, so the tables use a chosen reference, traditionally the standard hydrogen electrode assigned zero volts under its reference conditions. The entries let us predict the standard voltage for a cell by subtracting the anode's standard reduction potential from the cathode's. For the zinc–copper example, the familiar values are approximately minus zero point seven six volts for zinc reduction and plus zero point three four volts for copper reduction. Their difference is about one point one zero volts.

Do not mistake a table of potentials for a table of fixed battery outputs. The zinc–copper number describes a specified reaction under defined standard conditions and an idealized reversible potential. Change concentrations, activities, temperature or gas pressure, and the chemical potentials change. Draw substantial current, and internal resistance, electrode kinetics and transport limitations can lower the observed terminal voltage. A real battery has finite capacity, a maximum practical power delivery, and performance that depends on its design and state of charge.

The connection to the thermodynamics we learned earlier is exact. For a cell reaction transferring n moles of electrons per mole of reaction as written, its Gibbs-energy change equals negative n times Faraday's constant times the reversible cell potential. Faraday's constant is the electric charge carried by a mole of elementary charges, about ninety-six thousand four hundred eighty-five coulombs per mole. The negative sign expresses that a positive spontaneous galvanic voltage corresponds to a negative Gibbs-energy change for the written forward reaction. A zinc–copper cell transferring two moles of electrons at about one point one volts has a standard free-energy change of roughly negative two hundred twelve kilojoules per mole of net reaction. That is a thermodynamic upper limit for reversible electrical work, not a guarantee that a practical device delivers all of it.

Composition changes voltage because equilibrium changes the driving force. If zinc ions accumulate and copper ions are depleted, the reaction quotient shifts toward the products of the zinc oxidation and copper reduction. As the cell discharges, the forward thermodynamic advantage diminishes. The Nernst equation translates this into voltage: the reversible cell potential equals the standard potential minus a term proportional to temperature and the natural logarithm of the reaction quotient, divided by the number of electrons transferred and Faraday's constant. There is no need to memorize the formula immediately. The important thought is that a battery's chemistry changes while it operates, so its available free energy per transferred charge can change too.

That is why voltage and capacity are different characteristics. Voltage describes energy available per unit charge for a specified state and operating regime. Capacity describes how much charge can be transferred before the cell reaches a practical cutoff, often expressed in ampere-hours. Energy is the integral of voltage over transferred charge, so multiplying a rough average voltage by capacity can provide a useful estimate, but the real voltage may vary during discharge. Two battery designs can have similar nominal voltages but very different capacities, power abilities, degradation and temperature behavior.

An early historical turning point came when Alessandro Volta assembled alternating metals and electrolyte-soaked separators into a pile and publicly described his electrical source in 1800. It provided a sustained current that could be used for experiments. The subsequent Daniell cell, developed by John Frederic Daniell in 1836, used two different electrolyte environments to improve sustained operation. The now-familiar classroom drawing of two separated half-cells connected by a salt bridge is a teaching model, not a literal copy of every historical Daniell battery. Chemical changes and electrical behavior can be designed together.

The distinction between disposable and rechargeable batteries adds another layer. A primary battery is generally designed for one useful discharge. A rechargeable cell can be driven in reverse by an external electrical source under suitable conditions, restoring a useful fraction of its original chemical state. Reversibility is never perfect. Side reactions, structural changes, loss of active material, electrolyte decomposition and heat can limit cycle life. A favorable reverse *overall* chemical accounting does not guarantee that every microscopic change can be cleanly undone.

What about corrosion? Corrosion is often a dispersed electrochemical cell we did not intend to build. Different regions on a metal surface can act as local oxidation and reduction sites. Water and dissolved ions supply an ionic path; oxygen or other oxidants can participate at cathodic regions. The metal slowly loses material through oxidation, sometimes producing corrosion products that change future behavior. Connecting different metals electrically in an electrolyte can create galvanic interactions, but the outcome depends on the actual environment, surface films and geometry, not simply on a ranking memorized from a textbook.

Engineers sometimes protect a metal by making another, more readily oxidized material serve as the anode, or by applying a controlled electrical potential. These methods illustrate that the important system is the whole electrochemical circuit: the metals, electrolyte, reactions and paths for current. The same chemical language used to understand a battery can help explain a corroding bridge support or a marine pipeline.

The central insight of electrochemistry is that an electron-transfer reaction has both a chemical and an electrical description. The electrons do not know that we have drawn a boundary between chemistry and physics. Their chemical potentials, the compensating motion of ions, and the energy changes of the whole system determine what happens. A wire gives us a way to intercept part of the energy that a spontaneous reaction can release. In the next kind of process, we can reverse the arrangement: use supplied electrical work to make chemistry proceed in a direction it would not otherwise favor.

## Scientific companion: one zinc–copper cell

The half-reactions are

$$\text{anode (oxidation):}\quad \mathrm{Zn(s)\rightarrow Zn^{2+}(aq)+2e^-},$$
$$\text{cathode (reduction):}\quad \mathrm{Cu^{2+}(aq)+2e^-\rightarrow Cu(s)}.$$

Net: $\mathrm{Zn(s)+Cu^{2+}(aq)\rightarrow Zn^{2+}(aq)+Cu(s)}$.

Using representative standard **reduction** potentials at $25\ ^\circ\mathrm C$: $E^\circ_{\rm Cu^{2+}/Cu}\approx+0.34$ V, $E^\circ_{\rm Zn^{2+}/Zn}\approx-0.76$ V,

$$E^\circ_{\rm cell}=E^\circ_{\rm cathode}-E^\circ_{\rm anode}\approx1.10\ \mathrm V.$$

With $n=2$ and $F\approx96485\ \mathrm{C\ mol^{-1}}$,

$$\Delta_r G^\circ=-nFE^\circ\approx-212\ \mathrm{kJ\ mol^{-1}}.$$

For the corresponding quotient $Q=a_{\rm Zn^{2+}}/a_{\rm Cu^{2+}}$, with pure-metal activities unity,

$$E=E^\circ-\frac{RT}{2F}\ln Q.$$

At $298.15$ K, a tenfold increase in this quotient lowers the reversible potential by approximately $0.0296$ V, assuming the same reaction model and other fixed conditions. Under load, terminal voltage also reflects ohmic and kinetic losses. Current is charge per time; voltage is available reversible work per unit charge; capacity is transferred charge; energy is approximately $\int E\,dq$ for the appropriate terminal voltage.
