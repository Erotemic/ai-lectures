# Why a Buffer Resists Change: Weak Acids, Titrations, and Chemical Capacity

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

Imagine two glasses of clear water. A pH meter gives almost the same reading in each. Now add the same small amount of acid. One glass changes pH dramatically. The other barely moves. The initial reading did not reveal what would happen next. To understand the difference we need a chemical quantity that is not simply acidity at one instant: the capacity to absorb a disturbance. The second glass contains a buffer, a mixture whose composition can change while its hydrogen-ion activity changes comparatively little.

A common buffer contains a weak acid and a significant amount of its conjugate base. Acetic acid and acetate provide an example. Acetic acid can donate a proton; acetate can accept it. They are not two substances cancelling each other into chemical inactivity. They are a reserve of molecules able to respond in opposite directions. Add a small amount of strong acid, and much of its donated proton load can be taken up by acetate to make acetic acid. Add a small amount of strong base, and acetic acid can donate protons to neutralize the base, becoming acetate. The ratio of the two forms changes, but the mixture avoids the much larger pH shift that a comparable unbuffered solution might undergo.

This is a matter of chemical bookkeeping. If a mixture contains one tenth of a mole of acetic acid and one tenth of a mole of acetate, adding one hundredth of a mole of strong acid, in an idealized closed setting, converts approximately that amount of acetate into acetic acid. The quantities become nine hundredths of a mole of acetate and eleven hundredths of a mole of acetic acid. They are no longer equal, but both remain substantial. The added acid has been accommodated mainly by shifting the ratio of chemical forms, rather than leaving its entire amount as free hydronium in solution.

That is the central mechanism. It does not imply that the buffer destroys acid, that the pH never moves, or that buffering is unlimited. A buffer has only so much of each form available. If we add enough strong acid to consume nearly all the conjugate base, it can no longer respond effectively in that direction. If we add enough base to consume the weak acid, its ability to respond to further base additions collapses. Buffer capacity is finite and depends on total concentrations as well as the relevant acid-base equilibria.

There is a compact mathematical relationship that reveals the geometry of this behavior. For a weak acid HA and its conjugate base A, the equilibrium constant connects the activity of hydronium to the ratio of A and HA. Taking logarithms gives the Henderson–Hasselbalch equation: pH equals p K a plus the logarithm of conjugate-base activity divided by acid activity, in the appropriate approximation. P K a is the negative logarithm of the acid dissociation constant. It names the characteristic region where acid and base forms have comparable activities. In dilute idealized conditions we often substitute concentrations.

Suppose the p K a is approximately four point seven six, as it is for acetic acid near room temperature under a commonly used convention. If acetate and acetic acid are present at equal effective activities, the logarithm of their ratio is zero, and the pH is near four point seven six. If the base form is ten times as abundant as the acid form, the logarithm is one and the pH rises by about one unit. If the acid form is ten times as abundant, the pH falls by about one unit. This is why buffers work particularly well in a region around the p K a, where neither partner is almost exhausted.

Now return to the example with one tenth of a mole of each form. After adding one hundredth of a mole of strong acid, the approximate base-to-acid ratio is nine to eleven. The pH falls by only about nine hundredths of a unit in the idealized calculation. Compare that with adding the same amount of strong acid to a liter of unbuffered pure water: ignoring the water's small initial ionization, the resulting hydronium concentration would be about one hundredth molar, corresponding to pH near two. The comparison is not intended as a recommendation for mixing chemicals. It shows the size of the effect that a reservoir of weak-acid chemistry can have.

The amount of acid or base required for a particular change in pH is a way to describe buffer capacity. A buffer with ten times as much total acetic acid and acetate at the same ratio can generally absorb more added acid or base before its ratio changes appreciably. Yet its initial pH may remain almost the same. This is why two samples sharing a pH can behave so differently when disturbed. pH tells us where a system is; buffer capacity tells us something about how difficult it is to move it.

How do we discover such behavior experimentally? Through titration. A chemist begins with a measured amount of sample, adds a solution with a known concentration of acid or base, and monitors the response. Often the result is a curve of pH against added volume. A strong acid titrated by a strong base has one characteristic shape. A weak acid titrated by a strong base has another, because the weak-acid and conjugate-base pair forms a buffer over part of the titration.

Consider titrating a weak acid with a strong base. Initially most of the weak acid may be in its protonated form, with a pH governed by its dissociation. As base is added, some HA becomes A. A useful buffer mixture develops. At the half-equivalence point, half of the original analytical moles of a monoprotic weak acid have been converted to conjugate base, ignoring secondary effects; the two forms are approximately equal in the simple model. The pH there is near the p K a. This gives a laboratory way to estimate acid strength from a titration curve, rather than merely memorizing a table.

The equivalence point is different from the half-equivalence point. At equivalence, the amount of titrant supplied matches the stoichiometric amount needed to neutralize the original acid according to the chosen reaction. For a weak acid titrated with a strong base, the solution can be basic at equivalence because the conjugate base reacts with water. For a strong acid and strong base at twenty-five Celsius, the ideal equivalence pH is near seven. The equivalence point is a matter of reaction quantities, while the pH at that point depends on the species present and their equilibria.

An acid-base indicator changes color over a limited pH interval because its protonated and deprotonated forms absorb visible light differently. It can signal a practical endpoint near the intended equivalence point if its transition range is well chosen. But endpoint and equivalence point are not defined as the same thing. A colored transition can occur before or after exact stoichiometric equivalence. Instruments and calibration can reduce errors, yet the chemistry of the indicator still matters. A titration is an argument based on a reaction model, not just a ritual of watching color.

Real buffers can be more complicated than a single conjugate pair. Biological systems use mixtures of phosphate, proteins and other weak-acid groups. In blood, the bicarbonate–carbon dioxide system is important because carbon dioxide exchanges with the lungs and bicarbonate is regulated by the kidneys. It cannot be described as a sealed flask of bicarbonate alone. Changing ventilation changes a gas-related component of the system; changing renal handling affects the supply and removal of bicarbonate and other acid-base equivalents. The chemistry is coupled to transport and physiology. We can understand its buffering role without pretending that blood is a simple equilibrium beaker.

Natural waters present similar complications. A stream flowing through carbonate rock may have considerable alkalinity. Add an acidic input, and dissolved inorganic carbon forms can take up some of the acid, with mineral dissolution sometimes contributing further buffering. But the magnitude and duration of the response depend on how fast reactions occur, how much reactive mineral surface is available, whether carbon dioxide can escape, and whether more water flows through. Buffering is not a permanent shield; it is a system of reservoirs and reactions with physical limits.

There is also an important difference between a buffer that resists a quick small addition and a process that maintains a steady pH over long periods. A cell or an ocean basin may appear stable partly because matter is continually moving through it and because regulation or external reservoirs replenish what is consumed. That is not identical to the small closed-vessel equilibrium described by a textbook buffer formula. The formula helps with one well-defined regime. When the boundaries or timescales change, the model must change too.

High ionic concentrations add another layer. The Henderson–Hasselbalch form written in concentrations assumes activity coefficients can be treated adequately. At high ionic strength, or with significant complexation and multiple equilibria, a naive concentration ratio can mislead. Accurate work may require activities, charge balance, total analytical concentrations, additional equilibria and an explicit model of the solution. The simple equation is valuable because it explains the mechanism clearly, not because it eliminates the rest of chemistry.

A buffer is often described as a chemical shock absorber. That captures one feature: the same disturbance makes less difference to the measured pH. But a physical shock absorber dissipates mechanical energy, whereas a chemical buffer redistributes protons among species and changes the equilibrium composition. The better mental picture is a pair of reservoirs connected by a reversible valve. A disturbance moves material from one reservoir to the other. The water level in the combined system changes less quickly while both reservoirs have useful capacity.

Return to our two glasses. They began with almost the same pH but contained different potential responses. A single number described the starting condition; a titration exposed the hidden inventory. This is a recurring lesson in chemistry. To predict what a system will do, we need to know not only its present state but also the stores of material and the equilibria that can respond when conditions change. A buffer is chemistry's clearest demonstration that stability can be active, finite and measurable.

## Scientific companion: a buffer calculation and a titration map

For $HA\rightleftharpoons H^++A^-$ at a fixed temperature, an idealized Henderson–Hasselbalch relation is

$$\mathrm{pH}\approx pK_a+\log_{10}\frac{[A^-]}{[HA]}.$$

For acetic acid/acetate near $25\ ^\circ\mathrm C$, use illustrative $pK_a=4.76$. If one liter initially has $0.100$ mol $HA$ and $0.100$ mol $A^-$, then $\mathrm{pH}\approx4.76$. Add $0.010$ mol of strong acid, assuming quantitative conversion of $A^-$ to $HA$, volume change negligible and ideal activities:

$$n_{A^-}=0.090\ \mathrm{mol},\quad n_{HA}=0.110\ \mathrm{mol},$$

$$\mathrm{pH}\approx4.76+\log_{10}(0.090/0.110)=4.673.$$

This is a **pedagogical approximation**. Real measurements require activity and ion-balance corrections when appropriate; the initial contribution from water and the weak acid is ignored in the simple mole ledger.

For a titration of a **monoprotic weak acid** by a strong base:

- Before substantial addition: solve the weak-acid equilibrium.
- Buffer region: HA and A both present in significant amounts; Henderson–Hasselbalch may be useful.
- Half-equivalence: $n_{A^-}\approx n_{HA}$; pH near $pK_a$ in the simple model.
- Equivalence: strong-base equivalents match the original acid equivalents. The conjugate base may hydrolyze, so pH is generally not seven.
- Beyond equivalence: excess strong base often dominates hydroxide concentration.

A titration indicator's **endpoint** is a measurement criterion; the **equivalence point** is stoichiometric. They may differ.
