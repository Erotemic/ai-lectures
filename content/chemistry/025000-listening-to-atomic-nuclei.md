# Listening to Atomic Nuclei: How NMR Reveals Molecular Structure

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

Two organic molecules can contain exactly the same atoms and still be different substances. Ethanol and dimethyl ether both have two carbon atoms, six hydrogen atoms and one oxygen atom. One is an alcohol with a carbon-carbon bond. The other connects the two carbons through an oxygen. An elemental analysis can tell us the overall composition but not which arrangement is present. Mass spectrometry may tell us that the two molecules share a nominal molecular mass. To identify their structure, we need measurements sensitive to the environments of individual atoms. Nuclear magnetic resonance, abbreviated N M R, provides such measurements.

Some atomic nuclei carry a magnetic moment associated with their quantum spin. Hydrogen nuclei containing a single proton are particularly convenient for organic chemistry, because many organic molecules contain abundant hydrogen. Carbon thirteen, a less abundant stable isotope of carbon, is also useful. These nuclei do not rotate like tiny macroscopic balls, despite the historical language of spin. Their magnetic behavior follows quantum mechanics, yet an accessible physical picture begins with tiny magnetic moments in a strong external magnetic field.

In that field, relevant nuclear spin states have different energies. Radio-frequency electromagnetic radiation can drive transitions between them under appropriate resonance conditions. The exact resonance frequency depends on the field experienced by the nucleus. Electrons around the nucleus respond to the external magnetic field, creating small local magnetic effects that shield the nucleus. A hydrogen bonded to carbon next to oxygen experiences a different electronic environment from a hydrogen on a simple alkyl group. The resonance frequency shifts slightly, and those small shifts become chemical clues.

Because the laboratory magnetic field can be different from one instrument to another, chemists usually report chemical shift in parts per million relative to a reference. This expresses the tiny frequency difference as a fraction of the main resonance frequency. A shift of one part per million does not mean the molecule contains one part per million of a proton. It means the resonance is displaced by one millionth of the reference frequency in the specified convention. The same chemical environment can be compared across instruments, allowing for solvent, temperature, concentration and referencing effects.

Imagine measuring the proton NMR spectrum of a simple alcohol such as ethanol. We expect signals from several kinds of hydrogen. Three hydrogens are attached to one carbon in a methyl group. Two are attached to a neighboring carbon that also bonds to oxygen. Another hydrogen is attached to oxygen itself. These are not all in the same electronic environment, so they need not resonate at the same chemical shift. The hydrogens on the carbon next to oxygen tend to appear farther downfield than those of an ordinary methyl group, because the nearby electronegative oxygen changes the shielding environment.

A striking benefit of proton NMR is integration. Under suitable acquisition conditions, the area of a signal is proportional to the number of contributing protons. In ethanol, a spectrum might show an approximate three-to-two ratio for the carbon-bound hydrogens, along with a hydroxyl signal that may be broad or variable. The integral does not count all hydrogens automatically if peaks overlap, signals are not completely observed or acquisition conditions are inappropriate. But when used correctly, the area ratio is evidence that a signal represents three equivalent hydrogens and another two.

NMR supplies another clue through spin-spin coupling. Neighboring nuclei with suitable spins can alter each other's resonance energy slightly. Instead of one simple line, a signal can split into multiple closely spaced lines. For an idealized first-order system, a methyl group next to two equivalent hydrogens often appears as a triplet; the methylene group next to three equivalent hydrogens often appears as a quartet. The repeated splitting spacing, called the coupling constant, is the same in hertz for the coupled partners. This reciprocal pattern is useful evidence that the groups are connected through an appropriate bonding relationship.

It would be a mistake to treat the schoolbook N plus one rule as universal. It works for common simple cases with suitable coupling relationships and sufficiently separated resonances. When coupled nuclei have similar chemical shifts, strong coupling can produce more complicated patterns. Multiple nonequivalent neighbors create more complex multiplets. Exchangeable hydrogens, such as some oxygen-hydrogen and nitrogen-hydrogen protons, may not display clear coupling because they exchange with other hydrogen sources, depending on conditions. A missing hydroxyl splitting is not proof that the hydroxyl group is absent.

Now compare dimethyl ether. Its six hydrogens are arranged as two equivalent methyl groups connected through oxygen in a symmetric molecule. Under ordinary conditions they can appear together as one proton environment, rather than ethanol's three distinct groups of hydrogens. Both compounds have the same elemental formula. Their proton spectra nevertheless offer different sets of chemical shifts, integrations and splitting patterns. A structure assignment is a way of explaining that joint pattern.

What about carbon thirteen NMR? Natural carbon thirteen is much less common than carbon twelve, and its nuclear spin permits observation by NMR. Carbon NMR gives a view of carbon environments that is often easier to map onto the molecular backbone. In a proton-decoupled carbon spectrum, many signals appear as singlets, simplifying the pattern, but their intensities generally do not equal the numbers of carbons. Relaxation behavior and nuclear Overhauser enhancements make routine carbon peak heights and integrals unreliable for direct carbon counting unless a quantitative method has been designed.

A modern pulse NMR experiment is more sophisticated than slowly sweeping across one radio frequency at a time. A pulse perturbs the nuclear spin system, and the instrument records a decaying oscillating electromagnetic signal in time, called a free induction decay. A Fourier transform converts this time-domain signal into a frequency-domain spectrum. The frequencies and phases depend on the sample and acquisition conditions. This is a close conceptual cousin of Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy, although the measured physical interactions and practical details differ.

A nucleus does not keep broadcasting a strong signal indefinitely. Its magnetization relaxes, with characteristic processes often described by longitudinal and transverse relaxation times. If repeated pulses come too quickly for the relevant magnetization to recover, different signals can be attenuated by different amounts. That is why quantifying a mixture by NMR requires suitable delays, pulse conditions and reference strategy. A visually attractive spectrum collected for structural assignment is not automatically a validated quantitative measurement.

NMR is also a way to probe relationships between atoms. Two-dimensional experiments use sequences of pulses and delays to correlate resonances. Some correlations indicate through-bond coupling; others can contain through-space information. For a complex molecule, a proton spectrum alone may be crowded with overlapping peaks, but combined proton-carbon correlations help assign which hydrogen belongs to which carbon. Other experiments can identify networks of coupled atoms or spatial proximity that supports a three-dimensional structural model. Interpretation still requires chemical reasoning: the experiment reports correlations, not a sentence naming the molecule.

The technique has limits. Some nuclei are easy to observe; others are not. Large molecules may have broad overlapping signals, although specialized methods exploit rather than surrender to those challenges. Paramagnetic substances can alter relaxation and shifts. Water and solvent signals may obscure analytes. NMR usually needs more sample than highly sensitive trace mass-spectrometric approaches for many ordinary molecules. The advantage is the richness of structural and environmental information and, for appropriate methods, the ability to make non-destructive measurements.

There is a historical turn worth noticing. In 1946, research groups associated with Felix Bloch at Stanford and Edward Purcell at Harvard developed nuclear magnetic resonance measurement methods in condensed matter. Their work was recognized with the 1952 Nobel Prize in Physics. NMR grew from precision studies of nuclear magnetism into a major tool for chemical structure, materials and biology. The underlying phenomenon also supports magnetic resonance imaging, although imaging and high-resolution chemical spectroscopy answer different questions and use different methods to encode position or molecular environment.

Return to ethanol and dimethyl ether. Their identical atomic counts do not make them spectroscopically indistinguishable. NMR asks how each observable nucleus is situated among the electrons and neighboring spins of a molecule. Integration, chemical shift, coupling and correlation experiments all constrain possible structures. None is a magical label, and ambiguity can remain. But when these clues agree with one candidate and contradict another, an arrangement of invisible atoms becomes an experimentally supported explanation rather than merely a plausible drawing.

## Scientific companion: NMR observations and inference

The two constitutional isomers $\mathrm{C_2H_6O}$ are ethanol ($\mathrm{CH_3CH_2OH}$) and dimethyl ether ($\mathrm{CH_3OCH_3}$). They share formula and nominal molecular mass but differ in number and relationships of proton environments.

**Chemical shift** relative to a standard:

$$\delta\,(\mathrm{ppm})=10^6\frac{\nu-\nu_{\mathrm{ref}}}{\nu_{\mathrm{ref}}}.$$

Reporting conventions normally use an appropriate reference and the instrument's operating frequency. At a 400 MHz proton frequency, 1 ppm corresponds to 400 Hz.

**First-order coupling:** the $(n+1)$ rule is a useful approximation for $n$ equivalent spin-half neighbors coupled equally, provided relevant chemical-shift differences are large compared with couplings. It is not a general solution to strongly coupled systems.

**Quantitation caution:** proton peak areas can reflect relative amounts when signals are fully acquired with appropriate relaxation and integration. Routine proton-decoupled $^{13}\mathrm{C}$ peak intensities are not automatically quantitative.
