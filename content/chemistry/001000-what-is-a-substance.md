# What Is a Substance? The Chemical Identity of Matter

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

A glass of water looks simple. It is transparent. It pours. Leave it on a table and nothing especially interesting seems to happen. Yet the glass might contain dissolved calcium, carbon dioxide, traces of copper, or an invisible amount of a biological toxin. Two glasses that look identical may be chemically very different. And a glass of pure water, a solution of salt in water, and a mixture of sand and water are different in a way that goes deeper than appearance.

Chemistry begins with a demanding question: what, exactly, is present? Not merely what does the sample look like, but what kinds of particles make it up, in what proportions, and how do we know? This turns the everyday experience of matter into an investigation. The answer has to survive measurement, separation, and sometimes a chemical reaction that makes the components reveal themselves.

Think about a bowl of seawater. Evaporate the water and crystals remain. They did not grow out of nothing. Much of what looked like a single liquid was a mixture of water and dissolved substances. More surprisingly, the separated crystals do not themselves look much like the elements from which they are made. The familiar sodium chloride in table salt contains sodium and chlorine, but it is neither a lump of reactive sodium metal nor a cylinder of chlorine gas. The identity of a compound cannot be read off from the familiar properties of its constituent elements.

A useful first distinction is between a mixture and a pure substance. A mixture contains multiple substances whose proportions can vary. Salt water can be barely salty or close to saturated; its composition is not fixed by the name. A pure substance has a definite chemical identity. Under ordinary chemical conditions, a sample of pure water consists of water molecules with two hydrogen atoms bonded to one oxygen atom per molecule, irrespective of whether we obtained it from a glacier, condensed steam, or a laboratory purification system. Pure water can still contain naturally occurring isotopes of hydrogen and oxygen; chemical purity does not require every nucleus to have the same isotope.

Mixtures come in several forms. In a heterogeneous mixture, different regions can have different composition. Sand suspended in water may settle to the bottom, leaving a clearer liquid above. In a homogeneous mixture, the composition is uniform at the scale under discussion. When salt is thoroughly dissolved, you do not find separate visible pieces of salt among little pools of water. The sodium and chloride ions are dispersed through the liquid. But uniform appearance at the scale of our eyes is not proof of molecular uniformity. Milk looks uniform from a distance, yet its droplets and other dispersed structures can make it a colloid rather than a simple molecular solution.

There is a practical reason for taking these classifications seriously. They tell us what kinds of operations might work. A filter removes sand grains if the pores are smaller than the grains. It will not ordinarily catch individual dissolved sodium ions. Distillation can exploit differences in volatility; chromatography exploits different interactions with a mobile phase and a stationary phase. Even when two compounds look identical, separation may reveal that the material was never one substance in the first place.

Now imagine decomposing a compound. Heating some compounds releases gases; passing electric current through water under suitable conditions can produce hydrogen and oxygen. These are chemical changes, not just sorting. Chemical bonds are broken and others formed; the identities of the participating chemical substances change. The products have new properties. By contrast, melting ice into liquid water changes the arrangement and motion of water molecules, but it does not, by itself, convert the water into a different chemical compound.

The distinction between physical and chemical change is useful but not an infallible test applied by appearance. Dissolving a substance can leave its molecular identity intact, or it can involve reactions with the solvent. Some processes involve both. The better question is whether the chemical species have changed, and what evidence supports the claim. Color change, heat, bubbles, or a precipitate can suggest a reaction, but none is universally sufficient proof. Heating a liquid to boiling produces bubbles without necessarily creating a new substance.

Scientists had learned to measure chemical transformations long before they could look inside atoms. If a reaction occurs in a properly closed system, its total mass remains the same to ordinary chemical accuracy. That is because the reaction rearranges the atoms rather than making them vanish. If a burning sample becomes lighter on an open balance, perhaps a gas escaped. If a piece of metal gains mass as it oxidizes, perhaps oxygen from the air joined it. The surroundings are part of the accounting. Conservation becomes convincing only when the boundaries of the experiment are explicit.

Working in France in the late eighteenth century, Antoine Lavoisier and other investigators made precise mass balances central to chemical reasoning. Their work did not settle every question about matter, but it helped replace stories about substances being mysteriously created or destroyed with measurements of materials entering and leaving an apparatus. The detail matters: a theory of chemistry must explain not only spectacular changes but where every measurable portion of matter went.

That leads to a second distinction: elements and compounds. An element is defined chemically by its type of atom; in the modern picture, the atoms of an element have the same number of protons in their nuclei. Oxygen, copper, and carbon are elements. A compound contains atoms of more than one element chemically combined in a characteristic composition and structure. Water, carbon dioxide, and sodium chloride are compounds. A diamond is composed of the element carbon, although its atoms form an extended bonded crystal. The same element can occur in different structural forms; diamond and graphite are both made of carbon, with radically different properties because their bonding arrangements differ.

A molecule is not the same thing as a substance, and not every chemical substance consists of separate molecules. Many molecular substances do, like water. But a sodium chloride crystal is a repeating array of positive sodium ions and negative chloride ions; picking out a single little sodium-chloride molecule from the solid would misrepresent the structure. A copper wire is an extended metal lattice with mobile electronic states. The simple categories we learn first are starting points, not rigid boxes into which every material must be squeezed.

How could scientists infer that compounds have definite formulas without being able to see individual atoms? One clue is the law of definite proportions: a chemically pure compound has reproducible elemental mass proportions under the stated conditions. If eighteen grams of water are decomposed into its constituent elements, approximately two grams of hydrogen and sixteen grams of oxygen can be accounted for. Those values express atomic ratios and atomic masses. A mixture of hydrogen and oxygen gases, by contrast, could have almost any proportions. Similar-looking samples with different ratios may be different compounds, different phases, or mixtures, and analysis is required to distinguish them.

The modern formula for water says H two O. In spoken language, that means two hydrogen atoms for each oxygen atom in a molecule. It does not tell us the molecule's shape or the arrangement of its electrons. A formula is useful precisely because it leaves many details aside while retaining the composition we need for counting reactions. For a salt such as sodium chloride, the formula expresses the simplest ion ratio in the crystal rather than a discrete molecule. For some solid materials the composition can vary within a range, and the simplified formula is an approximation to a more complicated lattice chemistry. Chemistry is not defeated by these complications; it becomes more exact by naming them.

What makes an atom of oxygen different from an atom of nitrogen? It is not its size, its color, or whether it happens to be part of a living thing. It is the number of protons in its nucleus: eight for oxygen and seven for nitrogen. Electrons and the arrangement of their possible states largely govern chemical behavior. Neutrons change an atom's isotope. Carbon twelve and carbon thirteen are both carbon; they have the same number of protons but different numbers of neutrons. Their chemical reactions are similar, though isotope-dependent differences can be measurable and useful. Carbon fourteen is radioactive, so nuclear transformations introduce a distinct process from everyday chemical rearrangement.

Chemists learn the identities of unknown substances using several independent clues. Mass spectrometry separates ions by mass-to-charge ratio and reveals characteristic patterns; it does not simply present a photograph of a molecule. Infrared spectroscopy probes molecular vibrations. Nuclear magnetic resonance reports on the environments of certain nuclei. Chromatography separates components whose transport through a system differs. A melting point or boiling point can provide a useful clue about purity, but never makes an identity certain by itself. The strongest claim is often the one supported by several measurements that could have disagreed but did not.

Here is a test of the distinction between amount and identity. Suppose two identical-looking white powders sit in labeled jars. You know that one contains sucrose, a common sugar, and the other contains sodium chloride. A quick glance does not reveal which is which. Both can dissolve in water. Their solutions behave differently: ordinary sodium-chloride solutions conduct electrical current because they contain mobile ions, whereas pure sucrose dissolves largely as neutral molecules and contributes little to conductivity. That is one discriminating observation, not a general rule that every conducting solution contains sodium chloride. The point is the pattern of evidence. We ask what a proposed chemical identity predicts, then look for differences that distinguish it from alternatives.

Chemistry is sometimes introduced as the study of the smallest building blocks of matter. That is true only with a qualification. Particle physics explores constituents deeper than atoms. Chemistry specializes in the stable patterns produced when nuclei and electrons form atoms, molecules, crystals, solutions, and reacting materials under conditions where nuclear identities usually remain fixed. Its power is to connect those microscopic structures to things we can weigh, heat, dissolve, detect, manufacture, and observe in organisms and landscapes.

The quiet glass of water has therefore become a surprisingly demanding object. To call it water is to make a chemical claim. To call it pure water is to make a stronger claim. To say it contains a dissolved compound adds an account of particles and proportions. None of these claims is guaranteed by the surface appearance. Chemistry advances by making identities testable and by treating change not as magic but as an accountable rearrangement of matter.

## Scientific companion: substance, mixture, and evidence

### Definitions and boundaries

- **Element:** atoms characterized by the same proton number (atomic number). The term may also designate the pure elemental substance; distinguish the meanings when discussing O atoms versus O₂ gas.
- **Compound:** material whose constituent elements are chemically bound in a characteristic composition; formulas can refer to molecules or repeating formula units, not necessarily isolated molecular particles.
- **Mixture:** a combination of substances in proportions not fixed by a unique chemical formula. Homogeneous and heterogeneous describe spatial composition at a specified observational scale.
- **Chemical species:** a chemically identifiable entity (atom, molecule, ion, radical, etc.) appropriate to the mechanism or measurement.
- **Conservation:** ordinary chemical reactions conserve each element's atom count and charge in an appropriately specified closed-system bookkeeping equation. Relativistic mass-energy equivalence is not needed at typical chemical precision; it does not justify treating atmospheric gases as absent from a mass balance.

### Worked quantitative example

Water has a nominal molar mass near 18.015 g/mol. Its hydrogen mass fraction is approximately $2(1.008)/18.015=0.1119$ and oxygen mass fraction about $15.999/18.015=0.8881$. Decomposing 18.015 g of pure water gives *at most*, if the process runs fully with collected products and no losses, approximately 2.016 g of hydrogen gas and 15.999 g of oxygen gas. The total mass is conserved; the chemical identities change. The measured mass of a vented vessel alone does not track the mass of escaped gas.
