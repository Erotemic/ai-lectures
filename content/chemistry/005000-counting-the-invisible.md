# Counting the Invisible: Moles, Equations, and Limiting Reactants

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

A recipe for bread can ask for two cups of flour and one cup of water, but a recipe for a chemical reaction must work with quantities far too small to count one at a time. A beaker holding a few grams of liquid contains an astronomical number of molecules. How can a chemist use ordinary balances and measuring cylinders to control reactions among entities that no person could count individually?

The answer is to combine two kinds of accounting. A chemical formula says which atoms are in each molecular entity or formula unit. A mole gives us a standard-sized group of those entities. Together with the masses of atoms, they connect microscopic numbers to the macroscopic quantities we can weigh. A balanced chemical equation then tells us how the required groups of reactants relate to the groups of products.

Before counting, think about the difference between a unit and a substance. A dozen means twelve of something, but a dozen eggs and a dozen bricks have different masses. A mole plays the same structural role at a much larger scale. One mole contains exactly six hundred two sextillion, two hundred fourteen quintillion, seventy-six quadrillion elementary entities, often written as approximately six point zero two two times ten to the twenty-third. The exact count is 6.02214076 times ten to the twenty-third. Since the international redefinition that took effect in 2019, that number is exact by definition. A mole is a counting unit; it is not itself a fixed mass.

One mole of carbon atoms and one mole of oxygen molecules contain equal numbers of the specified entities, but their masses differ. A carbon atom and an oxygen molecule are not the same kind of entity. In common chemical calculations, one mole of carbon atoms has a mass near twelve grams, while one mole of oxygen molecules has a mass near thirty-two grams. The amount is one mole in both cases. The mass reflects the type of entity we counted.

Sometimes the entity is not a molecule. For sodium chloride crystals, chemists count formula units representing the ideal sodium-to-chloride ratio. One mole of NaCl formula units contains one mole of sodium ions' worth of sodium atoms and one mole of chloride ions' worth of chlorine atoms. The crystal is an extended lattice, but the formula provides a counting convention. A mole of electrons is another possible quantity; the definition of the mole does not privilege any particular chemical substance.

We need one more piece: molar mass, the mass per mole of the specified entity. For water, the formula has two hydrogen atoms and one oxygen atom. Using customary atomic weights, hydrogen contributes about one gram per mole of atoms and oxygen about sixteen. Two hydrogens plus one oxygen give water a molar mass close to eighteen grams per mole. If you have thirty-six grams of pure water, then approximately two moles of water molecules are present. If you have nine grams, approximately half a mole is present. The calculation divides sample mass by molar mass.

The masses are not mathematical constants equal to the rounded numbers we remember. Standard atomic weights depend on isotopic composition, and reported measurement precision matters when making exact calculations. The mole definition itself is exact, while the measured molar mass of a natural sample can carry uncertainty. Chemistry uses approximations deliberately and should not confuse them with the definitions of its units.

How can these counts control a reaction? Consider the formation of water from hydrogen and oxygen. The balanced chemical equation is spoken this way: two molecules of hydrogen gas react with one molecule of oxygen gas to produce two molecules of water. A balanced equation can also apply to moles: two moles of hydrogen molecules react with one mole of oxygen molecules to produce two moles of water molecules, if the reaction proceeds with that stoichiometry.

Why do we need the two in front of hydrogen and the two in front of water? Hydrogen gas occurs as molecules containing two hydrogen atoms, and oxygen gas as molecules containing two oxygen atoms. To put those atoms into water molecules, each with two hydrogens and one oxygen, we need to keep both kinds of atoms conserved. Writing two hydrogen molecules and one oxygen molecule on the left gives four hydrogen atoms and two oxygen atoms. Writing two water molecules on the right gives exactly the same count. Nothing in this arithmetic explains how a flame propagates; the balanced equation is a ledger of net change, not a complete movie of the reaction mechanism.

Here is a practical puzzle. Suppose we have four moles of hydrogen molecules but only one mole of oxygen molecules. How much water can we form if the reaction goes to completion according to the equation, with no side reaction? To use all four moles of hydrogen, we would need two moles of oxygen. We have only one. The oxygen is the limiting reactant. It can consume two moles of hydrogen and form two moles of water. Two moles of hydrogen remain unused. A calculation that assumes all four moles of hydrogen must produce water would be incorrect, even though hydrogen is the larger number on the bench.

The word limiting is not a judgment about which reactant is more important. It names which available quantity first becomes exhausted under the chosen reaction stoichiometry. The limiting reactant depends on the starting proportions. If instead we supplied two moles of oxygen and one mole of hydrogen, the hydrogen would be limiting. Chemical equations express ratios; the actual mixture determines which ratio constrains the reaction.

How does this translate into masses? In our four-mole-hydrogen, one-mole-oxygen example, the starting masses are roughly eight grams of hydrogen gas and thirty-two grams of oxygen gas, a total of forty grams. The reaction consumes about four grams of hydrogen and all thirty-two grams of oxygen, producing about thirty-six grams of water. About four grams of hydrogen remain. Add the mass of water to the mass of the unused hydrogen and we get forty grams, matching what we began with. The atom ledger and mass ledger agree.

A second example uses limestone. Calcium carbonate, written CaCO three, can decompose when heated to make calcium oxide and carbon dioxide. The balanced equation has one formula unit of calcium carbonate giving one formula unit of calcium oxide and one molecule of carbon dioxide. One mole of calcium carbonate, around one hundred grams, gives one mole of calcium oxide, around fifty-six grams, and one mole of carbon dioxide, around forty-four grams. If the gas escapes from the apparatus, the residue seems to have lost nearly forty-four percent of its original mass. In a closed accounting system, the carbon dioxide must be counted among the products. The apparent loss becomes a prediction of how much gas ought to have formed.

The limestone reaction is a reminder that chemical equations are statements about transformations, not automatically promises that a particular outcome will occur under any conditions. Calcium carbonate does not simply decompose completely at room temperature when we write the equation. The temperature, surrounding carbon dioxide pressure, and other conditions matter. Even in a hot furnace, actual conversion can be incomplete. The equation gives the stoichiometric relationship for the specified reaction. Thermodynamics and kinetics determine whether it proceeds, how far, and how fast.

This brings us to theoretical yield. Suppose the chemical equation and the limiting reactant indicate that at most ten grams of a desired product can form. If you isolate eight grams, the percent yield is eighty percent. The missing two grams do not have one universal explanation. Some starting material may remain, some may form other products, some product may decompose, and some may be lost in purification or handling. A student may also have weighed an impure or wet sample, producing an apparent yield that is too large. Yield is a comparison between a model-based expectation and a real operation.

In solution chemistry, quantities often arrive as concentration and volume rather than mass. Suppose an aqueous sodium chloride solution has a concentration of one mole per liter, and you measure one tenth of a liter. You have one tenth of a mole of NaCl formula units' worth of dissolved sodium and chloride ions, provided the stated concentration is in molarity. One tenth of a liter is one hundred milliliters. The calculation is concentration times volume. But remember that the volume is the final solution volume, not necessarily the volume of pure water used to prepare it.

Analytical chemistry makes use of that stoichiometric accounting. To determine the amount of an unknown reactant, a chemist may add a solution of known concentration and measure how much is required for a specified chemical endpoint. Titration makes the stoichiometric ledger into a measurement tool. A chemical change detectable by color, electrical signal, or another instrument marks the vicinity of a relevant reaction condition. The endpoint may require calibration and may not coincide perfectly with the exact equivalence point. Measuring reagents does not erase the need to understand the chemistry of the indicator or detector.

The arithmetic also extends to complicated molecules. One mole of glucose molecules contains six moles of carbon atoms, twelve moles of hydrogen atoms, and six moles of oxygen atoms. If the glucose reacts in a fully specified way, its atoms must appear somewhere among the products. A chemical equation for glucose oxidation can help predict oxygen consumption and carbon dioxide production. Whether the oxidation happens rapidly in a flame or through enzyme-mediated steps in a living cell, the net atom accounting is related. The reaction path and control are radically different, even though the gross stoichiometry can look similar.

There is a difference between choosing a unit for convenience and discovering a law. The mole itself is a conventionally defined quantity. Atom conservation in ordinary chemical reactions is a physical constraint. Atomic masses come from measurement and models of the nucleus. Chemical formulas come from structural and analytical evidence. The balanced equation combines those ingredients into a predictive relationship. The logic is strong because each piece has a different job, not because chemists memorized one enormous number.

A balanced equation is sometimes criticized for looking dry. In fact, it is a compressed account of an extraordinary ability: weighing a handful of material and knowing, within stated assumptions, the quantity of another substance it could produce. When the measured output disagrees with the prediction, the disagreement is useful. It can point to a limiting reactant, an incomplete conversion, a side reaction, a leak, or an incorrect assumption about the substance itself. The counting is the foundation for an experimental question, not the end of it.

The next time you see a chemical reaction, imagine a ledger beneath the visible change. The ledger counts atoms, charge, and groups of molecules. It cannot tell you everything about energy, speed, or mechanism. But it states exactly what must add up if the proposed chemical change is to make sense. A good chemical explanation starts by refusing to lose any atoms in the telling.

## Scientific companion: stoichiometric accounting

### Key quantities

- $N_A=6.02214076\times 10^{23}\,\mathrm{mol^{-1}}$ is the **exact** Avogadro constant under the SI redefinition effective 20 May 2019. Always specify the counted entity.
- $n=m/M$ where $n$ is amount in moles, $m$ is sample mass, and $M$ is molar mass. For molarity $c=n/V_{\mathrm{solution}}$.
- **Stoichiometric coefficients** encode the ratios of specified species in the net equation; the equation does not state the reaction's microscopic mechanism or its kinetic law.
- Theoretical yield assumes complete consumption of the limiting reactant into the specified products without losses or competing transformations. Percent yield $=100\,(\text{actual isolated amount})/(\text{theoretical amount})$ for consistent units.

### Worked examples

**Hydrogen and oxygen:** $2\mathrm{H_2(g)}+\mathrm{O_2(g)}\rightarrow 2\mathrm{H_2O}$. Starting with 4 mol H₂ and 1 mol O₂, oxygen limits the reaction. Produce at most 2 mol water and retain 2 mol H₂. Rounded masses: starting 8 g H₂ + 32 g O₂; ending 36 g H₂O + 4 g H₂. Total mass about 40 g on both sides.

**Limestone:** $\mathrm{CaCO_3(s)}\rightarrow \mathrm{CaO(s)}+\mathrm{CO_2(g)}$. At ideal complete conversion, 100.09 g CaCO₃ yields approximately 56.08 g CaO and 44.01 g CO₂. Mass loss from an *open* heated vessel is not disappearance of matter; it is primarily escaping gas in this simplified example.

**Concentration:** 100 mL of a 1.00 mol/L solution contains $1.00\times 0.100=0.100$ mol of the specified dissolved formula units. The mass needed depends on their molar mass.
