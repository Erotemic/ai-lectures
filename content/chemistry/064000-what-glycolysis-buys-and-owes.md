# What Glycolysis Buys and What It Owes: Following Sugar's Carbon and Electrons

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

Imagine a glucose molecule arriving at the entrance to a biochemical factory. It contains six carbon atoms and a supply of chemical energy. A pathway diagram tells us that glucose becomes two molecules of pyruvate. But that summary conceals the decisions that make the transformation useful. Why does the cell spend ATP before it earns ATP? Where do the electrons go? Why does glycolysis stop if one apparently unrelated cofactor is not regenerated? To answer, we will trace not just the carbon, but the payments, commitments, and debts involved in changing its chemistry.

Glycolysis operates in the cytosol of many kinds of cells. The name means sugar splitting, yet splitting is only part of the story. The full conventional pathway turns one six-carbon sugar into two three-carbon pyruvate molecules. In the familiar accounting it consumes two ATP, generates four ATP, and reduces two NAD plus molecules to two NADH. The net is two ATP and two NADH, provided the pathway can keep replenishing its starting materials. Those are useful bookkeeping totals, not the mechanism.

The first ATP investment is made by hexokinase, or in some tissues glucokinase. Glucose receives a phosphate group and becomes glucose six phosphate. This does more than add a charge. The phosphorylation changes the molecule's chemical possibilities and generally helps retain this form of sugar within the cell, because ordinary glucose transporters do not carry glucose six phosphate in the same way as free glucose. But the phosphorylation reaction alone does not guarantee that glucose will continue down glycolysis. Glucose six phosphate is itself a branching point: it can be stored, rearranged, or sent into other pathways.

An isomerase shifts the carbonyl position, converting glucose six phosphate to fructose six phosphate. Then phosphofructokinase one uses another ATP to create fructose one, six bisphosphate. This second expenditure makes the six-carbon sugar suitable for a particular cleavage. It is also a major point of regulation. Calling it an investment is precise: the ATP has been spent, and so far the pathway has not produced ATP back. If the sugar now disappeared into an unproductive side reaction, the cell would have paid for nothing.

Next comes the split. An aldolase divides the six-carbon intermediate into two three-carbon phosphates. One is glyceraldehyde three phosphate. The other, dihydroxyacetone phosphate, can be converted into the same glyceraldehyde compound by triose phosphate isomerase. A single glucose therefore gives two molecules that enter the payoff half of the pathway. This is why the later ATP-generating reactions happen twice per glucose. The carbon count is simple but essential: six carbons in, two times three carbons out. No carbon dioxide has yet escaped.

Now we reach the chemical heart of glycolysis. Glyceraldehyde three phosphate dehydrogenase oxidizes the aldehyde group while adding inorganic phosphate. The reaction forms one, three bisphosphoglycerate and reduces NAD plus to NADH. Notice the distinction from the earlier phosphorylation reactions. This phosphate is not donated by ATP. It comes from inorganic phosphate, and the oxidation makes the resulting acyl phosphate an energized intermediate. The reaction has joined electron transfer to the creation of a compound that can drive ATP formation.

The next enzyme, phosphoglycerate kinase, transfers a phosphate from that energized intermediate to ADP. It makes ATP by *substrate-level phosphorylation*: a direct transfer from a chemical substrate rather than from a membrane-driven ATP synthase. Because this reaction occurs for each of the two three-carbon molecules, it generates two ATP per glucose. At that moment the pathway has recovered the two ATP invested earlier, though there are still later reactions to come.

A series of rearrangements moves the remaining phosphate into a position that can support another ATP-generating step. Enolase forms phosphoenolpyruvate, usually abbreviated PEP. PEP can transfer its phosphate to ADP through pyruvate kinase. The favorable rearrangement of the resulting product helps drive this transfer. Once again the reaction occurs twice per original glucose, producing two more ATP. After subtracting the two ATP invested at the beginning, the conventional net gain is two ATP. The two molecules of pyruvate contain all six original carbons, distributed into two three-carbon compounds.

What about the electrons? At the glyceraldehyde dehydrogenase step, each three-carbon intermediate transfers reducing equivalents to NAD plus. The pathway therefore produces two NADH per glucose. If a cell had only a fixed supply of NAD plus and never oxidized NADH back, glycolysis would eventually stop at that oxidation step. The missing cofactor would be a bottleneck even if glucose, ADP, and all the enzymes were abundant. The NADH is a useful product only when the surrounding network has a way to handle it.

One possibility is aerobic respiration. In a eukaryotic cell with functioning mitochondria, reducing equivalents from cytosolic NADH can ultimately enter respiratory electron transport through shuttle systems. The electrons can help support proton pumping and ATP formation. Importantly, an NADH molecule does not literally move across every membrane as a universal package; the mitochondrial inner membrane generally requires specific shuttles to transfer cytosolic reducing equivalents. Their costs and consequences differ.

Another possibility is lactate formation. Lactate dehydrogenase reduces pyruvate to lactate while oxidizing NADH to NAD plus. In the idealized overall reaction, glucose becomes two lactate molecules with a net formation of two ATP. The fermentation step is not an extra ATP-making step; it enables the earlier ATP-making pathway to continue by restoring the oxidized cofactor. This process can operate in cells with little oxygen supply, but lactate production is not a reliable yes-or-no diagnostic for the presence of oxygen. Lactate also forms in oxygenated tissues and can be exported and used as fuel elsewhere.

Yeast can solve the same redox problem differently. One familiar alcoholic fermentation route converts pyruvate to acetaldehyde with release of carbon dioxide, then reduces acetaldehyde to ethanol while regenerating NAD plus. Carbon accounting now gives ethanol and carbon dioxide rather than two lactates. The route is chemically different, but the systems requirement is the same: the cell must balance the electrons freed during sugar oxidation against an acceptor that can be reduced.

Let's audit the popular phrase that glycolysis is the breakdown of glucose to release energy. It is true but not sufficiently explanatory. The path spends two ATP to prime and commit the sugar. It splits a six-carbon skeleton into two. It oxidizes a three-carbon aldehyde using NAD plus, storing part of that redox potential in NADH while building an acyl phosphate. It extracts ATP from two chemically activated intermediates. It ends with pyruvate, which is not an inert ash. Pyruvate can become acetyl CoA, lactate, alanine, oxaloacetate, or other products depending on the organism and its needs.

That branch point is why a measured glycolytic rate does not tell us the whole metabolic story. Two cells might both consume one hundred glucose molecules per second. One converts most of the resulting pyruvate into lactate. Another oxidizes much of it through the citric acid cycle. A third diverts intermediates to growth. Their glucose uptake rates could match while their oxygen consumption, biomass synthesis, and redox requirements differ sharply. To determine the internal routing, we need measurements beyond glucose disappearance.

There is a related issue of reversibility. Some glycolytic reactions operate near equilibrium in living cells, with forward and reverse microscopic traffic influenced by concentration. Others are strongly driven under ordinary operating conditions and require distinct reactions when a cell needs to synthesize glucose instead of degrading it. We should not assume that turning every arrow backward yields gluconeogenesis. A network can run material in the reverse overall direction only if it provides the necessary alternative chemical steps and pays their energy costs.

We began with a sugar and asked why the factory has a cashier at the entrance. Now the answer is concrete. ATP investment changes the substrate into a form that can be processed; an oxidation step moves electrons to a carrier; energized intermediates pay ATP back; and redox regeneration determines whether the process can be repeated. Glycolysis is not just a ten-arrow sequence to memorize. It is a tightly balanced arrangement of carbon transformations, phosphate transfers, and electron exchanges—and a useful example of how one pathway depends on the chemical network around it.

## Scientific companion: glycolysis accounting

**Canonical net glycolysis (common biochemical convention):**

\[
\mathrm{Glucose+2NAD^+ +2ADP+2P_i \to 2pyruvate+2NADH+2H^+ +2ATP+2H_2O}.
\]

Depending on pH and protonation conventions, displayed hydrogens and charges vary. The *net* energy-carrier production is two ATP and two NADH per glucose; direct ATP production is four and consumption is two. No net CO₂ is released in glycolysis itself.

**Investment:** hexokinase/glucokinase (1 ATP) and PFK-1 (1 ATP). **Payoff:** phosphoglycerate kinase (2 ATP) and pyruvate kinase (2 ATP). **Oxidation:** glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase reduces 2 NAD⁺ per glucose, using inorganic phosphate.

**Redox closure in lactate fermentation:**

\[
\mathrm{2pyruvate+2NADH+2H^+ \to 2lactate+2NAD^+}.
\]

Lactate dehydrogenase regenerates NAD⁺, not additional ATP. Coupled with glycolysis, the glucose-to-lactate network has net ATP generation of two per glucose.

**Worked rates:** A sample consuming 12 μmol glucose per minute through conventional glycolysis forms 24 μmol pyruvate equivalents, generates a *gross* 48 μmol ATP and consumes 24 μmol ATP per minute, for net 24 μmol ATP per minute. If all pyruvate is reduced to lactate, the corresponding lactate outflow is 24 μmol per minute. These are stoichiometric calculations, not assertions about a real tissue's routing.
