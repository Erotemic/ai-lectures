# Follow the Electron Pair: How to Read an Organic Reaction

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

An organic reaction can look forbidding when it is presented as a page full of zigzags and curved arrows. But a great many of those arrows are telling a simple story: electron-rich material approaches electron-poor material, a bond is formed, and another bond may be weakened or broken. Once we learn what an arrow claims, we can stop memorizing every reaction drawing as though it were a separate fact. We can ask whether the proposed sequence makes chemical sense.

The crucial first distinction is between an overall reaction and a mechanism. An overall equation tells us which starting materials are consumed and which products appear, after accounting for the other species. A mechanism proposes one or more elementary transformations that explain how this change occurs. Multiple mechanisms can lead to the same overall products. A mechanism is not established merely because we can draw arrows that conserve atoms. It earns credibility by predicting rates, stereochemistry, isotope effects, product distributions, and other observable features.

Imagine a molecule of methyl bromide. Its carbon is attached to three hydrogens and one bromine atom. Bromine draws some electron density toward itself, and the carbon is correspondingly electron-poor. Now introduce hydroxide, which has an oxygen bearing an extra electron pair relative to a neutral hydroxyl group. In one common reaction, hydroxide approaches carbon and forms a new carbon-oxygen bond while bromide leaves, carrying the pair from the original carbon-bromine bond. The result is methanol and bromide ion. The word substitution describes the change of attachments: one group replaces another at the carbon.

In a textbook mechanism, a curved arrow from a lone pair on hydroxide's oxygen points toward the carbon; another arrow runs from the carbon-bromine bond toward bromine. A full-headed curved arrow describes the reorganization of an electron pair. Its tail must begin at an electron pair or bond that actually exists in the proposed starting structure. Its head indicates the new location of that pair. Drawing the arrow from a positive carbon toward the incoming oxygen would suggest the wrong flow of electron density. The arrow convention is bookkeeping for a proposed bond reorganization, not footage of a little pair of dots flying across a flask.

Chemists call a species donating an electron pair to form a bond a nucleophile, and the accepting site an electrophile. A nucleophile need not be negatively charged. Water and ammonia can donate lone pairs while carrying no overall charge. An electrophile need not be a bare positively charged ion; the carbon atom of a carbonyl group is often electrophilic because its bond to oxygen is polarized. That combination lets neutral species participate in polar reactions without producing a cloud of isolated ions first.

The same word 'electron rich' is relative to a particular interaction. Water can be a nucleophile when its oxygen donates a lone pair to a carbon center, and it can take part in proton transfer as the proton source or sink. An aldehyde can be electrophilic at its carbonyl carbon while its oxygen can act as a base toward a suitable acid. Classifying a whole molecule as nucleophile or electrophile forever is less useful than locating the particular reactive site and describing the conditions.

To decide which electron pair might move, begin with the charge and valence of the atoms. If a neutral oxygen has two ordinary bonds and two lone pairs, asking it to make a third bond without changing any charge would not be sound bookkeeping. Oxygen with three ordinary bonds is generally shown carrying positive formal charge in a typical closed-shell Lewis structure. A carbon with five conventional full single bonds is likewise a signal that a step has been drawn incorrectly unless the drawing is explicitly a transition-state picture with partial bonding. The arrows have to make a chemically plausible intermediate or product.

Now imagine adding a proton to an alcohol. The alcohol's oxygen can use a lone pair to bind a proton delivered by an acid. Its oxygen becomes positively charged. The protonated alcohol now has a possible route to lose water, which is a better leaving group than hydroxide in many reaction contexts. Acid is not simply making the reaction 'stronger' by adding heat or force. Proton transfer changes the electronic character and the leaving-group ability of a part of the molecule. If a later step regenerates the proton donor, the acid can function as a catalyst.

This is why acid-base chemistry is everywhere in organic mechanisms. A carbonyl oxygen can be protonated, making its carbon even more susceptible to attack by certain nucleophiles. An acidic hydrogen adjacent to a carbonyl can be removed to form an enolate, an electron-rich species whose charge is distributed between oxygen and neighboring carbon in the resonance description. The new species has different reaction possibilities from the original carbonyl compound. Proton transfers often determine which molecular form is present before the supposedly main bond-making step even begins.

A useful case is the carbonyl double bond. In acetone, the oxygen attracts electrons, and the carbonyl carbon becomes relatively electron-poor. Suppose a suitable nucleophile donates an electron pair to that carbon. At the same time the pi electrons of the carbon-oxygen double bond move onto oxygen. The carbonyl carbon changes from approximately planar geometry to a tetrahedral arrangement. A later proton transfer can give an alcohol-type product. Notice how geometry, polarity, and electron bookkeeping all meet in one explanation. The mechanism is not just an arrangement of arrows on a page; it predicts a new three-dimensional structure.

Resonance must not be confused with the sequence of a mechanism. If we draw two resonance contributors for an enolate, we are describing one delocalized species with more than one useful Lewis structure. We should not insert a fictional elementary step claiming that the molecule literally bounces between those resonance drawings. If we draw protonation followed by addition and then deprotonation, those represent proposed changes in bonding and composition over time. Keeping these two arrow languages separate prevents a common confusion about what actually happens in solution.

Not all changes require a single electron pair. Radical reactions involve species with unpaired electrons and are represented with different single-headed arrow conventions. In radical chain polymerization, for instance, an unpaired electron can be transferred through successive bond-forming steps. Electrocyclic and other pericyclic reactions may involve coordinated changes across several bonds. The polar curved-arrow patterns we are learning are powerful, but they are not the sole architecture of organic chemistry.

Even for polar reactions, the surroundings matter. A negatively charged nucleophile in water is solvated; water molecules arrange themselves around it, affecting both its stability and its ability to approach a substrate. The same nucleophile in a different solvent may be more reactive, less reactive, or lead to a different product distribution. Solvent polarity, hydrogen bonding, counterions, temperature, concentration, and the shape of the substrate all influence which path wins. A reaction diagram that omits the solvent is a simplified map, not an announcement that the solvent is irrelevant.

We also have to separate the direction a reaction favors from the route it takes. Thermodynamics compares the free energies of initial and final states under defined conditions. The mechanism describes barriers along a pathway. A molecule can have a large energetic reward available in products yet remain unchanged because the accessible barriers are too high. Catalysts can provide new paths with lower barriers without changing the equilibrium constant for the overall reaction. A good arrow-pushing proposal should be consistent with both the reaction's thermodynamics and its kinetics.

One simple way to pressure-test a proposed mechanism is to write what each step predicts about concentration. If the slow step involves one substrate molecule before another molecule participates, the rate may be largely independent of that second molecule's concentration over the measured range. If the slow event requires an encounter between two reaction partners, both concentrations may appear in the experimental rate law. This does not mean every reaction has one easily isolated slow step, but it gives a concrete bridge between a drawing and a measurement.

Stereochemistry provides another bridge. In one substitution pathway, an entering group approaches from the side opposite a leaving group, inverting the arrangement at a reacting tetrahedral carbon. In a different pathway, an initially planar charged intermediate may be attacked from more than one side. If experiments preserve a single stereochemical outcome with clean inversion, they support one class of mechanisms. If they give mixtures, we investigate whether intermediates, competing pathways, or secondary reactions explain the result. The distinction between a mechanism as a hypothesis and a mechanism as established chemistry matters more than the elegance of the arrows.

Imagine receiving an unknown reaction scheme. Instead of searching memory for the named reaction, identify the electron donor and electron acceptor. Check which bonds could form and which groups might leave. Ask whether acid-base steps change the active species. Then use charge conservation, atom balance, spatial access, and rate evidence to discard impossible or unsupported routes. Sometimes you will end with several plausible mechanisms and need data to distinguish them. That is not a failure of chemistry. It is the point at which drawing becomes a scientific question rather than a mnemonic exercise.

## Scientific companion: reaction-arrow audit

**Polar electron-pair arrows.** The tail starts at a lone pair or a bond. The head points toward the atom or bond location receiving that electron pair. Full arrowheads indicate two-electron movement; single-barbed arrows are used for one-electron movement in radical chemistry.

**Formal-charge check.** For a usual Lewis structure, $\mathrm{FC} = V - N_{\mathrm{nonbond}} - \tfrac12 N_{\mathrm{bond}}$, with $V$ the atom's valence-electron count. Formal charge is not the same as measured partial charge or atomic oxidation state.

**Example: addition to a carbonyl.** A model nucleophile $\mathrm{Nu^-}$ adds to $\mathrm{R_2C=O}$ to make $\mathrm{R_2C(O^-)(Nu)}$, followed by protonation when a suitable proton donor is available. The carbonyl carbon changes from approximately trigonal planar to tetrahedral geometry. For neutral nucleophiles, additional proton-transfer steps and charge accounting are needed.

**Testing mechanisms.** Compare proposed pathways with measured reaction orders, isotope substitution, temperature dependence, stereochemical outcomes, trapping of intermediates, and isotope labeling. An arrow scheme alone does not establish a pathway.
