# One Carbon, Four Pathways: Substitution and Elimination

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

Imagine a carbon atom bonded to a bromine atom. We add a reagent, let the mixture react, and discover that bromine is no longer attached. What happened? It might have been replaced by oxygen or nitrogen. Or the carbon framework might have lost a hydrogen as well, creating a carbon-carbon double bond. Even when the overall reaction looks simple, several pathways can compete. Studying that competition teaches us more than memorizing a favorite reagent for every named product.

Begin with substitution. In a basic substitution reaction, an incoming group takes the place of a leaving group attached to carbon. If hydroxide replaces bromide on a simple alkyl bromide, the product can be an alcohol. The bromide ion carries away the electron pair that previously made its bond to carbon. The incoming group makes a new bond. There are two famous pathways for this change, called S N two and S N one. Those labels refer to what takes part in a key kinetic event, not to the number of products or the number of atoms changed overall.

The S N two pathway is a coordinated exchange. Picture hydroxide approaching methyl bromide from the side opposite the bromine. As the new oxygen-carbon bond forms, the carbon-bromine bond weakens and bromide departs. There is no freely existing methyl carbocation intermediate between these changes. Instead, the reaction passes through a high-energy transition configuration where bonding to the incoming and departing groups is partial. This description explains why crowding around the carbon can matter so much: the incoming group must find room to approach along a particular direction.

There is a direct kinetic test. Under appropriate conditions, the substitution rate for methyl bromide and hydroxide depends on both the concentration of methyl bromide and the concentration of hydroxide. Doubling either one while holding the other and the relevant conditions fixed approximately doubles the rate. The observed rate law is proportional to their concentration product. That supports an event involving both reaction partners in the rate-determining process. The S N two mechanism accounts for that rate law and also predicts a spatial consequence at a stereogenic carbon.

Suppose the reacting carbon has four distinct attachments, including its leaving group. When the entering nucleophile attacks from the opposite side, the three other groups move into the arrangement familiar from an umbrella turning inside out. The stereochemical geometry inverts. This does not mean the R label must always become S; the priority ordering of groups can change when the entering group replaces the leaving group. The reliable mechanistic statement is inversion of the local tetrahedral arrangement, with the formal configuration label determined afterward by the normal naming rules.

Now replace methyl bromide with a highly branched tertiary alkyl bromide. The reaction center is shielded by three carbon groups. Direct backside attack is strongly hindered. But a different pathway can become accessible if the departing bromide leaves first, producing a carbocation, a positively charged carbon center. The carbocation can be approximately planar, and a nucleophile can then approach from more than one side. This is the classic S N one picture: a rate-determining ionization of substrate followed by capture by a nucleophile. Over a suitable range, the rate is first order in the substrate and does not depend on the concentration of the nucleophile in the same way that an S N two rate does.

Why is it easier to produce some carbocations than others? Carbon groups can stabilize a positively charged center through electron donation and orbital interactions. Allylic and benzylic carbocations can also benefit from delocalization. A simple primary carbocation is usually much less favorable than a tertiary one in comparable settings. This gives a qualitative preference for S N one chemistry at certain crowded or resonance-stabilized substrates. But it would be wrong to say that every tertiary bromide always follows S N one and every primary bromide always follows S N two. Reagent, solvent, temperature, neighboring groups, and competing chemistry can change the result.

The two pathways also differ in stereochemical consequences. A freely equilibrated planar carbocation can be approached from either face, so an initially pure enantiomer may give both configurations of a substitution product. Actual mixtures are often not perfectly fifty-fifty. The departing ion may remain nearby as an ion pair, or solvent molecules may orient the reactive intermediate and obstruct one approach. In some cases, rearrangements occur before capture: a hydrogen or carbon group shifts to place the positive charge in a more stable location. A rearranged product is evidence that the system had access to a carbocation-like intermediate or another pathway that permits skeletal reorganization. It is not explained by a simple one-step backside displacement.

The word leaving group deserves careful attention. Anions that are relatively stable after departure often serve as better leaving groups than extremely strong bases. Bromide and iodide commonly function as leaving groups in saturated-carbon substitution chemistry. Hydroxide itself is often a poor leaving group unless it is protonated to water or chemically transformed into a better leaving group. A hydroxyl-bearing substrate does not become a typical alkyl bromide simply because the chemist would prefer the hydroxyl group to depart. Activation can be an essential mechanistic step.

Solvents influence these patterns. A polar protic solvent, capable of hydrogen bonding, often stabilizes separated ions and can support ionization pathways. A polar aprotic solvent can dissolve ionic reagents while solvating many anionic nucleophiles differently, often increasing their nucleophilic availability for S N two displacement. Those are useful trends rather than universal solvent rankings. A complete account includes specific ion pairing, substrate solvation, and interactions between solvent and transition state.

But substitution has a rival: elimination. In elimination, the reacting carbon loses a leaving group while a neighboring carbon loses a hydrogen. A new carbon-carbon double bond forms between them. The original reagent may be capable of acting both as a nucleophile, attacking carbon, and as a base, removing a proton. That is why the same starting material may yield both an alcohol-like substitution product and an alkene elimination product.

The E two pathway involves a base taking a hydrogen from the carbon adjacent to the leaving group. As the carbon-hydrogen bond breaks, its electron pair helps form the new pi bond and the leaving group departs. These changes occur in one coordinated step. E two therefore often displays a second-order rate law involving both the substrate and the base. Like S N two, the transition state has a particular geometry, but the spatial requirement is different. The relevant hydrogen and leaving group often need to be approximately anti-periplanar: the bonds point opposite each other in a common plane, allowing suitable orbital overlap.

For an open-chain molecule, rotation about single bonds may provide a conformation with that geometry. For a substituted cyclohexane, the ring can impose a much stronger constraint. In a chair conformation, a neighboring hydrogen and leaving group may need to occupy particular axial positions on opposite faces. Chemists call this the trans-diaxial requirement for many E two eliminations in cyclohexane systems. A group can be present at the right carbon and still be geometrically unavailable until the ring adopts a less favorable conformation. An apparently minor change in stereochemistry can then alter the reaction rate and the alkene formed.

E one elimination is different. A leaving group departs first, producing a carbocation. A base then removes a neighboring proton, allowing a double bond to form. Because S N one substitution and E one elimination can share that first ionization step, they often compete under similar conditions. The observed mixture depends on which subsequent reaction captures the intermediate. Temperature can shift the relative outcomes, but it does not act alone; substrate structure, concentration and solvent are part of the explanation. The label E one tells us the canonical kinetic mechanism, not that a single product is guaranteed.

Which alkene should elimination produce if several positions have suitable hydrogens? Often a more substituted alkene is favored, a trend called Zaitsev orientation. But a bulky base may preferentially remove a more accessible hydrogen, giving a less substituted alkene. And stereochemical constraints can be even more decisive: the more substituted alkene may be inaccessible through the allowed anti-periplanar arrangement. The more general principle is to examine both product stability and the pathway that can actually reach that product. A memorized slogan is no substitute for a drawing of the accessible conformations.

Consider how we would distinguish a mixture of pathways experimentally. First measure how the initial rate changes when we vary substrate concentration. Then vary the nucleophile or base while holding other relevant conditions fixed. Analyze the products: has a carbon substituent been exchanged, or has a double bond formed? Check whether the stereogenic carbon inverts, partially scrambles, or rearranges. Change solvent or temperature carefully and test whether the predicted sensitivity appears. Even when the chemistry looks like an alphabet soup of S N one, S N two, E one, and E two, each label corresponds to falsifiable predictions.

The payoff extends far beyond alkyl bromides. In synthesis, a chemist chooses conditions to favor installing a group, creating an alkene, or avoiding rearrangement. In biology, enzymes position substrates to prefer a specific path, often using acid-base catalysis and orientation to control selectivity. In materials chemistry, elimination can create unsaturation that becomes a starting point for further transformations. The central lesson is that chemical products are shaped not only by what atoms are present but by which route is fast and geometrically available in that environment.

## Scientific companion: competing mechanisms

| Pathway | Key elementary event | Representative measured dependence | Typical stereochemical signal |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $S_N2$ | Concerted backside nucleophile attack and leaving-group loss | $v=k[\mathrm{RX}][\mathrm{Nu}]$ | Inversion at reacting stereogenic carbon |
| $S_N1$ | Ionization to carbocation before nucleophile capture | $v=k[\mathrm{RX}]$ under suitable conditions | Loss of initial stereochemical purity often, not necessarily exact racemization |
| $E2$ | Base removes adjacent H as leaving group departs | $v=k[\mathrm{RX}][\mathrm{Base}]$ | Often anti-periplanar stereospecificity |
| $E1$ | Carbocation formation, then deprotonation | $v=k[\mathrm{RX}]$ under suitable conditions | Alkene distribution influenced by carbocation and conformations |

These are canonical simplified rate laws, not interchangeable universal formulas. Some reactions are complex mixtures or show additional mechanistic phenomena.

**2-bromobutane thought experiment.** Base-mediated reaction can generate but-1-ene and/or but-2-ene by removing H from different neighboring carbons. The but-2-ene product also has E and Z stereoisomers. Product fractions depend on base identity, conformation, solvent, temperature, and mechanism; they cannot be inferred from the substrate formula alone.

**Geometric caveat.** In cyclohexanes, anti-periplanar E2 often requires trans-diaxial H and leaving group in a chair conformer. A ring flip may alter whether the geometry is accessible and at what energetic cost.
