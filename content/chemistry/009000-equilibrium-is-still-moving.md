# Equilibrium Is Still Moving: Reaction Quotients and Chemical Balance

Picture a sealed transparent container containing a gas that can exist as two chemically related species. One species is colored; the other is much less colored. As the temperature changes, so does the shade of the mixture. The color is not simply announcing that the gas expanded or contracted. Molecules have changed partners, and the proportions of two chemical species have changed. If we restore the original conditions and allow time for the system to respond, the mixture tends toward a reproducible composition. This is chemical equilibrium: not a frozen endpoint, but a relationship between chemical potentials, molecular transformations and the conditions around them.

An ordinary reaction arrow points from reactants to products. That arrow can make it sound as if matter has one designated destination. But most chemical transformations have reverse processes too. Whether the reverse is noticeable depends on the conditions and on the relative amounts of material present. A product that is stable in one mixture may participate in a reaction returning it to something else in another. Write the arrow in both directions, and the real question becomes: what determines the mixture toward which the system evolves?

For an elementary reversible change in a closed, well-mixed system at fixed temperature, imagine that forward reactions convert A into B and reverse reactions convert B into A. At first, if mostly A is present, forward events may outnumber reverse events. As B accumulates and A is depleted, reverse events become more frequent. The system can arrive at a composition where the two directions occur at the same average rate. A and B continue reacting, yet their average concentrations stop changing. This is dynamic equilibrium. Equal rates do not mean equal concentrations. They mean that the transfers in opposite directions balance.

If the system were complicated, involving several linked reversible reactions, equilibrium would require the appropriate thermodynamic balance for the full network. Different pathways could continue exchanging molecules, but no unbalanced macroscopic change would remain at equilibrium in the closed system under the stated constraints. Our simple forward-and-reverse picture is a useful entry point, not a complete account of every chemical equilibrium.

To predict the direction of net change, chemists construct a reaction quotient, often called Q. Imagine a reaction with reactants A and B forming product C. The quotient compares the present activities of products with those of reactants, each raised to the power specified by the balanced equation. For a simple one-to-one-to-one reaction, think of it loosely as the effective abundance of C divided by the product of the effective abundances of A and B. The precise definition uses activities, which relate chemical potential to a standard state. In sufficiently dilute solutions, normalized concentrations are often adequate approximations; for ideal gases, relative partial pressures are useful. In concentrated or strongly interacting mixtures, treating activities as plain concentrations can produce meaningful errors.

At a particular temperature and with specified standard states, equilibrium corresponds to a particular value of Q, called K, the equilibrium constant. The numerical value of K may be very large or very small. A large value indicates that the equilibrium composition tends to favor products in the chosen expression, but it does not say all starting reactants must vanish. A small value indicates the other direction is favored. The number depends on how the chemical equation is written: reverse the equation, and K becomes its reciprocal. Multiply every coefficient by two, and the new K is the square of the old one. An equilibrium constant belongs to a defined reaction and its conditions, not to a substance in isolation.

Here's an important prediction. If Q is smaller than K, the mixture has relatively too little product compared with its equilibrium relation, so the thermodynamically favored net change is forward. If Q is larger than K, net change is favored in reverse. If Q equals K, the reaction has no net thermodynamic driving force at that composition under the specified conditions. This comparison tells us a direction, not a speed. A kinetically trapped system can have a Q far from K for a long time. We have already encountered that distinction with diamond and graphite, and with mixtures that require ignition.

Where does the comparison come from? The Gibbs-energy change associated with an infinitesimal advancement of a reaction depends on the current mixture. It equals a standard reaction Gibbs-energy change plus the gas constant times temperature times the natural logarithm of Q. At equilibrium, that reaction Gibbs-energy change is zero. We can therefore rewrite it using K: the reaction Gibbs-energy change is the gas constant times temperature times the natural logarithm of Q divided by K. If the quotient is below one, the logarithm is negative, and net forward advancement lowers Gibbs energy. If it is above one, reverse progress is favored. The sign test is not a separate magic rule; it is the free-energy criterion expressed through composition.

We can make it concrete with a deliberately simple model. Suppose A reversibly becomes B, and the equilibrium constant for this direction is three. In an idealized dilute setting, that means the equilibrium effective abundance of B is three times that of A. If we begin with only A, some changes into B. In a one-liter thought experiment that starts with one mole of A and no B, ignoring nonideality and any volume change, the approximate equilibrium is one-quarter mole of A and three-quarters mole of B. Both substances remain. The fact that most molecules are in state B does not make the reverse molecular events disappear.

Now imagine we suddenly add more A while keeping temperature unchanged. Right afterward, Q has decreased. The system is no longer at the equilibrium ratio, and net conversion toward B becomes favored. People often summarize this response by saying that equilibrium opposes an added reactant. That is a version of Le Châtelier's principle. It is helpful when its assumptions are respected, but the quotient calculation tells us more precisely what changed and why.

The slogan can also mislead. A catalyst does not shift the equilibrium position at fixed temperature; it accelerates the approach from both sides of a reversible pathway. Adding an inert gas at constant volume to a mixture of ideal reacting gases does not change the relevant partial pressures of the reacting components, so it does not shift the equilibrium merely because the total pressure increased. Adding an inert gas at constant total pressure can change volume and partial pressures, and may then affect equilibrium. The details of what was held constant matter as much as the claim that some variable was increased.

Consider the industrial synthesis of ammonia. Nitrogen gas and hydrogen gas can combine to make ammonia, with one nitrogen molecule and three hydrogen molecules on the reactant side corresponding to two ammonia molecules on the product side. The net reaction is exothermic. Higher pressure generally favors the side with fewer gas molecules under appropriate gas-phase assumptions, which is the ammonia side. Lower temperature favors ammonia at equilibrium for this exothermic reaction. But at lower temperature the reaction can become too slow to be useful. Industrial design must therefore negotiate pressure, temperature, catalysts, equipment limits, gas recycling and product separation. There is no one-number solution to this optimization problem, and equilibrium thermodynamics is only one part of it.

Another kind of equilibrium involves a solid in contact with a solution. A crystalline salt can dissolve, releasing dissolved ions, while dissolved ions can also join the crystal. At a saturated equilibrium, dissolution and crystallization balance in the relevant sense. If a pure solid phase is present, its activity is conventionally one, so its amount does not appear as a concentration factor in the usual equilibrium expression. Yet the solid is not irrelevant: its presence defines the phase equilibrium. Remove the entire solid, and you may no longer have the same phase assemblage or constraints.

The same qualification helps explain why a saturated solution does not mean an unlimited reservoir of dissolved chemical. If we add enough reagent to precipitate a solid, material is being transferred from dissolved species into another phase. The solution composition can become buffered by that precipitation process while solid is present. If we add acid to dissolve a carbonate mineral, the process couples dissolution to acid-base chemistry and perhaps to carbon dioxide exchange. A single balance can be linked to several others. Real waters, soils, and biological fluids are full of these coupled equilibria.

Open and closed systems require different thinking. Uncap a carbonated drink and carbon dioxide begins escaping. The gas pressure above the liquid is no longer maintained at the sealed-bottle value. The liquid can move toward a new equilibrium with the atmosphere, but the visible fizz is part of a changing, open process. Stirring and bubble nucleation alter how rapidly dissolved gas escapes. The equilibrium solubility depends on the gas's partial pressure and other conditions; the rate of bubbles appearing depends on kinetics and transport. Here, too, the observed event has both a destination and a clock.

Temperature adds a special kind of change. The equilibrium constant itself generally depends on temperature, because temperature changes the relative free energies of the reactants and products. Changing the concentration at fixed temperature changes Q immediately but not K. Changing temperature can change both the equilibrium target and how quickly the mixture approaches it. For an exothermic reaction, increasing temperature often shifts equilibrium toward reactants, but the quantitative answer depends on the relevant reaction enthalpy and its temperature dependence. Applying a one-line slogan to a complex biochemical or geological network without specifying its reaction and boundaries is not a reliable substitute for thermodynamics.

Finally, an equilibrium description makes assumptions about time. If gases or solutes are transported into or out of a system, reaction rates can balance the flow of matter in a persistent nonequilibrium steady state. A living cell may maintain stable concentrations by continuously taking in food and oxygen and exporting waste. Its concentrations are approximately steady, but the system is not thermodynamic equilibrium. A still number on a measuring instrument is not enough to decide between those situations. We have to ask whether matter or energy is being supplied, and whether a net process is sustained.

The colored gas in the sealed container was our starting point. Its shade became a way to see a changing chemical balance, but only because composition affected light absorption. What governs the composition is not the color: it is the relationship between chemical potentials under the imposed conditions. Reaction quotients let us predict the direction of change. Equilibrium constants specify the destination. Kinetics and transport tell us how the system gets there, if it gets there during the time we can observe. Chemical equilibrium is powerful precisely because it is not another word for stillness.

## Scientific companion: Q, K, and Gibbs energy

For $aA+bB\rightleftharpoons cC+dD$ at specified temperature $T$, standard states and activities $a_i$,

$$Q=\frac{a_C^c a_D^d}{a_A^a a_B^b},\qquad \Delta_r G=\Delta_r G^\circ+RT\ln Q=RT\ln\frac{Q}{K},$$

$$\Delta_r G^\circ=-RT\ln K.$$

Activities and $Q,K$ are dimensionless. At fixed $T$ with $Q<K$, forward advancement has $\Delta_rG<0$. For sufficiently dilute ideal solutions, $a_i\approx [i]/c^\circ$ is an approximation, not a definition. At equilibrium, $Q=K$; balanced opposing microscopic events may continue.

### Constructed example: A reversible conversion

For hypothetical $A\rightleftharpoons B$ with $K=3.0$ and initial amount $n_A=1.0$ mol, $n_B=0$, fixed suitable volume and ideal behavior: $K\approx n_B/n_A=x/(1-x)$, hence $x=0.75$ mol, leaving $0.25$ mol A and producing $0.75$ mol B. The example is deliberately not assigned to a named chemical system.

### Gas and phase cases

- $\mathrm{N_2+3H_2\rightleftharpoons2NH_3}$ has $Q=(a_{\rm NH_3})^2/(a_{\rm N_2}a_{\rm H_2}^3)$. Pressure effects require specifying whether volume, total pressure or partial pressures are controlled and whether ideal behavior is appropriate.
- If a pure solid is present in a heterogeneous phase equilibrium, its activity is one in the standard formulation. It cannot be ignored if the entire solid phase disappears.
- A catalyst alters rates, not $K$ at fixed $T$. The net reaction enthalpy helps determine the temperature dependence of $K$ (van ’t Hoff relation), subject to its temperature dependence.
