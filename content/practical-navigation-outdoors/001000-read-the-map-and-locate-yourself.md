# Read the map and locate yourself

## Read the Land, Not Just the Arrow: Maps, Bearings, Terrain, and Uncertainty

You are on a hiking trail and your phone shows a dot just east of a blue line. The dot seems precise, but you notice that the trail on the ground bends west instead. Which should you trust? Neither without thought. Your phone estimates a position with errors, the map represents selected features, and trails may move or branch. Navigation is the skill of combining imperfect information to maintain a useful estimate of where you are and how to reach a destination.

A map is not the ground reduced in size. It is a model. A compass does not tell you where you are. It tells you a direction relative to a magnetic field when interpreted correctly. A satellite receiver does not tell you which path is passable. It estimates coordinates. Good navigation combines each tool with terrain, weather, distance, and direct observation.

### Orient before moving

Imagine a paper map of a valley. A river runs from the northwest to the southeast. A ridge rises on the west side, and a road crosses the river at one bridge. If you stand at the bridge, you have a strong known location. From there, a route description should specify more than "walk north." It should identify the trail or terrain feature you intend to follow and the decision points at which you will verify progress.

Orienting a map means relating its directions to the physical world. This can be done from a known road, river, ridge and other landmarks, or with a compass that is interpreted with respect to the map's north reference. The goal is to be able to point from a mapped landmark toward its real-world counterpart and understand which way a route turns.

### North has more than one meaning

True north points toward the geographic North Pole along a meridian. Magnetic north is related to the local direction of Earth's magnetic field. Grid north is the direction of the grid lines in a particular map projection. These directions need not coincide. The horizontal angle between true and magnetic north is magnetic declination, and it varies with location and time. Maps may provide a declination diagram and a date; a current local value can be obtained from a reliable geomagnetic model.

This is why copying a bearing from an old guide without knowing whether it uses magnetic, true or grid north can lead to error. The significance depends on distance: a few degrees may be negligible for a short walk to a clearly visible tree but consequential after traveling across open terrain. Reliable practice is to state the north reference whenever recording a bearing and to verify the route with terrain rather than relying on one angle alone.

A handheld compass can also be affected by nearby ferrous metal or electrical equipment. If the needle changes strangely when you move near a vehicle or metal fence, the observation is not evidence that the planet's magnetic field suddenly turned. Move away from local interference and compare bearings against independent landmarks.

### Contours turn a flat sheet into a landscape

A topographic map uses contour lines joining points of equal elevation. Closely spaced contour lines indicate steeper ground than widely spaced ones when the contour interval is the same. Closed contours can indicate a hill or depression, with symbols or surrounding pattern needed to interpret which. Contours crossing a stream valley often form a V-shape pointing upstream, although careful interpretation requires the full context.

Picture a map where your route crosses five contour lines of equal interval over a short horizontal distance. You are likely facing a substantial climb or descent. If the same elevation change occurs over a much longer distance, the grade is gentler. The map cannot tell you exactly how slippery the ground is or whether a fallen tree blocks the path, but it can warn you about the broad physical effort involved.

Scale matters. On a map with scale one to twenty-four thousand, one centimeter on the map represents two hundred forty meters on the ground, assuming the map is used as specified. This distance is horizontal projection, not the actual length of a steep or winding trail. A curved route, switchbacks, and elevation gain all increase the distance and effort beyond a straight ruler measurement.

### Dead reckoning is controlled accumulation of error

If you know a starting point, travel direction, and estimated distance, you can estimate a new position. This is dead reckoning. The method is useful but accumulates error. A compass bearing may be off by a few degrees, your distance estimate may be wrong, and the path may force detours. As you continue, the position estimate becomes less certain unless new landmarks constrain it.

A practice called terrain association periodically compares what you see with what the map predicts: an intersection, saddle, stream, ridge, bridge or clearing. Strong landmarks can reset uncertainty. Small or repetitive features may mislead you. The aim is not to worship a blue arrow, but to make repeated cross-checks that have independent explanatory value.

A useful route plan identifies handrails, such as following a stream or ridge, and catching features, such as a road or shoreline beyond the intended turn. These are not substitutes for situational awareness; waterways can become hazardous and ridges may branch. The idea is to choose evidence that will tell you whether you have gone too far or turned wrong.

### Weather changes the problem itself

A perfectly understood route can become impassable with heavy rain, snow, high wind, heat or poor visibility. Weather affects both personal capability and terrain. Water crossings can change depth and current. Fog removes distant landmarks. Snow conceals trail tread and changes the effort of travel. Lightning and rapidly changing mountain conditions can invalidate a plan even if the map is accurate.

A navigation decision is therefore conditional: given the time, conditions, equipment, people, and remaining daylight, is the route still reasonable? Time estimates should include climbing, terrain, rest, and group pace rather than just straight-line distance. A turnaround criterion established before the hardest section can reduce the temptation to continue merely because you have already invested effort.

### Technology gives estimates, not guarantees

GNSS, a family that includes GPS, uses satellite signals and receiver models to estimate location. Accuracy can degrade in canyons, dense cover, near reflective surfaces, or when satellite geometry is poor. Maps stored on a phone may be old, and downloading a map is different from ensuring you can access it offline. Battery life, cold temperatures, water damage and network coverage impose additional constraints.

A paper map and compass do not require network access or device power, but they require knowledge and practice. A phone can be an excellent navigation instrument, particularly when cross-checked against terrain. The useful redundancy is not having three apps that all display the same underlying inaccurate basemap. It is having different sources of evidence.

### A map-reading exercise

Find a topographic map of a familiar park. Choose a known start and a destination. Estimate the route distance, major elevation gain, water crossings and intersections. Identify two places where your location could be checked against distinctive terrain. Pick a point where the group would turn around if it was taking longer than expected. Then visit the area on an ordinary outing and compare the map to the ground. Record what surprised you: an unmarked trail, a contour that seemed steeper than expected, a bridge not shown, or a landmark you failed to notice.

Navigation improves when we become better at estimating and revising, not when we learn to stare more intently at a screen. A bearing tells a direction. A map offers a model. The land supplies evidence. The skill is making those sources agree often enough that you know both where you intend to go and when you should change your plan.

## Topographic Contours, Slope, and Landforms

The map becomes more useful when its contour pattern is connected to the shape of the ground.

Unfold a topographic map and you may see hundreds of thin brown lines winding around the page. To a beginner they can resemble spilled spaghetti. To someone who knows what they represent, those lines are a compressed model of the land: ridges, valleys, bowls, steep slopes, gentle benches, and the route water is likely to follow. The map does not simply show where you are. It gives you a way to imagine the ground before you reach it.

A contour line connects map locations of equal elevation relative to a specified vertical datum. Walk along one contour in an idealized map, and you maintain the same elevation, even as the ground rises and falls to your sides. Each neighboring contour differs by a fixed elevation increment, the contour interval. On many maps, every fifth or otherwise regularly spaced contour is emphasized and labeled; these are index contours. Always read the actual map margin for the interval and units rather than assuming a familiar value.

### Close lines mean steep ground

Imagine two hills rising by the same two hundred feet. One gains that elevation over half a mile. The other gains it within a few hundred feet. The second is much steeper. On a map, the same number of contour intervals squeezed into a short horizontal distance means a steeper grade. Wide spacing usually indicates gentler ground.

A small calculation makes this concrete. Suppose adjacent contours differ by forty feet. A route crosses five contour *intervals*, gaining two hundred feet. If the horizontal map distance between the starting and ending elevation locations is eight hundred feet, the average rise over run is two hundred divided by eight hundred, or twenty-five percent grade. This is about fourteen degrees as an angle, because grade is the tangent of the slope angle, not the angle itself. The actual walking distance over sloping ground is longer than the horizontal map distance, and the route may include steeper and gentler pieces. Average grade does not describe every step.

There is an ambiguity worth noticing. “Crosses five lines” is not always the same as “crosses five intervals.” Counting from an unlabeled elevation can introduce a one-interval error. It is better to identify two known elevations and calculate the difference directly.

### Shape distinguishes a ridge from a valley

Contour lines curve and form V-shaped or U-shaped patterns where they pass across ridges and drainages. A common map-reading rule is that a contour crossing a stream valley bends toward higher ground, pointing upstream. Crossing a protruding ridge or spur often produces the opposite bend, toward lower ground. But the shape can be subtle, and you need nearby elevation labels or the surrounding pattern to know which direction is actually uphill.

A closed contour is another clue. Concentric closed loops whose labeled elevations rise inward may mark a hilltop. Closed loops with inward-pointing hachure marks often indicate a depression under conventional USGS symbol systems. A plain unlabeled loop alone does not certify a summit; read the neighboring contours and symbols.

A saddle is a lower passage between two higher areas, often represented by hourglass-like contour patterns. On the ground it may be a natural crossing between drainages or a way over a ridge. Learning to recognize saddles is useful for planning routes that avoid unnecessary climbs.

### The map's scale controls what it can say

A topographic map is a scaled representation of the horizontal positions of features. At one to twenty-four thousand scale, one unit on the map corresponds to twenty-four thousand of the same units on the ground. One centimeter represents two hundred forty meters horizontally. But a narrow ravine, boulder field or small cliff may not be depicted with every geometric detail. Contour intervals and map generalization hide fine-scale hazards.

Contour lines are interpolations of terrain elevation data and cartographic choices. Accuracy depends on data source, survey method, age and map series. A trail may have moved since the map was produced. A new road cut may change a hillside. The map is evidence about the terrain, not the terrain itself.

### Route choice is not merely shortest distance

Draw a straight line from your location to a campsite. It is the shortest horizontal route on paper, but it may cross a steep ravine, difficult vegetation and a stream. A contour-aware route may follow a ridge, traverse a gentler hillside or cross a drainage where the slopes are manageable. The longer horizontal route could be faster or safer because it reduces steep climbing or difficult ground.

Contouring—moving roughly along the same elevation—can save elevation change but may require side-hill travel where footing is awkward. Following a ridge can be an excellent navigational handrail, yet some ridges contain cliffs or exposed sections. Drainages offer strong terrain references but can contain steep banks, flooding or dense vegetation. No landform is universally the right path. The map helps you anticipate what to verify on the ground.

### Orienting the map to the world

A printed north arrow, grid lines and a compass can help align the map with the landscape, but they do not all refer to exactly the same kind of north. True north, grid north and magnetic north may differ. Declination and grid convergence vary with location and map projection; they are not properties you can ignore by repeating a single conversion for every map.

An alternative orientation method is terrain association. Identify two or three conspicuous features—such as a lake, ridge crest and road intersection—and rotate the map until their relative positions agree with what you see. A compass provides an independent check, particularly where landmarks are sparse. Electronic positioning can supply a location estimate, but it does not eliminate the need to interpret where that location sits relative to a slope or drainage.

### A small map-reading experiment

Choose a contour map with a labeled hill, a stream and a saddle. Start at a marked trail junction. Before looking at any profile tool, describe how the route would feel: gentle climb, steep ascent, level traverse, short descent. Then draw an elevation profile by taking elevations at several points along the route. Finally, compare your predictions with the resulting profile and, if available, field observations. The exercise trains the connection between symbols and physical terrain.

The map's brown lines are not a decorative background. They are statements about height. Spacing encodes slope. Curvature reveals landform shape. Labels and interval establish scale in the vertical direction. Reading them converts a flat sheet of paper into a three-dimensional prediction that you can test as you travel.

## Compass Bearings, True and Magnetic North

Imagine standing at a trail junction with a map in one hand and a magnetic compass in the other. The map says the next ridge lies northeast. You align the compass and head away, but later the ridge appears farther to your right than expected. Perhaps you followed the wrong spur. Perhaps your map and compass used different definitions of north. Before either explanation can be tested, we need to understand what a bearing actually measures.

A bearing is a direction expressed as an angle, often clockwise from a chosen north reference. Zero degrees points north, ninety east, one hundred eighty south, and two hundred seventy west. But the reference word 'north' hides several possibilities.

### True north and magnetic north

True north is the direction along Earth's surface toward the geographic North Pole, as defined by the local meridian. Magnetic north, for a compass, is the direction of the horizontal component of Earth's magnetic field at that location. It does not necessarily point exactly toward the moving north magnetic pole, and it does not generally match true north.

The angular difference between the local magnetic direction and true north is magnetic declination. Under the convention used by NOAA, east declination is positive and west declination is negative. The value depends on location and changes with time. A diagram printed on an old map therefore describes its survey or publication context; it may need updating for present-day navigation.

There is also grid north, the direction of a map's chosen north-south coordinate grid. On many map projections, grid north differs slightly from true north except along selected lines. A topographic map may show a declination diagram with true, magnetic, and grid north distinguished. Those arrows are not decorative. They tell you how to translate between two different coordinate systems.

### A conversion that can be checked

Suppose the local magnetic declination is twelve degrees east. You take a magnetic bearing of seventy degrees with a correctly used compass. Under NOAA's signed convention, the true bearing is seventy plus twelve, or eighty-two degrees. Now reverse the question. If the map indicates a true bearing of eighty-two degrees, you would subtract the twelve-degree east declination to obtain a magnetic bearing of seventy degrees.

For a west declination, use a negative number. If the declination were eight degrees west, a magnetic bearing of seventy degrees would correspond to a true bearing of sixty-two degrees. That is much safer than memorizing an ambiguous phrase like 'add east, subtract west' without knowing which direction the conversion is going.

Different compass designs allow different workflows. With an adjustable-declination compass, you may set the correct local declination and work directly in true bearings according to its instructions. With a non-adjustable compass, you may convert map bearings before setting the dial. Be consistent about the reference used by GPS, map, and compass. Do not apply the declination correction twice.

### Orienting the map

In simple terms, orienting a map means rotating it so features on paper correspond to directions in the terrain. A compass can assist, but first you must know what its north arrow and the map's north reference represent. Metallic objects, electronic devices, power lines, and nearby magnetic materials can distort a compass reading. If the needle behaves erratically, move away from the source and repeat the observation.

You can also orient by recognizable terrain. If two distinctive ridgelines or a visible road junction align with known map features, that provides an independent check. A compass heading that contradicts every clear terrain feature deserves investigation, not automatic obedience.

### Follow a bearing as a path, not a spell

Suppose a hilltop lies somewhere ahead on a given bearing. You set the bearing, then select a visible landmark on that line: a tree, boulder, or terrain notch. Walk toward it while observing ground conditions, then repeat. This is more reliable than staring continuously at the compass and drifting around obstacles without tracking the original direction.

When detouring around a pond or steep slope, the direction of travel temporarily changes. The navigation task is to recover your intended route using terrain, map features, or a planned offset. 'Keep walking northeast' is not enough because terrain is not a frictionless plane. Distance, elevation, and identifiable features are as important as azimuth.

A bearing is one constraint on position, not a complete location. If you know neither your starting location nor distance traveled, a perfect bearing still leaves many possible places on the map. Conversely, two well-identified landmarks and their bearings can help narrow location when geometry permits. Accuracy depends on landmark identification, declination, compass handling, and the map's own precision.

### The compass works only inside its assumptions

Near certain polar regions, the horizontal magnetic field can be weak enough that magnetic-compass directions become unreliable. More commonly, local magnetic interference and user error dominate. A compass tilted far from level, held near steel, or read with the wrong end of the needle can lead to substantial errors. The compass responds to a real field, not to your intention.

Today's phones and GPS devices can display true or magnetic courses, sometimes with their own conversion settings. Their electronic compasses may require calibration, and a displayed heading can differ from direction of travel when the device is stationary. The ability to read a topographic map and compare multiple observations remains useful when electronics fail or disagree.

### A small field exercise

In an open, familiar area, identify a distinctive landmark whose position is clear on a map. Record the map's north reference and the date of its declination information. Obtain a suitable current declination for your location, then take a magnetic bearing to the landmark. Convert it to true, and compare with the map bearing. Repeat the observation after moving away from cars, metal railings, and other potential interference.

The value of the exercise is not merely getting close to the right number. It is learning to state which north you used, how you converted, and how you checked the result. Navigation improves when directions become documented measurements rather than instincts dressed in degrees.

## Map Scales, Distance, and Elevation Gain

A trailhead and a summit appear close together on a topographic map. A straight line connects them in about two inches. The map scale says one inch represents two thousand feet, so the distance is four thousand feet, less than a mile. The hike sounds short. But the trail bends around cliffs, crosses a stream valley, and climbs repeatedly over ridges. The route may be much longer, and the vertical effort may matter more than the straight-line distance.

A map is a measurement model. Its scale tells you how distances represented on paper relate to horizontal distances on the ground. In a representative U.S. Geological Survey map at one to twenty-four thousand scale, one unit on the map represents twenty-four thousand of the same units on the ground. One inch corresponds to two thousand feet. One centimeter corresponds to two hundred forty meters. The ratio is independent of whether you use feet or meters, as long as you keep units consistent.

### The line you measure must match the question

If you measure directly from one landmark to another, you have the map-plane distance between them. That can help with line-of-sight or a rough bearing problem, but it is not necessarily the length of the walking route. A trail twists through switchbacks. Measuring along it requires following the centerline closely, perhaps with a flexible string, map wheel, or digital path tool. The measurement remains approximate because the map generalizes terrain and the trail may not match its printed location exactly.

There is also the difference between horizontal and slope distance. A map usually represents positions in a horizontal reference system; steep terrain adds a vertical component. In a simple example, moving three hundred meters horizontally while gaining four hundred meters vertically would be a five-hundred-meter straight slope distance by the Pythagorean relationship. Real paths are rarely straight slopes, and a hiker usually follows a longer route. For ordinary trails with moderate gradients, horizontal and ground distance can be close enough for rough planning, but steep terrain deserves more careful thought.

### Contours are a way of measuring terrain

A contour line joins points of equal elevation. If the map's contour interval is twenty feet, crossing one contour to the next generally represents a twenty-foot elevation change, provided you're moving monotonically uphill or downhill. Closely spaced contours indicate steeper slopes; widely spaced contours indicate gentler terrain. Index contours are often drawn heavier and labeled so that you can orient your count.

A summit labeled twelve hundred feet and a trailhead at eight hundred feet give a net elevation gain of four hundred feet. But suppose the trail first climbs from eight hundred to a thousand, descends to nine hundred, and then climbs to twelve hundred. The total positive climbing is two hundred plus three hundred, or five hundred feet, even though the summit stands only four hundred feet above the start. The descent in the middle forces a second ascent. This is the difference between net elevation change and cumulative ascent.

Real route profiles have many small ups and downs. GPS elevation estimates and digital route planners may disagree because of noisy altitude readings, map resolution, smoothing methods, or different elevation datasets. A cumulative-ascent figure based on noisy point-to-point measurements can be exaggerated by counting every tiny fluctuation. The number should be understood as an estimate with a method behind it, not a precise physical constant.

### Slope is rise divided by horizontal run

Suppose a path gains one hundred meters over one kilometer of horizontal distance. The average grade is one hundred divided by one thousand, or ten percent. This does not mean every segment is ten percent; one part may be flat and another very steep. A ten-percent grade corresponds to an angle whose tangent is one tenth, which is about 5.7 degrees. Percent grade and angle are not the same units.

For planning, grade explains something that mileage alone cannot. A short, steep climb can demand more effort than a longer gentle approach. Surface conditions, loose rock, mud, ice, exposure, weather, daylight, and a traveler's capabilities further affect time and risk. The map's contour pattern can flag places where a direct route may be slower or impractical, but a contour map does not show every fallen tree, closed trail, or dangerous ledge.

### The map itself may be imperfect or old

A paper map has a date, scale, contour interval, coordinate reference, and intended use. An older edition may show a bridge or trail that no longer exists. A digital map can provide current routing for some areas, but not all tracks are accurate. Verify critical features against multiple sources when possible and preserve a navigation method that does not depend on a working cellular connection.

On your next map, identify the scale bar and the contour interval before making any distance claim. Measure one straight line, then measure the actual route, then estimate its net climb and its total positive climb. You will have four different numbers, each answering a different question.

The map is not a prediction of how hard the day will feel. It is a compact mathematical account of location and terrain. Learning what its measurements do and do not mean is the first step toward choosing routes based on evidence rather than a misleading straight line.

## Terrain Association and Catching Features

Scale and bearings need to be checked against recognizable terrain, rather than followed without context.

You follow a trail toward a ridge. After a while the path bends, your phone loses its position estimate, and two valleys appear below. Which valley is the one shown on your map? An arrow on a compass can tell you which way is north. It does not tell you which hill you are standing on. Navigation becomes far more reliable when you compare the shape of the real land with the contours and features drawn on the map.

Terrain association means recognizing landforms and using them to determine location or choose a route. It is a continual process of matching predictions with observations. Before moving, predict what the ground ahead should look like. Then check whether the real terrain supports that prediction. A mismatch is evidence that your position, orientation or interpretation may be wrong.

### Contours are a picture of shape

Contour lines connect points at equal elevation. Where they crowd together, the mapped slope is steep; where they spread apart, it is gentler. Closed nested contours may indicate a hilltop, unless hachure marks or other map conventions indicate a depression. A ridge descends outward from higher ground; valleys often carry streams and draw contours into upstream-pointing V shapes on a conventional contour map. This rule is useful but should be confirmed with actual elevations and drainage context rather than treated as an absolute from line shape alone.

A saddle is a lower pass between higher areas. On a contour map it can resemble an hourglass between neighboring high points. On the ground it may be a natural crossing between valleys. A spur is a projection of a ridge extending downhill. A reentrant is a small drainage feature or indentation running into a slope. Learning these forms gives you a vocabulary for interpreting both map and landscape.

Try to imagine walking downhill from a ridge crest. One side falls toward a stream, another toward a second drainage. The crest divides the watersheds. If the map shows you standing just south of the crest but the ground falls away to the south and east, that observation can help narrow your position. Orientation, water flow, sun exposure and vegetation can provide additional clues, though none alone is perfectly reliable.

### Handrails reduce the navigation problem

A handrail is a linear feature you can follow or keep alongside your route: a stream, ridge, road, shoreline or conspicuous forest edge. Rather than attempting to walk a precise bearing through featureless woods for a long distance, you may choose a route that meets a known creek and follows it to an intersection. Handrails make small positional errors easier to correct because they constrain where you are likely to be.

A catching feature is a recognizable landmark beyond your intended destination that tells you you have gone too far. If you are heading toward a small trail junction but there is a wide river beyond it, the river can serve as a catching feature. You should know the river exists before setting out; only discovering it after wandering past the junction is less useful.

An attack point is a prominent, easy-to-identify location near a less obvious destination. You might navigate to a bridge first, then take a short bearing and measured distance to an obscure trail entrance. This reduces the amount of navigation performed without strong external references. Aiming off is a related technique: deliberately approach a known linear feature to one side of a target so that, upon reaching the line, you know which direction to turn. These ideas work only if the chosen features are real, current and unambiguous on the ground.

### Bearings are checks, not spells

A bearing helps maintain direction over a segment, but local obstacles force deviations, and small angle errors accumulate into large lateral offsets over distance. Magnetic declination changes with location and time, and some metal objects or electronics distort a compass reading. A GPS display can reduce uncertainty when functioning, but it may give false confidence through poor reception, stale maps or ambiguous tracks.

A sensible route combines methods. Follow a visible ridge where possible. At a saddle, check that the adjacent valleys and high points match the map. Use a compass bearing across a short section where terrain features are weak. At the next obvious feature, re-establish your position instead of assuming that the previous heading guarantees the correct location.

Distance estimates help but are uncertain. Map distance is horizontal; walking distance increases with bends and slope. Pace counting varies with terrain, fatigue and load. Time estimates are affected by weather and obstacles. If your expected stream crossing has not appeared, the answer is not always to walk another twenty minutes; it may be to stop and recheck your hypotheses.

### Design a route with a recovery strategy

Picture crossing mixed woodland toward a pond. Rather than drawing the shortest straight line on the map, identify a track that leads to a broad ridge, then a distinct saddle, then a stream that flows toward the pond. Choose a road beyond the pond as a catching feature. Mark where you expect to see each change in terrain. If the expected saddle never appears, return to the last confidently recognized feature or otherwise follow a prepared recovery plan rather than escalating uncertainty.

Weather, darkness, flooding and seasonal vegetation can change how visible or passable a feature is. A stream that is a reliable reference in dry weather may become a dangerous barrier after heavy rain. A vegetation boundary may not match an older map. Choose features according to their stability and the conditions of the trip.

Navigation is therefore a dialogue between representation and reality. The map predicts, the terrain answers, and your job is to update your position estimate. A compass gives direction; terrain association gives context. Used together, they turn a walk into a series of verifiable decisions instead of one long guess.

### Topographic Contours, Slope, and Landforms

**Track:** Navigation and Outdoor Skills. **Background:** Map and compass fundamentals. **Purpose:** read three-dimensional landforms from contour spacing and shape while respecting mapping limitations.

#### Technical companion: examples and map limitations

**Definitions:** Contour = constant mapped elevation; interval = elevation difference between adjacent standard contours; index contour = emphasized/labeled guide; scale `1:N` = horizontal distance ratio in equal units. `grade = rise / horizontal_run`; `angle = arctan(grade)`. Example: five 40-foot intervals = 200-foot rise; over 800-foot horizontal run, grade = 0.25 = 25%, angle ≈ 14.0°. This is an average over the selected route segment.

**Terrain clues:** Contour V at drainage commonly points upstream toward higher elevation; at ridge/spur, bend often points downslope. Confirm using labeled elevations. Closed elevated loops and depression hachures depend on symbol convention. Map marginalia contain datum, interval, edition and source information.

**Evidence:** USGS *US Topo Map Symbol Guide* and *Finding Your Way with Map and Compass* give contour, spacing and symbol conventions. Modern USGS maps also vary by series and data date. Check map updates, access restrictions and current weather separately when planning actual travel.

**Exercise:** Sketch two elevation profiles over identical horizontal distances: one crossing tightly spaced contours and one widely spaced. Label total rise, maximum local grade and uncertainty due to map interval.

### Compass Bearings, True and Magnetic North

**Track:** Navigation and Outdoor Skills. **Background:** Topographic maps, contour lines, and map scale. **Purpose:** connect bearings on a map to directions shown by a magnetic compass.

#### Technical companion (non-spoken)

##### Signed declination convention

Using NOAA's convention, **true bearing = magnetic bearing + signed declination** (modulo `360°`), with east positive and west negative. The reverse is `magnetic = true − signed declination`. Example: `magnetic 70° + east 12° = true 82°`; `magnetic 70° + west 8° = true 62°`.

Grid bearings need separate **grid convergence** corrections based on map projection and location; do not silently equate true north with grid north. Device software may already have corrected heading and must not be corrected again.

##### Evidence and application

NOAA NCEI's magnetic declination pages explain signed conversion, variation by location/time, and local disturbances. The USGS 'How Should I Set My GPS and Compass?' provides a practical example of adjustable and non-adjustable compass workflows. Obtain a current location-specific value before real navigation. This narrated lesson is not a substitute for a current map, route plan, and field skill.

#### Selected reading and claim sources (non-spoken)

### Map Scales, Distance, and Elevation Gain

**Track:** Navigation and Outdoor Skills. **Background:** Contours, landforms, true and magnetic north. **Purpose:** translate a line on a topographic map into approximate travel distance and cumulative climbing effort.

#### Technical companion (non-spoken)

##### Worked scale and grade examples

At scale 1:24,000, 1 inch on paper = 24,000 inches = 2,000 feet on the ground, and 1 cm = 240 m. A map trace of 7.5 cm corresponds to approximately 1.8 km **horizontal/map-plane** distance. For constant gradient, \(\text{grade} = \Delta h / d_{horizontal}\); a rise of 100 m over a 1000 m horizontal run is 10% grade and angle \(\arctan(0.1)\approx5.71^\circ\).

Cumulative gain \(\sum_i\max(0,h_{i+1}-h_i)\) depends on sampling and smoothing. Do not confuse it with \(h_{finish}-h_{start}\). Ground distance is \(\sqrt{d_{horizontal}^2+\Delta h^2}\) for a *single straight segment*; a trail consists of multiple segments.

##### Map study

Choose a current USGS topographic map. Read its title, publication date, scale, contour interval, and north references. Trace a walking route and mark ridge crossings, valley bottoms, index contours, and likely steep sections. Estimate cumulative ascent without treating a dotted trail as current access permission. Cross-check closures, hazards, and local land access before travel.

### Terrain Association and Catching Features

**Track:** Navigation and Outdoor Skills. **Background:** map scale, contour lines and compass bearings. **Purpose:** use terrain as evidence of location, not merely follow a compass heading.

#### Technical companion (non-spoken)

##### Landform recognition worksheet

Sketch five contour patterns and corresponding cross-sections: hilltop, ridge/spur, reentrant, saddle, and valley. Mark stream direction by checking decreasing contour elevations rather than relying exclusively on the conventional V mnemonic.

##### Route-design exercise

Mark start, destination, attack point, handrail, catching feature, decision points, and a recovery route. At each decision point, write an expected observation such as 'ridge turns southeast' or 'stream junction below a saddle.' Terrain association is a position hypothesis verified repeatedly, not a substitute for route preparation.

##### Limits

Check map publication date, seasonal water crossings, magnetic declination, weather and access restrictions. Some line features shown on older maps have changed; do not rely on an unverified map trail or an unsafe river crossing as your only fallback.
