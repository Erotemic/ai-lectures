# Saint-Domingue: The Wealth of a Colony and the People Who Made It

A merchant in late-eighteenth-century Nantes, Bordeaux, or Paris might have praised a Caribbean colony for the wealth it brought to France. A person forced to cut cane in that colony would have had a very different account of the same wealth. Both accounts concerned the same fields, the same ships, and the same sugar. The difference was who could claim the profits, who endured the labor, and who was permitted to describe the arrangement as legitimate.

The colony was Saint-Domingue, on the western part of the Caribbean island now shared by Haiti and the Dominican Republic. In 1791, a revolt began there that eventually destroyed French slavery in the colony and helped create an independent country. The history is often told through generals: Toussaint Louverture, Jean-Jacques Dessalines, Henri Christophe, and others. They deserve careful study. But before their names became associated with armies, thousands of people had already built means of survival, exchange, and resistance under the conditions of colonial slavery. The revolution would emerge from those lives.

The first task is to understand the place the revolutionaries inherited. How had one island colony become so profitable? Why did French revolutionary language cause an especially explosive conflict there? And what kinds of relationships existed among people who were all subject to the same colonial government but occupied radically different places within it?

## The island before the plantations

Long before Europeans arrived, the island was home to Indigenous communities, including Taíno peoples with their own political networks, agriculture, trade, and religious lives. The name Haiti, derived from an Indigenous name for the island, would be reclaimed in 1804. When Christopher Columbus arrived in 1492, he entered a populated and politically organized world. Spanish conquest, enslavement, disease, displacement, and economic extraction devastated Indigenous societies. The later French plantation order was built on land whose earlier history had already been violently transformed.

Spanish settlers concentrated more heavily on the eastern part of the island. During the seventeenth century, French settlement expanded in the west. France's possession of the western territory received formal recognition from Spain under the Treaty of Ryswick in 1697. The resulting colonial boundary did not divide two different kinds of human being. It was the outcome of European imperial competition imposed upon an island with its own environmental and human histories.

By the eighteenth century, the French colony of Saint-Domingue was a major source of sugar and coffee. European consumers could enjoy sweetened drinks and coffeehouses far from the fields where those products were grown. The distance between consumption and production was part of the political economy. A fashionable habit in Europe became revenue for merchants and the French state; its labor costs were borne by people in the colony who had no legal freedom to refuse the work.

A plantation required more than plants. It demanded land appropriated for commercial cultivation, mills, boiling houses, storage, animals, roads, ships, credit, and tools. Above all it depended upon enslaved people, many forcibly transported from Africa. Sugar cane had to be cut and processed quickly after harvest. Boiling syrup near furnaces was exhausting and dangerous work. Coffee involved different seasonal routines but belonged to the same general system of compelled export labor. The sugar in a European cup represented decisions about land and bodies made across an ocean.

There is a temptation to describe Saint-Domingue's economy with a single spectacular figure: perhaps half a million enslaved inhabitants by the late 1780s, or a large fraction of Europe's tropical imports. Exact population and trade proportions vary by year and source. The essential relationship is secure. The enslaved population was overwhelmingly larger than the free white population, and export profits depended upon violence maintained by law and force.

## What an Atlantic invoice could conceal

In European commercial records, the colony's products appear with elegant clarity: quantities shipped, prices paid, debts owed, insurance arranged, profits anticipated. Merchants calculated whether a cargo would arrive safely and whether a season's prices would repay a loan. They also discussed the purchase of enslaved people as an investment in an estate's productive capacity. Accounts that seem precise in columns of figures can depend upon a profound refusal to consider what the numbers represent.

The financing of plantations stretched across many places. A proprietor might borrow through commercial houses; ships might carry provisions into the colony and sugar out; insurers might cover the loss of a vessel or cargo. Such arrangements connected rural estates to workshops, taverns, banks, dockworkers, and fashionable consumers in France. The plantation was a local site of violence and a node in an international financial system. The two realities should be studied together because their separation allowed some participants to regard themselves merely as suppliers or investors.

It would be inaccurate to imagine European people as one unified group enjoying the proceeds equally. Sailors faced dangerous voyages, urban workers endured poverty, and French society remained sharply unequal. But those inequalities did not dissolve the profound legal divide between a merchant who could negotiate wages and an enslaved worker who could be bought and sold. Understanding multiple forms of exploitation requires preserving the differences between them.

The owners' accounts also reveal instability. Hurricanes, crop diseases, wartime shipping losses, price shifts, and death among enslaved workers threatened investments. Planters sought replacement labor from the Atlantic slave trade, so the colony depended upon continued forced migration. Many enslaved inhabitants had therefore been born in Africa, and large numbers had recently experienced the violence of transportation. To study the revolt as if it were organized by a settled population living in unchanged local customs would be to miss this continuing movement of people and ideas.

Even a person who was not permitted to own property could possess knowledge that an investor lacked: when the cane was ready, how mills could fail, where rainwater collected, which paths connected neighboring estates, or where a companion had hidden food. The material expertise necessary to operate the plantation was concentrated among people whom the system regarded as subordinate. When a revolt came, that expertise could also be used to interrupt its operations.

## A household was not merely a labor unit

A colonial inventory might list people alongside property. Such records are indispensable evidence of slavery, but they tell us little by themselves about the relationships that kept enslaved families alive. People in Saint-Domingue formed kinship networks under conditions where relatives could be sold apart. They cultivated personal plots when allowed; they traded, prepared food, raised children, visited, worshipped, cared for the sick, and transmitted memories. Those acts were ordinary forms of human life made precarious by the institution surrounding them.

Many people arrived in the colony after surviving the Middle Passage. They carried languages, agricultural knowledge, ritual practices, and social experience from different African regions. A person born in the colony could have different expectations and connections from someone newly forced across the Atlantic. The colonial vocabulary distinguished people born locally from people born in Africa, but neither category describes a uniform identity. Alliances crossed lines of language, birthplace, occupation, and legal status; tensions also crossed them.

Some enslaved workers lived in fields and quarters far from urban administration. Others worked as domestic servants, artisans, cart drivers, boatmen, cooks, or skilled overseers. These differences affected daily opportunities and the information people could gather. A driver trusted to move between plantations could encounter people who rarely left one estate. A domestic worker might learn the anxieties of an owner's household. A person assigned to transport goods could observe roads, bridges, and military installations. The same economy that attempted to control movement also generated the routes by which news traveled.

These differences became politically consequential. A plantation uprising on a large scale required organization. It could not be produced by one famous speech to a perfectly uniform population. People needed to know whom they trusted, where others lived, how quickly they could move, and what weapons or provisions were available. The groundwork of revolt grew partly out of the ordinary geography of work and survival.

## A legal society divided many ways

The colony's population cannot be described accurately by a simple division between white masters and Black slaves, even though racial slavery was its organizing brutality. Some people of African descent were free. The legal category usually translated as free people of color encompassed men and women with different ancestries, occupations, and amounts of property. Some were artisans, soldiers, farmers, or merchants. Some were wealthy landholders, and some themselves owned enslaved people.

Within the white population, large planters, colonial administrators, merchants, and poorer whites did not have identical interests. A planter might resent commercial restrictions imposed from France while depending on French troops to enforce the labor system. A merchant might prefer reliable exports to the planter's political independence. A white worker with little property might still defend the legal privileges whiteness conferred.

Free people of color could possess wealth and education while being excluded from privileges reserved for whites. They could also be subject to racist regulations, humiliations, and political disabilities. Wealth, legal freedom, and equality therefore did not mean the same thing. A man could own land and still be denied full political participation. Another person could demand racial equality for free property owners while continuing to defend slavery. These contradictions would shape the first revolutionary years.

One figure who makes them visible is Vincent Ogé. He was a free man of color who traveled to France and participated in debates about rights. In 1790, after returning to Saint-Domingue, he joined an armed effort demanding political equality for eligible free people of color. The colonial authorities defeated it; Ogé was captured and executed in 1791. His case exposed the colony's refusal to treat property-owning free men of color as political equals. It was not the same uprising as the great revolt by enslaved workers later that year, nor did his campaign set out to abolish the plantation labor system.

Notice the difference between these demands. Political rights for free property holders could be pursued while slavery remained. Universal freedom from slavery required a much more radical change to ownership, authority, work, and the definition of a person in law. Different revolutionary actors would press those demands at different times. Some would move from one to another as the situation changed. Others would resist that change.

## The law and the threat behind it

French colonial slavery was shaped by regulations often associated with the Code Noir, issued under Louis XIV in 1685 and extended or adapted in colonial settings. The code combined provisions about religion, ownership, punishment, and manumission. Its formal language did not provide a complete picture of conditions on plantations. Colonial law both regulated and sustained the treatment of people as transferable property, while local practice could be still more violent than the written rule.

A plantation owner's control reached into the hour someone rose, the place they worked, and the conditions under which they ate or slept. Punishment could be inflicted for resisting or failing to meet demands. A legal system that classified a person as property enabled owners to treat disobedience as a threat to economic order. The structure made cruelty predictable without requiring every individual proprietor to behave identically.

This matters to the history of resistance. Enslaved people did not have to be taught that the labor system harmed them by reading the Declaration of the Rights of Man. They knew its consequences from lived experience. French revolutionary ideas would later provide a new language and political opening, but the desire to escape enslavement had a history of its own.

Some people fled plantations and formed communities beyond routine colonial control. The term maroon refers to people who escaped enslavement; it covers different experiences, from brief flight to long-lived communities. Some groups resisted armed patrols and maintained independent settlements in difficult terrain. Others entered shifting arrangements with colonial authorities. These communities complicated the fantasy of a colony in which owners knew and controlled everything.

François Mackandal, active decades before the great revolution, became a powerful figure in stories of resistance. He was an enslaved African who escaped, built a network, and was accused of organizing poisoning. French authorities executed him in 1758. Later accounts greatly amplified his powers and sometimes blurred documentary evidence with legend. What remains historically important is the fear he generated among slaveholders and the continued memory of organized Black resistance before 1791.

## Language, music, and the labor of belonging

People brought from different parts of Africa did not arrive sharing one language or cultural identity. They came from different polities and communities, with practices and memories that could resemble one another in some ways and diverge sharply in others. In a colony where French was an administrative language and many people were denied formal education, communication also developed through the language that became Haitian Creole. Its history reflects contact and the agency of people speaking, adapting, borrowing, and creating a way of life across imposed divisions.

A colonial officer might describe a group of Africans by a name taken from a trading port, a region, or a broad European classification. These labels are imperfect evidence of the people's own identities. The same person could be designated by place of purchase in one record and by cultural affiliation in another. When scholars investigate the influence of Kongo or other African traditions on the revolution, they must work with both these documentary limitations and the surviving cultural evidence. Serious historians disagree about the relative importance of different regions and networks.

The absence of a printed letter does not mean the absence of political thought. People debated, remembered, sang, performed, worshipped, and told stories. Knowledge moved orally, sometimes through people whose work allowed travel, sometimes through relationships between estates, and sometimes through religious gatherings. One person could not simply issue an order and expect a vast population to obey. Trust had to be developed and maintained.

We should also resist turning every act of ordinary sociability into a secret preparation for rebellion. A wedding, a funeral, a market visit, and a ritual gathering were important human events even when they did not lead to an uprising. They provided belonging to people whom the legal order attempted to reduce to laboring bodies. Their political significance partly came from the fact that the colony could not own the meanings its inhabitants created together.

## Religion and the problem of seeing other people's lives

The people of Saint-Domingue practiced forms of Catholic Christianity, African-derived religious traditions, and combinations that would develop into Haitian Vodou. The word Vodou names a living religious world, not simply a disguise for rebellion. Ritual gatherings could be occasions for healing, mourning, connection with ancestors and spirits, communal organization, and mutual aid. Religious life could also give groups the means to understand the justice of resisting domination.

European colonial accounts frequently described African-descended religious practice as superstition or criminal conspiracy. Such testimony may preserve evidence that gatherings occurred while misrepresenting what participants believed. Later nationalist retellings sometimes treated every ritual detail as a precise historical record. The challenge for historians is to recover the historical agency of worshippers without letting hostile observers or later legends supply fabricated dialogue.

There is also an intimate aspect of belief that a military timeline tends to miss. People separated from relatives, languages, and places of birth could sustain bonds through songs, stories, shared meals, and ritual. These practices helped a human community survive imposed dislocation. When organized insurgency came, it drew upon connections with purposes older and wider than a single campaign.

## Remembering Mackandal without mistaking memory for a transcript

François Mackandal's story became one of the colony's most memorable examples of defiance. Enslaved in Saint-Domingue, he escaped plantation control and developed relationships across communities. French authorities associated him with poisoning plots and regarded him as a major threat. They captured and executed him in 1758. Over time, his name came to carry meanings larger than any single legal proceeding: resistance, cunning, religious power, and the possibility that a person the colonial state had attempted to own could become its feared enemy.

The historical sources do not allow us to assign precise motives to every person linked to the alleged conspiracy. They include accounts produced by authorities trying to defend slavery, and later literary retellings written for different audiences. Some versions dwell on miraculous escapes or supernatural gifts. Those elements tell us something about the ways his story was remembered, but should not be narrated as ordinary verified events.

The deeper point is that 1791 had predecessors. Resistance had been organized at smaller scales for generations. Some enslaved people fled temporarily, negotiated for better circumstances, or sought manumission. Others escaped permanently or joined armed communities. Not all resistance sought the same immediate result, and none was risk-free. A colonial economy often described as prosperous was repeatedly forced to devote people and resources to preventing its laborers from leaving it.

For slaveholders, the fear of collective rebellion affected decisions about surveillance, punishment, and policing. For the enslaved, rumors of punishment could make organizing dangerous while also revealing how much their owners feared cooperation across estates. This tension between surveillance and clandestine communication is an important bridge from the pre-revolutionary world to the events of 1791. The revolt emerged from people who already understood both the reach and the vulnerabilities of colonial power.

## 1789 across the Atlantic

In France, 1789 brought revolution. The Estates-General met; the Bastille fell; the National Assembly adopted the Declaration of the Rights of Man and of the Citizen. The familiar language of liberty and equality circulated across the Atlantic through correspondence, ships, rumors, and printed documents. But the French political nation was arguing about the boundaries of its principles. Would rights apply to colonists? To free men of color? To enslaved people who were treated as property under colonial law?

These were practical questions about power. Plantation wealth supplied revenue and commercial influence to French interests. Colonists demanded rights against royal authority while many also defended their right to own people. Organizations representing free people of color contested exclusions. Abolitionists urged reform, often in an Atlantic political arena where commercial defenders of slavery had considerable influence.

Information traveled slowly enough for claims to contradict one another. A decree made in Paris could arrive after local officials had already acted under a different understanding. One group could announce that France had promised equality while another insisted it had not. War in Europe and rivalry among empires would further destabilize the colony. The revolution to come would be a sequence of decisions under imperfect and often frightening information.

The first revolutionary crisis in Saint-Domingue did not immediately unite all people resisting colonial hierarchy. White factions fought over local power and their relationship to France. Free people of color pressed claims that white assemblies denied. Meanwhile the enslaved majority saw how an old order could be shaken without their freedom being secured. The resulting conflict was not a simple copy of Parisian revolution. It had its own causes, organizers, and priorities.

## A new declaration and an old hierarchy

The Declaration of the Rights of Man was proclaimed in August 1789. Its universal language made a powerful impression, but universal language did not settle membership in the political community. French politicians debated whether a colony might claim political liberties from Paris while preserving laws that denied liberty to most people in the colony itself. Some plantation representatives were eager to protect local autonomy precisely because they feared metropolitan interference with their property claims.

For free people of color, the contradiction had a particular edge. A prosperous landholder who met the property requirements for political participation could be excluded because of ancestry. Vincent Ogé's demands arose from this clash between political ideals and colonial racial practice. His fate also shows the limits of reform from above. Colonial officials were prepared to use deadly force against even a restricted claim to equality. Yet the demand for equal rights among free landowners left unresolved the much larger question of human ownership.

For an enslaved worker hearing news of French liberty, the contradiction could be still more direct. A planter might celebrate freedom from royal interference while insisting that the field workers on his estate could not leave. This was more than hypocrisy in a speech. It was a conflict between competing ideas of property, political membership, and the authority to command another person's body.

The colony did not divide into two perfectly consistent ideological camps. Some white colonists supported elements of reform. Some free people of color owned enslaved workers. Some people who opposed racial discrimination did not call for general emancipation. Recognizing those differences enables us to understand why the revolution's first years produced multiple competing movements, each able to invoke liberty while disagreeing profoundly about what liberty required.

## Why a plantation economy was vulnerable

Think about the system's strengths from a planter's point of view. It had concentrated resources, a profitable overseas market, military support, and laws granting immense authority to a small group of property owners. Now turn each supposed strength around.

Large plantations concentrated people who understood how crops, animals, buildings, and equipment worked. Commerce connected the colony to volatile international politics and allowed rumors to travel. Military security depended on loyal forces that could be stretched thin. The system's profitability depended on continuing labor that most laborers had compelling reasons to resist. Slaveholders were materially powerful and numerically outnumbered.

These observations do not make revolution inevitable. Many slave societies persisted despite similar contradictions. Organizing a revolt required real risks, coordination, choices, and sacrifices. What those conditions did was make the political system dependent on a coercion that could fail when its authority was divided and the people it controlled acted together.

The geography also shaped possibilities. Northern Saint-Domingue contained densely populated plantation plains near the important port of Cap-Français, now Cap-Haïtien. Mountain routes could provide places of refuge and military opportunity. In the southern and western regions, landholding and racial politics followed their own patterns. The people who joined rebellions in different parts of the colony would not always have the same immediate aims or leaders.

This is one reason it is misleading to imagine that a single leader planned an entire revolution from the beginning. The process involved overlapping uprisings, battles, negotiations, betrayals, and changes of allegiance. A movement to end slavery would eventually become a movement for national independence, but these were not identical demands in 1791. Freedom, equality, and sovereignty had to be fought over as distinct political questions.

## Listen for the people behind the categories

Before leaving the pre-revolutionary colony, consider what our records make easy to see and what they obscure. We can often reconstruct the value of sugar exported, the number of ships sailing, the wealth of a plantation, the wording of a decree, or the career of an officer. It is harder to recover the life of a child born into slavery, the worries of a mother whose relatives might be sold, or the words spoken among field workers preparing to resist.

That difference is itself historical evidence about power. The owners and administrators of Saint-Domingue recorded property, taxes, laws, and threats to order because those were the things their institutions were designed to manage. People outside that official perspective did leave traces—in testimony, military records, religious memory, correspondence, and the actions their enemies were forced to report—but the archive is uneven.

A careful historian does not repair that imbalance by inventing the private conversations of people who left no written record. Instead, we ask what they did, what institutions constrained them, which stories other people told about them, and where the surviving evidence allows more than one interpretation. The result can still be a human history. It asks us to imagine ordinary experience responsibly, without pretending to know thoughts that cannot be recovered.

In 1791, those ordinary lives entered an extraordinary political struggle. People who had been expected to labor without freedom began coordinating attacks on the system that claimed to own them. The uprising would force French officials, colonial property owners, foreign governments, and formerly enslaved commanders to reconsider who could exercise political power. It would also force the insurgents to answer a question that was harder than any proclamation: how could people secure freedom while armies still fought over the land on which they lived?

The wealth of Saint-Domingue was made by people who were denied control over their own lives. The coming revolution began when many of those people acted to change that relationship. To understand their victory, and the conflicts that followed it, we must remember both sides of this opening account: the terrible effectiveness of the colonial economy, and the human relationships that its laws could never fully contain.

## Historical companion — not spoken

### Key distinctions

- **1492 and its aftermath:** Indigenous communities predated Spanish conquest; Indigenous displacement, coerced labor, and population collapse preceded the French colonial system.
- **1697:** Treaty of Ryswick formalized French possession in the west of Hispaniola.
- **1789:** French Revolution begins; citizenship claims diverge across colonial status groups.
- **1790–1791:** Vincent Ogé's movement pressed political rights for free men of color, not general abolition.
- **Late August 1791:** Mass uprising begins in northern Saint-Domingue.
- **Population figures:** Large late-eighteenth-century estimates are approximate and should not be confused with an exact census of one day.
- **Mackandal:** Execution in 1758 is historical; colorful accounts of supernatural influence and the precise extent of conspiracy are less secure.
