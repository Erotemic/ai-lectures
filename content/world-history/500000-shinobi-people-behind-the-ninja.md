# Shinobi: The People Behind the Ninja

In 1603, the Jesuit mission in Japan published a Japanese–Portuguese dictionary. Among its entries was a word transcribed as *xinobi*, or shinobi. Its definition was not a black-clad assassin, a martial artist who could disappear in smoke, or a member of a hereditary brotherhood hidden in the mountains. It described a spy who entered an enemy castle or camp secretly in wartime to obtain information.

Imagine opening that dictionary in the early seventeenth century. Japan had just passed through generations of civil warfare. Fortified houses, mountain passes, grain stores, and the loyalties of neighboring villages had determined the fortunes of great military families. In such a world, someone who could enter a guarded place, learn what was happening, and return with accurate news might be worth more than a fighter who could defeat several opponents with a sword. A lord who did not know where an enemy army was marching could lose a province. A commander who learned that a castle was short of food could avoid a costly assault. Intelligence was not an ornamental part of warfare. It could decide whether a battle had to be fought at all.

This lecture concerns the people who carried out that work, the societies that employed them, and the stories that transformed their memory. They are popularly called ninja. In much of the period we will examine, shinobi is the more useful historical term. But even shinobi did not always identify a distinct social class or a permanent occupation. It might identify what a person was doing: entering unseen, concealing an intention, discovering another person's plans, or disrupting an enemy's defenses.

The familiar ninja is a magnificent creation of literature and performance. That does not make the historical shinobi less interesting. It makes their history more complicated. We have to learn how to see an intelligence worker whose greatest professional success might leave no spectacular trace in an archive, while refusing to mistake a memorable invented adventure for evidence of an actual mission.

## The first traces are not an origin story

A good history of shinobi cannot begin with a secret founder who taught an unbroken lineage of students. Such origin stories exist, but they are not the same as a dated record of a clandestine operation. Espionage itself is ancient. Many governments and armies, in Japan and elsewhere, employed messengers, informants, scouts, and agents long before anyone used the name ninja. To say that the work existed is not to say that every ancient spy belonged to a single institution whose customs survived unchanged for centuries.

One early trace appears in the *Taiheiki*, a Japanese military narrative dealing with the wars between rival imperial courts in the fourteenth century. Its account includes a shinobi who enters the precincts of Iwashimizu Hachiman Shrine and sets fire to them to create confusion. The image is vivid: a sacred place becomes part of an armed struggle, and one concealed person can produce consequences that an entire formation might struggle to achieve.

But notice the kind of evidence. The *Taiheiki* is a war chronicle with a complex literary history, not an eyewitness report that transparently records every detail. It is evidence that this kind of covert action had entered the vocabulary and imagination of fourteenth-century warfare. It is not permission to supply the agent with a name, a training school, a particular costume, or a private conversation. It also does not establish that all such agents came from Iga, the province later most closely associated with ninja lore.

Other words circulated for irregular fighters and spies. Regional records mention terms such as *suppa*, *rappa*, *kusa*, and *kamari*. Their implications varied. Some could evoke thieves or rough men; others referred to concealment and observation. The labels do not line up neatly with a modern job description. The people in question might perform intelligence work one day, participate in raids another day, and return to farming, guarding, or ordinary military service afterward.

A title can turn intermittent work into the appearance of a permanent identity. Once the word ninja suggests a fixed caste, it is tempting to search for a uniform, a single code, and a central organization. The records point instead toward the particular jobs people performed and the people who employed them. Who had the motive to hire an informer? What did the employer need to learn? Who knew a dangerous route through the hills? And what happened when the work was over?

## The wars that made information valuable

In the late fifteenth and sixteenth centuries, Japan experienced the disorder usually called the Sengoku period, the age of warring states. Great houses fought over territory and authority. Provincial military rulers, known as *daimyo*, tried to expand their holdings or survive the ambitions of their neighbors. They depended on retainers, local landholders, temples, villages, merchant networks, and people whose loyalty might change when the balance of power changed.

A castle did not stand alone. It needed food, water, equipment, laborers, carriers, communications, and some expectation that nearby villages would not guide an opposing army to the gates. Armies moved along roads whose condition could be altered by weather. Their commanders depended on reports that might arrive late or might be deliberately false. A defending garrison had to judge whether an apparent retreat was a genuine withdrawal or a trap. A besieger had to judge whether an apparent abundance of provisions was real.

This is where the history of shinobi belongs. A successful reconnaissance mission could establish which gate was watched most carefully, whether a garrison had been reinforced, or whether a road was passable. An agent might talk to residents, enter a household under an ordinary pretext, observe the movement of supplies, or bring back news of an alliance. Other missions involved alarms, incendiary attacks, or the confusion created by a nighttime disturbance. None of this required supernatural powers. It required knowledge, access, patience, social judgment, and sometimes a willingness to take extreme risks.

The historian of warfare tends to inherit the records of the people who paid scribes. We know much more about a lord's decisions than about the person who crossed a boundary to gather the facts on which the decision depended. A successful agent had reason not to leave a signed description of the mission. His employer might also prefer not to record the work in detail. The surviving record is therefore uneven for reasons built into the occupation itself. Yet that is not a license to invent missing evidence. Secrecy explains why an archive may be thin; it cannot transform a legend into a documented event.

The relationship between shinobi and samurai was not the simple opposition sometimes seen in films. The neat popular contrast imagines honorable samurai fighting openly and dishonorable ninja handling everything covertly. That depends on an idealized and often later picture of samurai morality. In actual war, military commanders used ambushes, deception, raids, hostage-taking, negotiation, and intelligence as circumstances demanded. Shinobi work could be performed by men connected to the military class, by locally powerful landholders, or by people of less established status. An activity was not necessarily an identity, and a samurai household was not necessarily opposed to clandestine methods.

The most important question is usually not, 'Was he really a ninja?' It is, 'What does the source show him doing, and for whom?'

## Iga and Kōka: a landscape of families, passes, and alliances

To understand why Iga and Kōka became famous, place them on the map. Iga was a province in the mountainous interior of what is now Mie Prefecture. Kōka lay nearby, in the southern part of what is now Shiga Prefecture. Both were relatively close to Kyoto, the political and cultural center around which many late-medieval conflicts unfolded, but hills and mountain routes complicated an outsider's efforts to dominate them.

It is tempting to treat this geography as the setting for a single academy of secret warriors. The more interesting history concerns local power. Families held land, managed relationships with temples and neighboring communities, and could assemble for mutual defense. In these areas no one external lord enjoyed uncontested authority over every village. The local organizations were not miniature modern democracies. They contained hierarchies, quarrels, property interests, and armed men. But they could negotiate and resist in ways that made conquest difficult.

The skills of people who understood the terrain could also be sold or offered to powerful neighbors. To cross a pass without being seen, a traveler needed more than good legs. He needed to know which households could provide shelter, whose fields bordered the path, where water could be found, and which groups were hostile. Such knowledge was not confined to a small number of picturesque mountain specialists. It circulated through lived relationships. A local guide, a messenger, or a farmer with kin across a ridge might possess information a visiting commander lacked.

Over time the names Iga and Kōka became associated with particular expertise in intelligence and irregular warfare. That association had historical grounds. But it does not mean every person in these districts was a shinobi or that every covert operation elsewhere in Japan was directed from these two places. Other regional commanders recruited their own intelligence workers. The Hōjō, for example, are associated in war literature with groups called *rappa*. The vocabulary of clandestine work was wider than the later tourist map of ninja country.

We should be careful with the language of 'clans' and 'schools' as well. Kin groups and lines of instruction certainly mattered. Yet a modern story of a unified Iga ninja clan battling a unified Kōka ninja clan can obscure the fact that actual families formed shifting local alliances. Their loyalties were shaped by survival and political opportunity. The mountains did not isolate them from the wider world. Their proximity to contested regions made them part of it.

## Iga confronts the Oda armies

The conflict that makes these local relationships most visible came near the end of the sixteenth century. Oda Nobunaga was constructing a powerful military and political order. His campaigns brought many communities under the authority of larger rulers, often at the cost of the independence that local families had previously defended.

In 1579, Nobunaga's son Oda Nobukatsu launched an invasion of Iga. Local forces repelled it. Accounts emphasize the difficulties experienced by the invading army and the resistance of people who knew the country. The loss was not a magical triumph of a few masked masters over overwhelming numbers. It was a military defeat in terrain where local organization and knowledge mattered. Nobunaga rebuked his son for the unauthorized undertaking.

Two years later, in 1581, a far larger Oda campaign entered Iga. The balance changed. The invasion devastated settlements, temples, and fortifications and broke the capacity of local groups to maintain their earlier independence. The record is not a sports story in which one side's technical skill merely exceeds the other's. It is a story about the expansion of central military power and the destruction that could follow when local autonomy stood in its way.

Imagine the choices confronting a community that had defeated an invasion once. Confidence in the defenders' knowledge of paths and forts was not groundless: it had helped before. Yet the attacking coalition could return with resources and coordination on another scale. Every defensive advantage depended upon a surrounding human landscape. A pass could be watched; a fort could hold out; but if multiple approaches were attacked and nearby settlements were devastated, familiarity with the terrain might no longer compensate for the difference in force.

Later ninja stories often prefer the first campaign because it produces a satisfying legend of superior cunning. The second campaign belongs in the same account. It shows both why local specialists were valuable and the limits of those advantages. Iga's history was not the history of a secret state that survived untouched. It was folded into a changing political order, and people associated with its fighting traditions found new employers and new roles.

There is a larger lesson here about the relationship between secrecy and power. A state wants to control a road; the people who live beside that road know its alternatives. An army wants to seize a castle; those who maintain it know its daily routines. Local knowledge can frustrate conquest. But intelligence cannot replace every other resource of war. A commander still needs the means to act on what has been learned, and local advantage can be overwhelmed by a larger force.

## A dangerous crossing after 1582

In 1582, Nobunaga was killed at Honnōji in Kyoto after rebellion by a subordinate. The event transformed the struggle among Japan's leading military houses. Tokugawa Ieyasu, not yet the shogun who would later establish the Tokugawa government, was away from his own territory and needed to return to safety. Accounts of his journey associate his escape with a crossing through the region of Iga and Kōka.

This became one of the great adventures of ninja legend. In familiar versions, Hattori Hanzō appears as a master of the shadows who personally arranges a passage through hostile country, summons devoted ninja, and saves the future ruler through extraordinary skill. The actual historian's problem is more modest and more interesting. Ieyasu's dangerous return is historical. Connections between his household and people of Iga and Kōka are also significant. But the exact route remains debated, and strong contemporary evidence for every dramatic act attributed to Hanzō is lacking.

Hattori Hanzō Masanari was a real warrior and a retainer of Ieyasu. His reputation in sources is associated with military leadership and skill with the spear. He had family connections with Iga, and he was associated with Iga men in Tokugawa service. That does not establish him as the masked operative of later stories. Researcher Yamada Yūji has argued that Hanzō himself was probably not a shinobi in the popular sense, although he may have commanded or worked with people who performed such tasks.

Why does this distinction matter? Because the escape was not a feat that could be accomplished by one man's athleticism. A small party attempting to move through politically uncertain country had to depend on people who knew local loyalties and could assess danger. If a network helped, its achievement was organizational and social: which roads remained available, whom the travelers could trust, and how quickly arrangements could change when new reports arrived. Legendary retellings collapse a collective problem into an individual hero.

There is nothing wrong with enjoying that hero in fiction. There is a great deal wrong with treating the fictional details as proof of what happened in June 1582.

## A mission at the edge of a new peace

As warfare diminished under the Tokugawa rulers, the work available to descendants of shinobi families changed. An especially revealing episode occurred during the Shimabara Rebellion of 1637 and 1638. The rebellion in Kyushu had several causes, including crushing local burdens and the persecution of Christianity. Rebels fortified themselves at Hara Castle, and the shogunate assembled forces to suppress them.

A regional heritage account, based on Kōka historical materials, records that a small party of ten Kōka men was dispatched to obtain information within the castle. Their task was not to win a duel with the rebel leader. It was to learn what the defending force was doing. The account describes an attempt to interfere with food supplies and an encounter in which differences in local speech helped expose the intruders. We should read these particulars as a transmitted historical report, not reconstruct the scene with invented dialogue or assert that every detail is independently verified.

Even with that qualification, the case reveals what makes covert activity difficult. An agent may know how to cross a physical boundary and still fail a social test. In a village or fortress, the wrong accent, an unfamiliar expression, or the absence of an expected relationship can be as revealing as a bright uniform. A person carrying good intelligence may still be unable to get out. Stealth has a human geography as demanding as the geography of mountains and walls.

The rebellion also makes visible the political position of intelligence workers. These were not independent guardians of a timeless ninja creed. They were men whose services could be used by a government against a rebellion with complicated local causes. The information they obtained served the goals of those who employed them. Their skill did not make the politics of the mission morally simple.

## The long peace and the changing job

The Tokugawa shogunate governed Japan from the early seventeenth century until the nineteenth. Organized military conflict did not disappear altogether, but generations of large-scale civil war ended. Some men from Iga and Kōka entered the service of the shogunate or regional domains. Records describe Iga-mono and Kōka-mono in positions that could involve guarding gates, protecting residences, serving as firearms men, maintaining order, or performing occasional confidential assignments.

This can be disappointing to an audience expecting constant rooftop raids. The descendants of celebrated intelligence workers might spend ordinary days in guard duty. Yet an institutional history should explain why. A government that survives for decades needs reliable access control, observation, reporting, and protection of officials more often than it needs spectacular acts of wartime sabotage. A specialist's status may endure even when the tasks of his office change.

The Tokugawa world also developed covert inspection and reporting through several kinds of official service. The best-known later example is the *oniwaban*, a group associated with the shogun Tokugawa Yoshimune in the eighteenth century. They could be sent to investigate conditions in the domains and report sensitive matters. It is misleading to turn all such offices into proof of a single unbroken ninja agency. Intelligence was a function of government; different institutions could perform it for different reasons. Some had histories linked with Iga or Kōka service, while others developed within administrative structures of their own.

The quieter careers of the Iga-mono matter because they challenge the idea that historical importance requires an uninterrupted series of exciting missions. A household might be proud of its military ancestors while struggling to obtain a favorable appointment. A group might petition for better treatment by emphasizing the expertise of its forebears. The old language of dangerous service could become a claim to income, status, and remembrance.

In 1789, according to records discussed by the Japan Tourism Agency, Kōka men presented a copy of the great ninjutsu compilation *Bansenshūkai* to the shogunal authorities as part of a campaign to affirm their inherited skills and position. The episode is telling. Knowledge that once promised a tactical advantage was now being used to make an administrative and social claim: remember what our families know, and remember what that ought to mean for how we are treated.

## The manuals that survived the wars

Most modern people who want to know what ninjutsu meant turn to a small group of manuals. These books are invaluable, but we have to pay attention to when they were written. Two major surviving works belong to the late seventeenth century, after the civil wars that gave shinobi their reputation.

One is the *Bansenshūkai*, traditionally dated to 1676. The title evokes many streams gathering into a sea. It collects an extensive range of teachings: preparation, intelligence, routes of approach, deception, equipment, fire, natural observation, and the discipline expected of an agent. It cites the Chinese military classic *The Art of War*, whose treatment of foreknowledge helps explain the strategic importance the compiler assigns to secret information.

Another is the *Shōninki*, written by Natori Masazumi in 1681. It pays close attention to the problem of moving among people without attracting suspicion. Its advice on disguise is especially revealing. A disguise did not necessarily mean a clever mask. It could mean adopting the clothing and conduct of a merchant, an itinerant religious figure, an entertainer, or another person whose presence would have a plausible explanation. The point was to be accepted as ordinary.

One passage describes a strategy for gradually winning access to a household by presenting oneself as someone needing assistance, returning with thanks, and cultivating goodwill. Whether this specific device was widely used in the sixteenth century cannot be established from a manual written in 1681. But its inclusion tells us something important about the author's understanding of intelligence. Entering a household by making its occupants trust you could matter more than finding a hole in a roof. A voice, an attitude, and knowledge of etiquette could be operational tools.

The books also discuss weather, medicine, ways of carrying provisions, fire-making, techniques of concealment, and equipment for crossing obstacles. They contain religious and magical elements alongside practical observations. To dismiss them as pure fantasy would be careless; to treat every magical instruction as a verified battlefield technique would be equally careless. They represent attempts to preserve, organize, justify, and transmit knowledge in a world where the work for which the knowledge was valued had changed.

The manuscripts have histories of their own. Textual research on the *Bansenshūkai* distinguishes multiple manuscript lines and shows that the surviving versions cannot simply be treated as identical copies of one untouched original. Takamasa Fukushima's study, for example, reevaluates the conventional attribution of the compilation and traces its movement among Iga, Kōka, and shogunal circles. This is the sort of research that rarely enters a ninja film but matters greatly to history. Before asking what a manual proves about a sixteenth-century agent, one must ask which version survives, what a compiler could have known, and how the text reached later readers.

A manual can preserve a practice, prescribe an ideal, advertise a tradition, or invent an ancestry. It may do several of those things at once. Historical reading begins by distinguishing them.

## What ninjutsu meant in practice

We have been using the word *ninjutsu*. It names the arts or techniques associated with shinobi work. What belongs inside that category? If we begin with a duel between two masked fighters, we have already made combat the organizing principle. The manuals suggest a wider starting point: how can someone obtain information, act on it, and return without being discovered?

Consider an imagined scouting assignment. A commander wants to know whether a road is passable and whether an opposing force controls the crossing. The scout must first reach the place. That makes travel, provisions, and an explanation for being on the road part of the problem. Next comes observation: seeing armed men at a crossing is different from learning who commands them or how regularly they are relieved. Then comes the return. An accurate observation that never reaches the commander cannot inform a decision. This example is a way of unpacking the task, not a reconstruction of a particular recorded mission.

From that starting point, the variety in the books becomes easier to understand. Disguise helps someone approach. Knowledge of terrain and weather helps with movement and timing. Attention to conversation and conduct helps with access to people. Equipment can help with an obstacle or an escape. Physical endurance matters because observation, waiting, and returning all take effort. Fighting may become necessary, but the object of the assignment need not be a fight. Yamada Yūji describes ninjutsu as strategies and tactics closely connected with ordinary life, drawing on a wide range of knowledge and experience.

There is also an intellectual setting to this knowledge. The *Bansenshūkai* repeatedly invokes *The Art of War*. Its compiler presents intelligence as a military necessity and gives the agent's moral discipline a place in the account. These are teachings organized for a reader, with arguments about why the work deserves respect. That helps explain why a book of ninjutsu can move between practical devices, judgment, and ideals of conduct.

A modern school teaching something called ninjutsu is another historical object we might study. Its present curriculum, its account of its ancestry, and a seventeenth-century manuscript are three different sources. If a school claims an unbroken medieval lineage, the claim needs its own documentary evidence. The existence of an old manual does not, by itself, establish the transmission history of a modern organization. Conversely, a modern practice can be meaningful to its participants without every account of its antiquity being secure. We can ask what is taught today and how it developed while keeping those questions distinct from what a particular wartime shinobi did.

## The weapons and costumes we remember

Now return to the black-clad figure. The disguise that makes a ninja instantly recognizable to an audience would be a very poor disguise for someone who did not want to be recognized as a spy. The National Diet Library's exhibition on ninja history makes the point by contrasting the familiar costume with the range of ordinary clothes advised in the *Shōninki*. Dark colors could be practical, but a standard head-to-toe ninja uniform is not what these seventeenth-century instructions establish.

The theatrical explanation is more revealing. On stage, a performer may need to show the audience that a character was hidden, even while everyone can see the performer. Black garments and covered faces became a convention for making a ninja's secret identity instantly legible. Such conventions developed through Japanese theatre and illustrated literature. A device that communicates 'unseen' in a play is not thereby documentary proof that historical agents dressed alike.

The same distinction applies to the shuriken, especially the flat throwing stars now almost inseparable from ninja pictures. Throwing blades existed in Japanese martial traditions, and some forms were used by warriors. But researchers examining surviving ninjutsu manuals have not found evidence that star-shaped shuriken were the characteristic weapon of historical shinobi. That is not proof that no shinobi ever threw a blade. It is a warning against turning an entertaining emblem into the definition of an occupation.

Other objects fare differently under scrutiny. Manuals discuss climbing aids, ropes, fire implements, and *makibishi*, small sharp obstacles that could hinder pursuit. These are more firmly connected with the written tradition than the modern expectation of endless spinning metal stars. Even here, the presence of a device in a manual does not tell us how frequently agents carried it or how effectively it worked in a particular operation.

Ninja hand signs form another familiar image. Some have connections with esoteric Buddhist and mountain-ascetic practices. Religious ideas were part of the broader world in which shinobi lived, and a historical practitioner might draw on them. But films and comics often turn symbolic gestures into a visible system for commanding supernatural powers. The historical manuals offer a narrower and less uniform picture.

## Kunoichi: women, access, and a changing name

What about the *kunoichi*, the female ninja so often depicted as an elite counterpart to the male assassin? The word itself needs a history. Before its familiar modern use, *kunoichi* could refer to a woman. A common explanation treats the written character for woman as three shapes read as ku, no, and ichi. That wordplay does not establish the existence of a military office. The National Diet Library places the spread of the female-ninja meaning after the Second World War, especially in entertainment from the 1960s onward.

Women certainly could carry information, enter spaces closed to men, and participate in political and household networks. One manual discusses using women as part of an intelligence operation. More specifically, the *Bansenshūkai* includes *kunoichi-no-jutsu*: a technique involving the use of a woman. The passage concerns access to a household and the opportunities that access creates. In his account of the research, Yamada points to women gathering information while working in enemy households, including kitchens. That is a different setting from the rooftop pursuit familiar from films.

A household is a place where political and military affairs leave traces in ordinary activity. For an imagined example, additional meals might suggest visitors, but they would not by themselves identify those visitors or explain their business. Someone who hears a conversation has another sort of evidence. Someone who repeats a rumor has another again. Thinking through the difference helps us see why access mattered while avoiding the assumption that every person with access was an agent. These examples illustrate an intelligence problem; they are not additional incidents recorded in the manual.

The wording of the manual raises a further question: whose perspective are we hearing? A compiler can describe a woman as a means of gaining entry while telling us little about her choices, training, payment, or understanding of the operation. A technique described in a book is evidence that the compiler envisaged such a use. It is not a personnel register. We cannot turn that one passage into a roster of a permanent female corps, or assume that women performing intelligence work all followed the same career as men described as shinobi.

The sparse record also leaves questions open. Which women acted deliberately as informants? How did relationships within a household shape what they could learn? What risks did they bear? Answering those questions requires evidence about particular people and situations. Neither a confident story about a secret organization nor a claim that women had no part in intelligence can substitute for it. The useful distinction is between women's possible and documented roles, the terminology of a surviving text, and the female ninja developed by modern storytellers.


## When the hidden worker became a hero

For most of the Tokugawa period, ordinary audiences were not able to watch a genuine wartime shinobi operation. They could, however, read stories and watch plays. In these settings, secrecy created irresistible narrative possibilities. The audience could enjoy the sudden revelation that a harmless stranger was an infiltrator, a theft performed inside an apparently secure residence, or a character who transformed into an animal. On the page or on the stage, the practical skills of deception could expand into magic.

Early-modern stories sometimes portrayed these figures as sinister or treacherous adversaries to be punished by heroic warriors. They also supplied a roster of characters who would become difficult to separate from historical memory. Ishikawa Goemon, a thief associated with a celebrated execution in the late sixteenth century, became the subject of lavishly fictional adventures. Jiraiya, a magical hero of nineteenth-century illustrated tales, acquired powers involving toads; his fictional world included figures later recognizable to readers of modern manga. A cultural history of ninja has to study these inventions in their own right rather than dismissing them because they did not happen.

During the early twentieth century, new forms of popular fiction reshaped the ninja into a figure suitable for heroic adventure. Sarutobi Sasuke, promoted through the popular *Tatsukawa Bunko* stories, became part of an increasingly familiar tradition of clever, daring ninja heroes. The figure was mobile: a story could place him in a recognizable historical age while giving him the freedom of a modern adventure protagonist. Through fiction, school-age audiences encountered a ninja who belonged less to a record of military employment than to an expanding imaginative universe.

The process accelerated with twentieth-century novels, films, television, and then manga and games. 'Ninja' became the common label by which a worldwide audience recognized the character. The older word shinobi continued to matter in Japanese, but the modern shorthand made the type instantly marketable. Black garments, spectacular jumps, quick changes of identity, throwing stars, and magical signs were useful because audiences already knew what they meant. The character could cross borders more easily than any historically specific description of an Iga retainer.

This cultural history contains a trap. A fictional scene can be copied into a popular history, and a later author may cite the earlier popular history rather than checking the scene's origin. The result is a story that accumulates footnotes without accumulating evidence.

## The assassination that came from a movie

Consider one famous tale told about Ishikawa Goemon. In the story, he enters a room above Oda Nobunaga's sleeping chamber and lowers a thread through the ceiling. He uses it to guide drops of poison toward Nobunaga's mouth. At the last instant Nobunaga moves, and the attempt fails. It sounds exactly like the kind of scene a ninja thriller would invent. For decades, popular English-language books repeated versions of it as a historical incident.

In a study published in 2025, Robert Tuck tracked the story backward. He found no identified historical source for the attempted assassination. The method appears in the 1962 Japanese film *Shinobi no mono*, whose screenplay adapted a novel by Murayama Tomoyoshi. The memorable poison-thread device was not part of the source novel. Tuck connects it to earlier detective fiction, including a story by Edogawa Ranpo, and traces a possible further literary influence. His conclusion is not merely that a colorful story lacks proof. It is that a piece of twentieth-century fiction was read as a window onto the sixteenth century.

An invented film sequence enters a popular work of history. Later authors find the sequence in that book and repeat it, sometimes with reassuringly specific dates. Readers encounter multiple accounts that seem to confirm one another. Yet those accounts may all lead back to the same movie. The true historical subject has become the transmission of the story, not a poisoning attempt.

The trail from film to printed history to repeated legend gives historians another subject to investigate: how a fictional scene acquires authority through retelling. Asking 'Who first recorded this?' can expose the chain.

It also suggests a useful method for other celebrated ninja stories. When a tale is told with perfect suspense, a perfectly timed escape, a clever named device, and a hero whose thoughts are known in full, ask what source is actually doing the work. Is it a chronicle close to the event, a later manual, a play, a novel, or a modern retelling? None is worthless. But they answer different questions.

## What the shinobi leave us

We have arrived at two histories that are intertwined but should not be collapsed. One concerns the historical shinobi: people engaged in reconnaissance, infiltration, sabotage, covert communication, and sometimes ordinary military or guard service. They worked in a society of competing rulers and local alliances. The other concerns the ninja as a cultural figure: an inhabitant of theatre, illustrated fiction, film, manga, games, festivals, and global imagination.

The first history is harder to recover than the second. Its surviving witnesses include war chronicles, administrative records, local documents, and manuals copied after the great wars. Those records do not yield a roster of every agent or a complete technical manual for every century. They do, however, establish that clandestine work was important and that particular communities developed reputations for performing it. They reveal a human trade in uncertainty. Someone had to assess a road without being trapped on it, find out which promises could be trusted, and decide whether a reported threat was genuine.

The second history has its own depth. The fictional ninja offered storytellers ways to represent secrecy and skill. The figure could be a villain who violated social order, a local hero who outwitted the powerful, a master of disguise who questioned the appearance of ordinary life, or an adventurous child with powers no human being has ever possessed. That versatility helps explain why ninja stories have traveled so far beyond Japan.

Neither history is improved by pretending that the other does not exist. It is not necessary to claim that historical shinobi ran across rooftops in matching black uniforms to find their work compelling. Nor is it necessary to insist that fictional ninjas are failed documentaries. Theatre and manga are not military archives, and they do not have to be.

What stays with me is the 1603 dictionary entry. The person it describes is not invisible because of a magical talent. He is invisible, if he succeeds, because others have mistaken him for someone ordinary. His work depends on being overlooked. Historians face the opposite problem. They are trying to notice him, centuries later, in documents written for other purposes. The most responsible thing we can do is preserve the difference between what those documents reveal and what stories invite us to imagine.

The shinobi who mattered to a commander might have returned with a description of a gate, the name of a reliable guide, or news that a rival was preparing to march. The modern ninja may be able to vanish into a puff of smoke. The historical worker had a more difficult task: to learn something another person wanted to conceal, and to bring that knowledge back alive.

## Historical companion — not spoken

### A short, qualified chronology

- **Fourteenth century:** The *Taiheiki* represents shinobi performing covert arson; as a war chronicle, it must not be treated as a stenographic eyewitness report of every event.
- **Late fifteenth–sixteenth centuries:** Intelligence gathering, raiding, and local defense take place across Sengoku Japan. Iga and Kōka develop strong associations with covert military specialists.
- **1579:** Oda Nobukatsu's invasion of Iga fails.
- **1581:** A much larger Oda campaign subdues and devastates Iga.
- **1582:** Tokugawa Ieyasu's dangerous return through the Iga–Kōka region follows Nobunaga's death; the route and Hattori Hanzō's precise role remain contested.
- **1603:** A Jesuit Japanese–Portuguese dictionary defines *xinobi* in terms of clandestine military intelligence.
- **1637–1638:** Records and later interpretation associate Kōka intelligence workers with operations during the Shimabara Rebellion.
- **1676 / 1681:** The *Bansenshūkai* and *Shōninki* are compiled in Tokugawa-era contexts; they are not direct records of all practices in the Sengoku centuries.
- **Edo to twentieth century:** Illustrated literature, stage conventions, adventure fiction, cinema, and manga progressively create the internationally recognizable ninja.

### Distinctions essential to interpreting the sources

1. **Shinobi was not always an exclusive class:** It could refer to an assignment or activity undertaken by a warrior or other operative.
2. **A manual is not a battlefield observation:** Later written prescriptions reveal the compiler's knowledge and ideals, not necessarily how often a practice was used in the sixteenth century.
3. **Iga and Kōka were places and networks:** They were not necessarily two centralized, immutable schools or all-inclusive secret societies.
4. **Hattori Hanzō was historical; the familiar master-ninja figure is legendary:** Tokugawa service and Iga connections are secure; specific dramatic actions need their own evidence.
5. **Shuriken existed; their standard association with shinobi is not established:** Lack of documented use is narrower than proof of impossibility.
6. **Women could be involved in intelligence:** The Bansenshūkai passage and Yamada’s discussion of household access support a narrower account than a permanent female corps. The word’s older use for women and its postwar entertainment meaning must be distinguished.
7. **Ninjutsu is broader than combat:** Travel, observation, disguise, judgment, and return help explain the manuals’ range. The scouting and household examples above are explicitly imagined teaching examples, not historical incidents. Modern lineage claims require separate evidence.
7. **Cultural history is also history:** The black costume, heroic ninja, and Goemon poison-thread tale have identifiable histories as representations, even where they do not describe Sengoku events.

### Source criticism and suggested reading

The primary source objects are distinct from modern accounts of them. The *Taiheiki*, the 1603 Jesuit dictionary, the late-seventeenth-century manuals, and shogunal service records are not interchangeable kinds of testimony. The National Diet Library's online exhibition supplies manuscript context, reproductions, and an accessible explanation of the differences. Yamada Yūji's Mie University research and interviews provide a careful overview of the documented profession. Fukushima's manuscript study gives a direct example of how attribution and copying histories remain under active investigation. Robert Tuck's 2025 article is a case study in tracing fictional invention into purported historical fact. ### Questions for further study
