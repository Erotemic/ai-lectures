**Track:** Star Wars: A Galaxy Worth Arguing About. **Audience:** Film and Star Wars fans; technical background not assumed. **Prerequisites:** None. **Scope:** Music, sound, editing, design, performance, visual effects and changing production methods across the films and *The Mandalorian*. **Spoilers:** Major scenes from the original, prequel and sequel trilogies, including the outcomes of several lightsaber duels and the endings of *The Empire Strikes Back*, *Revenge of the Sith*, and *The Last Jedi*.

# Making Star Wars: Sound, Image, and Motion

Imagine turning off the screen during *Star Wars*. Keep only the sound. A low mechanical breath enters the room, and you know who is near. A blade seems to ignite with a dense electric hum, and you can almost see its color and shape. A series of electronic chirps rises and falls, and you know whether a little robot is frightened, indignant, or pleased with itself. Music announces the arrival of a scale too large for the room in which you are sitting.

Now imagine turning off the sound. A helmet moves through smoke. A tiny fighter passes the mouth of an enormous station. A dusty robe crosses a floor so bright and clean it seems designed to deny the existence of dust. A blue sword and a red sword appear in the same frame. Even without voices or music, the fictional world is often immediately legible.

These are not merely recognizable trademarks. They are ways of telling stories. Star Wars became a shared visual and musical language because filmmakers, designers, performers, editors, engineers, and composers made an immense variety of decisions seem to belong to one place. The result can be enjoyed as adventure, but it can also be examined as craft: how a sound gives a character an inner life, how an edit creates suspense, how a miniature acquires mass, how a duel expresses a relationship rather than merely an ability.

The easiest mistake when discussing this work is to describe it as a contest between old-fashioned practical effects and newer digital tools. A camera does not become emotionally persuasive just because its subject was physical. A computer image does not become meaningless because its subject was invented. The interesting question is what each method lets the filmmakers reveal, and whether the audience can feel the result.

## A melody can tell you who is changing

John Williams's music is part of the common language of popular cinema. In the original films it grants an enormous fantasy a sense of historical weight. The opening fanfare does not simply tell the audience that excitement is about to begin. It declares that the story deserves the scale of a legend, then sends that claim into a universe of dented equipment, frightened people, and risky improvisation.

It would have been possible to score the film with sounds suggesting a technological future. Instead, Williams uses a large symphonic orchestra and themes associated with characters, relationships, ideas, and institutions. The contrast is decisive. The images say that the machines are strange; the music insists that the feelings are old. We recognize aspiration, terror, loyalty, melancholy, and a kind of moral grandeur before we have learned the politics of the setting.

Take the familiar theme associated with the Force. Its appearances do not always mean that a supernatural event is being performed. It can mark memory, longing, or the possibility of a destiny not yet understood. When Luke looks toward the setting suns on Tatooine, the music expands a simple visual gesture into a desire for a different life. Later, when the same musical idea returns in radically changed circumstances, the audience may feel an echo before consciously identifying the relationship. A score can create a continuity of emotion that dialogue would make clumsy.

The Imperial March, introduced in *The Empire Strikes Back*, offers almost the opposite function. It organizes the sense of an institution around an unmistakable musical character. It can be threatening, triumphant, or ominously ceremonial. Its repeated association with Vader and Imperial power helps unify scenes that involve different locations and military actions. Yet it also creates the possibility of changed meaning. Once the audience knows the figure inside the armor, the music can carry the history of intimidation into a more personal setting.

Williams also knows how to make the absence of a familiar tune significant. A theme may arrive late, be withheld, or appear in a changed setting. The listener experiences the emotional relationship without needing a character to announce what is happening. That is part of the advantage of recurring musical material. It can preserve memory across a series of films even when the screenplay has moved to a new place.

Consider the very different music for the duel on Naboo in *The Phantom Menace*. The choral urgency of *Duel of the Fates* gives the fight a ritual dimension. The characters move quickly and acrobatically, but the music refuses to let the scene become only a demonstration of physical skill. It sounds as though something larger than these combatants is being decided. Whether the surrounding film fully earns that weight is open to discussion. The sequence nevertheless demonstrates how a score can frame action as tragedy before the tragedy is complete.

There is no need to reproduce any of the music to understand its function. Its artistry lies not only in memorable themes but in where those themes enter, where they disappear, and what they connect. The same image can suggest wonder, dread, or nostalgia depending on the musical relationship around it. Williams's achievement is as much dramatic arrangement as melodic invention.

## Music that the characters can hear

Not all Star Wars music comes from a narrator's invisible orchestra. Some originates inside the fictional world. The Mos Eisley cantina has a band. Jabba's palace has entertainment. Later films and shows place music in bars, ceremonies, and ordinary social life. This distinction between music heard by the audience alone and music heard by the characters is useful, because the two kinds of music build the galaxy differently.

The orchestral score gives distant places emotional meaning. The cantina band gives a particular room a social history. Its rhythms imply that somebody hired musicians, somebody wants to dance or listen, and somebody has gone to an establishment that may be a terrible place to make a business deal. The music continues around the argument and danger as though both are ordinary features of the evening. We are not just in a heroic myth; we are also in a bar that has regular customers.

The shift between those scales is part of the franchise's charm. A huge emotional score may accompany a character crossing a vast desert. The next scene may be dominated by an irritating mechanical noise, a local performance, or a conversation nobody is enjoying. High fantasy and everyday inconvenience coexist. The music helps the audience understand which aspect of the world is in focus at any given moment.

This is why a new Star Wars score can be faithful without imitating every orchestral gesture. A story about a small bounty hunter's assignment may benefit from a different instrumental palette than a dynastic space opera. A political thriller may choose tension, texture, and restraint where a heroic saga chooses declarative themes. The identity of Star Wars is strong enough to permit meaningful differences. What the sound needs to preserve is the ability to tell us something about the people, not a quota of recognizable musical quotations.

## Ben Burtt gives machines a voice

Ben Burtt's work on the original film established sounds that audiences now treat almost as natural laws of this invented universe. Yet many originated in ordinary physical noises recorded, transformed, layered, and performed for dramatic effect. The lightsaber's characteristic tone combines a mechanical hum derived from a film projector with electrical interference associated with a television. Movement and variation can then be produced by changing the relationship between sound source and microphone. A fantasy weapon is assembled from the audible behavior of real equipment.

The achievement is not merely a clever recipe. The sound has a particular texture: dense enough to suggest dangerous energy, sustained enough to make a weapon feel present even while nobody is swinging it. When two sabers collide, the acoustic change gives the impact a force that a bright line on a screen cannot provide by itself. A lightsaber without that sound might resemble an illuminated prop. With it, a moving blade seems to push against the air.

R2-D2 presented a different challenge. The character needed to convey intentions, moods, and reactions without human speech or an expressive face. Burtt has described experimenting with his own vocalizations and electronic processing to find a mixture of mechanical and emotional sounds. The result is a performance. Artoo can object, panic, celebrate, and make an unmistakably rude-sounding remark while remaining incomprehensible to the audience in the ordinary linguistic sense.

C-3PO helps complete the exchange. Anthony Daniels's performance is full of anxious precision, but his partner's noises are not merely punctuation after the human-language lines. Their timing creates arguments. We infer tone from Artoo partly because Threepio responds so vividly. A machine becomes a person through the rhythm of a conversation.

Chewbacca, too, depends on the construction of a vocal character. Animal recordings and manipulated sounds allow him to express frustration, pain, tenderness, and warning. Peter Mayhew's physical performance supplies posture and timing, while the sound gives the audience access to moods that the costume alone might conceal. The fact that Han appears to understand Chewie perfectly is one of the franchise's most productive comic devices. Their relationship teaches us to read a language we are never formally taught.

The Imperial machines have their own sound identities. An approaching TIE fighter is not only a shape; its screaming passage warns us of speed and threat. A walker needs audible weight to support the illusion that an effects model is an enormous vehicle. Vader's breathing turns a mask into a bodily condition. In each case, an invented object acquires a recognizable acoustic behavior. The audience soon expects that behavior, and filmmakers can use the expectation to create anticipation even before the object is visible.

Burtt was never the only person responsible for everything audible in Star Wars. The films have large sound editorial, recording, foley, mixing, and effects teams. The original film's Academy recognition includes the craft of a sound department as well as Burtt's special achievement. Subsequent films involved collaborators such as Randy Thom and many others. The cultural shorthand of one named sound designer should not erase the ensemble that records, edits, balances, and places sounds in a world.

## Silence is also an effect

The sound of an object can communicate its scale, but the arrangement of sounds can communicate danger. When a space battle is crowded with engines, laser fire, alarms, and communication, the audience receives information through layers. We need to know who is speaking, which threat is closest, which event has become important, and whether the characters can hear it too. The mix is an interpretation of the battle. It directs attention even when the image is filled with motion.

Consider Vader moving through a quiet interior. His breath can dominate because other sounds recede. A figure who does not need to raise his voice can seem to control the space by existing within it. A very different effect occurs when alarms, impacts, and shouted commands crowd a corridor. The same character may seem less like a specter and more like one participant in the collapse of a plan.

Star Wars has occasionally been criticized for making vacuum space sound like a loud battlefield. That observation is physically correct and dramatically beside the point. These movies generally do not pretend to deliver a laboratory simulation. They organize sensation so a viewer can understand speed, proximity, danger, and changing intention. Film sound is not simply a microphone placed near an event; it is a composition of what the audience needs to experience.

This choice can also be overdone. If every action is accompanied by a continuous roar, there is little room left for surprise. Silence, pauses, restrained room tone, and the sudden withholding of music can be more alarming than another explosion. A film's dramatic soundscape is strongest when the creators can distinguish between hearing much and understanding something.

## Editing is the art of deciding when

Film editing changes the audience's sense of time. A shot can last long enough for discomfort to develop. A cut can reveal information a character lacks. Two events filmed separately can be made to feel simultaneous. A face can answer a question before the screenplay supplies a line. Star Wars relies on these operations constantly.

The 1977 film's credits name Paul Hirsch, Marcia Lucas, and Richard Chew as its editors. Their shared Academy Award is part of the historical record. So is the fact that the film went through extensive editorial revision. It is not necessary to turn that revision into a legend in which a single editor saved the director from himself. It is more useful to watch what editing accomplishes in the finished picture.

The Death Star attack alternates among fighter cockpits, the station's operations, the rebel command center, targeting displays, and the approaching weapon's countdown. At first the audience has more information than any one pilot. We can see the strategic situation. As the attack continues, the cuts narrow our attention. A damaged ship becomes a person in danger. A voice drops off the radio. The target approaches. The station prepares to fire. The crosscutting keeps converting a huge military operation into a sequence of immediate choices.

The same technique has different moral consequences in *Return of the Jedi*. During the climax, the editing connects a space battle, a forest battle, and an intimate confrontation in the Emperor's chamber. A tactical success on the ground alters the prospects of the fleet. The fleet's danger intensifies Luke's anger. The Emperor exploits that anger. Editing is not simply accelerating three scenes to make them more exciting. It makes the meaning of each depend on the others.

A quieter example is found in *The Empire Strikes Back*. During Luke's confrontation with Vader, the cuts often preserve uncertainty. Vader appears, vanishes, and reappears in another area. A piece of space that seemed navigable becomes threatening. The audience can follow Luke, but cannot always anticipate Vader. The editing supports an imbalance of knowledge as well as power.

And sometimes an editor's most important work is to remain with a reaction. A triumphant rescue, a confession, or the sudden recognition of a family connection needs a human face on which the audience can register what has changed. The technology of Star Wars is most persuasive when there is enough time to watch someone respond to it.

Editing also joins performances. Han's confident claim acquires humor when it is followed by Chewbacca's reaction or a malfunctioning engine. Threepio's complaint becomes comic when Artoo answers with a sequence of indignant sounds. The performers may not have been recorded in the same session, and the final timing may be an editorial construction. On screen, it becomes a friendship.

## The silhouette does half the talking

Many Star Wars designs can be recognized without internal detail. Darth Vader is a dark vertical figure, severe and enclosed. An Imperial officer is angular and controlled. A stormtrooper's white armor has a face that is also a barrier. A rebel pilot's flight suit is comparatively soft, busy, and human. A Star Destroyer is a wedge of military geometry. The Falcon looks like a piece of machinery that has acquired its shape through use rather than ceremony.

Concept artist Ralph McQuarrie helped the early filmmakers visualize a galaxy that did not yet exist. The production-design departments, costume makers, prop builders, model makers, and performers then turned images into environments the camera could inhabit. The designs needed to work at more than one scale. An actor must be able to move in a costume. A miniature must retain its identity when photographed against a black background. A prop must survive being handled repeatedly. An object meant to appear for a few seconds has to be comprehensible before the shot changes.

The most important detail may be contrast. The original trilogy uses weathered and repaired rebel technology against an Empire that presents itself through repeating units and controlled surfaces. The design gives the viewer a moral intuition before providing historical explanation. When Luke sees the Falcon, he sees a dubious pile of metal. When we see a Star Destroyer, we see an apparatus built by someone able to impose uniformity on a vast scale.

Design can also invite criticism. A fictional culture represented almost entirely by masked or exotic background figures may feel visually rich while receiving little human depth. A villain's intimidating armor can become merchandise more easily than the political conditions that made his actions possible. Recognizable shapes are powerful, but recognition is not the same as interpretation. The films are strongest when costumes and sets imply lives rather than functioning only as logos.

Consider the difference between the first film's Death Star and Cloud City in *Empire*. Both contain smooth, ordered interiors. Yet the Death Star seems designed to enforce an institution's will, while Cloud City's luminous spaces advertise comfort and cultivated prosperity. By the time Vader appears there, the visual elegance has become a trap. The setting has not changed, but its meaning has. Good production design gives the story something it can transform.

## Models move, and the audience believes

Industrial Light & Magic was founded to solve a production problem that existing facilities could not readily handle at the required scale. The original film's space combat needed miniatures to travel convincingly through apparent three-dimensional space. The camera had to repeat carefully controlled movements so multiple elements could be combined. Motion-control systems and optical compositing helped turn physically small objects into ships whose apparent size, speed, and relative position could be followed across shots.

This work is easy to describe as technology and harder to describe as perception. A small model does not automatically look like a huge ship. Lighting, camera movement, lens choice, surface detail, and the pace of motion influence what scale the audience reads. Too quick a movement can make a model look like a toy; an ill-chosen highlight can expose a painted surface as something smaller and closer than the story requires. The visual effects crew had to make the behavior of models agree with the imagined behavior of enormous vehicles.

The results are not uniformly invisible by later standards, nor do they need to be. Some images reveal optical softness or the limitations of the available methods. Yet the scenes retain power because the choreography is legible. We understand which craft pursues another, which fighter enters the trench, and what a maneuver costs. The images are not an end in themselves. They make the structure of the action clear.

The original trilogy also employed matte paintings, mechanical creatures, puppets, miniature environments, and elaborate physical sets. Yoda's puppet in *Empire* is a particularly useful case. His emotional credibility arises from Frank Oz's performance, the figure's movement, lighting, eyelines, and other actors responding to him. A technically impressive model is not necessarily a character. A comparatively constrained puppet can be one if the camera and performers treat it as a participant in the scene.

That insight survives every change in effects technology. The audience does not only ask whether the pixels are convincing. It asks what an object seems to want, what it can do, and how the people around it react. The language of performance is never entirely separate from the language of special effects.

## The digital revolution is not a simple fall from grace

The prequel films expanded the toolbox dramatically. *The Phantom Menace* combined elaborate physical environments and miniatures with extensive computer-generated imagery. *Attack of the Clones* pushed digital acquisition and effects workflows much further; the film was a landmark for major-feature digital cinematography. Computer-generated characters, including Yoda in the second prequel, could now perform actions that a puppet would have made extremely difficult. Massive armies and impossible cities could be designed with a degree of freedom unavailable to the earliest trilogy.

It is tempting to decide that one technique is inherently more authentic. The actual production histories resist that division. Even in the prequels, miniature photography remained important. Industrial Light & Magic artists chose among models, animation, digital compositing, physical sets, and other methods based on their strengths and the demands of the schedule. A computer-rendered landscape might be built from photographic observation; a photographed miniature might be digitally revised until little of the original shot remained untouched. The categories blur as soon as one looks closely.

What can be criticized is the resulting image and its dramatic relation to the performers. A digitally expansive environment may offer extraordinary views of a planet while giving the actors too few tangible objects with which to interact. A battle containing thousands of animated soldiers can become a moving pattern if no individual perspective anchors it. Conversely, the freedom to control an impossible camera can make a chase or duel exhilarating. The relevant distinction is not analog good, digital bad. It is between effects that help us understand and feel a scene, and effects that merely expand the quantity of what we can see.

The prequels are full of both possibilities. The podrace has a strong physical grammar: engines approach, machines overtake one another, obstacles are immediate, and the sounds give competitors different identities. The enormous battle on Geonosis communicates the scale of a new war but sometimes keeps its suffering at a distance. Coruscant's skyscrapers and traffic establish a bureaucratic civilization even as some scenes struggle to make that civilization feel inhabited. A good critique should distinguish those achievements from the disappointments instead of issuing a blanket judgment.

The later sequel films combine digital techniques with locations, built sets, creatures, practical effects, and a renewed interest in the tactile feel of the original trilogy. That mixture can produce images of remarkable immediate presence. It can also become a style of quotation, where familiar textures function as reassurance even if the story itself needs to move somewhere new. Craft can serve a creative discovery or reproduce an already beloved memory. The technology does not decide which.

## The new screen around the old set

*The Mandalorian* presents another change in method. Industrial Light & Magic's StageCraft process uses large LED surfaces displaying digital environments, coordinated with a tracked camera, so that actors and physical set pieces can be photographed within an apparent landscape. The virtual scenery can produce light and reflections on costumes during filming. It is a different relationship between live action and image construction from a traditional approach in which actors perform before a blank green screen and most of the surrounding world is added later.

The technique required collaborative development among visual effects artists, engineers, cinematographers, and filmmakers. It is also not magic. The illusion depends on decisions about camera movement, distance, lighting, image fidelity, and where physical construction ends. The team may use a real floor and doorway with a digital continuation, or build a comparatively small physical area that appears to open onto an enormous environment. When the approach works, performers have visual information available during the scene and the camera captures the environmental light as part of the photographed event.

There are tradeoffs. A screen has limits of resolution, geometry, and usable angle. The method may be more effective for some scenes than others. It is not a universal replacement for location work, physical sets, or post-production effects. Its value lies in bringing some of the world into the moment of performance. The production can make decisions about an imagined space while the actors are actually occupying part of it.

There is a pleasing continuity with the original film's ambitions. Lucas wanted a galaxy beyond the limitations of an ordinary studio. The first ILM artists built new systems to move miniature spacecraft convincingly across a screen. Decades later, artists build systems that let a camera move through scenery that is simultaneously physical and computed. The techniques differ radically. The dramatic desire is similar: make the invented world sufficiently present that a human performer can inhabit it.

## The camera must believe in the actor

Technical innovation often receives attention because it produces a memorable object or environment. The performances inside those environments deserve equal care. A costume can constrain movement, a puppet can change the timing of a scene, and a digitally created character can make another actor's eyeline a question of choreography rather than ordinary conversation. In every case, the filmmakers have to create the conditions in which emotional exchange appears possible.

The original Yoda provides an especially clear example. A puppet is an object operated by people whose work the audience cannot see. Yet Luke reacts to Yoda as a teacher with intentions, moods, and the capacity to disappoint him. The camera allows the creature time to think and hesitate; Mark Hamill treats him as someone whose judgment can wound. The performance is distributed across Frank Oz, the puppet, the staging, the editing, and Hamill's attention. We do not need to forget that it is a puppet for the scene to move us.

A digital creature requires a different arrangement of labor but faces the same dramatic task. A performance made from animation, voice, reference acting, and digital lighting still has to persuade us that it can affect the person standing opposite it. When a scene fails, we may blame the pixels, when the deeper problem is that the exchange has no rhythm or the characters have nothing interesting to do with one another. When it succeeds, we stop allocating separate credit to the tangible and simulated pieces for the length of the scene.

This is why practical and digital methods can work so powerfully in combination. The physical surface gives a performer something to touch, the imagined extension gives a place scale, sound supplies the object's weight, and editing chooses when the relationship becomes clear. A good effects shot can be an intimate shot. The technology disappears not because it is invisible in a literal sense, but because the audience has something more urgent to attend to.

## A lightsaber duel is a conversation

The most revealing way to compare Star Wars filmmaking across eras may be to watch the lightsaber fights. They involve special effects, choreography, performance, sound, music, and editing, but their success depends on something simpler. What do the combatants want from one another?

The duel between Obi-Wan and Vader in the first film is restrained. Two old adversaries circle and test each other. The choreography may look limited beside later acrobatics, but the story is not about which man can complete the more elaborate movement. Obi-Wan is protecting the escape. Vader is confronting a figure from his past. The decisive act is Obi-Wan's decision to stop resisting in the expected way. The scene is about the acceptance of death under chosen terms.

On Bespin, Vader's duel with Luke feels like a predatory demonstration. Vader has the advantage and can afford to wait. Luke is emotionally exposed, physically overmatched, and increasingly desperate. The environment changes around them from chamber to corridor to an exposed ledge, as though the space itself were withdrawing his options. The final strike and revelation are dramatic blows. A technically clean exchange of sword moves would not have achieved the same effect.

The duel between Obi-Wan, Qui-Gon, and Darth Maul on Naboo has an entirely different physical vocabulary. The movements are fast, patterned, and athletic. The combatants seem to belong to a Jedi culture that treated these weapons as the basis of specialized training. The spectacular choreography and choral score give the fight the sense of a ritual competition with mortal stakes. But there is a dramatic question inside the spectacle: what happens when Obi-Wan is separated from the teacher whose judgment steadied him? The barriers and divided space are not merely obstacles for stunt performers; they isolate a student at a decisive moment.

The confrontation between Anakin and Obi-Wan on Mustafar in *Revenge of the Sith* pushes physical excess much further. The environment is violently unstable, the movements prolonged, and the swordsmen intimately familiar with each other's abilities. The duel can be criticized for moments when elaborate motion seems to overwhelm emotional clarity. Yet its core is devastating: a teacher and a former student know each other so well that they can no longer find a way not to fight. The technical virtuosity serves a relationship that the political disaster has already broken.

Later duels can strip the exchange back down. In *The Force Awakens*, the snowbound confrontation includes wounded, angry, physically struggling combatants whose skills are uneven. The fight feels messy rather than ceremonial. In *The Last Jedi*, the throne-room encounter and Luke's confrontation on Crait alter the meaning of fighting altogether. A spectacle can be a trap; a refusal to supply the expected physical duel can be its own act of mastery.

These examples resist a ranking by how quickly the blades move. Fast fights can be emotionally precise. Slow fights can be inert. A duel communicates through the fighters' aims, the camera's distance, the duration of contact, the music's response, and the moments when someone hesitates. The blade is not the content. It is the instrument through which a relationship becomes visible.

## The part of filmmaking that stays human

There is a sense in which every major Star Wars innovation returns to an old problem. The filmmakers have an imaginary thing they need an audience to feel. A robot with no face must act like a person. A model must feel immense. A score must tell us something not contained in the dialogue. A duel must reveal what one character has become to another. A digital environment must seem like a place where bodies could live.

None of those goals is accomplished solely by increasing technical capacity. More polygons cannot supply an interesting motive. A louder sound cannot replace a consequential silence. A larger set cannot create an emotional relationship if the staging refuses to attend to the people inside it. A recognizable musical theme cannot carry a scene whose dramatic choices are empty. Technology can make possible what could not previously be shown, but it does not settle what is worth showing.

The strongest Star Wars filmmaking lets immense machinery and fragile human experience occupy the same frame. A helmet is a piece of sculpted material until performance, light, and sound turn it into an enclosing life. A two-meter model is a toy-sized object until photography, movement, and editing make us believe it can eclipse a planet. A hand moving toward a weapon is ordinary until the scene gives us enough history to know why touching it would change a family.

This is also why crediting collaborators is not an act of distributing compliments after the main story. Collaboration is the story of how the work exists. Lucas's ambition depended on a community capable of translating rough intentions into precise sounds, images, physical objects, and timings. Later writers and directors have inherited not just a fictional universe but a dense professional craft tradition. Some use it to develop new artistic languages; others use it mainly to recreate reassuring effects. The difference is visible in the finished scenes.

When we remember Star Wars, we tend to remember things that were never actually present: a ship the size of a city, a sword made of energy, a place at the edge of an impossible desert, a monster breathing inside his own prison. Film crews made lenses point at models, recorded motors and animal sounds, shaped plastic and fabric, cut strips of footage, wrote musical themes, tracked cameras through digital space. From those real acts came experiences that feel like remembered events.

The fictional galaxy has changed enormously in fifty years of filmmaking. Its methods will change again. What remains worth pursuing is not the permanent reproduction of any one technique, but the moment when sound, image, movement, and a person's decision become inseparable. That is when Star Wars stops looking like a demonstration of things that can be made and becomes an adventure that seems, for a little while, to have happened.
