# 006000 — Rebellion from Below

**Track:** Star Wars: A Galaxy Worth Arguing About. **Audience:** General listeners and Star Wars fans. **Prerequisites:** None. **Scope:** Selected arcs from *Star Wars: The Clone Wars* (2008–2020), *Star Wars Rebels*, both seasons of *Andor* (2022, 2025), and *Rogue One* (2016). **Spoilers:** The fates of Fives and the clones during Order 66; the Siege of Mandalore; major *Rebels* losses; both seasons of *Andor*, including the Ghorman massacre and Mon Mothma's departure from the Senate; the complete ending of *Rogue One*.

# Rebellion from Below

At the beginning of the first *Star Wars* film, some rebel spies have already stolen the plans to a weapon capable of destroying a planet. The audience learns this through words moving up the screen. When the action starts, the plans are being carried away by a desperate ship, and the people who stole them are absent. We meet the consequences of their work before we meet the workers.

That gap is an invitation. A story about heroes can begin after somebody else has completed the hardest, least glamorous part of the mission. Someone has stolen codes, passed messages, built an alliance, taken a chance on an unreliable contact, and died in a corridor. A princess now has the plans; a farm boy will eventually use them; the work that brought the plans into their hands has been pushed into the history behind the image.

Decades later, *Rogue One* turned that history into a film. *Andor* moved farther back to show people who do not yet think of themselves as a coherent rebellion. *Rebels* showed a tiny band of companions making trouble on one occupied planet. *The Clone Wars* gave individuality to soldiers who appeared in the prequel films as identical ranks of armor. These works differ enormously in tone and audience. What joins them is a change of scale. The hero is no longer only the person who takes the decisive shot. The hero may be the person who learns where to aim, keeps the engine running, makes a dangerous introduction, or sends a message they will not live to hear answered.

The result is not a rejection of the mythic *Star Wars* we already know. It makes the myth harder to take for granted. A rebellion is a word for many people whose motives, methods, and sacrifices do not always align. The larger story becomes more convincing when we can see how difficult it is to make those people act together.

## The army that has no choice

The 2008 computer-animated *The Clone Wars* series begins inside a contradiction inherited from the prequel films. The Republic claims to defend free institutions, yet its principal army consists of men engineered to fight. They are called clones, produced in enormous numbers, and typically introduced as members of numbered military formations. They have faces identical to one another, but the series gives them names, habits, loyalties, rivalries, and lives.

That attention changes how we see the war. A battle in a feature film may present a formation of soldiers as a moving mass whose advance changes the strategic map. A television series can spend time with one of those soldiers before the attack and return to him afterward, or show the empty place he leaves behind. Dee Bradley Baker voices the clone soldiers with enough distinction that their shared genetic template ceases to define their personalities. Rex, Fives, Echo, Cody, and their comrades are not simply interchangeable units carrying the Republic's ideals on their backs.

The clones are often admirable. They cooperate under impossible conditions, improvise, rescue each other, and take pride in their competence. They are also expected to obey a hierarchy that sees their labor as a resource. The series does not have to resolve this tension by declaring the entire army either heroic or evil. It can show courage inside a system that was never designed for those soldiers' freedom.

The Umbara arc makes the contradiction almost unbearable. Anakin leaves a campaign, and Jedi General Pong Krell takes command of the 501st Legion. Krell treats the soldiers with contempt, orders wasteful frontal assaults, and demands obedience even when the tactical cost is obvious to the people who will pay it. Captain Rex tries to balance discipline with responsibility toward his men. Fives and other troopers resist orders that seem designed to get them killed.

At first, this might be a familiar military story about a callous commander. Then Krell arranges for clone units to attack one another while believing they face enemy troops. The horror arrives when the soldiers discover that the bodies before them are their own comrades. The scene does not need a lecture on the ethics of command. The soldiers' identities have been erased by the system that issues their instructions; only after the shooting do the faces and names return.

Krell is also a villain in his own right, not merely a man obeying bad military rules. Yet his betrayal is effective because the institution supplies him with the authority to carry it out. Rex must decide whether loyalty to a chain of command can remain a virtue when the command itself is murderous. The clones who refuse to execute Fives and Jesse after their supposed insubordination make a similar choice. Their resistance is small compared with the war. It is enormous compared with the expectation that they are only equipment.

One of the series' strongest achievements is to make a soldier's refusal feel like an act of independent moral judgment. The clone is not valuable merely when he fulfills the assignment for which someone made him. He is valuable before any assignment is given. His capacity to think, care, and object is evidence of a personhood the Republic should have recognized from the beginning.

## Fives and the investigation nobody wants

The story of ARC trooper Fives turns this contradiction into a conspiracy. A clone named Tup abruptly kills a Jedi commander. The incident appears to be an inexplicable breakdown, and the official response is to examine the soldier as a defective unit. Fives refuses to leave the question there. He follows medical evidence to Kamino, where he discovers the inhibitor chips implanted in the clones and gradually approaches their true purpose.

The tragedy is procedural as much as military. Fives knows something that could alter the future of the war. He has to persuade institutions that are invested in treating the problem as isolated and technical. Medical experts have incentives to conceal the design of their products. High officials have incentives to avoid a crisis in the army. The person with urgent knowledge has almost no authority to make anyone act on it.

Fives is not flawless. He becomes frightened, agitated, and increasingly desperate. His attempts to expose the conspiracy make him easier to dismiss as unstable. That is part of the cruelty. He has reason to be afraid, but fear undermines his credibility with people who could help him. The story does not need the viewer to believe every character is foolish. It shows a system in which the truth can be located below the level at which an official decision is made.

When Fives dies, the audience knows the warning has failed. We also know what the soldiers do not: that the chips will be used to command them to murder the Jedi. The later Order 66 montage gains a new layer of grief. The clones' betrayal is not simply an abrupt revelation that brave men were secretly disloyal. It is a theft of their capacity to refuse. That distinction does not erase the victims or the violence, but it changes the nature of the injustice. Both the Jedi and the soldiers have been made instruments of a political design concealed from them.

In the final *Clone Wars* arc, Ahsoka and Rex are aboard a Republic vessel when the command arrives. Rex struggles against it, and Ahsoka works to remove the chip that has forced him to turn on her. Their survival is not a triumph that saves everybody. The ship crashes, soldiers die, and the two survivors must look at a catastrophe they cannot undo. Ahsoka's response is marked by an effort to preserve lives even while she is being hunted by former comrades.

The emotional power of the finale is the gap between the Republic's imagined moral certainty and the physical remains of the people who served it. Helmets sit over graves. A weapon from the war remains in the snow. The episode lets an audience feel what the political collapse means to people who were not at the center of the Senate's speeches or the duel in the Emperor's office.

## The small ship that becomes a home

*Star Wars Rebels*, which began in 2014, moves into Imperial rule. Its central image is not a fleet but a small freighter, the Ghost, crewed by people whose strengths rarely match neatly. Hera Syndulla pilots and organizes. Kanan Jarrus has hidden his Jedi identity. Sabine Wren brings art, explosives, and a complicated Mandalorian history. Zeb Orrelios carries the wounds of an atrocity committed against his people. Ezra Bridger is a streetwise orphan who has learned to survive under occupation. Chopper, the ship's droid, is loyal, argumentative, and sometimes alarmingly eager to cause trouble.

The group is a family because its members repeatedly decide to become one, not because all of them are easy to live with. Their vehicle contains the practical details of intimacy: rooms, routines, arguments over space, the inconvenience of depending on others. The Ghost is a home as well as a weapon. That difference gives their resistance an emotional meaning beyond the number of Imperial facilities they disrupt.

Early operations are local. The crew steals supplies, breaks people out, broadcasts messages, and tries to keep Lothal from becoming an entirely obedient Imperial possession. A small operation may barely register on the Empire's strategic map. To the people receiving food or escaping detention, it is the difference between abandonment and evidence that somebody cares. The series understands that a resistance movement can begin through repeated acts that do not yet have the grandeur of a single decisive battle.

As the story expands, the crew encounters other rebel cells, hidden leaders, former soldiers, and competing ideas about risk. A spontaneous rescue does not require the same coordination as a large attack. Someone has to share intelligence, establish trust, transport equipment, plan an exit, and decide how much danger an operation may impose on civilians. Hera gradually takes on greater responsibilities. Her leadership becomes less about pulling off a clever mission and more about preserving enough people and resources to fight again.

This is not merely adult bureaucracy inserted into a children's adventure. It gives the adventure consequences. The people on the Ghost are skilled, but they cannot free a galaxy by themselves. To participate in a larger rebellion they must accept limits on their independence while remaining capable of objecting when a plan is wrong. The series' found-family warmth coexists with the hard fact that resistance requires organization.

Kanan's death becomes especially painful in this context. He saves Hera and his companions from an explosion, and the crew loses someone who has been teacher, partner, and family. The episode does not claim that a noble sacrifice makes grief disappear. The survivors have to keep living with an absence that no later successful mission can erase. Ezra's eventual choices also involve stepping away from the hope that he can personally save everyone. He has learned that being needed is not the same as being entitled to determine every outcome.

The liberation of Lothal is therefore not just a display of special powers. It rests on local relationships, trust, timing, and a population whose lives continue after the famous crew's story changes direction. At its best, *Rebels* joins the comfort of returning to a familiar home with the unsettling knowledge that no home can be protected by courage alone.

## The same war, seen from another genre

The shift from animated adventure to *Andor* is visually dramatic. The 2022 first season presents workshops, rented rooms, corporate offices, prison corridors, aging machinery, and ordinary people trying to earn money or stay out of trouble. Its 2025 second season follows the movement toward *Rogue One*. The Jedi and the Force barely occupy the narrative foreground. The Empire is still recognizable from the films, but its reach is measured less often by a black-armored villain entering a room than by an official who can change someone's life with a form, a fabricated charge, or a surveillance report.

The change of register does not invalidate the animated shows. *Rebels* can make a family's loyalty visible through an adventure with flamboyant action. *Andor* asks what those loyalties look like when a victory requires years of clandestine work and when the person receiving the consequences may not know a rebellion exists. The shows illuminate different parts of the same fictional society. Their methods need not match in order for their arguments to speak to one another.

Cassian Andor begins as a man with little reason to believe in institutions or organized causes. He has known displacement, violence, police pressure, and the need to conceal things about his origins. He is capable and resourceful, but his skills are largely devoted to getting himself through the next problem. The show's opening troubles result from his personal search and from encounters with security officers who abuse their authority. The response to the incident grows into a much larger intrusion on Ferrix, the industrial community where Cassian has a family and friends.

Ferrix is among *Andor*'s most persuasive inventions. It is a place with its own economy, salvage work, mechanical expertise, funeral customs, and neighborhood obligations. People signal warnings by striking metal; they know whose door has been forced and who needs assistance. The community has an existence apart from Cassian's adventures, which means the Empire can harm it even when Cassian is absent. Its people are not waiting in a corridor to become extras in his rise to heroism.

Maarva Andor, Cassian's adoptive mother, is part of that social world. She has survived enough to recognize the cost of submission, and she is impatient with the small accommodations that allow an occupying force to become normal. Her final recorded address to the community does not make Ferrix's people powerful in the military sense. It gives language to an anger and solidarity they already possess. Their uprising is the culmination of daily relationships rather than the invention of a single magnetic leader.

The Empire cannot understand this fully because it measures the town primarily as a security problem. It arrests, interrogates, watches, and tries to discover the central person whose removal will make everyone else obedient. But what keeps Ferrix together is distributed. The Empire can identify a suspect and still misunderstand the practical ties through which people protect each other. That failure is as important as any failed gunfight.

## Money, intelligence, and moral debt

Luthen Rael is the opposite of a mythic rebel hero introduced through clear moral symbols. He operates behind an elegant public identity and maintains clandestine contacts who do not necessarily know one another. He conceals information, manipulates allies, and accepts losses that would be intolerable if he were speaking only about people he knew personally. His calculations may produce successes while leaving lives damaged beyond repair.

The Aldhani heist reveals the appeal and danger of such an approach. A small team takes enormous risks to seize Imperial funds. The operation is thrilling as an adventure in planning, disguise, and timing. But it is not solely about escaping with money. The stolen wealth can sustain organizing. The Empire's retaliation can also expose its nature, pushing frightened people toward resistance. Luthen is willing to rely on repression as a catalyst. The logic may be strategically plausible, and it is ethically difficult. He is prepared for other people to suffer as a consequence of the response he provokes.

Cassian encounters members of the team with different reasons to fight. One young idealist believes that the rebellion needs an intellectual account of why the Empire is wrong. Another has seen personal devastation. Others are motivated by money, guilt, or loyalty. The mission succeeds only because these motives are temporarily coordinated. The differences do not evaporate. Some people die, some betray, and some survive with knowledge that will disturb them later.

Mon Mothma occupies a different position. She has political visibility, wealth, and the ability to move through rooms closed to Cassian. Those advantages make her useful, but they also surround her with surveillance and obligations. Funding resistance through hidden accounts requires deception. Her public speeches must operate within a Senate in which the room for meaningful opposition is shrinking. The more useful she becomes to the movement, the more threatened her private relationships become.

The show does not make her sacrifices uncomplicated. The arrangements surrounding her family's social standing and her daughter's marriage expose the degree to which public duty and private life have become entangled. A viewer can admire Mon's courage while objecting to the circumstances in which her family pays for political decisions it did not wholly make. The rebellion's ethical difficulty is not confined to people who carry guns.

Luthen and Mon also demonstrate that a resistance movement may contain people who disagree about what they are building. She retains a commitment to public legitimacy and the possibility of better institutions. He sees a present in which almost every usable path is compromised. The series gives each argument consequences without insisting that their methods are interchangeable. A successful clandestine operation can help make freedom possible while cultivating habits that will be dangerous if carried into government afterward.

## The prison that keeps working

The Narkina 5 prison arc turns the Empire into a factory. Cassian is arrested on an ordinary pretext unrelated to his clandestine activity and sentenced to hard labor. He finds himself in a facility where prisoners assemble components under relentless quotas. The floors, the shifts, the ranking tables, and the system of punishment form a process designed to consume human bodies while concealing the purpose of the work.

The prisoners are organized into teams, and each team has an incentive to make its members work faster. The system converts fear into cooperation with the institution harming them. Kino Loy, the floor manager played by Andy Serkis, initially believes that following the rules is the best available method of surviving to release. His authority is real within the prison, but it has been delegated to him by people who do not regard him as free. He can direct his fellow prisoners, yet he cannot change the fundamental conditions of their confinement.

The revelation that prisoners are not being released destroys the bargain. A man who completes his sentence is reassigned within the system, and the threat of that knowledge spreading prompts further cruelty. Cassian has suspected that the system will never operate fairly, but suspicion by itself does not free anyone. He needs other prisoners to act, and Kino must accept that the order he has helped maintain is a trap.

The escape is an extraordinary sequence because it uses the mechanics of the prison against its designers. The prisoners know shift routines, vulnerable controls, and where guards rely on automatic compliance. The same division of labor that made them efficient workers makes coordinated resistance possible. Kino's announcement over the public address system is not a magical speech that causes freedom. It gives thousands of people, already moving toward a decision, one intelligible direction at the moment when their cooperation can matter.

Then the series refuses a clean reward. Kino reaches the edge of the escape route and reveals that he cannot swim. There is no convenient solution offered in the moment. The prisoners have acted together and broken the system's immediate control, but the victory does not guarantee that everyone who helped make it will reach safety. Kino's fate is left out of view. The uncertainty is painful because he is no longer merely the authoritative floor manager; he has become a particular person whose sacrifice we understand.

The arc also explains something about Cassian's development. He does not become a rebel because someone finally delivers an inspiring definition of liberty. He has worked inside a machine of oppression, discovered its dependency on the labor of its victims, and participated in a collective escape. He knows that resistance can require trust among people who were strangers the day before. He also knows that victory can leave people behind.

## When an atrocity becomes an official story

The second season of *Andor* expands the problem from local repression to a public event the Empire expects to control. On Ghorman, officials plan to exploit resources and displace a population while constructing a narrative in which the population appears responsible for the violence directed at it. Surveillance, provocation, a manipulated protest environment, and a prepared security response are parts of one design.

The Ghorman massacre is not merely a large battle in which the villains kill civilians. The preparations are the essential horror. People hold meetings, argue about tactics, disagree over who can be trusted, and try to protect a community with its own history and pride. The Empire studies those very habits to find a way to provoke resistance and justify crushing it. A cruel government does not always need to prevent people from speaking. Sometimes it needs them to speak in a setting that has been arranged to make the speech appear criminal.

Syril Karn and Dedra Meero give the machinery human faces. Syril longs for status and a place in a competent order. Dedra is capable, disciplined, and committed to a brutal institution. Their relationship does not make them separate from the political system; it reveals how personal ambition, intimacy, and career advancement can be absorbed into its work. Syril's realization of how he has been used comes far too late to repair the harm.

Mon Mothma's response to Ghorman transforms her public position. She uses her place in the Senate to condemn what happened and then must escape the consequences of that speech. The danger is not that everyone in the chamber is ignorant. The Empire's power depends partly on making truthful public judgment ineffective and risky. Mon's words become meaningful because she accepts that they may end her ability to operate openly within the institutions she has tried to preserve.

The production makes the crowd at Ghorman legible as people rather than a decorative mass. We see attempts to organize, confusion during the assault, and personal calculations made in seconds. The violence is followed by the struggle over the account of the violence. In that sense, Ghorman links the show's political argument to a problem familiar far beyond science fiction: power does not only determine what happens; it also tries to determine what the public is permitted to believe happened.

The rebels cannot simply answer this with a more efficient gun. They need evidence, witnesses, channels of communication, public courage, and the willingness to lose secure positions. They also need to decide which kinds of clandestine conduct they will accept. As their movement grows, those questions become more pressing, not less. The price of victory is not paid once, at a climactic battle. It accumulates in choices made when no cheering audience is present.

## The argument inside the rebellion

Saw Gerrera's presence across *The Clone Wars*, *Rebels*, *Andor*, and *Rogue One* offers a thread connecting decades of fictional history. He begins as an insurgent fighting for Onderon and becomes a militant whose methods and distrust separate him from other rebel leaders. His devotion to resisting domination is not in doubt. His judgment about what resistance authorizes is far harder to accept.

Saw is useful to a movement because he is willing to act where others hesitate. He is dangerous to it for the same reason. When people learn to treat secrecy and violence as permanent habits, a war may consume the political future it claims to defend. Other rebels cannot always afford to reject his help, but accepting his every method would also change what their alliance is becoming.

*Andor* does not make these dilemmas into a courtroom exercise in which every act receives a final philosophical grade. It shows the argument happening amid deadlines, fear, betrayals, and incomplete knowledge. Luthen keeps secrets from allies because he believes their ignorance protects a larger operation. Mon conceals political funding because open opposition has become nearly impossible. Cassian takes assignments that would horrify someone who sees only the public face of rebellion. The characters may be correct about immediate dangers and still incur moral debts they cannot later settle.

This is one of the important ways the surrounding stories deepen the original trilogy. When we first meet the Rebel Alliance in the films, it already has military discipline, recognizable commanders, communications systems, pilots, and a shared objective. We are inclined to treat its coherence as a natural fact of the setting. The newer stories ask how such coherence gets built from people with incompatible motives. They reveal that solidarity is not the absence of disagreement. It is a fragile achievement in the presence of disagreement.

The political stakes extend beyond defeating one ruler. A rebellion that hopes to restore a republic will eventually have to govern people who disagree with it. Military success cannot answer all the questions of legitimacy, rights, trust, and restraint. The Empire's inability to tolerate dissent explains much of its brutality. A movement defined only by hatred of that Empire may not yet know how to avoid reproducing the same habit. The series lets the problem remain open.

## Scarif and the invisible work behind a legend

*Rogue One*, released in 2016 and directed by Gareth Edwards, approaches the famous opening crawl of the 1977 film from the side of people who will not become household names inside the galaxy. Jyn Erso has a deeply personal connection to the Death Star project because of her father, Galen. Cassian is an experienced intelligence officer who has already made painful compromises. Bodhi Rook is a defecting Imperial pilot carrying information he can barely afford to possess. Chirrut Îmwe and Baze Malbus bring faith, loyalty, and formidable skill without occupying the familiar position of Jedi knights. K-2SO supplies practical help and a dry sense of humor, while reminding us that a machine can be a companion rather than a prop.

The film gathers these people around a mission the Rebel Alliance itself hesitates to authorize. That reluctance is understandable. Leaders must consider whether an apparent opportunity is reliable and how much of their movement can be lost on a desperate assault. The characters who go anyway are not all motivated by identical hopes. Some are trying to redeem old mistakes, some to save a threatened future, and some because the immediate injustice has become impossible to live beside.

On Scarif, the theft of the Death Star plans is a sequence of interlocking tasks. Someone must get inside an archive. Someone must find the file. Someone must keep a communication channel open. Someone must protect the transmission equipment. The battle outside is spectacular, but the objective is a piece of information moving through a system under fire. The enormous machinery of war is being contested by people performing specialized work in the hope that someone else can use the result.

The film's final sacrifices are devastating precisely because there is no last-minute extraction for the central team. Jyn and Cassian transmit the plans and cannot escape the Death Star's strike. Others die opening routes, buying time, disabling obstacles, and holding positions. Their deaths are not interchangeable. Each task has a person attached, with a face and a history. The plans reach the next ship, then Leia, then the droids, and eventually the pilot who takes the decisive shot in the original film.

The ending also restores a sense of dread to Darth Vader. When he enters the corridor to seize the plans, he is not a father who may yet choose redemption. He is an almost unstoppable military terror, advancing toward people who can only pass the information out of his reach. The famous image of the armored villain and the humble physical work of handing on a data card now belong to the same moment. The rebellion survives not because the people in that corridor can defeat him, but because they have something to pass along.

A compressed description of the operation could say that rebel spies stole the plans. That statement is accurate and almost empty. *Rogue One* gives it weight by making us witness the people who completed the mission, the details that nearly prevented success, and the fact that the eventual victory will be enjoyed by people the spies never meet.

## What hope costs, and what it does not excuse

There is a danger in these darker stories. An audience may begin treating moral compromise as automatically mature, as though the more brutal or secretive a character becomes, the more realistic the franchise has grown. *Andor* does not require that conclusion. Its harshness is not a prize awarded to cynicism. It makes the cost of oppression and resistance visible so that the value of something better can be taken seriously.

A rebel who kills a prisoner to protect an operation is not necessarily admirable because the operation succeeds. A leader who manipulates public anger may be shrewd and still owe the public an explanation. A commander who sacrifices subordinates may be right about a battle and wrong about the responsibilities that accompany command. The stories are strongest when they let these statements coexist, rather than washing every act clean in the light of a later victory.

Nor should hope become an empty demand that suffering people endure cheerfully. The people of Ferrix have a culture worth preserving before a rebellion gives them a banner. The clones are persons before they become heroes of the Republic. Kino deserves freedom whether or not his escape helps defeat the Emperor. The inhabitants of Ghorman deserve safety before their suffering galvanizes the Senate. Resistance is not what gives their lives value. It is one possible response to a political order that refuses to recognize value already present.

The animated stories sometimes articulate this through friendship and home. *Rebels* returns to a small ship where people can argue and share a meal. *The Clone Wars* gives the soldiers names and relationships that transcend their military purpose. *Andor* dwells on the workbenches and funeral customs of Ferrix, the claustrophobic rhythms of Narkina 5, and the compromises of a senator who cannot freely say what she believes. *Rogue One* makes an entire doomed operation into a relay among people who trust one another only enough to complete the next necessary task.

Their different forms create a larger picture than any one could provide. A family adventure can preserve the feeling that solidarity is joyful. A political thriller can demonstrate how solidarity must survive surveillance and disagreement. A war film can show the final price when a particular mission succeeds. Neither innocence nor grimness is a sufficient description of rebellion by itself.

## The plans arrive

Now return to the opening of the 1977 film. A ship runs from a much larger one. Soldiers defend a hallway. Princess Leia has information that must get away from the battle. Two droids become the unlikely carriers. The film moves brilliantly from the scale of a galactic conflict to the practical confusion of small beings entrusted with a piece of the future.

After *The Clone Wars*, *Rebels*, *Andor*, and *Rogue One*, the same beginning acquires more history. We can imagine the families that lost people long before a Rebel fleet was assembled; the soldiers who learned too late that their obedience had been stolen; the factory workers, witnesses, donors, mechanics, pilots, and spies whose efforts could be undone by one intercepted transmission. The opening crawl has not become wrong or naïve. It has become densely inhabited.

The most powerful image in *Rogue One* may be the last act of passing the plans forward. The person who hands them on does not need to be famous, certain of victory, or present at the celebration. They need to get the information to the next pair of hands. Political change often looks much less like a final duel than that sequence of fragile transfers. Someone knows something. Someone else agrees to help. A person takes a risk because another person has taken one already. There is no guarantee that the chain will hold.

A hero's legend can compress all of this into a single shot down a narrow trench. That shot is still magnificent. What these other stories add is not a reason to admire it less. They give us a reason to understand how many people were there with Luke Skywalker, even though the cockpit had room for only one pilot.
