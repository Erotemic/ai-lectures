# The Original Trilogy: Rebels, Family, and Redemption

A boy destroys a battle station. Three films later, the same boy throws away his weapon rather than kill his father. These are both victories, but they are not the same kind of victory. The first is a successful attack on a machine. The second is a refusal to become part of one.

The original *Star Wars* trilogy begins as a rescue adventure with an extraordinarily effective villain and ends by asking whether the villain is still capable of making a human decision. Between those points, it changes its idea of courage. In the first film, the hero is brave because he can take the shot. In the last, he is brave because he can decide not to. What makes that change convincing is not a sudden philosophical revelation. It is three films of relationships, betrayals, failed instructions, injured loyalties, and people repeatedly returning for one another.

There is also a war. Entire planets can be destroyed, an authoritarian state can terrorize its subjects, and the rebels are fighting for something more substantial than a young man's family history. The best passages in the trilogy keep these two scales in conversation. Personal love is capable of changing a person; it cannot by itself design a just government. Military victory is necessary to resist an empire; it cannot dictate what kind of person a victor becomes. The finale is built around both problems, even though it resolves one more completely than the other.

## Three films that change the assignment

It is easy to remember the original trilogy as one perfectly prearranged story. Watch the films in order, however, and you can feel how the narrative develops. The 1977 film, directed and written by George Lucas, is lively, comic, and mostly self-contained. The rebel base is saved. Luke has found friends and purpose. The threat of the Empire remains, but the adventure has delivered an ending.

*The Empire Strikes Back*, released in 1980 and directed by Irvin Kershner from a screenplay credited to Leigh Brackett and Lawrence Kasdan, rearranges those terms. Victory does not lead to a settled position. The rebels are discovered. The heroes separate. Their judgments become less reliable. A mentor's instructions can conflict with a friend's need. A romantic declaration occurs when there is no reassuring way to respond. And Darth Vader, previously a terrifying agent of a larger regime, begins to dominate the emotional architecture of the story.

*Return of the Jedi*, released in 1983 and directed by Richard Marquand, with a screenplay by Lawrence Kasdan and Lucas, is more divided in tone and ambition. It wants to restore the pleasure of the adventure, complete the war against the Emperor, settle an intimate family tragedy, and give several beloved supporting characters satisfying moments. At times those goals pull in different directions. The movie can be playful to the point of distraction and frightening to the point of despair, sometimes within a few minutes. But its climax finds a surprisingly stark answer to the questions the previous film raised.

An audience watching in 1977 did not yet know that Vader was Luke's father. An audience watching *Empire* in 1980 did not yet know that Leia would be revealed as Luke's sister. It is better to treat the films as evolving works than to flatten them into a destiny that was fully available at the start. Their revisions, additions, and changes of emphasis are part of the creative history. A story can acquire depth as its makers discover what to do with it.

The trilogy therefore has three different rhythms: an adventure that gathers a company, an ordeal that breaks the company apart, and a conclusion that reunites its members while testing what those reunions mean. It is not a neat three-step diagram. There are detours, tonal changes, and awkward solutions. But the emotional movement is clear enough to survive them.

## Hoth, or the indignity of surviving your victory

The opening of *The Empire Strikes Back* makes an important correction to the ending of the first film. The rebels destroyed the Death Star. They did not destroy the Empire. Their reward is to be driven into hiding on an ice planet, occupying a base from which they may have to flee at any moment. A military victory has bought them some time, not a durable new order.

Luke is almost killed before the principal Imperial assault even begins. He is attacked by a creature in the snow, dragged into a cave, and forced to escape with resources he barely controls. Han finds him in the storm and keeps him alive. The scene establishes a reversal of the first film's final battle: Luke survived because the Force and Han's help came together; now he survives because he still needs both other people and a kind of perception that he does not entirely command.

The Hoth battle then expands the scale of the war. Imperial walkers cross the white landscape like mobile buildings. The rebels have aircraft and courage, but they cannot simply trade firepower with the enemy. They improvise, slowing the walkers with cables, buying minutes to evacuate. The sequence is spectacular, yet its real objective is retreat. Every small success is measured by the number of people who can escape, not by enemy territory captured.

It is good for the trilogy that its heroes must occasionally run. A rebellion consisting only of triumphant attacks would risk becoming an adventure club with a political logo. On Hoth, the machinery of the Empire is visibly too big to defeat in a fair contest, and the rebels' strength lies in keeping their people and purpose intact.

The film also changes how we watch its protagonists. Luke leaves the group to pursue his training. Han and Leia escape together on the Falcon with Chewbacca and C-3PO. The story will cross between them, but they cannot help each other by being present. They must learn who they are in different kinds of danger. The original film's group dynamic becomes an experiment in separation.

## Leia is not a prize for the hero

Leia's role is more complicated than the title princess ever suggested. In the original film she is a political courier and an armed member of the rebellion. In *Empire* she appears as someone with command responsibilities, able to speak to troops and help direct an evacuation. She is not simply travelling alongside Luke and Han while they become heroes. The war is her work.

Carrie Fisher gives Leia a sharpness that keeps the character from being turned entirely into an emblem. Leia is impatient with boastfulness, suspicious of evasions, and capable of genuine fear without becoming passive. She can be cruel in a moment of anger, especially to Han, and the narrative does not always demand that she apologize for being the most practically competent person in the room.

The romantic exchanges between Leia and Han are remembered for their insults and timing. But the comedy of their courtship conceals a real difference in their habits. Leia has devoted her life to a cause that requires the self to be subordinated to something larger. Han has survived partly by refusing permanent commitments. Their arguments are more interesting when understood as an encounter between these ways of living. She distrusts his selfishness; he suspects her public duties leave no room for a private life. Each uses sarcasm to avoid making a vulnerable claim.

That tension reaches a peak on Bespin. Leia finally tells Han she loves him as he is about to be frozen in carbonite. His answer is famous because he does not suddenly become a polished romantic hero. The moment preserves his personality while allowing the audience to see the feeling he has been protecting. The line has theatrical confidence, but the circumstances remain horrifying. Han may never be returned to her. The scene works because the humor is subordinate to the loss, not a substitute for it.

Leia's later revelation as Luke's sister has a different effect. It answers a puzzle about family but compresses her independent story into Luke's developing mythology. She has a separate political history, courage, loss, and competence; she does not need a hidden blood relationship to justify them. In *Return of the Jedi*, the sibling revelation can feel more like a solution to a screenplay problem than the natural culmination of Leia's own arc. That criticism does not invalidate the emotional tenderness when Luke shares the truth with her. It asks us to notice how the trilogy sometimes gives its male hero a growing symbolic world while asking its principal woman to fit into it.

The final film nevertheless allows Leia a crucial place in the war. On Endor she goes into the field, works with local allies, and helps turn a mission that has gone wrong into a chance for success. The rebellion is not an abstract dream behind the Skywalker family. Leia is one of the people who make its practical survival possible.

## Han Solo keeps coming back

Han's first instinct is to leave. He has debts, a battered ship, and a co-pilot who has repeatedly helped him survive by concentrating on what works. He can recognize a suicidal venture, or at least he claims to. In the first film he returns to help Luke at the decisive moment. In *Empire* he stays long enough to become a central member of the rebellion's life, even while maintaining the vocabulary of someone about to take off.

Harrison Ford's performance keeps Han's charm from becoming untouchable. He is entertaining because he is vain and sometimes mistaken. The Falcon's engines fail. His apparent mastery of a situation often gives way to desperate improvisation. He has a knack for finding an escape route that requires another escape route immediately afterward. Chewbacca's exasperation and C-3PO's alarm puncture his self-image as surely as Leia does.

The asteroid-field pursuit shows him at his best. There is a wildly unsafe decision, exceptional piloting, and considerable confidence that he can survive things other people consider impossible. But the same episode also shows the cost of that style. The group cannot spend the whole war living by the fraction of a second separating a brilliant maneuver from disaster.

When Han seeks refuge at Cloud City, the story introduces Lando Calrissian, played by Billy Dee Williams. Lando is not merely another charming gambler. He is an administrator. He runs a community, has employees and dependents, and must consider what an Imperial occupation would do to them. Vader's pressure turns his apparent betrayal into a case of political coercion. Lando makes a bad bargain under threat, then discovers that bargaining with an authoritarian power offers little security.

His eventual resistance matters because it does not retroactively make the original choice harmless. Han is captured. Leia is terrified. Chewbacca is furious. Trust has been damaged. Lando's later contribution to the rebellion is a form of repair, not the cancellation of his previous actions. The trilogy is richer for including someone whose obligations cannot be reduced to friendship alone.

By *Return of the Jedi*, Han himself has changed. He is freed from Jabba's captivity, reconnects with the people who risked their lives for him, and volunteers to lead the ground mission on Endor. This is not a perfect transformation from rogue to exemplary officer. He remains impulsive and jealous, sometimes adolescent. But his commitments no longer need to be denied every time they are visible. The man who wanted the reward now has people for whom he will take an unrewarded risk.

The rescue at Jabba's palace is one of the film's more cumbersome passages. It is colorful, full of strange creatures, and gives the heroes theatrical entrances, but the elaborate plan can be difficult to reconstruct as a coherent operation. It also gives Leia a striking act of self-liberation when she kills Jabba, even after the film has displayed her as a captive spectacle. Those contradictions are revealing: *Jedi* simultaneously wants a pulpy adventure's thrills and a story in which its heroes have grown beyond some of those conventions.

## The droids are witnesses, not decorations

The trilogy has another pair of characters whose experience cuts across the grand divisions of the war: R2-D2 and C-3PO. They are sometimes treated as comic equipment, but their presence allows the films to show the central drama from a less heroic angle. Artoo is often the one who preserves the information, opens the door, repairs the ship, or delivers the message. Threepio supplies panic, protocol, and the voice of someone who thinks the adventure is conducted by people with an alarming disregard for procedure.

The opening of the first film famously trusts the droids to carry the audience into an unknown galaxy. Later, this perspective remains valuable. On Hoth, Threepio notices practical dangers while commanders make strategic decisions. On Dagobah, Artoo watches a world of mysterious instruction without being invited to share Luke's growing spiritual identity. On Endor, Threepio becomes the focus of the Ewoks' misinterpretation of a gilded machine as a divine presence. The joke reflects one of Star Wars's recurring ideas: technological superiority and spiritual authority can be confused, especially by those who lack the context for what they see.

The films do not pursue the politics of that confusion very far. Threepio's apparent divinity is put to use in a rebel plan, and the Ewoks largely remain a storybook community defined through their assistance to outsiders. Yet the scene also displays the strength of the droids' narrative roles. They are objects that everyone keeps treating as people or symbols depending on the convenience of the moment. They are servants who make themselves indispensable and witnesses who are rarely consulted about what they know.

Chewbacca occupies a related but different position. Peter Mayhew gives him loyalty and individual feeling, although most of the spoken conversation passes through Han. Chewie is not an accessory to Han's charisma. He is the friend whose reaction continually tests Han's self-presentation. When Han is frozen, Chewbacca's anguish refuses to let the loss remain another adventurous reversal. When he endangers himself in a rescue or protests a reckless decision, the audience is reminded that the celebrated rogue has someone beside him who has shared the costs of his choices.

These figures are easy to neglect in a summary of the Skywalker family. On screen, they make that family's drama playable as a story about a company of different beings. The heroes' actions affect people who do not possess a famous surname, a destiny, or the ability to explain their feelings in the dominant language of the film.

## Dagobah is not a training montage

Luke arrives on Dagobah expecting to find a great Jedi master. He finds a small, mischievous creature who annoys him, wastes his time, and tests his manners. This is a lovely piece of dramatic misdirection. The audience has been primed to expect grandeur, and the film offers a shabby hut and an instructor who seems determined to behave like an inconvenience.

Frank Oz's performance as Yoda gives the character a presence beyond the mechanics of the puppet. Yoda is funny, evasive, affectionate, and stern. His smallness is not an obstacle for the audience to overlook. It is a challenge to Luke's assumptions about what authority and power look like.

The training scenes are sometimes remembered as a list of moral lessons. In the film they are more unsettling. Luke has been rewarded for acts of courage, and now he is told that courage without patience may be another version of recklessness. He wants to learn to lift objects; Yoda wants to change his way of seeing himself. Luke asks about the strength of enemies while the teacher asks what he has brought into a place. The cave on Dagobah is the clearest example. Luke enters armed, sees an apparition of Vader, strikes it down, and encounters his own face inside the fallen mask.

The scene does not explain itself. It does not need to. Luke has been offered an image of the enemy as an external thing he can destroy. The image returns the danger to him. The violence he carries can transform the self as well as eliminate an opponent.

The submerged X-wing adds another dimension. Luke can perform impressive exercises but struggles to believe that the impossible task is possible. Yoda lifts the ship from the swamp, and Luke is forced to confront a limit that was partly his own. The point is not that training replaces effort with wishing. It is that Luke's ordinary categories of size and difficulty cannot explain the power he is trying to understand.

Yet Yoda's instruction is not beyond criticism. He and the ghostly Obi-Wan warn Luke against leaving to help his friends. They see a larger danger and fear the catastrophe of an unprepared confrontation. Luke sees people he loves suffering now. The decision pits two genuine obligations against one another. The training can protect him from becoming like Vader; refusing to act could abandon people who trusted him. The trilogy never gives a simple, permanently comfortable rule for such conflicts.

Luke leaves anyway. He is not ready, and the film makes him pay. That consequence gives the choice substance. His compassion is real. His confidence is not yet earned.

## Luke's inheritance is also an education in mistrust

Luke's growing confidence has an unsettling side. He needs teachers, but he also learns that his teachers cannot tell him everything, and in some cases have chosen not to. Obi-Wan gave him a story about his father as a brave Jedi murdered by Vader. On Bespin, that story falls apart. In *Return of the Jedi*, Luke confronts Obi-Wan's ghost with the contradiction, and the explanation makes plain how heavily the old man had shaped the truth to serve what he believed Luke could bear.

It is possible to sympathize with Obi-Wan and still see the damage. He is trying to protect a vulnerable young person from a terrible history. He is also asking Luke to face a dangerous man without knowing their actual relationship. The difference between withholding a burden until someone is ready and withholding knowledge that person needs to make a free decision is not easy to settle. The trilogy is more interesting because it never reduces the mentor to a perfect source of moral information.

Yoda carries a similar burden. He offers profound insight into fear and violence, but his understanding comes from a failed older order. His insistence on discipline may be exactly what Luke needs. His assumption that the proper end of Luke's confrontation is the defeat of Vader may be narrower than Luke's developing hope. The student ultimately has to decide which parts of the inheritance deserve obedience, and which require an answer nobody has supplied.

That is one reason the father revelation is more than a family surprise. It makes Luke uncertain about the reliability of the world described to him by respected people. He cannot simply switch allegiance to Vader, who has his own coercive account of what it means to be father and son. Nor can he return to uncritical belief in his teachers. He must begin judging for himself, in the space between two profoundly compromised authorities.

A film about growing up can describe the process as learning the correct rules from wiser adults. These films offer a harder version: acquiring the capacity to love and respect people who may be mistaken, to inherit their work without inheriting every falsehood, and to act while the past remains incomplete. Luke's final choice comes from this difficult education. He refuses both the Emperor's command and the suggestion that destroying Vader is the only outcome available to him.

## Bespin: the adventure breaks its promise

Cloud City begins as a place of apparent safety. Its bright spaces, floating architecture, and courteous welcome suggest a reprieve from the dark machinery pursuing the Falcon. By the end of the visit it has become a trap. Han is frozen. Leia and Chewbacca barely escape. Lando's control has proved conditional. And Luke is lured into a confrontation he cannot win.

The duel with Vader is not primarily a contest of swords. It is an education in unequal knowledge. Vader has experience, power, and a clear advantage. Luke has a weapon, months of training at most, and the desperate assumption that confronting the enemy may save everyone else. The staging repeatedly expresses the imbalance. Vader tests, advances, vanishes into darkness, and returns from unexpected directions. Luke's movement grows less confident. Spaces that seemed manageable become pits, chutes, and ledges.

Then Vader cuts off Luke's hand. In a saga filled with mechanical limbs and spectacular injuries, this is a particularly intimate violation. Luke's body becomes the record of his failure. The revelation that follows does something even more radical. Vader claims to be Luke's father, turning the enemy from an obstacle on the path to adulthood into part of the origin of the self.

What makes the revelation devastating is not simply the surprise. It corrupts several assumptions at once. Luke's image of his father as a noble figure is threatened. His mentor's account of the past is now suspect. The moral division between himself and Vader becomes frighteningly porous. He cannot believe the claim without losing a cherished story of who he is, yet the film has made the claim impossible to dismiss emotionally. His response is not a clever line of defiance. He lets himself fall.

The ending of *Empire* refuses to restore the comfortable old company immediately. Luke survives, but he has lost a hand and certainty. Han remains captive. Leia and Luke look outward from a rebel ship toward a galaxy not yet reclaimed. There is love among the survivors, but it cannot manufacture a victory. For a film with so many dazzling spaceships, this is a remarkably intimate conclusion: the people are still together in some form, but they cannot fix what happened.

The episode's greatness lies partly in a creative risk. The first film had taught audiences that resourcefulness, bravery, and loyalty would be rewarded with a triumphant ceremony. The second film allows the same qualities to lead people into mistakes, captivity, and grief. It does not abandon the virtues of the adventure. It makes them harder to practice.

## A villain performed by several people

Darth Vader is sometimes discussed as though he were the achievement of one actor, one line, or one mask. The screen character is an ensemble of contributions. David Prowse's physical performance gives Vader size and bodily authority in the original films. James Earl Jones's voice lends deliberation, menace, and surprising variations of restraint. The breathing, designed within the sound work associated with Ben Burtt, makes the costume feel like an enclosing machine rather than a simple disguise. Sebastian Shaw appears as the unmasked Anakin in the original theatrical *Return of the Jedi*, giving the finale a frail human face unlike the figure who has dominated the trilogy. Bob Anderson contributed to the sword-fighting performance, particularly the duels. The costume, helmet, lighting, editing, and musical language are parts of the same construction.

This collaboration matters because Vader's identity is built around division. He seems half person and half mechanism; he speaks with terrifying control while his breath announces permanent physical dependence. His body is both imposing and imprisoned. A film that showed his face too soon might make him too legible. A film that left him only as a black silhouette could struggle to support the final story. The trilogy gradually lets the audience suspect an injured person inside the armor without granting him innocence.

*Empire* gives Vader something he did not possess in the first film: an emotionally targeted purpose. He wants Luke alive. The details of that interest initially appear mysterious, then become the engine of the family story. The Emperor enters more directly as an authority above him. Vader, once the figure everyone else feared, is revealed to fear or obey someone else. The hierarchy reaches into his body and history.

It would be a mistake to treat the later revelation as an excuse for what Vader has done. He helped sustain a regime of murder and conquest. He participated in torture and political terror. He supervised destructive operations. The story is not asking whether those actions were secretly harmless because he once had another name. It is asking a harder question: can a person who has committed terrible acts still choose, at some final moment, not to commit another?

James Earl Jones's measured voice is especially effective in setting up the question. Vader seldom sounds as though he is pleading. His speech seems designed to eliminate conversation by announcing reality. But when he calls Luke to join him, the invitation contains a damaged form of attachment. He imagines father and son together as rulers. He has translated a desire for connection into the only political language he still trusts: command.

That is why Luke's answer in the final film must be more than a refusal of a bad employment offer. It must be the rejection of an entire way of thinking about family and power.

## Endor's two battles

The final act of *Return of the Jedi* moves among three connected struggles. A rebel fleet attacks a new Death Star. A ground force attempts to disable the shield protecting it. Luke confronts Vader and the Emperor aboard the station. The structure is thrilling because each operation depends on a different kind of skill. Pilots need timing and courage. Han, Leia, and their allies need improvisation and cooperation. Luke needs emotional clarity in an environment designed to destroy it.

The Endor material is also the source of a persistent argument. The Ewoks are small, furry inhabitants of the forest who help defeat better-armed Imperial troops. Their role extends the trilogy's interest in asymmetrical warfare: a technologically superior army, trained to dominate, can be disrupted by enemies who understand the terrain and refuse the expected terms of engagement. That makes thematic sense. Some of the comedy and action, however, strain the credibility of the threat. A film that asked us to fear an immense war machine sometimes invites us to laugh as its soldiers are defeated by apparently improvised forest tactics.

The answer is not to pretend there is no problem, nor to demand that every minute of a space fantasy reproduce modern military doctrine. The Ewoks are partly a storybook device. Their playfulness and vulnerability create contrast with the Emperor's machinery. They also represent people whom the Empire would not regard as significant enough to plan around. The scene at its best says that large institutions are vulnerable to communities they barely recognize. At its least convincing, it asks the audience to forget too quickly how dangerous those institutions were supposed to be.

Above the forest, Lando commands the Falcon in the fleet assault. His presence is a good conclusion to the Cloud City betrayal. The man who once tried to preserve his city through compromise with Vader is now risking himself in open resistance. He has not become Han; he is making a distinct choice. The battle moves through ships, orders, losses, and sudden discoveries as the rebels learn the shield remains active. Again, the heroic plan fails at first. The film does not simply reward confidence.

On the station, meanwhile, the Emperor wants Luke to watch his friends die. This is not incidental cruelty. He is staging a lesson in power. If Luke believes his anger is justified, the Emperor can direct it. If the boy attacks in fury, he may begin to resemble the man he hopes to defeat. The entire military operation becomes a weapon aimed at Luke's judgment.

The intercutting is therefore more than a device for multiplying excitement. Each battle changes the meaning of the others. The ground fighters create a chance for the fleet. The fleet's danger creates unbearable pressure on Luke. Luke's struggle with himself tests whether military necessity has become an excuse for any act of violence. The trilogy has arranged a spectacular external victory around an internal decision.

## The son refuses the father's idea of victory

Luke's confrontation with Vader and the Emperor contains the trilogy's most important reversal. Vader threatens Leia. Luke loses control, attacks furiously, drives Vader backward, and finally cuts off his hand. For a moment, the son has become exactly the kind of fighter his enemies wanted to create. His victory is physical and immediate, but it is also frightening.

Then he sees Vader's severed mechanical hand and looks at his own artificial hand. The visual connection is not subtle, and it should not be. It interrupts the momentum of the duel with a question the audience can answer almost before Luke does. What has this fight made him? What will he become if he now delivers the final blow?

He stops. He throws away his lightsaber. He refuses to kill Vader for the Emperor's satisfaction. That decision does not destroy the military danger. The Death Star is still operating. The rebels are still fighting. The Emperor is still powerful. Luke makes the choice knowing it may lead to his death.

It is a choice his mentors were not sure he could make. Their urgent focus on confronting Vader and surviving the Emperor left comparatively little room for the possibility that refusing the expected final killing might change the family relationship itself. Luke has learned from his teachers, but he does not become merely a vehicle for their judgment. He completes his education by taking responsibility for an action they could not guarantee would succeed.

The Emperor answers with torture. His lightning strips away any lingering idea that Luke can win by making the correct argument. Vader watches his son suffer. The mask is unreadable, and for a few moments the film needs it to be. We are left to listen to the violence and imagine what might be happening inside the figure who caused so much of it.

Then Vader acts. He lifts the Emperor and throws him into the station's depths, saving Luke at the cost of his own life. The action is brief, awkwardly physical, and final. For the first time in a long while, Vader uses his immense strength not to dominate his son but to prevent his son's destruction.

What does redemption mean here? Not acquittal, and not the restoration of everyone Vader killed. The Empire's victims do not reappear. The film's moral resolution is narrower and more emotionally precise. Anakin Skywalker, hidden inside the armor, makes a decision against the power that has defined him. His last choice is not the sum of his life, but it is a real choice. Luke was right that there was still a person capable of making it.

The unmasking gives the achievement a cost. Anakin's face is pale, injured, and human. The object that seemed almost identical with his identity is removed, and what remains is not an all-powerful father but a dying man who can barely speak. Luke has recovered a relationship only at the moment he must lose it. There is no comfortable reunion ahead. The scene asks us to accept the dignity of a final moment without mistaking it for the undoing of a violent history.

This is why the trilogy ends with a celebration and a funeral, not a celebration alone. The rebellion has won a decisive battle. Luke has buried his father. The public victory and the private loss coexist.

## What the trilogy leaves unresolved

The films never give us a developed model of the Republic that might replace the Empire. They do not explain how a rebel alliance transforms military success into constitutional legitimacy, how former Imperial personnel are treated, or what daily life looks like on all those planets now supposedly freed. They do not offer much space for the ordinary people most affected by the war. Their understanding of collective struggle is most vivid in battle and weakest in reconstruction.

There are also character imbalances. Leia's political authority is essential to the story but increasingly shares space with revelations that define her through Luke and Vader. Han's development culminates in commitment, yet his behavior in the final film sometimes repeats old romantic squabbles rather than investigating what commitment would now demand of him. Lando completes a graceful movement from compromised administrator to rebel leader but seldom receives the same interior attention as the central trio. The droids sustain the comic perspective and link the adventures, though the films sometimes treat them as tools when the plot needs a convenient switch to be thrown.

The trilogy's imaginative pleasures remain enormous. It offers distinct worlds that seem to possess lives outside the hero's itinerary: the cold distances of Hoth, the swamp and solitude of Dagobah, the beautiful danger of Cloud City, the criminal theater of Jabba's palace, the forests of Endor. It gives us spaceship battles that are clear enough to follow, arguments that are funny because the characters cannot stop being themselves, and a villain who becomes more difficult to understand just as his danger becomes more personal.

It is also, finally, a story about the limits of heroism as domination. Luke does fight. He can be stubborn, impatient, and dangerously sure of himself. His eventual wisdom does not consist in becoming strong enough to make every other person obey. He refuses a particular use of his strength. Leia persists in her public commitments. Han learns to return. Lando accepts a risk he once tried to bargain away. Their growth is related, but it is not identical.

The Death Star's destruction in the first film and the Emperor's defeat in the last are outwardly similar climaxes: an immense imperial threat ends, and the heroes are saved at the final moment. Look more carefully and the trilogy has moved its center of gravity. The first triumph depends on Luke trusting something he cannot measure and taking a shot. The last depends on trusting something he cannot guarantee in another person and declining to strike.

Those are complementary gestures, not opposites. Both require training, friendship, and courage. Both take place while other people are fighting for survival. But only the last can turn the monster's defeat into a family tragedy that contains a small act of mercy.

The trilogy leaves us with the image of people celebrating under an open sky, and of a young man mourning someone most of those people knew only as an enemy. A political victory has been won. A private relationship has been recovered too late. The galaxy is not finished, and the future is not settled. For these three films, that is enough of an ending.
