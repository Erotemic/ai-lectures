# 004000 — The Prequels: How a Republic Falls

**Track:** Star Wars: A Galaxy Worth Arguing About. **Audience:** General listeners and Star Wars fans. **Prerequisites:** None. **Scope:** *The Phantom Menace* (1999), *Attack of the Clones* (2002), *Revenge of the Sith* (2005), with selective context from the 2008 *Clone Wars* series. **Spoilers:** All three prequel films, including the fall of Anakin, Order 66, Padmé's death, and the establishment of the Empire; selective *Clone Wars* story outcomes.

# The Prequels: How a Republic Falls

A queen stands in a magnificent chamber and asks its assembled representatives to take seriously an invasion of her planet. They do not immediately help her. A committee may be appointed. A commission may investigate. There are procedures, objections, and people with good reasons to delay. The aggressor has guns; the Republic has a schedule. In the confusion, a senator from the invaded world gently suggests that his own government can no longer be trusted. The queen demands a vote of no confidence in its leader. The senator who advised her, Palpatine, is delighted.

It is one of the cruelest jokes in *The Phantom Menace*: a principled leader, trying to rescue her people, becomes an instrument in the political ascent of the man who engineered their crisis. The movie does not ask us to regard her as a fool. She has a real emergency, an inadequate institution, and an apparently helpful ally. She is making a decision among terrible options. That is exactly why the trick works.

When people describe the *Star Wars* prequels, they often begin with Anakin Skywalker. Understandably so. The three films promise to explain how a boy becomes Darth Vader. But they tell a second tragedy alongside his: how a Republic becomes the Empire, and how people who wish to defend the Republic help bring about its destruction. The two plots are not separate. Palpatine understands that public fear can become emergency authority and that private fear can become personal obedience. He manipulates both with startling patience.

The prequels are awkward films in places. They contain strained dialogue, episodes of comedy that do not sit comfortably beside the tragedy, and characters whose emotions are sometimes spoken more stiffly than they are felt. They also contain an exceptionally ambitious story about democracy, coercion, love, institutional failure, and the wish to make death obey. To understand their achievement, we should resist two temptations: pretending the execution never falters, and letting its faults obscure the story that is actually there.

## The ending is known

*The Phantom Menace* arrived in 1999, *Attack of the Clones* in 2002, and *Revenge of the Sith* in 2005. George Lucas wrote and directed all three. Viewers who knew the original trilogy already knew the great outcome. The Emperor would rule. The Jedi would nearly disappear. Obi-Wan would survive, and Anakin would enter the black armor. The films had to make a catastrophe interesting when its destination was never in doubt.

There are at least two ways to construct such a tragedy. One is to conceal the mechanism: we know the catastrophe is coming but not precisely how. Another is to let us recognize warning signs that the characters cannot recognize. The prequels do both. They turn the familiar iconography of the Empire into the future of buildings, institutions, uniforms, and practices we are slowly learning to trust. We look at clean Republican cruisers and can imagine the Imperial war machine coming into focus. We see a Senate so immense that no person can grasp it all, and we remember a later Empire in which dissent has been abolished altogether.

Yet knowing the outcome creates a special problem for the central character. Anakin cannot merely grow up to be evil because the next movies need a villain. He must make decisions that become intelligible without becoming justified. The prequels often achieve that distinction when they give him something to lose, then let us watch him respond to that fear. They stumble when they assume that blunt declarations of feeling can substitute for watching two people come to understand each other. Both tendencies belong to the films. They are not grounds for dismissing the entire project.

The first movie is also stranger than a modern origin story might lead us to expect. It spends a great deal of time on taxes, trade, the structure of a Republic, parliamentary maneuvering, and an elected queen. One could begin a movie about the future Darth Vader with a sinister child. Lucas begins instead with an apparently limited commercial dispute and two diplomats. In retrospect, this may be the more revealing choice. An empire does not come into existence simply because one talented person makes a monstrous choice. Conditions have to exist in which that choice can reorganize the whole society.

## Naboo and the machinery of legitimacy

The Trade Federation's blockade of Naboo gives the story its first political fact: armed commercial interests have acquired enough power to threaten a member world. The Republic has agents, law, and a Senate, but its ability to turn a grievance into decisive protection is uncertain. Qui-Gon Jinn and Obi-Wan Kenobi arrive as negotiators and quickly discover that someone is prepared to kill them. The veneer of ordinary diplomacy is already broken.

The Federation's leaders are often played for cowardly comedy. That tone creates an odd contrast with the invasion's consequences. Enormous transports carry armies of battle droids into cities and across green fields. Ordinary people become hostages. Queen Amidala must decide how to save them while knowing that the machinery of legitimate government can be used against her. The droids look mechanical in a nearly literal sense: they are an army whose soldiers can be ordered about and discarded without disturbing its owners. This will become more important when another army, made of living beings, appears in the next film.

Amidala's flight to Coruscant gives us the Senate as spectacle. The building is a huge circular chamber filled with separate platforms, every delegation physically visible yet no one near the center of the whole. It is an impressive image of representation. It also offers an image of paralysis. Palpatine does not need to invent the chamber's weaknesses. He only needs to make use of them. He interprets bureaucratic delays for Amidala, directs her outrage, and presents the chance to replace the chancellor as the only realistic road to relief.

Notice the precision of the trap. Padmé's political instincts are fundamentally sound: people under attack require help, and a government that cannot provide it deserves challenge. Palpatine supplies an incomplete explanation of why the failure occurred and then offers himself, indirectly, as the remedy. He uses her genuine public responsibility to advance a private project of power. He has manufactured the fire and arrives carrying the plan for a new fire department.

The film's conclusion offers an outwardly reassuring victory. Naboo is liberated. A fragile alliance with the Gungans is made. The queen and her supporters show courage. Palpatine becomes chancellor. The victory music is sincere, yet it is also not the whole truth. The smaller battle was won. The larger political design was improved by the very effort to win it.

One virtue of treating the trilogy as a political tragedy is that it makes this ending intelligible. Without the later films, the coronation looks like a tidy conclusion. With them, it becomes the moment an audience applauds while the trap finishes closing.

## The boy who can leave, and the mother who cannot

On Tatooine, the political story becomes intimate. Anakin Skywalker is a child enslaved with his mother, Shmi. He possesses startling technical talent, mechanical curiosity, good instincts, and the ability to pilot at speeds that terrify adults. None of that frees him. The conditions of his life are determined by ownership, chance, and the choices of visitors passing through.

Qui-Gon is moved by the child and suspects a destiny larger than the desert. He arranges a contest that may give Anakin freedom. What follows is a podrace, among the prequels' clearest demonstrations of cinematic excitement. The machines look dangerous and improvised; they race so close to the ground that the landscape itself seems to attack them. The race communicates Anakin's instinctive feel for technology without a speech. He is not merely announced as extraordinary. He has to do something extraordinary under conditions in which failure could kill him.

Then comes the cost that shadows everything afterward. Anakin is freed. Shmi is not. There is a goodbye in which a child gains an astonishing future and loses the one person whose love has been secure. The Jedi, concerned with exceptional abilities and the question of training, can offer little in response to the ordinary fact of a mother left in bondage. Qui-Gon's concern is real, yet the result is incomplete.

This is the emotional beginning of Darth Vader, not because separation automatically makes someone violent, but because it gives Anakin a loss he cannot control and a world that associates exceptional power with rescue. He will spend years learning how to move objects, anticipate threats, and overpower opponents. He will not learn how to guarantee that a loved person remains alive. Those are different capacities, and the prequels repeatedly force him to confuse them.

The Jedi Council senses fear in the boy and hesitates to train him. From their standpoint, they face a genuine responsibility: power in the hands of someone governed by anger can be catastrophic. From Anakin's standpoint, people with immense authority seem to treat his concern for his mother as evidence against him. We can see the danger in his attachment without pretending their response offers a satisfying answer to his circumstances. The institution is very good at detecting a problem and less successful at caring for the person who has it.

The film ends with Qui-Gon's death, Obi-Wan's promise to train Anakin, and a victorious celebration that sounds almost too complete. One relationship capable of understanding Anakin's extraordinary nature has vanished. Another, founded in affection and obligation, begins under the weight of a promise. The world is still celebrating when the story quietly places a child on a path that will lead to disaster.

## A movie that asks two incompatible things of its audience

Some of the prequels' harshest criticism attaches to the way *The Phantom Menace* combines that political story with broad comedy. Jar Jar Binks, a digitally animated Gungan, repeatedly stumbles through dangerous situations and accidentally helps. The character belongs to the film's love of physical slapstick, but his mannerisms, speech, and prominence irritated many viewers, while some found racial caricature in aspects of the performance. An assessment need not declare that either reaction cancels every other. The film was trying to invite children into a galaxy that could also sustain a story of corrupted government. Sometimes the methods of invitation jar against the argument.

The problem is not the presence of comedy itself. The original trilogy makes essential use of jokes, especially from people too frightened or impatient to behave heroically. The difference is whether the comic rhythm seems to arise from the same social world as the danger. At its best, *The Phantom Menace* manages this: the droid army can be both faintly ridiculous in its mechanical obedience and terrifying in its numbers. At its weakest, the comic performance feels as though it has wandered in from another movie.

The performances also face a peculiar challenge. Characters frequently discuss matters of great political and spiritual significance in formal language that does not always reveal their inner lives. Natalie Portman plays Amidala with a queen's cultivated composure, at times deliberately concealing her identity behind the handmaiden arrangement. That reserve is dramatically useful when she negotiates or confronts the Senate; it can also make a central figure harder to know. The filmmaking depends heavily on costumes, architecture, music, and actors' bearing to supply meaning that the dialogue withholds.

And then comes the final duel. Qui-Gon and Obi-Wan face Darth Maul, played physically by Ray Park, whose presence gives the Sith a kinetic menace. The fight is brilliantly legible: long corridors, separated spaces, barriers that open and close, bodies circling and pausing. John Williams's *Duel of the Fates* does not require us to know the whole history of the Sith to feel the scale of this confrontation. The title attached to the music is apt in a dramatic sense: more than the life of a single combatant is at risk. Qui-Gon's death alters who will raise Anakin, and the character most inclined to look beyond rigid procedure will no longer be there to challenge the Order from within.

This blend of superb visual invention and sometimes opaque characterization runs through the prequels. It is more illuminating to examine how the elements interact than to classify the films as either failures rescued by nostalgia or misunderstood masterpieces beyond criticism. Sometimes a great scene carries an argument that the surrounding film has not fully earned. Sometimes a supposedly boring political exchange carries more of the trilogy's meaning than an expensive battle.

## The detective story that discovers an army

*Attack of the Clones* opens in a Republic that has grown more frightened and divided. A separatist movement threatens to break away. Padmé, now a senator, is under attack. Obi-Wan pursues the mystery of an assassin and ends up on Kamino, where an army is being manufactured for the Republic. The clones appear as a solution to a military emergency, but the film's detective-story structure turns that solution into a warning.

The Kaminoans treat the clone project as a technical accomplishment. The troops are obedient, standardized, trainable, and ready for deployment. Their sameness is visually impressive. It is also disturbing. A government that claims to defend freedom is preparing to field soldiers produced under circumstances that deny those soldiers ordinary freedom to choose their occupation. The Republic's eventual dependence on these troops should trouble us even before we learn who organized their creation.

The Jedi are caught in a particularly corrosive position. They discover that an army has been ordered in the Republic's name by means they do not fully understand, while the political crisis seems to leave no alternative to using it. At Geonosis, the Jedi enter an arena to rescue prisoners and are overwhelmed by organized mechanical force. The clones arrive in transports and transform near defeat into military victory. It is staged as rescue; it is also the birth of a war machine.

The scene ends not with peace but with columns of soldiers boarding ships under the gaze of senior officials. Yoda names the Clone War, and the music lets us sense that the triumphant movement of personnel is actually a catastrophe. The Republic has acquired the organized violence it needs to survive an immediate crisis. It has become more dependent on the political center that authorized the army. Palpatine can now present extraordinary executive authority as the reasonable answer to an extraordinary situation.

The Jedi, meanwhile, become generals. That word changes their social role. An order that claims to preserve peace is now responsible for planning assaults, accepting casualties, and directing an army whose members were created for obedience. The war gives Jedi characters courage and solidarity; it also places them inside a structure designed by their enemies. In a later animated series, those contradictions are explored through soldiers who have names and friendships, not merely matching faces in a march. But the contradiction is already present in *Attack of the Clones*: protectors have adopted the machinery of a state moving toward dictatorship.

## The difficult romance

The other half of *Attack of the Clones* follows Anakin and Padmé into secrecy and marriage. It is the most contested part of the prequels for understandable reasons. Hayden Christensen and Natalie Portman are asked to make earnest declarations in language that often sounds less like spontaneous feeling than like a young person struggling to invent an adult vocabulary for it. Their relationship is supposed to bear enormous dramatic weight. The movie does not always supply the small shared experiences that would make its emotional leaps convincing.

Yet the structure of the romance is essential to the tragedy. Anakin is now a young man who knows he has exceptional abilities and who has learned to resent authority. He desires Padmé and fears losing her. He is also governed by an older fear: his mother is still on Tatooine, still beyond his reach. When he finally returns, he finds that she has been taken by Tusken Raiders. He locates her but cannot save her. She dies in his arms.

Anakin's response is horrifying. He slaughters the camp, including children, then confesses what he has done. The film offers grief as the cause of his rage, not a moral defense of it. This is the moment at which the viewer must refuse the comfortable conversion of every harmful choice into evidence of trauma. His suffering is genuine. So is his agency. He has chosen to punish people who did not all cause his loss, because he could not prevent the loss itself.

Padmé's response is difficult to read charitably. She offers sympathy rather than confronting the full moral magnitude of his admission. Perhaps the film wants us to see how affection narrows judgment; perhaps it simply moves through a terrible event too quickly. Either way, the moment reveals a problem that later becomes fatal. People around Anakin learn to recognize his pain, but they do not establish a reliable boundary between his pain and the violence he permits himself to commit.

Their secret wedding is therefore not simply a romantic endpoint. It seals a relationship built in defiance of an institution whose rules they fear and whose understanding they do not trust. There is tenderness in the choice to be together and an unstable arrangement beneath it. Anakin believes love means preventing separation at almost any cost. Padmé believes they can build a private life while remaining committed to public service. The trilogy will take both hopes seriously, then demonstrate how Palpatine exploits the gap between them.

## The chancellor who never needs to hurry

Palpatine's great advantage is that he does not always appear to be asking for power on his own behalf. He is the sympathetic senator from Naboo, the embattled chancellor of a divided Republic, and the older confidant who seems willing to acknowledge Anakin's talents. Every institution encounters him in a different role. Few people see the whole arrangement.

A procedural democracy is vulnerable when an ambitious leader persuades each constituency that its own good intentions require an exception. The Senate grants emergency powers to fight the separatists. Military leaders accept central coordination because the war demands speed. The Jedi continue serving because the alternative appears to be abandoning civilians to conflict. Anakin trusts Palpatine because the chancellor seems to value the parts of him that Jedi discipline attempts to restrain.

This is not a claim that institutions always fail or that every compromise is corruption. It is a specific political structure. The public is frightened. The war has been manipulated from both sides. The executive benefits whether a battle is won or lost, so long as the emergency persists. When a leader controls the apparent opposition as well as the government's response, elections and procedures can continue to look legitimate while the range of real choices narrows.

Ian McDiarmid gives Palpatine an agreeable surface and an appetite for command that becomes more visible as the trilogy progresses. He is often theatrical, even gleeful, when the disguise falls away. Before that, he is patient with Anakin, respectful toward Padmé in public, and certain that every crisis proves the need for stronger authority. The performance asks us to enjoy a villain's cleverness without mistaking that cleverness for wisdom.

This is why the Senate scenes have staying power. They are not a thesis about the inefficiency of a particular committee. They dramatize a more serious problem: legal procedures can be used to ratify a transfer of power that those procedures were supposed to constrain. In the climactic declaration of the Empire, the Senate applauds. The audience can see the guns behind the constitutional language. Many of the senators cannot, or refuse to, act on what they know.

Padmé's response is grief rather than surprise. She has spent years trying to work through the Republic's institutions, and now sees them producing the political opposite of everything she believed they existed to protect. Her observation about liberty dying amid applause is a concise diagnosis of the moment. It also exposes a limitation in the films' treatment of her. She is politically perceptive, but the final movie increasingly gives her the role of witnessing collapse rather than organizing resistance within it. Some material about dissent survives outside the theatrical cut, but in the released film her narrowing agency is palpable.

## The opera and the bargain

*Revenge of the Sith* opens with warfare already consuming the galaxy. Anakin and Obi-Wan are experienced comrades. The film lets them be funny and efficient together during a rescue mission, and for a moment we glimpse the exciting friendship that their later destruction will make painful. Soon the celebration gives way to another private crisis. Padmé is pregnant. Anakin dreams she will die in childbirth.

The dream provides Palpatine's opportunity. The chancellor doesn't begin by ordering Anakin to join the Sith. He offers a story about a master of the dark side who could allegedly influence life and death. The conversation occurs at an opera, with strange, slow motion in a suspended sphere while two men speak almost quietly amid a crowd. It is one of the prequels' strongest scenes because temptation finally becomes specific. The promise is not military promotion, political influence, or the abstract excitement of evil. It is the possibility of saving a person Anakin loves.

The bargain is deceptive. Palpatine implies access to knowledge without demonstrating that he can deliver it. He encourages Anakin to believe that obedience may buy protection from loss. A lesser version of this scene would have the villain promise unrestricted power and the hero suddenly desire it. Here, power is presented as a means to an emotionally urgent end. That does not make Anakin's future actions reasonable. It makes the mechanism of his self-deception visible.

The Jedi respond to growing danger with suspicion. They ask Anakin to observe the chancellor, but deny him the trust and status he thinks he deserves. Their concern about Palpatine is justified. Their handling of Anakin makes his resentment easier to manipulate. When he turns to Yoda with fear of loss, he receives counsel about learning not to cling to what will pass away. As spiritual advice, there is a real point: no living person can guarantee the survival of another. As help to a frightened young husband who cannot tell the truth about his circumstances, it is disastrously incomplete.

Anakin does not reveal what he most needs help with. His secrecy means the Jedi cannot respond to the full situation. Their institutional habits give him little reason to expect compassionate understanding if he does. Palpatine, who knows how to offer selective sympathy, can fill the gap. Once again the tragedy is not that a single exchange explains the fall. It is that several imperfect relationships create an opening through which a careful manipulator can act.

## The room in which everything changes

The confrontation in Palpatine's office is the crucial moral hinge. Mace Windu discovers the chancellor's identity and tries to arrest him. Their duel ends with Windu prepared to kill an apparently defeated Sith Lord. Anakin arrives, desperate to preserve the person who claims to hold the secret of saving Padmé. Windu insists that the danger cannot be contained by ordinary detention. Anakin argues that Palpatine should stand trial. It is a morally serious principle spoken by a character who has ulterior motives he barely admits to himself.

He has alternatives. He can oppose Windu without defending Palpatine. He can refuse to strike. He can choose to leave the promise of forbidden knowledge unfulfilled. Instead he intervenes violently against Windu, and Palpatine kills the Jedi. Anakin sees at once the catastrophe he has helped cause. He does not move away from it. He submits to the authority that can now make his guilt seem irreversible.

This is important because the prequels are not a story in which one bad deed mechanically converts someone into an evil person. Anakin's fall consists of repeated opportunities to stop, each made harder by the previous choice. Fear gives way to violence. Violence produces guilt. Guilt encourages further obedience to someone who promises to make the violence useful. By the time Palpatine names him Darth Vader, Anakin has begun to confuse loyalty with the need to justify what he has already done.

The slaughter in the Jedi Temple refuses to make the transformation merely symbolic. The victims include children. The film does not show every act, and it does not need to. We know that Anakin has crossed a boundary from betraying colleagues to participating in systematic murder. At the same time, Palpatine sends Order 66 through the clone forces. Jedi commanders who have fought beside their soldiers are suddenly attacked by them. The montage intercuts different planets and settings so that the scale of the betrayal becomes continental, then galactic.

The original theatrical prequels do not stop to explain every mechanism that makes the clones obey. Later *Clone Wars* stories develop the implanted inhibitor chips and the tragedy of men whose capacity to resist has been deliberately undermined. This added detail intensifies rather than erases the original sequence. The soldiers' obedience is not merely a military fact; it has been designed into their bodies. The Republic's use of engineered soldiers was never a neutral tactical convenience. Its architecture of violence always contained a hidden political purpose.

## Mustafar is not the beginning of the story

The duel between Obi-Wan and Anakin on Mustafar is sometimes remembered primarily as a visual spectacle: platforms over molten rock, enormous leaps, the final exchange on unstable ground. Its emotional force depends on everything already lost. Obi-Wan is fighting someone he taught, trusted, and loved as a brother. Anakin believes he has been betrayed by everyone except the man who made him into an instrument. Padmé comes hoping to retrieve the person she loves and discovers someone claiming to commit horrors for her sake.

The film gives the actors formidable obstacles. Their lines sometimes state feelings that the movement and faces already convey more powerfully. But the basic dramatic image is strong: two people who shared danger and jokes now use that intimacy to anticipate each other's movements in a fight neither wants in quite the same way. The battle is fierce because the relationship was real. Every spectacular leap is a distorted memory of the trust that used to make them effective together.

Anakin's accusation that others have turned Padmé against him is especially revealing. He promised to protect her from death and now cannot tolerate her independent judgment about the killings he has committed. The wish to save the beloved has become the wish to command what the beloved sees, believes, and chooses. He is not simply overcome by an external dark power. He is turning love into a claim of ownership.

At the end, Anakin is left grievously wounded. Palpatine arrives and the familiar armored figure takes form. The suit keeps him alive but also makes physical his dependence on the master who promised control over death. He tried to become powerful enough to prevent loss and has lost almost everything, including the ordinary use of his own body. The respirator that will announce Vader's entrance in the earlier-released films is, in this story, a monument to the bargain's failure.

Padmé dies after giving birth to twins. The way the film connects her death to grief and exhaustion has often drawn justified criticism; it makes a capable political character's final act seem troublingly passive. We can recognize that weakness while seeing the designed contrast. Two children, each a possible future for the galaxy, are born as the new Empire establishes itself. The next generation survives the failure of the present one. There is no happy ending to *Revenge of the Sith*, but there is a continuation of life that the Emperor cannot fully control.

## The politics are in the pictures

The prequels are sometimes discussed as though their political ideas reside entirely in speeches about the Senate. They reside just as strongly in the changing visual world. Naboo's palace contains open spaces, elaborate dress, ceremony, and a public sphere worth defending. Coruscant's administrative and urban architecture creates a sense of immense complexity. The Jedi Temple, with its high windows and formal chambers, conveys an institution built to endure. The clone army presents a chilling alternative: mass coordination whose members share a body plan and a mission but not equal independence.

By the third film, the Republic's spaces have begun to look like the system that will replace it. Star Destroyer shapes, officers' arrangements, and mechanical regimentation all make the political change visible before the founding speech. As the Senate applauds, the camera's compositional order tells us something no individual speech needs to explain. A republic can preserve the buildings and rituals of authority while changing who may make decisions and who must obey.

The prequels also altered what large-scale cinematic fantasy could put on screen. Their digital characters and environments sometimes look conspicuously fabricated, particularly when compared with the tactile wear of the 1977 film. Yet the films' willingness to create impossible cities, battlefields, and crowds also expanded the setting's imaginative range. *Attack of the Clones* was a landmark in digital feature cinematography. That technical history is not separate from the story: these films attempted to depict not a handful of rebels in borrowed ships but a whole republic before its fall.

An audience may reasonably find that abundance exhausting. An intricate composition can leave too little space for a person to breathe. The first trilogy often built credibility through a room, a prop, and three people arguing; the prequels sometimes try to convey historical scale through additional armies and buildings. When they work, the result feels like an enormous civic world turning. When they don't, spectacle becomes a screen between us and the people we need to understand. The strongest moments usually combine scale with a comprehensible decision: a queen refusing to wait, a child leaving his mother, a soldier turning against a friend, an apprentice choosing the wrong hand to defend.

## The tragedy that outlives the villains

The prequels explain the origins of a monster, but their deeper achievement is to make a political system's failure feel cumulative. Naboo has a real invasion. The Senate really is compromised. The separatist war kills people. The clone army does save lives in individual battles. The Jedi are sometimes brave, sometimes wise, and sometimes obtuse. Padmé works earnestly for peace. Anakin's love is real. Palpatine's art is to turn these facts into arguments that everyone should grant him just one more exception.

This is what distinguishes the story from a simple tale of a republic conquered from the outside. The decisive violence comes from within institutions that believed themselves to be doing necessary work. The Jedi become generals. The Senate transfers its authority. A defender of public responsibility helps remove a chancellor. A young man seeking to prevent death helps murder children. None of those transformations happens in an instant, even if a movie compresses several of them into a single moment.

It would be too simple to say the prequels teach that fear is bad. Fear is often sensible. Padmé ought to fear an invasion; Anakin has reason to fear losing someone; the Senate has reason to fear the war. The danger comes when frightened people are persuaded that their immediate security requires surrendering the freedom to judge the person promising protection. Palpatine sells control in the language of care, survival, and order. He wins because people accept the price of those promises without seeing the whole bill.

At the end of the trilogy, the famous black mask is not merely a character reveal. It is a political result. Anakin has been made into the violent servant of the government he helped create. The Republic's institutions have become those of a regime that demands submission. A man who cannot endure losing a loved person has become the instrument of a state that treats countless lives as expendable.

And there, quietly, are the infants. Their lives belong to a future that will have to challenge both the Emperor and the story he has taught Anakin to tell about himself. The prequels are grim because the right people do not always recognize the trap in time. They are still recognizably *Star Wars* because the future is not entirely owned by the people who won.
