# 005000 — Jedi, Sith, and the Problem of Power

**Track:** Star Wars: A Galaxy Worth Arguing About. **Audience:** General listeners and Star Wars fans. **Prerequisites:** None. **Scope:** The Force, Jedi and Sith in the Skywalker films, with selected developments from the 2008 *The Clone Wars* series and *Star Wars Rebels*. **Spoilers:** Major original, prequel and sequel trilogy endings; Ahsoka's departure, Kanan's death, and Ezra's later choices.

# Jedi, Sith, and the Problem of Power

On the Millennium Falcon, an old man puts a helmet over a boy's eyes. The helmet makes it difficult to see, and a small floating training remote begins firing harmless but painful bolts. The boy holds a lightsaber. He is trying to learn a skill that nobody he grew up with could have explained. The old man tells him to reach beyond ordinary sight, to use a sense he has barely begun to recognize. The exercise looks simple. Avoid being hit. It is also the beginning of one of the strangest moral languages in popular cinema.

A few scenes later, Luke Skywalker must decide whether to trust that feeling while attacking the Death Star. The machinery of the rebellion is all around him: pilots, communications, flight computers, detailed plans, a nearly impossible military objective. The Force enters as something that cannot be reduced to an additional instrument panel. Luke turns away from the targeting system, trusts his developing awareness, and makes the shot. At this point, the Force does not yet require a manual of powers, an elaborate genealogy, or a theory of constitutional government. It gives the film a way to dramatize trust.

But as *Star Wars* expanded, the Force had to carry more weight. It became the foundation of a lost religious order, the object of political struggle, the justification for training children, the instrument of enormous violence, and a disputed inheritance. It produced heroes who tried not to become tyrants and villains who believed that domination was freedom. It also gave fans years of arguments about what a Jedi is supposed to do.

The interesting question is not who can throw the largest object or win the most spectacular duel. It is this: **when a person can exercise power that most people cannot, what should govern the choice to use it?** Jedi and Sith offer competing answers, but the films are at their most alive when even a good person has trouble knowing what a good use of power would be.

## Before the rulebook

Obi-Wan Kenobi's first explanation is deliberately mysterious. The Force is an energy field made by living things, surrounding and connecting them. He speaks as a survivor of a vanished tradition, but not as someone reciting a textbook. Alec Guinness lends the explanation gravity without allowing it to become an abstract classroom demonstration. The old man is telling Luke that what he sees is not the whole of the world, and that his own capacity to respond to it may be greater than he knows.

This original presentation combines two ideas that later become difficult to reconcile. The Force exists independently of Luke. It is not merely confidence or determination renamed. Yet it is experienced through acts of attention, feeling, and trust that are available in ordinary human life. The science fiction invention becomes compelling because the audience recognizes part of the experience even while accepting that the characters are doing something impossible.

The dark side is also introduced before it has detailed definitions. Obi-Wan and Yoda warn Luke about anger and fear, but the story shows what those words can mean. Vader has the capacity to overpower most opponents. He uses it to intimidate, punish, and control. Luke can become more capable, too. The question is whether greater capability will make him resemble the father he fears.

In *The Empire Strikes Back*, Yoda's training on Dagobah makes the Force almost domestic. There are stones, roots, mud, small animals, a crashed spacecraft, a tiny teacher with a stubborn sense of humor. Luke expects greatness to look large and obvious. Yoda's appearance is an argument against that expectation. Frank Oz's performance gives the little puppet amusement, exasperation, and moral seriousness, often within a single exchange. The power in this place does not come dressed like the Emperor.

The famous attempt to lift the X-wing shows Luke's failure of imagination. He thinks of the ship as too big. Yoda does not deny the difficulty, but rejects the assumption that physical scale sets the relevant limit. The effect is magical without becoming a simple claim that anything can happen. Luke's mental habits are restricting what he attempts. He is learning that power requires a different relationship to the world, not merely stronger muscles.

Yet the lesson can be misunderstood if taken in isolation. A person who believes that limits are illusions can become grandiose, and *Star Wars* contains many characters eager to confuse spiritual capacity with entitlement. The films ask us to make a distinction between trust that makes action possible and conviction that makes other people's choices irrelevant. Yoda teaches Luke to act with attention rather than appetite. Palpatine teaches Anakin that sufficient power should make the universe obey his wishes. The two views are not different techniques for the same goal.

## The cave and the father

Dagobah's cave is a small scene with a large afterlife. Luke enters a dark space carrying weapons even after Yoda cautions him about what he brings. He encounters an apparition of Darth Vader, fights it, and sees his own face inside the shattered mask. The scene does not need an exact supernatural rule to work. It makes Luke confront an aspect of himself that a battlefield victory will not remove: the possibility that defeating a monster by becoming like it is another form of defeat.

This is why Luke's confrontation with Vader in *The Empire Strikes Back* is not just a ranking of combat abilities. Vader overwhelms him, but the true defeat is the collapse of Luke's picture of who he is. The revelation of parentage gives his fear a new shape. He wanted to become a Jedi partly because it meant following the example of a good father. He now has to ask whether the father was good, whether he himself can be good, and whether his mentors have told him enough to make either judgment.

There is another warning in the journey to Bespin. Luke senses his friends in danger and leaves training to help them, despite Yoda and Obi-Wan warning that he is not ready. His concern is honorable. He does not invent a crisis out of vanity. Yet the rescue he imagines fails: Leia and the others escape through different efforts, Vader defeats Luke, and Luke himself must be retrieved. The ability to perceive suffering did not tell him what action would end it. Even a genuine vision is not a complete plan.

This is a useful corrective to the idea that the Force simply transmits true instructions to a gifted person. Luke has access to information beyond ordinary sight, but his response remains open to judgment and error. He may have been right to care and wrong about what he could accomplish alone. Yoda may have been right about the danger and insufficiently responsive to the moral pressure of leaving friends behind. The movie allows the conflict to hurt. It does not resolve every question by giving one character a perfect ethical algorithm. When Luke later refuses to obey the Emperor's demand in the throne room, the maturity of that choice becomes clearer. He is not abandoning his friends; he is learning that feeling urgently responsible for someone does not make every immediate action wise.

The teachers' authority becomes part of the story. Obi-Wan told Luke that Vader killed his father. Later he offers a qualified account of what he meant. A listener may sympathize with the motives for concealment while still recognizing how poorly it serves the young man who must bear the truth. The Jedi are not merely repositories of perfect wisdom. They are people who make protective decisions, sometimes by limiting another person's access to information. Their tradition survives through a student who needs to question them.

In *Return of the Jedi*, Luke is strong enough to threaten Vader. When Leia is threatened, his anger becomes the lever by which the Emperor nearly turns him. The film's moral climax arrives after a furious assault. Luke looks at Vader's severed mechanical hand and then at his own gloved hand. The comparison is visual, immediate, and devastating. He can win this duel, but victory of the wrong sort would complete the Emperor's plan. He stops.

That refusal is sometimes summarized as the triumph of pacifism, but the film is not a simple argument that a good person must never fight. Luke has fought throughout the trilogy, and the rebellion is conducting a necessary military operation outside the throne room. His choice is more exact: he will not murder a defeated father to satisfy the Emperor's demand for hatred. He refuses to let his adversary dictate the ethical meaning of his victory.

Vader's final intervention then becomes possible. Seeing his son suffer, he turns against the Emperor. The Force enables the action, but the decisive fact is a choice about another person. He abandons the power structure that has defined him. The father cannot undo what he did to the galaxy. He can perform a final act that is not obedience to the master who has owned his life. The moral weight comes from that change, not from the mechanics of the lightning or the height of the reactor shaft.

## What an Order is for

The prequels change the scale of the Jedi story. Instead of a few survivors improvising instruction, there is a Temple, a Council, rules of admission, ranks, ceremonies, archives, and a role in the affairs of the Republic. The Jedi are admired as guardians of peace. They are also an institution whose members exercise power over others and are expected to restrain themselves.

That structure should not be dismissed as merely a hypocritical mistake. If people possess dangerous abilities, collective training and ethical expectations make sense. An apprentice needs teachers. A mediator needs some independence from personal appetite. Rules against exploiting vulnerable people or taking rewards for intimidation are not unreasonable just because one sympathetic character struggles with discipline. A Jedi code offers a response to the risk that exceptional people will start believing themselves exempt from ordinary obligations.

The prequel Jedi also occupy a troubling political position. They are simultaneously spiritual teachers, investigators, diplomats, and military commanders. The Republic asks them to defend it, yet the institutions they defend are becoming compromised. When war comes, they lead soldiers into battle. That arrangement may be imposed by the emergency, but it changes the meaning of their vocation. People trained to preserve peace are now making tactical decisions that determine who is sent into danger and who will die.

The institutional question is particularly visible in the way the Council considers Anakin. Its members have genuine reasons to fear a child with enormous potential and a difficult attachment. Their measured caution is not sadism. But the Order's response often treats the problem at the level of risk to the institution rather than through the experience of the individual. Anakin learns that his feelings are dangerous, conceals them, and becomes vulnerable to someone who promises unrestricted sympathy. The Sith exploit exactly the pressures that a disciplined Order is meant to resist.

Still, there is a difference between a failure of care and proof that restraint itself is wrong. Many popular arguments about the prequels collapse that distinction. They suggest that the Jedi forbade love and thereby caused everything. The films depict a more complicated tension. Jedi characters routinely show tenderness, loyalty, grief, and courage on behalf of others. Obi-Wan loves Anakin, and the tragedy is unintelligible without that love. Qui-Gon treats the vulnerable with concern even when the Council disagrees. Ahsoka cares deeply for people she is trained to protect. The restriction is not a total absence of affection. It concerns possession, attachment, and responsibilities that might be overridden by private demands.

A person may reasonably question whether the Order expresses that distinction well or enforces it wisely. Anakin's secret marriage suggests that the rule as practiced makes honest discussion difficult. But the alternative cannot simply be that someone with immense power should always do what love tells him. Anakin believes that he is acting for Padmé when he turns on people who would never harm her. His actions show how fear of loss can become a claim of ownership. The ideal of compassionate attachment without possessiveness is hard to live; the danger of abandoning that ideal is real.

## The Sith and the promise of control

The Sith's offer is strikingly different. Their language promises power, the defeat of limitation, and the right to pursue desire without the inhibitions imposed by the Jedi. Anger, fear, and ambition become sources to draw upon rather than tendencies requiring scrutiny. For a person who feels overlooked or constrained, that invitation can sound like liberation.

Palpatine makes the appeal personal. He tells Anakin a story in which forbidden knowledge might overcome death. He praises the young Jedi's abilities and cultivates his resentment toward the Council. His apparent sympathy is always conditional: trust me, assist me, subordinate yourself, and you may acquire the means to achieve what you want. The bargain grows more binding precisely because its promised reward remains beyond reach.

Watch what happens to the Sith once their promises are enacted. Vader seems enormously powerful, yet he lives in fear of the Emperor and carries out orders he might otherwise refuse. Kylo Ren tries to escape the shadow of his family through violent independence, only to become entangled in another hierarchy of command and manipulation. The Sith describe mastery as freedom, then structure their relationships around domination. The apprentice wants the master's power; the master needs the apprentice's strength but fears replacement. There is little room for trust in that arrangement.

The contrast is not between a flawless Jedi Order and a purely irrational Sith ideology. The Sith do offer genuine power, and Jedi institutions can become rigid or arrogant. The essential moral difference is what the power is for. A Jedi ideal asks someone capable of imposing a will to recognize obligations beyond that will. The Sith ideal makes the expansion of the will its highest project. One tradition can fail to live up to its restraint. The other often treats restraint itself as weakness.

This is why Sith triumphs are rarely shown as happy lives. Palpatine enjoys control, but the social world he creates is defined by terror and suspicion. Anakin wants to keep loved ones safe and emerges from his bargain estranged from every sustaining relationship. Even the Emperor's most successful servant cannot be reassured that he is secure. The system cannot offer peace because it needs the fear of loss and the promise of more power to keep its members obedient.

A lightsaber duel can compress this philosophical argument into movement. In the original throne room, the Emperor sits and watches a son and father turn their relationships into weapons. On Mustafar, former friends fight through a landscape that looks as though it is destroying itself. In a later sequel, opponents may fight while being pulled by competing bonds, loyalties, or memories. The choreography is not always philosophically coherent, but the best fights are arguments about how one person can stand in relation to another.

## Ahsoka and the right to walk away

*The Clone Wars*, the computer-animated series launched in 2008, develops these questions by giving Anakin a student. Ahsoka Tano is introduced as young, confident, sometimes impulsive, and willing to challenge her master's assumptions. Their relationship allows the story to show Anakin as a teacher, not only as a future villain. He is patient with some of her mistakes, protective of her, and proud of her growth. We see the person he might have remained under different circumstances.

The series also lets Ahsoka discover that the Jedi Order may not provide the justice it teaches. After a bombing at the Jedi Temple, she is framed and faces prosecution. The Council loses faith in her before the truth is established. When she is cleared and invited to return, she declines. She is not rejecting compassion or defending the dark side. She is refusing to let an institution's apology erase what its process revealed about trust.

The decision is powerful because leaving is neither defeat nor treason. She can remain committed to helping people without treating institutional membership as the measure of her worth. Her departure is also devastating for Anakin, who experiences it through his own fear of abandonment. The same event has different moral meanings for the people inside it. Ahsoka needs freedom to make an honest choice; Anakin wants the relationship preserved. A good response to her would recognize that she is allowed to leave.

Later, during the Siege of Mandalore, Ahsoka works alongside clones who have become friends. When Order 66 reaches them, Captain Rex is forced toward a murderous command by an implanted chip, and Ahsoka struggles to save him. Here the question of a person's freedom is literal. A soldier has been designed to obey against his ordinary loyalties. Ahsoka responds not by declaring him a villain but by trying to restore his capacity to choose. The episode gives a concrete moral meaning to her independence from the institution that failed her. She is not above rules because she is powerful; she is responsible for the lives in front of her.

Ahsoka's story sometimes provokes the easy slogan that the Jedi were always wrong. The series supports a more specific conclusion. The Order's purposes can be admirable while its disciplinary and political practices are fallible. A person can preserve the ethical insight of a tradition while declining the authority of the organization that claims to embody it. That distinction opens possibilities that the vanished Order alone could not provide.

## Learning after the school has burned

*Star Wars Rebels* offers a different experiment. Kanan Jarrus survived the destruction of the Jedi and spent years concealing his identity. He becomes a teacher to Ezra Bridger, an orphaned teenager living under Imperial rule. Kanan is neither an all-knowing master nor an ordinary adventurer with a lightsaber. He is someone who needs to teach a tradition he never had the chance to complete learning himself.

This changes how they learn together. Kanan cannot resolve every problem by citing the lost Council or consulting an archive. He must learn alongside Ezra, admit errors, and adapt instruction to a world in which survival itself is a daily challenge. The Force becomes less like an authorized curriculum and more like a practice sustained by imperfect people trying to live responsibly.

Ezra is tempted by power and shortcut solutions. He encounters figures who encourage him to believe that fear and anger can be put to useful work. Those temptations are not foolish because he is young. He has seen what the Empire does to ordinary people. Sometimes anger is a truthful response to harm. The story's challenge is to distinguish an honest reaction from a reliable guide to action. Anger can tell Ezra that something is wrong; it cannot by itself tell him what he should do.

Kanan's final sacrifice protects the people he loves. Later, Ezra confronts an apparent opportunity to undo that loss through a mysterious realm associated with the Force. The choice not to seize every possible rescue is a painful development of the lesson Anakin failed to learn. Ezra must distinguish between the longing to bring someone back and the responsibility to respect the consequences of the person's chosen sacrifice. The series does not pretend that accepting loss feels good. It lets the refusal to undo it become a form of love.

The Ghost's crew also complicates the notion that Force users are the only significant moral actors. Hera is a skilled pilot and organizer, Sabine an artist and fighter, Zeb a survivor of a targeted people, and Chopper a temperamental machine whose loyalty is not always expressed politely. Their work sustains the Jedi characters and gives their extraordinary abilities a social context. If everyone around a Jedi becomes background furniture, the moral problem of power disappears. *Rebels* repeatedly reminds us that a gifted person belongs to a community of people whose contributions are not measured by the same powers.

## The Force changes when its mythology grows

As the franchise expanded, it supplied new explanations: biological sensitivity to the Force, inherited potential, elaborate techniques, ancient traditions, contested prophecies, and places where the boundaries of ordinary space and time seem less stable. The concept of midi-chlorians in *The Phantom Menace* is often singled out as though it replaced every earlier spiritual mystery with a blood test. The film actually presents a biological indicator of connection, not a complete explanation of the Force's origin or moral meaning. Even so, the emphasis changes what the audience notices.

In the 1977 film, the excitement lies in discovering that ordinary perception is incomplete. In a story centered on measuring sensitivity and selecting children for training, the audience naturally starts asking who qualifies and why. This can enrich the fictional world by turning the Jedi into a historically situated organization with policies, mistakes, and consequences. It can also narrow the original democratic feeling that a person might learn to perceive more than they first knew.

Later films explore that tension through lineage. The Skywalkers are a family in which extraordinary power appears repeatedly, and their actions reshape the galaxy. The dramatic inheritance of the Force risks becoming an argument that a small group of bloodlines are the only people who can decide history. That is why the story sometimes needs characters who resist the pedigree plot: people who fight the Empire with no supernatural assistance, students whose teachers are incomplete, and heroes who choose relationships rather than genealogical destinies.

None of this requires deciding which fictional explanation is the one true metaphysics. Different installments do different work. A movie can treat the Force as an almost wordless invitation to trust; a prequel can explore the hazards of regulating extraordinary talent; an animated series can ask what survives when the training institution disappears; and a sequel can question what a future student owes to the damaged reputation of the past. The challenge for the storyteller is to make the rules serve the people, not to reduce every dramatic conflict to whether an ability is allowed by a previously published diagram.

## Luke's exile and the dangerous idea of a perfect teacher

*The Last Jedi* brings the institutional problem back to Luke himself. By this point, he is an old teacher who has failed in a disastrous way with his nephew, Ben Solo. A moment of fear about what Ben might become has shattered their trust. Luke retreats to an island, convinced that the legend of the Jedi may do more harm than good. Rey arrives expecting a master who will restore the world by explaining it. Instead she finds someone who distrusts his own place in that world.

The decision to depict Luke this way divided viewers, and the reasons for disagreement are comprehensible. The Luke who refused to murder Vader seems, to some, unlikely to have reacted with such fear toward his nephew. Others see the moment as a credible flash of weakness in a person who never became immune to terror simply by doing one magnificent thing in youth. The film's real interest lies in what Luke does with his failure. He makes it into a general verdict: because he failed as a teacher, perhaps the Jedi ought to end.

That logic is another kind of overreach. A legendary person's guilt can become so large that he denies others the right to attempt something different. Rey is not obliged to inherit his confidence or his despair. The possibility of learning from failure lies between pretending the old tradition was perfect and deciding it had nothing worth preserving.

The climactic projection on Crait is a striking response. Luke confronts the First Order without using his power to kill its soldiers. He protects the retreat, buys time for others, and allows the image of himself to carry hope rather than punishment. The action does not undo what happened with Ben. It shows Luke choosing a different use of his abilities in the time he still has. The legendary fighter becomes most powerful by refusing the obvious fight.

Rey's story continues to negotiate inheritance, especially as the later film associates her with Palpatine's bloodline and with the symbolic name Skywalker. The trilogy's approaches to lineage do not fit together without friction. Yet Rey's central moral action remains recognizable: she is not bound to become the person a predecessor's name or blood would seem to demand. Her identity is made through the loyalties she chooses. Whether every twist of the sequel narrative earns that conclusion is a fair question; the question itself belongs to the long conversation *Star Wars* has been conducting about destiny.

## Love, attachment, and the words that cause fights

One persistent source of misunderstanding is the word *attachment*. In ordinary speech, it can simply mean a caring bond. In the Jedi stories, it often means an inability to let another person be separate, mortal, or free. Characters are expected to love and protect others; they are warned against letting fear of loss turn that love into possession. The distinction is harder to dramatize than a simple prohibition and the franchise does not always communicate it consistently.

Consider three responses to possible loss. Anakin fears Padmé's death, seeks an extraordinary means of preventing it, and eventually destroys the possibility of their life together. Luke fears losing his father to evil but refuses to kill him in anger; he risks his own life on the chance that Vader can choose differently. Ezra longs to restore Kanan but must accept that doing so would betray the very sacrifice he seeks to honor. These stories do not advocate indifference to loved ones. They argue that love cannot grant unlimited authority over another person's fate.

The same distinction explains why a purely technical description of the Force is unsatisfying. A diagram might tell us who can move an object at a distance. It cannot tell us whether moving it would be an act of rescue, coercion, revenge, or vanity. Those judgments require attention to the people involved. The most consequential uses of the Force often happen at a scale smaller than a starship: a son refusing to execute his father, a student leaving an Order, a teacher admitting that the student needs a future he cannot control.

We should also be careful about drawing a straight line from the Force to a single real-world religious tradition. Its presentation borrows broadly from ideas associated with spiritual discipline, interdependence, contemplation, and the struggle with desire, but the films combine those ideas with western adventure, fairy tale, samurai cinema, science fiction, and family melodrama. It is a fictional moral language shaped for dramatic purposes. Its internal tensions are part of the storytelling, not a faithful exposition of one theology that audiences have somehow failed to memorize.

The Force feels largest when it cannot be captured by an inventory of doctrines. Its strangeness opens a space for characters to confront habits that ordinary military calculation will not solve. But spiritual talk can also become an excuse for ignoring real obligations. A Council that tells someone to accept loss still has to care for that person. A master who advocates restraint must still act when others are in danger. A gifted student's insight is not proof of moral maturity. The stories become richer when these gaps are acknowledged rather than hidden behind glowing blades.

## The people who cannot lift the ship

Imagine the universe of *Star Wars* if every meaningful decision were made by Jedi or Sith. The rebellion would be a dispute among a few families with extras providing transport. Yet the movies and series repeatedly deny that picture. Leia organizes and leads without needing to throw anyone across a room. Han must learn to treat his friends as more than a temporary business arrangement. Mon Mothma works through political danger, secrecy, and compromise. Cassian Andor's resistance is shaped by ordinary people and by the costs imposed on them. Hera and her crew keep one another alive through skills, arguments, and persistence.

These characters do not possess a lesser version of moral agency because they lack supernatural talents. Often they encounter the consequences of Force users' decisions more directly than the users themselves. An Empire formed in a Sith victory is lived by people who have no access to its esoteric struggle. The destruction of Alderaan is not less terrible because the victims cannot intervene in the Emperor's metaphysics. The ability to choose, to organize, to refuse an unjust order, or to care for a neighbor belongs to ordinary people, too.

This brings us back to the distinction between power and its use. In a superhero story, the answer to tyranny may be an even stronger champion. *Star Wars* sometimes indulges that expectation, and the spectacle can be wonderful. But its enduring scenes often upset it. Luke wins his most important confrontation by putting his weapon away. Ahsoka preserves her ethics by walking away from an institution that claims authority over them. Kanan teaches without having become the master he expected to be. Leia and the rebels must carry on regardless of which ancient teachers approve.

The Force is therefore most useful not as a guarantee of right action but as a magnifier of moral choice. It gives people enough power that their desires can become events. The person who wants to control another can make that desire terrifyingly physical. The person who wants to protect another can take risks impossible by ordinary means. Neither desire is self-justifying. More power makes the need for judgment greater, not smaller.

## An ending without a final rule

The Jedi are not at their best when they insist that they have never made mistakes. The Sith are not convincing because they have a secret source of happiness; their promise of mastery repeatedly creates new forms of dependence. A teacher may be wise and wrong. A student may be angry and still have reason to criticize the teacher. An institution may preserve valuable knowledge while failing the people it was created to serve.

A fictional mythology that admits these possibilities has room to grow. It can tell stories about building and abandoning institutions, about inheriting mistakes without repeating them, and about the difference between a power that can save someone and a power that can make another person's life belong to you. It can also tell the simpler, essential story of a frightened young person discovering a capacity they do not know how to use.

The first time Luke puts on the training helmet, the goal seems modest: feel what cannot be seen, and avoid the next bolt. We know that he will eventually be asked to confront an Emperor, reject an easy victory, and trust the possibility of another person's change. The distance between those moments is the moral education of *Star Wars*. Not the accumulation of abilities, but a slowly earned ability to decide which actions his power does not entitle him to take.

The old teacher has given the boy a weapon. More importantly, he has begun teaching him that possessing one does not settle what he should do.
