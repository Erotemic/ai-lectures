# 007000 — After Endor: Sequels and the Weight of Inheritance

**Track:** Star Wars: A Galaxy Worth Arguing About. **Audience:** General listeners and Star Wars fans. **Prerequisites:** None. **Scope:** The New Republic after *Return of the Jedi*, *The Force Awakens* (2015), *The Last Jedi* (2017), *The Rise of Skywalker* (2019), with selected reference to *The Mandalorian* and *Ahsoka*. **Spoilers:** All major events of the sequel trilogy, including Han's death, Luke's final act, Rey's ancestry, Ben Solo's ending, and the fate of the New Republic; limited political and character spoilers for New Republic-era television.

# After Endor: Sequels and the Weight of Inheritance

What happens the morning after the Empire falls?

The ending of *Return of the Jedi* does not ask. There are celebrations, embraces, music, and the relief of people who have escaped a government capable of destroying planets. The Emperor is dead, Darth Vader has turned against him, the second Death Star has exploded, and the camera moves from one joyful gathering to another. The victory is real. We can watch it without needing anyone to explain the next constitutional convention.

But an ending in a story is not necessarily an ending in history. The surviving Imperial officers have ships, money, weapons, habits, and allies. The people who fought together to destroy the Empire do not necessarily agree on how to govern the peace. The woman who led a rebellion may be less persuasive to a legislature than she was to soldiers risking their lives. The child who grows up hearing about Luke Skywalker will inherit both an inspiration and an impossible comparison.

The sequel trilogy begins with this problem, though it does not always explore the problem as deeply as its premise deserves. *The Force Awakens*, released in 2015, brings the old story's images back into view: a desert, a masked tyrant, a young person discovering the Force, a droid carrying something important, a battered freighter that moves as though the galaxy itself has missed it. *The Last Jedi* asks whether those images have become traps. *The Rise of Skywalker* tries to close a family saga while carrying the expectation that every beloved image must find its final meaning.

The resulting films are not one smooth argument. They contain compelling performances, scenes of striking imagination, thinly developed institutions, and choices that seem to pull against one another. They are nevertheless worth examining as a single story about inheritance. These people have been handed victories, names, weapons, fears, and myths. None of that tells them what they ought to do with their lives.

## A Republic is more difficult than a rebellion

The original trilogy turns rebellion into a dramatic engine. An oppressive empire exists; a relatively small movement resists it. The practical questions of funding, logistics, secrecy, and political compromise appear when the drama needs them, but the moral direction is clear. Destroying the Death Star is an excellent ending because there is no doubt that the Death Star should be destroyed.

Governing is different. It requires people to disagree without becoming enemies. It requires an authority to make decisions while remaining subject to correction. After Endor, the New Republic restores a Senate and sharply reduces the military that the war had made necessary. Its leaders do not wish to reproduce the Empire's permanent war footing. This is a reasonable fear. A galaxy that has been ruled by the threat of military force has reason to distrust an enormous centralized army.

At the same time, surviving Imperial networks regroup. Eventually the First Order emerges from remnants and hardliners, equipped with an ideology that treats the Empire's defeat as an insult to be avenged. Leia Organa sees the threat and creates a Resistance because the Republic will not act decisively. Official Star Wars background material describes a political system in which many leaders regard Leia's warnings as the fixation of a veteran who cannot stop fighting the old war.

There is a painful possibility here: the people who build a peaceful society may fear the habits of the very people who kept it alive. Leia's authority on the battlefield does not automatically become authority in a Senate. That is not, by itself, proof that the Senate has failed. Democracies are not meant to hand permanent power to war heroes. The failure comes when institutions confuse skepticism of militarism with a refusal to recognize an actual military danger.

The films, unfortunately, give us relatively little of that public life. In *The Force Awakens*, Starkiller Base destroys the Hosnian system, where the Senate is sitting, in one spectacular attack. Viewers understand that millions are being murdered and a government is being decapitated. But many viewers have barely met that government. The Republic is a shadow behind the Resistance, then a burst of light across the sky. It is hard to mourn the collapse of an institution that the story has offered almost no time to know.

This is more than an objection about missing exposition. The audience is being asked to accept the transformation of the victorious Rebel Alliance into another desperate band of outmatched fighters. That familiar dramatic arrangement gives the new films instant energy, but it also withdraws the most intriguing reward of the earlier story: the possibility of seeing what the heroes accomplished after winning. By arranging the galaxy to resemble the one in 1977, the sequels risk making the earlier victory feel temporary for reasons that occur offscreen.

New Republic-era television returns to this interval from another angle. In *The Mandalorian*, bounty hunters, isolated communities, former Imperial personnel, and overstretched officials occupy spaces where formal authority is thin. The show works best when it allows the political transition to be visible in a small job: an officer with incomplete information, a settlement with no reliable protector, or a former enemy adapting to a new uniform. *Ahsoka* shows leaders reluctant to credit reports of a renewed Imperial threat. These stories do not fully reconstruct galactic politics, but they make one observation compelling: the end of a war does not distribute peace evenly.

## The missing face of victory

The destruction of Hosnian Prime exposes a difference between a compelling dramatic shorthand and a fully developed political world. The film tells us the Republic has fallen. The camera has shown us its capital only briefly. This enormous loss occurs less as the end of a society we have come to know than as a demonstration that the First Order possesses a weapon worse than the last one.

Compare that with Alderaan in the 1977 film. We also spend almost no time there before its destruction. Why does that loss register so strongly? Partly because the first film makes the absence a personal fact. Leia must endure the murder of her home and family in front of a man who knows exactly what that suffering will mean to her. Obi-Wan feels the catastrophe from far away. The destroyed world has a human witness whose grief continues. Alderaan does not need a lengthy political introduction to be legible as a victim.

Hosnian Prime has public significance, but the sequel films give it few surviving witnesses whose personal and political lives we then follow. Leia is devastated by the larger war, yet she is not shown struggling with the destruction of institutions she helped create in the way she is shown grieving individuals. A viewer can intellectually understand the stakes while experiencing them as another escalation of weapon size. More megatons do not automatically create deeper emotion.

What might have changed had the Senate been present as a functioning, fallible organization for even a few scenes? We could have seen people refusing Leia's requests for good reasons as well as bad ones. A representative might have warned against renewed military centralization because their world had been exploited by the Empire. Another might have discovered that an Imperial network was rearming while others dismissed the evidence. These are not missing facts that a reference book must supply. They are missing dramatic relationships. Without them, the Republic is chiefly the thing that has to disappear so the old form of rebellion can return.

The New Republic-era television stories offer partial remedies by showing the bureaucracy that succeeds the war, but they are not substitutes for the films' own choices. A sequel should be comprehensible as its own drama. When the audience is asked to grieve a political achievement that the story has hardly allowed it to experience, continuity information can explain the loss but cannot retroactively make it felt.

This is the particular challenge of following a triumphant ending. It is easy to invent a new enemy. It is harder to show that the old heroes accomplished something lasting even while the world remains capable of new evil. A mature continuation need not protect the original victory from all failure. It does need to make the meaning of that victory visible before taking it away.

## Finn takes off the helmet

The strongest new beginning in *The Force Awakens* is not a lightsaber being picked up. It is a stormtrooper putting down a weapon.

Finn begins the film as FN-2187, a young soldier raised and conditioned by the First Order. During a raid on Jakku, he sees violence that he cannot make himself treat as routine. A comrade dies. Civilians are killed. He freezes instead of firing. The decision looks small because it is a refusal, not an attack, but in the world that made him, refusing is the most dangerous choice he can make.

The camera gives him a face only after establishing that a stormtrooper might possess a conscience. His helmet comes off. Underneath is John Boyega, playing fear, quick thinking, embarrassment, and a stubborn refusal to return. His performance is alive with movement. He is not a serene hero who knew his destiny from childhood. He is someone trying to escape a system while discovering what kind of person he is without it.

His next actions are wonderfully compromised. He helps Poe Dameron escape because Poe can fly. He lies about belonging to the Resistance because he wants Rey to trust him. He is attracted to the possibility of leaving the war entirely. He also keeps finding himself in situations where another person needs help. His heroism is made from these imperfect decisions, not from a secret royal heritage or a mystical bloodline.

Poe names him Finn, replacing a serial designation with something a friend might call him. This is an affectionate scene, but it also suggests the direction of an entire trilogy that was available for exploration. What becomes of people the First Order has trained to see themselves as equipment? How can they rebel without simply turning into the opposing army's weapons? What could a liberated stormtrooper say to the soldiers still inside the helmets?

The later films do not fully pursue that possibility. *The Last Jedi* pairs Finn with Rose Tico, a Resistance mechanic who challenges his instinct to measure loyalty only by his affection for particular friends. At Canto Bight, she points to the exploitation and war profiteering that finance the magnificent casino's wealth. The film wants Finn to understand that resisting the First Order is not identical with rescuing Rey. There is a world of people he has not met who need protection, too.

The idea is strong. The execution is less consistent. The casino sequence illustrates systems of exploitation through brief encounters, then turns into a chase that leaves little time for the people being exploited. Finn's growth becomes attached to an instructional excursion when his original experience as a soldier already contained material for an equally searching study of power.

In *The Rise of Skywalker*, he meets Jannah, another former First Order stormtrooper. The moment supplies proof that his conscience was not a singular miracle. Other soldiers resisted. But this potentially transformative development remains peripheral to a film busy settling dynastic mysteries. Finn fights courageously and helps win the battle, yet his experience of leaving an institution built to erase his individuality never becomes the trilogy's central political story. John Boyega's performance continues to suggest more possibilities than the narrative gives him.

## Rey and the question of being nobody

Rey begins on Jakku among scavenged wreckage, stripping useful parts from machinery abandoned by other people's wars. The setting is familiar enough to evoke Luke on Tatooine, but Daisy Ridley gives her a different kind of loneliness. Luke wants to escape a family and farm he knows. Rey refuses to leave the place where she imagines her family might return.

There is a cruel asymmetry in that hope. Rey can survive in a brutal environment. She negotiates, fixes equipment, fights, and improvises under pressure. But she cannot force absent people to come home. She has become competent at nearly everything except accepting that the waiting itself has become a kind of prison.

Her discovery of the Force is not simply a reward for courage. It confronts her with a larger identity before she has resolved her first and most private question: who is she, if the people she is waiting for never come back? In *The Force Awakens*, Maz Kanata urges her to look ahead rather than remain in the past. The lightsaber calls to her, and she resists because a destiny is no substitute for the ordinary relationship she has wanted.

*The Last Jedi* presses further. Rey seeks out Luke expecting the famous hero to tell her what her power means. Instead she meets a man who has rejected the narrative that makes him a legend. Kylo Ren offers another interpretation: the longing to belong can be exploited by anyone who promises to give you a place. He seeks to make Rey dependent on him by claiming that her parents were insignificant and that nobody else will understand her.

The most interesting part of that exchange is not a DNA result. It is the question of whether a person can accept being ordinary while still choosing an extraordinary responsibility. Rey wants a family, a teacher, an explanation. The film asks whether she can continue without all three. Her decision to reject Kylo's offer is meaningful because the offer is not merely a villain demanding obedience. It comes wrapped in an account of her pain that has an element of truth, and in the possibility that somebody who knows loneliness might finally share hers.

*The Rise of Skywalker* changes the factual terms by making Rey Palpatine's granddaughter. That revelation need not abolish the earlier argument. A person descended from a murderer is not obliged to become one; a family name is not a moral sentence. But it changes the pressure of the narrative. The film returns to heredity as a source of cosmic significance immediately after its predecessor had found drama in the possibility that lineage was irrelevant.

Rey is now asked to reject a terrible biological inheritance and choose a different name. The final decision is intelligible and can be moving: she names herself Skywalker because her chosen bonds, not the Emperor's ancestry, define her. Yet the trilogy has traveled a circuitous route to reach that point. It risks making a name feel like the prize for proving that one already belongs to an established family of legends. There was also a bolder conclusion available: Rey could have kept her own name, or made one, without permission from a famous house.

That is not an argument that one ending must be correct. It is an account of what the films trade away when they tie a young person's identity so tightly to an older generation's saga.

## Kylo Ren wants to kill the past and keep its costume

Ben Solo is the son of Han and Leia and the nephew of Luke. In another franchise, that ancestry could have been shorthand for natural virtue. Star Wars gives him an inheritance he experiences as suffocating. He is powerful, angry, ashamed, and unable to stop performing the image of Darth Vader.

Adam Driver gives Kylo a physical insecurity beneath the mask. When he takes it off, the terrifying voice disappears and the face is younger, more exposed, less certain. His anger arrives in bursts that suggest someone watching himself fail to become the person he has decided he should be. The mask is not only a threat to others. It is an attempt to convince himself.

The idea that he worships Vader is especially revealing when placed beside the ending of *Return of the Jedi*. Ben idolizes the part of his grandfather that the grandfather ultimately rejected. He knows the icon without accepting the conclusion of the story. The tragedy of inheritance is not merely that the past weighs upon him; it is that he has chosen the wrong fragment of the past to carry.

When Han walks onto the bridge at Starkiller Base, the film turns a planetary superweapon's machinery into a family stage. Han does not have a strategy capable of stopping the First Order. He approaches his son as a father hoping one personal relationship can interrupt a larger catastrophe. Kylo kills him, apparently trying to sever the last claim the family has on his identity. The scene is devastating because Harrison Ford and Driver allow the hesitation to last. The action is not a triumphant villain's announcement. It is an atrocity committed by a person who is still trying to talk himself into believing it will make him strong.

In *The Last Jedi*, Kylo kills Snoke instead of Rey, and the film briefly opens a possibility that he might reject both the old Sith-like hierarchy and the Resistance's picture of him. Yet he does not propose freedom. He proposes ruling together. He wants to destroy the past so that nothing can challenge his right to define the future. His rebellion against authority becomes a bid to inherit it.

The throne-room fight works because choreography and performance place this contradiction into motion. Rey and Kylo fight side by side against Snoke's guards, with no certainty that they agree about what victory means. Their partnership is real for those minutes. When the room is cleared, the moral distance between them becomes visible again. Shared opposition to a tyrant has not created a shared idea of justice.

The final film turns Ben back toward the light, first through Leia's intervention and then through Rey's mercy and the memory of Han. This can satisfy an emotional pattern established decades before: someone who has committed evil is not beyond choosing differently. Yet the repetition invites a hard question. Redemption need not mean absolution. Ben's sacrifice to save Rey is decisive, but the families and communities harmed by his earlier actions do not get to reckon with a living, repentant Ben Solo. Death provides the saga with a clean ending to a much messier human problem.

There are viewers for whom that conclusion feels like grace and viewers for whom it feels like a shortcut. The film gives reason for both responses. A final act can be sincere while leaving most of a lifetime's damage unrepaired.

## The dangerous appeal of being understood

Rey and Kylo Ren share some of the sequel trilogy's most intimate scenes without occupying the same room. Their Force connection makes it possible for one to intrude into the other's private space, to see an ordinary gesture, and to speak when neither can easily reach anyone else. The technique is visually simple. Instead of a vast cosmic effect, the film uses edits, eyelines, and performance to place two isolated people in apparent proximity. For a story filled with huge fleets and ancestral expectations, this is a startlingly small scale on which to represent supernatural power.

The connection permits honesty of a sort. They are less able to use the comfortable public roles that protect them from other people's judgment. Rey sees a man who is ashamed and furious. Kylo sees someone whose confident improvisation masks a profound fear of abandonment. That recognition is attractive to both of them. It would be simplistic to deny the tenderness of certain exchanges simply because Kylo is dangerous.

It would be equally simplistic to mistake that tenderness for a relationship based on trust. Kylo repeatedly uses private knowledge as leverage. He has committed violence and asks Rey to find in his loneliness a reason to join him. The film understands that recognizing somebody's wound does not require accepting their proposed cure. A person may be persuasive precisely because they tell a truth while making a destructive demand.

Later, *The Rise of Skywalker* describes their connection as a dyad in the Force, making the bond part of the story's supernatural structure. This gives their intimacy cosmic force, but supernatural explanation cannot perform the moral work of a relationship. The important action is not that a mystical bond exists. It is that Rey refuses to become Kylo's partner in domination and that Ben finally acts without trying to possess her. Whatever one makes of the films' romantic conclusion, that distinction is essential: understanding a person is not the same as owing them your freedom.

## Luke Skywalker and the disappointment of meeting a legend

Luke's presence in *The Force Awakens* is almost entirely absence. The galaxy talks about him; Han remembers him; Rey seeks him; the film ends when she finds him. The choice makes Luke an object of expectation. By the time the next film begins, both Rey and the audience have imagined the moment when a hero reappears and takes responsibility.

Rian Johnson's *The Last Jedi* deliberately makes that encounter uncomfortable. Luke takes the lightsaber and throws it away. A comic gesture rejects the solemnity of the previous film's ending. He has retreated after failing with Ben Solo and believes the Jedi's claim to special authority has contributed to disaster. His life is not a secret training montage; it is an exile in which shame has become a philosophy.

The most difficult revelation is that Luke briefly considered using violence against his sleeping nephew when he sensed the darkness growing in him. The film tells the incident from conflicting perspectives before presenting a fuller account. Ben remembers a master who came to murder him. Luke remembers an instant of fearful impulse that he did not carry out. The difference between intending, almost acting, and being seen with an ignited lightsaber does not make the event harmless. It makes the relationship's collapse tragically plausible.

Some viewers reject this Luke because he seems incompatible with the young man who refused to kill Vader. The objection identifies a genuine pressure point. Luke's earlier triumph came from seeing goodness in a father whom almost everyone else considered lost. Why would he be tempted to strike a nephew whose fall had not been completed? The film's answer is not that Luke stopped believing in redemption forever. It is that a person who has acted courageously once can still be frightened into a terrible moment years later. That answer can be psychologically compelling without resolving every viewer's sense that a beloved character has been moved too abruptly from one state to another.

Mark Hamill's performance carries the tension. He plays bitterness without turning Luke into a stranger. There is still impatience, wit, kindness, and a longing to belong to the people he has abandoned. When Yoda appears, the lesson is not that ancient books are worthless or that teachers should never teach. It is that failure is part of what teachers must pass on honestly. Luke's myth has become too heavy for him to inhabit; Yoda suggests that the next generation does not need a flawless monument.

Luke's final action on Crait brings the argument into a single astonishing image. He stands alone before the First Order's walkers, apparently facing impossible fire. He meets Kylo without trying to kill him. The spectacle draws the enemy's attention while the Resistance escapes. Only afterward does the film reveal that Luke has projected himself across the galaxy through the Force.

He has become the legendary hero the galaxy needs, but not by returning to the old fantasy of overpowering every enemy. He wins time. He protects lives. He allows others to continue. The surviving Resistance is so small that the victory is almost absurd measured in military terms; measured in the ability of a movement to survive, it is everything. Luke dies after this extraordinary exertion, and children elsewhere retell the story. The legend he feared has been remade by an act of mercy rather than domination.

## Leia, the adult in the room

The sequel trilogy's greatest missed political opportunity belongs to Leia. Carrie Fisher returns not as a princess awaiting a rebellion but as a general who has already built one and must keep it alive. She has survived the Empire, a difficult peace, the estrangement of her son, and the collapse of her marriage. Her private losses have not persuaded her that other people are disposable.

Leia's resistance is neither glamorous nor pure. In *The Last Jedi* she must choose which ships and personnel can be saved. She argues with Poe, whose aggressive attack on a dreadnought succeeds tactically while costing precious bomber crews. Later, Poe distrusts Vice Admiral Holdo's secrecy and attempts a mutiny. The film wants to distinguish courage from impulsive heroism, asking whether a talented fighter can learn the patience needed to preserve a movement.

The argument is strong, but its staging invites reasonable disagreement. Holdo withholds information from subordinates in a crisis, and the film supplies insufficient context for some of that distrust. Poe's misconduct is real; so is the leadership problem created when desperate people believe they have no intelligible plan. The interesting reading is not simply that Poe is wrong and the admirals are right. It is that a democratic resistance still needs accountable command, trust earned rather than presumed, and people willing to accept that not every dazzling attack is a worthwhile victory.

Carrie Fisher died in 2016, after completing her work for *The Last Jedi*. *The Rise of Skywalker* constructed Leia's remaining appearances using previously unused footage, a production fact that affects what the character can say and do. The film grants Leia a final effort to reach her son and gives her death the weight of that sacrifice. But the loss of Fisher also deprived the trilogy of a living performer capable of developing Leia's political intelligence through the final chapter.

That loss is especially painful because Leia represents a different model of inheritance from either Luke or Ben. She carries the history of Alderaan and the knowledge of who her father was. She does not need to resolve these facts by retreating from public life. She continues to organize, judge, and comfort people whose work will outlast her. The films often surround her with much more spectacular powers; her distinctive gift remains the ability to keep other people acting together.

## Han, Poe, and the costs of going back

Han Solo presents the sequel trilogy with another problem of inheritance. In the original films he moves from the man who thinks only about his own survival to someone willing to return when others need him. By *The Force Awakens*, he has lost his son and become a smuggler again, separated from Leia and moving among the same criminal dangers he had seemed to outgrow. The return is dramatically useful: Harrison Ford knows how to make the old evasions funny, and the audience knows the Falcon before Rey and Finn do.

It is also sad. Han's earlier transformation has not been permanently reversed, but the film shows him retreating to a role in which the stakes are familiar and the emotional obligations can be evaded. This could have become a profound portrait of how people respond to family catastrophe: the brave person who can risk his life for a friend but finds it harder to remain in a home where he failed to protect his child. The film gives us only fragments of the intervening years, yet Ford's performance makes the avoidance recognizable. He has gone back to a job he understands because he does not understand how to be Ben's father anymore.

When he finally approaches Kylo on Starkiller Base, the act is not simply repeating the original trilogy's ideal of a scoundrel discovering courage. Han chooses a danger that cannot be escaped by acceleration, a blaster, or a clever lie. He approaches an intimate moral disaster without knowing whether it can be repaired. The scene allows an older hero a different sort of bravery, even if the film's structure has been eager to return him to his earlier costume.

Poe Dameron's growth runs in the opposite direction. Oscar Isaac initially plays him as the pilot who believes every problem should be met with superior flying and confidence. Those qualities are delightful in an opening escape, but they cannot administer a threatened movement. A soldier who succeeds by taking spectacular risks may become dangerous when responsible for other people's lives. *The Last Jedi* makes his reckless attack costly; *The Rise of Skywalker* places him among the people who must coordinate a larger force instead of behaving as though one ace can win the war.

That development is incomplete. The films rarely spend enough time showing the practical intelligence required of leaders who operate without Leia's experience. Yet Poe's embarrassment and impatience make the problem visible. He wants the moral clarity that comes from being in the cockpit. Leadership requires him to endure the discomfort of people disagreeing, waiting, and sometimes refusing to tell him what he wants to hear.

Han and Poe therefore describe two different ways a hero can mistake an old competence for a complete identity. Han returns to the dangerous life he understands after domestic loss. Poe seeks the cockpit where his skills will always seem decisive. Both must confront circumstances where being brave is not the same as being useful. Their stories could have formed a richer conversation about how a rebellion matures if the trilogy had given the institutions around them greater life.

## A story made from competing instincts

The trilogy is sometimes reduced to a quarrel among its filmmakers. J. J. Abrams directed *The Force Awakens* and *The Rise of Skywalker*; Rian Johnson wrote and directed *The Last Jedi*. The films make different choices about nostalgia, mystery, family, and the status of their predecessors. That difference is visible on screen without needing to invent a private production conspiracy.

*The Force Awakens* excels at introducing people through action. Rey races to protect BB-8; Finn lies badly and then learns to be brave; Poe acts as though friendship can be established in seconds; Han and Chewbacca step onto the Falcon like returning musicians finding an old rhythm. Its pace creates pleasure, and its practical textures reconnect a generation of viewers to a particular cinematic language. It also rebuilds the old story's political arrangement so thoroughly that the question of what changed after Endor gets pushed aside.

*The Last Jedi* often prefers contradiction to reassurance. It offers a heroic admiral whose silence invites revolt, a cynical codebreaker who points out war's profits while betraying his clients, a legend who has ceased believing in himself, and a villain who rejects his master only to seek the throne. Some jokes interrupt scenes of peril sharply enough to weaken their weight. The Canto Bight detour diffuses momentum, and several plot devices need more convincing support. But the film consistently asks the audience to distinguish the surface of heroism from its moral substance. Its brightest image is not a glorious assault. It is the survival of people who almost did not escape.

*The Rise of Skywalker* has a different urgency. It races to resolve inheritances, find ancient objects, restore the Emperor, reunite old characters, reconcile Rey and Ben, and bring a massive fleet to battle. There are affecting moments: C-3PO's vulnerability, Chewbacca's grief, Rey's fear of becoming what she despises, and Ben's silent choice to help. There is also a sense that every unresolved uncertainty must be answered by a new family revelation or an ancient artifact. The resurrected Palpatine gives the saga a familiar final antagonist, but at a cost. The previous film's killing of Snoke had opened the possibility that the new generation would face a conflict of its own making. Restoring Palpatine moves responsibility back to the old family's original enemy.

The resulting trilogy contains more than one coherent film, but not always one coherent plan for the nine-film saga. That is a criticism of the works, not an assertion about what anybody privately intended or knew. The scenes themselves show the shifts: Rey's ancestry is recast, Kylo's independence becomes a new servitude, and a rebellion that sought to rebuild public life ends by destroying another planet-scale threat. A viewer can love many of these scenes and still wish that the wider political consequences had received the same attention as the family mysteries.

## The last name at the last homestead

The saga ends with Rey at the Lars homestead on Tatooine, burying the lightsabers of Luke and Leia and choosing the name Skywalker. The scene is designed for recognition. We remember the double sunset of 1977, Luke looking at a horizon that seemed to contain a future, and the farm that he wanted to leave. Now the camera returns with someone who has crossed the galaxy and survived a battle against the family's oldest enemy.

The gesture is tender, but it is not beyond question. Why does the continuation of Star Wars need to return to this particular sand? What becomes of Finn's interrupted political future, Poe's work of rebuilding an alliance, or the people who might finally inhabit a democratic Republic? An ending at a family grave closes one kind of story while leaving the larger history almost untouched.

Perhaps the sequel trilogy's central tension can be heard in those two possible endings. One is the ending of a family myth: a child who had no home chooses a name she can love. The other is the ending of a rebellion: a galaxy finally begins the difficult labor of governing itself. The films accomplish more of the first than the second.

That does not render the sequels empty. Finn's refusal is still a powerful moral act. Luke's nonviolent stand on Crait still turns a symbol of strength into a method of protection. Leia's perseverance still honors the labor needed to keep a movement alive. Rey still has to decide what she will do with a power she did not ask to inherit. Ben's turn still demonstrates that a person who has done harm can make a final choice not to keep doing it.

The older trilogy showed young heroes overthrowing an Empire. The newer one lives among its aftermaths, often reaching for the reassuring shape of the older adventure when the aftermath becomes complicated. Its strengths and weaknesses come from the same impulse: a desire to make Star Wars feel like Star Wars while asking whether the next generation can become something else.

A legacy is valuable, but it cannot do the living for the people who receive it. That may be the strongest idea these films offer, sometimes deliberately and sometimes in the distance between what they promise and what they finally manage to show.
