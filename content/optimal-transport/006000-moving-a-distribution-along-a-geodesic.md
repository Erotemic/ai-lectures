# Moving a Distribution Along a Geodesic: Transport Maps and Wasserstein Geometry

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

Two distributions can be compared by a number, such as their Wasserstein distance. But sometimes we want to know more than how far apart they are. We want a path between them. We might want to morph one density into another, interpolate between two collections of points, or understand what it means for probability itself to move at a constant speed.

The first attempt is often to mix the two distributions. At halfway, put half the probability mass under the first law and half under the second. This is a valid path of probability measures. Yet it usually does not describe mass *moving* from its starting locations to its destinations. It describes changing the proportions of two static populations.

Optimal transport offers a different interpolation. First choose how mass is paired between the initial and final distributions. Then let each paired piece of mass travel along a path in the underlying space. The intermediate measures record where the moving mass is at each time. In quadratic Wasserstein geometry, this construction creates geodesics: shortest, constant-speed paths in a space whose points are probability measures.

The claim is mathematically precise, but it depends on the coupling, the ground geometry, and the cost. Let us build it in one dimension first, where everything can be calculated.

## A mixture is not a moving point

Start with one unit of probability at position zero and end with one unit at position two. The Wasserstein-two distance between these point masses is two. The obvious physical motion sends the point from zero to two at constant speed. At time t, between zero and one, the distribution is a point mass at position two t. Between times s and t, its Wasserstein distance is two times the absolute difference of s and t. This is exactly the constant-speed condition for a geodesic.

Now use ordinary mixture instead. At time t, put probability one minus t at zero and probability t at two. For every intermediate t this describes *two piles*, one shrinking and the other growing. The W-two distance from the starting point mass is the square root of four t, because a fraction t of the unit mass must travel distance two and squared cost four. That equals two times the square root of t, not two t.

At the halfway time, the moving-point path is entirely concentrated at position one. The mixture path has half its mass at zero and half at two. Both paths have the same endpoints. Only the first is the constant-speed displacement path suggested by physically transporting the mass from zero to two.

This distinction is fundamental when averaging shapes, signals, or distributions. A mixture can create a ghost of two separated objects. Displacement interpolation moves their features through space. Neither is universally preferable: mixtures are natural for uncertain choices between populations, whereas displacement interpolation represents motion through the geometry we supplied.

## The one-dimensional monotone map

For distributions on the real line, optimal transport has a particularly simple ordering principle. Match low quantiles to low quantiles, high quantiles to high quantiles. If a source distribution has no atoms and its cumulative distribution function is sufficiently regular, we can often describe this coupling by a deterministic map. A source point x has quantile level F-mu of x; send it to the target's point at the same quantile level, using the generalized inverse of the target cumulative distribution.

The resulting map T is nondecreasing. For convex costs of the form absolute difference to a power p at least one, the monotone coupling is optimal. In a finite collection of equally weighted, sorted points, this simply says to pair the first with the first, the second with the second, and so on. The proof begins with a crossing argument: if x-one is less than x-two but they are paired in reverse order with y-one greater than y-two, uncrossing those two assignments does not increase the total convex cost.

There is a qualification that our earlier warehouse example already forced us to confront. If the source has atoms, a deterministic map may not exist. A source atom of mass one half cannot be split by a map into target atoms of one quarter and one quarter. Yet a monotone *coupling* still exists. We should not silently promote every optimal coupling into a function T. Even in the cases where a transport map exists, it is a mathematical conclusion requiring hypotheses, not a property of the word transport.

When a map does exist and the ground space is Euclidean, the path at time t can be generated by sending each source point x to one minus t times x plus t times T of x. The law of those intermediate positions is called displacement interpolation. If T is an optimal map for squared Euclidean distance, this path is a constant-speed W-two geodesic.

Consider two equally weighted points at zero and two, and a target with two equally weighted points at one and five. The monotone optimal pairing is zero to one and two to five. A crossing assignment would send zero to five and two to one, with much greater squared cost. The optimal squared cost is one half times one squared plus one half times three squared, which equals five. The W-two distance is the square root of five.

At time t, the moving points are at t and at two plus three t, each still with probability one half. At time zero this is our source. At time one it is the target. The squared distance between the distributions at times s and t, using the obvious corresponding particles, is five times the square of t minus s. The monotone order remains intact, so that pairing is optimal. Consequently their W-two distance is the square root of five times the absolute difference of t and s. We have verified the geodesic property by calculation.

The example is small, but the logic is substantial. A coupling between endpoints gives a joint law of starting and ending positions. Interpolating each pair gives intermediate laws. In Euclidean space, if the original coupling is optimal for squared distance, the interpolated path has exactly the constant-speed length predicted by the endpoints, even when it does not arise from a deterministic map. Maps make the formulas easier to read, but a coupling-based construction is more general.

## What Brenier's theorem supplies

The multidimensional map problem is harder. On the real line there is an order. In the plane and higher-dimensional spaces, there is no single total ordering of points compatible with all directions. Yet squared-distance transport has a remarkable structure.

In Euclidean space, under the standard hypotheses including absolute continuity of the source probability measure and finite second moments, Brenier's theorem gives an optimal transport map for quadratic cost. It is, almost everywhere with respect to the source, the gradient of a convex function. The map is unique in the relevant almost-everywhere sense under those hypotheses.

Why a gradient of a convex function? Convexity enforces a multidimensional analogue of the noncrossing principle. The gradient of a differentiable convex function is monotone in the sense that the dot product of T of x minus T of x-prime with x minus x-prime is nonnegative. This is related to the two-point comparison obtained by exchanging destinations in a squared-cost assignment. A deeper condition called cyclic monotonicity extends the comparison from two pairs to cycles of any length and leads toward the convex-potential representation.

We should not confuse this with saying every vector field is a transport map, or that every transport map is a gradient. The theorem is about optimality for a particular cost, with assumptions on the measures. For asymmetric costs, irregular spaces, or source atoms, a map may fail to exist or take a different form. Those hypotheses are part of the theorem's content.

The convex potential connects transport to partial differential equations. If the source and target have sufficiently regular densities, conservation of mass under T produces a Jacobian equation: source density at x equals target density at T of x times the determinant of the derivative of T. When T is the gradient of a convex function, that derivative is a Hessian matrix, leading to a Monge–Ampère-type equation. This is a doorway into nonlinear analysis, not a formula we can apply without regularity and invertibility considerations.

## The geometry of measures

Once probability measures have a distance, we can ask geometric questions just as we would on a curved surface. Which paths are shortest? What counts as constant velocity? How should we interpolate, average, or optimize over distributions? In Wasserstein-two geometry, displacement interpolation is the answer to the first question on Euclidean space.

This also clarifies what the distance is measuring. It does not compare distributions only by how much probability they assign to the same sets. It measures how far probability mass would need to move. The moving-particle construction turns that comparison into a path that has a physical interpretation.

Consider a distribution evolving with time under a smooth velocity field. In a continuum description, conservation of mass is written as the continuity equation: time derivative of density plus divergence of density times velocity equals zero. It is the differential form of the row and column conservation laws from finite transport. Mass flows rather than appearing or disappearing.

There is a beautiful dynamic characterization of W-two: subject to the continuity equation and prescribed endpoint distributions, the squared W-two distance is the least integrated kinetic energy, with density times squared speed integrated over space and time. This is known as the Benamou–Brenier formulation. A full functional-analytic treatment requires care with weak solutions and finite-action velocities, but the idea is accessible from our examples. To transport a point mass from zero to two, constant-speed motion is energetically optimal; speeding up and slowing down needlessly increases the integral of squared speed.

The static coupling problem chooses which starting mass ends at which target. The dynamic formulation chooses a density and velocity field over time. The fact that their optimal values agree is a deep bridge between optimization over joint distributions and geometric motion of mass.

## What this course equips us to distinguish

We can now separate several phrases that are sometimes used interchangeably. A coupling is a joint distribution with fixed marginals. A transport plan is a coupling regarded as shipments. A transport map is a deterministic function that pushes one measure to another; it is a special case, not the general definition. Wasserstein distance is a particular metric obtained by optimizing powers of a ground distance. An optimal transport algorithm is a procedure for computing or approximating one of these objects, and its output depends on the problem and objective it is given.

The finite linear program has dual potentials that certify optimality. Uniform assignments connect to matchings and integral polyhedra. Entropy produces a smoothly spread plan accessible through scaling. A quadratic optimal coupling produces an interpolation in Wasserstein space, and under additional hypotheses a convex-gradient transport map.

These ideas belong to one subject because the same marginal constraints and cost structure recur throughout. They are not identical theorems. The distinction is useful whenever we read a paper about optimal transport: we can ask whether it assumes a deterministic map, what ground cost it optimizes, whether it proves existence, how its numerical approximation is defined, and which properties survive regularization.

The elementary warehouse ledger has become a geometry on a space of probability distributions. At the beginning, we asked only where each fraction of a shipment goes. At the end, we can ask how an entire distribution moves along a shortest path and which convex potential governs that motion. Both questions are resolved by respecting the same principle: mass can be rearranged, and the rearrangement has a cost.

## Mathematical companion (not spoken)

For probability laws \(\mu,\nu\in\mathcal P_2(\mathbb R^d)\), choose an optimal quadratic coupling \(\pi\). For \(0\le t\le1\), define
\[
\mu_t=\big((x,y)\mapsto(1-t)x+ty\big)_\#\pi.
\]
Then \(W_2(\mu_s,\mu_t)=|t-s|W_2(\mu,\nu)\). If an optimal map \(T\) exists, \(\pi=(\mathrm{id},T)_\#\mu\) and \(\mu_t=((1-t)\mathrm{id}+tT)_\#\mu\).

**Explicit example.** For \(\mu=\frac12\delta_0+\frac12\delta_2\), \(\nu=\frac12\delta_1+\frac12\delta_5\), the monotone map sends 0 to 1 and 2 to 5. Thus
\[
\mu_t=\frac12\delta_t+\frac12\delta_{2+3t},\quad W_2(\mu_s,\mu_t)=\sqrt5|t-s|.
\]
Compare the mixture path \((1-t)\delta_0+t\delta_2\), which has \(W_2(\delta_0,\cdot)=2\sqrt t\), not \(2t\).

**Brenier theorem (statement with hypotheses).** For \(\mu,\nu\in\mathcal P_2(\mathbb R^d)\) and \(\mu\) absolutely continuous with respect to Lebesgue measure, quadratic-cost OT is realized by a \(\mu\)-a.e. unique map \(T=\nabla\varphi\) for a convex potential \(\varphi\) (up to the usual potential normalization). For smooth positive densities and smooth invertible T, mass conservation yields \(\rho_\mu(x)=\rho_\nu(T(x))\det DT(x)\), and with \(T=\nabla\varphi\) this is Monge–Ampère type.

**Dynamic quadratic OT.** Formally,
\[
W_2(\mu_0,\mu_1)^2=\inf_{(\rho_t,v_t)}\int_0^1\int_{\mathbb R^d}|v_t(x)|^2\rho_t(x)\,dx\,dt,
\quad \partial_t\rho_t+\nabla\cdot(\rho_t v_t)=0,
\]
where a rigorous theorem uses appropriate measure-valued continuity equations and finite action.

**Exercises with solutions.** (1) Compare midpoint mixture and displacement for \(\delta_0,\delta_2\). *Mixture: \(\frac12\delta_0+\frac12\delta_2\); displacement: \(\delta_1\).* (2) In the two-particle example, find the midpoint positions. *They are \(1/2\) and \(7/2\).* (3) Does an optimal coupling always come from a map? *No; atoms sometimes have to split, as in the quarter/three-quarter warehouse example.*
