# Giovanni's Room — The Story

A complete, spoiler-containing narrative account of James Baldwin's 1956 novel, in original prose. The accompanying critical lecture follows at `016000`.

## The morning that will not be postponed

A young American is alone in a rented house in the south of France. His name is David. He has been drinking, packing, and examining himself in the mirror with a kind of exhausted disbelief. The woman he had intended to marry has left for America. A man he loved is in prison, and before another day is over that man will be executed. David can neither undo what happened nor persuade himself that he had nothing to do with it.

This is where James Baldwin's *Giovanni's Room* begins. The whole story is told through David's memory, which means we meet its people after their futures have already been decided. The man in prison is Giovanni, an Italian who came to Paris hoping for another life. The woman who has gone is Hella, an American who thought she and David might make a life together. And David, the one person who could have told each of them the whole truth, is left to recount how he gave each a partial one.

To understand his predicament, we must go back, first to America and then to the Paris where David met Giovanni.

## A boy learns to fear what he has felt

David grows up without his mother, who died when he was small. His father loves him but has difficulty knowing what kind of father to be. He wants a cheerful, masculine son, someone who will be at ease with other men, with women, and with the future the father imagines. An aunt named Ellen worries that the household does not provide enough stability or discipline. David's father and his aunt do not have identical ideas about raising a boy, but both participate in the atmosphere in which David learns to inspect himself through other people's expectations.

When he is an adolescent, David spends a night with a boy named Joey. They are friends. Their intimacy arrives with tenderness and excitement that David cannot easily separate into approved and forbidden parts. The next morning he is frightened by what has happened. Not because Joey has hurt him, but because acknowledging his love or desire would change the story David tells himself about who he is allowed to become.

He begins avoiding Joey. He treats him cruelly, cutting off the connection rather than admitting its significance. Joey is not given an equal opportunity to tell us how that rejection feels; the story belongs to the boy who did it. Yet the memory remains inside David. What he calls forgetting is more like installing a locked door in his own life. A person and an experience have been made inaccessible by the fear of what they might reveal.

David grows older. He drinks, behaves recklessly, and at one point is involved in a serious accident while driving intoxicated. His father, distressed by what seems to him a directionless young man, attempts to intervene. David serves in the military and eventually goes to France. There is no single event after which he becomes free of the fear first awakened by Joey. Rather, each new setting permits him to postpone deciding what that memory means.

Paris seems to offer distance from the expectations of home. David can live among expatriates, speak a different language, and be less accountable to the people who knew him as a boy. But the self he carries across the ocean is still the person who deserted Joey. Changing countries does not settle that unfinished business.

## Hella, and a future spoken before it is chosen

In France, David becomes involved with another American, Hella. She is intelligent and affectionate, interested in the possibility of a relationship but not eager to become a possession. Their bond has genuine pleasures. They share an expatriate life and the freedom of being away from the people who would tell them what a respectable adulthood should look like.

David proposes marriage. It is not obvious that he is lying to Hella whenever he expresses affection. He is attracted to her, and he can picture the household, the public recognition, and the reassurance that marriage might bring. But the picture has another purpose. It promises a way not to examine his desire for men. David tries to make one part of his life prove that another part never existed.

Hella does not give him an immediate answer. She goes to Spain to think. The separation leaves David in Paris without the person whose presence has been helping him keep his future at arm's length. Money is running short. His father has become less willing to finance his drifting life abroad. David finds himself moving through cafes and bars, anxious about money, his own desires, and what Hella might say when she returns.

He knows a wealthy older man named Jacques, whose interest in younger men is widely understood in the circles where they meet. Jacques is sometimes generous and sometimes unpleasant; David accepts his help while looking down on him. The contradiction is important. David will take money and introductions from a man he regards as shameful, yet he refuses to see how much his disdain resembles the social judgment he fears for himself.

One evening Jacques takes him to a bar run by Guillaume, another older man with wealth, social connections, and power in Paris's gay nightlife. Guillaume is capable of charm, but he is also accustomed to using the advantages of his position. Behind the counter is a new bartender, Giovanni. He has come from Italy, has few secure resources, and is unmistakably alive to the people around him. He is quick, sociable, sometimes defiant. He does not present himself as a man asking permission to be attractive.

David and Giovanni notice each other. What begins as conversation becomes unmistakable interest. Around them, the bar has its usual traffic of watching, joking, flirting, and appraisal. David is aware of Jacques and Guillaume, of the danger of being seen, of the ease with which desire might be turned into a story for other people to tell. Giovanni seems less interested in preserving such appearances.

By the end of their encounter, the attraction has become something neither man can pretend not to understand. David enters a relationship that feels less like the life he had planned than like a life he has been refusing to admit he wanted.

## The room

Giovanni rents a cramped room in Paris. There is little money and little space. The disorder is not only that of an untidy bachelor; the room carries the marks of someone who has tried to build a home from small means, while never being certain that he will be allowed to keep it. When David moves in, the room becomes the center of their shared life.

At first David experiences happiness there. He and Giovanni sleep together, talk, quarrel, make up, cook, and pass time with one another. Paris continues outside, but the small room creates a private world in which David no longer needs to perform a prospective husband for Hella or a reassuring son for his father. Giovanni can be playful and tender. He wants their relationship to be real enough to survive the ordinary demands of a day.

David experiences the same closeness as a threat. A room in which he is loved becomes a room in which he might be known. He starts imagining that Giovanni wants to confine him, that living with another man will leave him without a recognizable kind of manhood, and that he will lose the freedom he once sought by leaving America. Sometimes these thoughts grow stronger precisely when Giovanni has done nothing frightening. It is the fact of their life together, not merely an act by his lover, that David struggles to accept.

Their economic positions are also unequal. David has an American father from whom he can ask for money, however humiliating that request may feel. He can contemplate marriage to Hella and eventually leaving Paris. Giovanni cannot change his circumstances so easily. His income depends on work at Guillaume's bar. His safety and social standing are entangled with men who control jobs, housing, and access to the limited community around them.

Giovanni's feelings deepen. David's do too, although he increasingly presents his own uncertainty as proof that the relationship cannot continue. When he thinks about Hella's approaching return, he begins treating the room as a place he must escape. Instead of explaining his position to Giovanni, he keeps living the relationship while secretly planning its end.

David's anxiety produces another betrayal even before he leaves. Hoping to reassure himself that he can desire women, he spends a night with a woman he knows slightly, Sue. She offers him ordinary human warmth and expectation, but he has come to her with a private test she has not been told she is taking. Afterward, he feels miserable and ashamed. He has used Sue without reaching the certainty he wanted. She too belongs among the people whose lives David damages by refusing to name his own motives.

When he returns to Giovanni, a new crisis has begun. Guillaume has fired his bartender, accusing him of dishonesty. Giovanni says the accusation followed his refusal of Guillaume's sexual advances. Whatever public explanation Guillaume offers, the disparity in power is clear: one man controls the job, and the other has lost it. Giovanni is furious and frightened. His wages are gone. The room that once held two people in precarious happiness now holds two frightened people, one of whom is preparing to leave.

Giovanni asks David not to abandon him. David cannot make the promise in the way Giovanni needs. The choice has already been forming in his mind, and he makes himself feel trapped by it rather than acknowledging that it is his own.

## A past Giovanni has not been able to tell

For much of their time together, Giovanni's life before Paris has remained incomplete to David. We know the Italian man has left something behind. He sometimes speaks bitterly of home, of religion, of the kind of life people expect a man to inhabit. His sorrow is not reducible to poverty or a difficulty finding work.

The full account comes later, when the two lovers have almost lost one another. Giovanni tells David that he once had a wife in Italy and that their child was stillborn. He remembers praying in desperation for the child to live and then rebelling against the religious forms that offered him no rescue. In the wake of that loss he left his wife and the life he had known. Paris was not simply an adventurous destination. It was a place he came after a grief he could not bear.

This changes what we know about him, even if it does not change David's decision. Giovanni has not been merely David's discovery, the man found behind a Parisian bar. He had a family, a child, a religious world, and a rupture that preceded their meeting. He came to David already carrying a history. He wants this new relationship to be a life, not an interlude that another man can abandon when the expected world calls him back.

Yet David is not ready to grant that account the weight it deserves. He wants to be released from responsibility without having to say that he is releasing himself. The more clearly Giovanni explains his pain, the more insistently David clings to the picture of marriage awaiting him.

## Hella returns

Hella tells David that she is coming back to Paris, and that she is ready to accept marriage. David goes to meet her. He leaves Giovanni's room without honestly explaining what he is doing, and does not return for days. The change is as sudden for Giovanni as it is conveniently simple for David: in one journey to a railway station, David begins acting as if his other life has ceased to exist.

He and Hella enjoy their reunion. Their familiar conversation and affection ease him back toward the identity he has been trying to preserve. He asks his father for money. He speaks as a man who intends to settle down. But every ordinary act requires him to conceal the presence of the person he has left waiting in Paris.

The concealment cannot last intact. David and Hella encounter Jacques and Giovanni, and the meeting exposes how much David has refused to say. Giovanni is wounded and angry. Hella can see that there is an intensity in the encounter she does not understand. David improvises explanations, presenting Giovanni as a former roommate who had become excessively dependent on him. The explanation protects David by making Giovanni's anguish seem like a personal failing rather than a response to abandonment.

David returns to the room to collect his belongings and speak with Giovanni. Their confrontation is painful because both men know what happened even while David struggles to say it plainly. Giovanni asks why David cannot recognize the life they have had. David replies with ideas about masculinity, nature, and the impossibility of two men making a future. These ideas sound final to him because he needs them to sound final. Giovanni presses against the distance between David's declarations and his desires.

Their last night together does not repair the break. It makes the break more intimate. Giovanni tells him more about the child he lost and his departure from Italy. David knows he is leaving a man who loves him and needs him, and still he leaves. The morning after, he walks away from the room and from the person who has made it into more than a rented space.

Afterward David tries to build a future with Hella. He can still imagine life with her, but the attempt is now burdened by two silences. He does not tell Hella whom he has left, and he does not tell Giovanni the full shape of the life he has chosen. He has exchanged the difficulty of an honest future for the temporary convenience of two incompatible stories.

## Giovanni's descent

David continues to hear about Giovanni. At times he sees him in the company of Jacques, wearing clothes that suggest someone else is paying for his survival. David is jealous, contemptuous, and ashamed. He cannot claim the right to be Giovanni's lover, yet he resents seeing Giovanni with another man. He suspects that Giovanni has become dependent on people whose affection and money carry humiliating terms.

Later Giovanni's circumstances worsen again. The relationship with Jacques no longer seems to protect him. He moves among the poorer men of the quarter, and the possibility of returning to work at Guillaume's bar comes up. For a man with limited money and few alternatives, the old bar is not only the setting of a humiliating dismissal. It is also a possible way to earn wages.

Then Guillaume is found murdered. He has been strangled in his rooms. The newspapers quickly turn the crime into a scandal, contrasting the respectable position of the dead man with the precarious status of the Italian suspect. Giovanni disappears, and the police search for him.

David forms a picture of what may have happened. He imagines Giovanni returning to Guillaume to ask for his job, Guillaume using his power to demand sex, and an encounter ending in rage and murder. But this is David's reconstruction. He was not present. Baldwin does not give us an independent, omniscient account of every moment in Guillaume's rooms. We know that Guillaume is dead and Giovanni is accused; David's elaborate account of their meeting reveals both his fear for Giovanni and his need to make the outcome intelligible.

Giovanni is eventually found and arrested. He is tried and sentenced to death. David follows the news and hears from Jacques, who has contact with the legal proceedings. The sentence makes final what David had hoped, perhaps, could remain a regrettable but distant chapter of his life. Giovanni is not merely out of reach. The state has decided that his life will end.

David cannot undo his refusal of love by finding a single explanation for a killing. Giovanni's actions have consequences of their own, and it would be false to say David committed the murder or issued the sentence. Still, David cannot separate the outcome from the sequence in which he abandoned Giovanni, concealed his relationship, and watched his former lover lose what little security he had.

## Hella discovers the other life

David and Hella have moved away from Paris to a house in southern France. They discuss a future together, but their life is no longer the simple escape David imagined. Hella talks about marriage, gender, and what women are asked to surrender when they join their lives to men. David hears these concerns while privately measuring how far he has gone to make himself look like the sort of man she is supposed to marry.

The news of Giovanni's trial and sentence reaches him. He drinks heavily and goes to Nice, where he spends days with a sailor. It is another attempt to step outside the life waiting for him at home, though now it is Hella who must live with the unexplained absence.

Hella finds him. When she sees him with the sailor and understands something of David's relationship to men, the story she has been told begins to collapse. The wound is not simply that David has desires she did not know about. It is that he has allowed her to make decisions about their future while withholding a truth that would have changed those decisions. She realizes that she has been asked to believe in a marriage David hoped would settle something he could not settle himself.

She leaves him. Her departure is not a punishment imposed from outside the story; it is an act by a woman who can finally see enough of the situation to choose for herself. David is now left without the marriage he had wanted as refuge and without the man he had loved.

## What David sees in the mirror

The narrative returns to its first room: the rented house in the south of France. Hella is gone. Giovanni's execution is imminent. David packs, drinks, and studies his reflection. He is no longer able to keep his memories apart. Joey, Hella, the room in Paris, Giovanni's anger, and the stories David told to protect himself all belong to the same history.

He imagines the machinery of the execution and the final moments of the man he loved. These scenes are not events he witnesses. They are images he cannot stop constructing. He has been trying to explain his life to himself from a distance, but the distance has vanished. The fact of Giovanni's death cannot be contained by the explanations David once used to make departure seem necessary.

He leaves the house and goes out into the morning, carrying the knowledge that recognition has come too late to rescue anyone. His confession does not bring Giovanni back, restore Hella's trust, or offer Joey the apology never given. It does, for a moment, make it impossible for David to tell the old story about himself without hearing everything that story excluded.

That is the ending of *Giovanni's Room*. One man awaits execution; another must go on living after discovering what his fear has cost other people. The novel has not followed a romance toward the comfort of reunion. It has followed the ways a person can turn love into danger by refusing to acknowledge it, and it ends when the person who did the refusing is left alone with the truth of his choices.
