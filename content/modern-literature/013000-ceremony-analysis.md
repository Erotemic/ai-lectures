# Ceremony: Healing, Story, and a World That Can Change

A critical lecture following `012200`, the full story of Leslie Marmon Silko's *Ceremony*.

## What would it mean for a story to help someone live?

At the center of Leslie Marmon Silko's *Ceremony* is a man who has survived something he cannot yet make part of his life. Tayo's body is back in New Mexico, but his mind is caught in the Philippines, in the death of Rocky, in the face that resembles Josiah, and in the sound of rain that makes him sick. The novel could have been organized around the question of how he will forget the war. It asks something much more difficult: how can a person remember without letting the memory dictate every action that follows?

That difference changes the shape of the book. Tayo does not simply receive correct information, after which his symptoms disappear. He needs relationships and responsibilities that move him from helpless repetition toward action. He needs to see his private suffering as connected to histories of land theft, racial contempt, and military destruction, while also recognizing that those forces do not absolve him of the choices he makes now. He needs stories that do more than explain the world after the fact. He needs a way of participating in it.

Silko's novel was published in 1977. She grew up at Laguna Pueblo, in a family whose history crossed Pueblo, Mexican, and European ancestries. The book's relation to Laguna storytelling matters, but it matters in a precise way. This is a literary work by a particular writer, not a documentary transcript of a ceremony for outsiders to imitate. Its form draws on specific experiences and narrative traditions. It is not evidence that all Indigenous communities organize stories, healing, or spiritual practice identically. That distinction protects the particularity of Silko's achievement.

## The form refuses to put the past behind the present

If one summarized *Ceremony* as a sequence, one might say: Tayo goes to war, returns sick, receives help, finds cattle, and survives a confrontation at a mine. That is a serviceable orientation. But it removes one of the book's central artistic operations. Silko does not let the events remain in their proper chronological boxes. War memories and childhood experiences break into the present. Passages shaped as verse and stories of earlier happenings run beside the prose. Names, images, and events recur in different registers. The reader experiences how the past can remain active without being reduced to a single flashback.

This is not disorder for its own sake. A conventional sequence might permit us to place the war safely in one chapter, close it, and proceed to recovery in another. The novel's arrangement makes such closure unavailable. Tayo cannot tell his body that the Philippines happened once and therefore has no power in New Mexico. Nor can the community put colonial disruption into a finished historical period while its land is still fenced, mined, and governed through unequal power. Form carries an argument about time: a past event can remain present through its consequences, and a seemingly current crisis may be intelligible only as part of a longer pattern.

At the same time, Silko does not make repetition identical with fate. The text returns to stories, but their return is never quite mechanical. The challenge is to recognize a pattern and then do something other than repeat its most destructive response. In the final confrontation, the old pattern offers Tayo an emotionally convincing part to play. He could kill Emo and claim a victory. His refusal makes the story return with a difference.

The Cambridge Companion to Native American Literature describes Silko's work as shaped by oral and written storytelling as well as the adaptive histories of Laguna Pueblo. This helps explain why the novel's forms resist a crude opposition between something 'traditional' and something 'modern.' The important distinction is not whether a narrative is old or new. It is whether it helps people respond to what has actually happened.

## Mixed ancestry and the lie of purity

Tayo's mixed parentage is made into an accusation against him. Auntie measures him against Rocky, the son whose educational and athletic achievements appear to promise approval in the larger white-dominated world. Emo exploits the same point of vulnerability, as though being of mixed ancestry makes Tayo less eligible to belong. That is not merely a private family quarrel. It reproduces classifications enforced by histories of colonization and racial hierarchy.

Yet the book continually supplies people who break those categories. Josiah's cattle are deliberately crossbred. Night Swan understands an identity that cannot be reduced to one inherited label. Betonie, who performs a changed ceremony, is not an image of a pure past kept untouched by the modern city. His dwelling and his knowledge refuse the premise that survival depends on absolute separation from contemporary materials. Tayo's relationship with Ts'eh exceeds the binaries that others use to sort his life.

The spotted cattle are especially effective because they are also animals. They do not merely stand for Tayo's identity in an allegorical equation. They need food, water, movement, and care. Their behavior has consequences in the physical landscape. Josiah selected them because he thought they might be better suited to the country. White property boundaries obstruct that adaptation. The cattle therefore bring an abstract argument about imposed categories down to the level of bodies looking for a way to survive.

But beware of turning the argument into a celebration of mixture for its own sake. Cultural exchange under conquest is not an innocent shopping trip through equivalent choices. Some people control the terms, the borders, and the law. Silko's novel recognizes the necessity of adaptation while showing why the powers demanding adaptation can be destructive. To say that the ceremony must change is not to say that domination has been harmless or that everyone must relinquish the right to define their own relationships.

## A ceremony is more than therapy with different vocabulary

When Ku'oosh first tries to help Tayo, he does not deliver a complete solution. Betonie then offers a different approach. An impatient reader may interpret this as a contest between an outdated healer and a clever reformer. That reading would flatten the book's intellectual situation. Ku'oosh takes Tayo seriously; the failure of one response opens the need for another. Betonie does not invalidate every earlier practice. He recognizes circumstances that earlier forms may not have been designed to encounter, including industrialized warfare and modern forms of dispossession.

The Western clinical language of post-traumatic stress can clarify much about Tayo's flashbacks, nausea, alienation, and survival after combat. It is also a description developed for particular diagnostic and therapeutic purposes. Silko's novel asks about additional dimensions: who has authority to interpret an injury, how a community receives a person back, what responsibilities link a wounded body to land and kin, and how collective history enters a person's suffering. There is no need to choose between treating Tayo as traumatized and reading the ceremony as consequential. They answer different questions.

The cattle search makes that visible. Betonie does not merely persuade Tayo to think more positively about Josiah. Tayo goes out, encounters risk, makes decisions, recovers the animals, and accepts help. Manual labor does not magically cure a veteran. It is that he finds a relation between memory and action. Josiah's death can no longer be approached only as something Tayo failed to prevent. It becomes tied to a responsibility he can still carry forward.

Similarly, Ts'eh cannot be reduced to the role of a love interest whose affection fixes the hero. She helps disclose a different mode of attention. Time with her includes conversation, food, gathering plants, seasons, and the movement of living creatures. The novel also frames her through narratives older and larger than Tayo's private life. Those layers resist the easy modern habit of demanding that she be either a purely realistic woman or a purely symbolic apparition. The book asks us to read across that division without pretending we can settle every aspect of her being.

## From the Philippines to uranium mining

One of the novel's strongest historical connections runs between the Pacific War and New Mexico. Tayo suffers in the Philippines; back home, he confronts the damage caused by uranium extraction and the development of nuclear weapons. The location of the final crisis means that the war's technologies and the region's land are part of a linked story. The enemy does not live only across an ocean. The means of annihilation are produced through institutions and resource decisions with consequences for the places people call home.

Literary scholar Kyoko Matsunaga has read Silko's fiction as dissent from Cold War nuclearism, emphasizing how boundaries and material extraction tie military power to the American Southwest. Another account in the Cambridge Companion to the Twentieth-Century American Novel and Politics situates the book within decolonization, environmentalism, antiwar thinking, and the possibility of world-destroying technologies. Such scholarship is useful because it stops us from treating the mine as a decorative symbol chosen simply to produce a dramatic scene.

This does not mean that every person in the novel who is white is identical with the pattern of destruction. Nor does the book's account of 'witchery' license the claim that a real historical people literally invented all evil. The tales within the fiction describe a logic of division and destruction. Emo's cruelty and his use of wartime violence for prestige show how people injured by a destructive order may nevertheless reproduce it. The pattern crosses tidy racial categories. Understanding it requires attention to power and complicity, not a new racial simplification.

The landscape around the mine also links local action to planetary risk. A fence cut to recover cattle and a hole dug for uranium operate at different scales, but each asks who has authority over land and what kinds of life count in decisions about it. Silko does not ask us to abandon the particular story of a young veteran in favor of an abstract slogan about the environment. She shows how the two are connected through the places he has been and the places he must return to.

## The choice not to kill

At the uranium mine, Tayo watches Emo's group torture Harley. He has a screwdriver. If he rushes out, he may kill Emo; he may also be caught and killed. It is tempting to make a moral diagram: violence is wrong, therefore Tayo does nothing. But the scene is more unsettling. Harley is suffering. Tayo's refusal cannot be credited with saving him. The novel does not give its protagonist an ethically clean option from which he can preserve every life.

What Tayo sees is that Emo wants him to act according to the same pattern of humiliation and killing that has overtaken Emo's sense of himself. To answer hatred by becoming its instrument would finish one particular story of what strength means. Tayo's choice is therefore neither simple passivity nor the easy superiority of a man who has no reason to be angry. He has every reason to be angry. He chooses not to make his anger the final authority over what he does.

The fact that the decision occurs at the mine matters. A novel that links the violence of individual men to mass destruction can no longer end with the old promise that the right act of killing will restore order. The scale of danger has made that promise suspect. Tayo completes the ceremony by refusing a role that appears to be waiting for him. His act is both intimate and political: a person's refusal to perpetuate a pattern in the only moment he can control.

The ending does not ask us to believe that one courageous restraint abolishes colonialism, war trauma, environmental poisoning, or the community's losses. Emo is not transformed into a friend; Harley does not reappear alive. Recovery does not cancel responsibility for public structures. The ending offers a way to begin living again, not an argument that the world has been repaired once and for all.

## The story told back to its listeners

After his journey, Tayo tells the elders what happened. Notice how much this changes the status of his experience. When he first came home, his memories erupted as private threats, impossible to organize and sometimes impossible to explain. Now he can tell a connected account to people who possess other accounts. Their hearing does not remove every ambiguity, but it makes his life available for recognition within a community.

There is a tempting analogy here between a ceremony that receives a person and a novel that receives its reader. We should state it carefully. Silko's published book is not a ceremony a listener may perform at home, and literary criticism is not a claim to religious authority. But the novel's narration can invite a change in how its audience attends to war, belonging, land, and vengeance. This is an artistic accomplishment, not permission to treat another community's knowledge as a collection of spiritual techniques we may extract at will.

One final question remains: what would this book look like if it had been organized only as a veteran's private recovery? We would still have suffering, cattle, and a choice not to kill. We would lose the sense that the illness enters a larger pattern of damaged relationships, and that the response requires changed relationships too. We would lose much of the work done by the braided form. We might even leave with the mistaken belief that history is a background a heroic individual can overcome by determination.

*Ceremony* does not promise that determination is enough. It lets the land, the dead, the living, and stories that outlast individuals press on every choice. Tayo returns to a world in which those forces remain. The change is that he can answer them without simply repeating their violence. His story is not the triumph of a solitary self over history. It is the difficult possibility of belonging to history without surrendering one's actions to its most destructive pattern.
