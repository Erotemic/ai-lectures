# One Hundred Years of Solitude — The Story

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

One Hundred Years of Solitude tells the story of a town and a family over generations. Many people have the same names, and some lives echo other lives so closely that a newcomer can lose track of who belongs to which century. For this telling, it will help to follow the family in approximate chronological order. We will begin with the founders of Macondo and end, in later episodes, with the last child born to their descendants. This account includes the entire ending of Gabriel García Márquez's novel.

José Arcadio Buendía and Úrsula Iguarán are cousins who marry despite family fears that the marriage of close relatives might produce a child with a pig's tail. Their early life is marked by a quarrel. After another man mocks José Arcadio Buendía, he kills him. The dead man's ghost returns, and the couple finally abandons their home, traveling with a group of people through difficult country until they establish a settlement beside a river. They call it Macondo. At first the town seems remote from the wars, industries, and governments that shape the wider world.

José Arcadio Buendía is curious about nearly everything. He is fascinated when a band of traveling gypsies arrives bearing inventions and marvels. Their leader, Melquíades, shows the villagers magnets, scientific equipment, and objects the town has never seen. José Arcadio Buendía becomes convinced that experimentation can transform his future. He pours energy into attempts at alchemy and discovery, often leaving Úrsula to manage the household and its practical needs. When the gypsies introduce ice to Macondo, the encounter becomes one of the childhood memories that will stay with the family for generations.

José Arcadio Buendía and Úrsula have children. The eldest boy, José Arcadio, is impulsive and physically powerful. Another son, Aureliano, is quieter and capable of extraordinary concentration. Their daughter Amaranta grows up amid complicated affections and rivalries. A girl named Rebeca also joins the household; she arrives with the remains of her parents and the habit of eating earth. Over time the household becomes a crowded center of Macondo's life, filled with relatives, servants, visitors, and the people who come asking Úrsula for help.

Another kind of change arrives with a magistrate from outside, Don Apolinar Moscote, who brings government expectations into a town that had believed it could govern its own affairs. José Arcadio Buendía resents the intrusion, yet the families also begin to intermarry. The peaceful, self-contained picture of Macondo is already changing before the Colonel takes up arms. Úrsula travels in search of the son who left with the gypsies and finds routes to settlements the founders had not known. New traders and visitors follow. The town is joining a larger map.

Rebeca brings an illness of forgetting. People in Macondo stop sleeping. At first the wakefulness sounds like a blessing, a chance to work and talk through the night, but soon memory begins to unravel. The villagers label objects with their names and functions, afraid they may forget what ordinary things are for. Melquíades returns and brings a remedy. He later lives in the family house and leaves behind manuscripts in a script that nobody can yet decipher. Those papers will survive long after the people who first saw him have died.

José Arcadio, the eldest son, leaves with the gypsies. Years later he returns as a huge, worldly man and marries Rebeca, scandalizing the family and interrupting her expected marriage to a musician named Pietro Crespi. Amaranta has also developed an intense attachment to Pietro, and the rivalries among the young women leave wounds that endure long after the immediate choices have been made. Aureliano marries a young woman named Remedios Moscote, but she dies while still very young. The death removes one of the people who had brought warmth into his increasingly remote life.

The settlement grows and becomes entangled with politics. Macondo is no longer so isolated that events in the nation can be dismissed as rumors. Aureliano is drawn toward the Liberal cause in civil conflict, while the government represents Conservative authority. He becomes Colonel Aureliano Buendía, a military leader who will launch many rebellions, face imprisonment and execution, and acquire a reputation far beyond Macondo. His wars will stretch across years and leave a trail of children, losses, and wounded relationships. Even when he comes home, he may feel less at home than before he left.

José Arcadio Buendía, the founder, loses his hold on the ordinary world. His obsession with time, invention, and the figures of the past grows into a condition his family can no longer manage indoors. He is tied to a chestnut tree in the courtyard, where he spends many years speaking with presences others cannot see. Úrsula continues to hold the household together around him. This is what the first generations leave their descendants: a growing town, a house of remarkable endurance, unfinished love affairs, a war about to consume the region, and manuscripts that appear to contain a future nobody understands.

The children and grandchildren of the founding family inherit both Macondo and its conflicts.

Colonel Aureliano Buendía is now a figure of national conflict. He has moved from the quiet courtyard of his father's house to the leadership of repeated Liberal rebellions. He fights, negotiates, loses, survives an execution he believed was imminent, and continues. He has sons with women in different places during the wars. Their names echo his own, and eventually seventeen sons are recognized publicly, though their lives will not be protected by their father's reputation. What began as political conviction hardens into a way of living that leaves him unable to imagine the end of war.

Macondo changes in his absence. Arcadio, an illegitimate son of the eldest José Arcadio, holds power in the town and becomes a tyrant. His rule is cruel and brief. When government forces retake the town he is executed, leaving children behind with Santa Sofía de la Piedad. Those children include Remedios the Beauty and twin boys, José Arcadio Segundo and Aureliano Segundo. Their similar names and uncertain habits of identity will cause confusion even within the family. The great house keeps receiving new generations while older members remain alive.

Aureliano's military fame also reaches into family life in unexpected ways. His sons arrive at Macondo at different times, marked by the same father yet raised in households far apart. Later many of them become targets of political violence, and the family is reminded that a general's reputation cannot shelter his descendants from the enemies he has acquired. Úrsula sees the damage in people who might otherwise have had ordinary lives. The war produces not only speeches and battles but fatherless children, frightened mothers, and households living with the fear of the next knock at the door.

The Colonel's wars eventually lose their meaning even to him. He survives betrayals and failed attempts at self-destruction, but returns to Macondo increasingly estranged from other people. He takes refuge in the repetitive making of little gold fish, melting down the metal and beginning again. His mother, Úrsula, sees how far away he has become. He is famous, but the fame no longer gives him a future. The cycle of conflict has reached a weary conclusion without resolving the family's deeper restlessness.

Other lives unfold with different kinds of strangeness. Remedios the Beauty grows into a woman whose beauty unsettles everyone around her and who seems largely indifferent to the obsession she inspires. One day she rises into the sky while helping with sheets, vanishing from ordinary life without the sort of explanation a realist chronicle would demand. Amaranta lives with refusals, lingering resentments, and grief, tending her own funeral shroud as death approaches. Úrsula grows old while remaining the household's practical center. She knows more of its quarrels, births, and secrets than almost anyone who comes after her.

The twins grow into very different men. Aureliano Segundo takes pleasure in abundance, festivities, and his relationship with Petra Cotes, whose animals reproduce with impossible fertility while he is with her. He marries Fernanda del Carpio, a woman from a family that preserves elaborate claims to nobility and respectability. Fernanda brings a colder, more rigid order to the Buendía home. Their marriage is marked by distance, while Aureliano Segundo continues to spend much of his life with Petra.

José Arcadio Segundo becomes involved with the workers of the banana company, a foreign business that transforms the economy and geography of Macondo. Trains arrive, strangers build new districts, and the town that once felt separated from the world is drawn into a system of plantations, imported managers, labor contracts, and armed authority. Workers complain about their treatment and organize a strike. The government intervenes on the side of order and the company. A crowd gathers, hoping to resolve the confrontation.

The troops fire on the workers. José Arcadio Segundo survives the mass killing and regains consciousness among bodies loaded onto a train. He understands that a massacre has taken place on a terrible scale. When he returns, he finds a town in which officials and many residents deny that anyone was killed. There is no accepted public account corresponding to what he saw. He retreats into the old room where Melquíades kept his manuscripts, haunted by the dead and determined that the massacre not be erased.

Afterward rain begins falling on Macondo and continues for years. The town's prosperity collapses under the water. Animals die, houses decay, and households that had seemed permanently rich begin losing their reserves. Úrsula grows blind and frail, though she conceals her blindness by learning the movements of the house so well that others scarcely notice. Aureliano Segundo tries to keep his family supplied. Fernanda struggles to preserve a formal household order in circumstances that no longer support it.

By the time the rain stops, the world that grew from the founders' little village has passed through civil war and foreign industrial rule. The survivors carry contradictory memories of what happened. Some have stories of miracles; some remember years of political bloodshed; José Arcadio Segundo knows that thousands of workers were killed and denied. Yet the Buendía house remains, damaged but standing, with still more descendants arriving to live beneath the weight of names they did not choose.

Generations later, the family house still holds memories that its inhabitants understand only in fragments.

A listener can keep the late family names straight by remembering three people in particular. Fernanda is the woman determined to control the household. Meme is her daughter, whose love for Mauricio Babilonia will be punished. And Aureliano, the child concealed after Meme's removal, will grow into the last reader of Melquíades's papers. The recurring names are deliberate, but these relationships are simple enough to hold onto while the final years pass.

The later generations of the Buendía family inherit a house already full of memories, some cherished and some deliberately hidden. Fernanda del Carpio has become its stern guardian, determined to preserve the behavior she associates with respectable family life. Úrsula, who helped found Macondo, is now very old. The Colonel is dead. The old joys and cruelties of the household are receding into stories told by people who cannot agree about what should be remembered.

Fernanda's daughter Renata Remedios, known as Meme, is lively and musically talented. She falls in love with Mauricio Babilonia, a young mechanic whose appearances are accompanied by yellow butterflies. Fernanda will not accept the relationship. When Mauricio comes secretly to see Meme, a guard shoots him and leaves him paralyzed. Fernanda sends her daughter away to a convent. Meme never returns to the life she had before, and for years her family knows little of what has become of her.

A child is later brought to the Buendía house. He is Meme and Mauricio's son, Aureliano, but Fernanda conceals the circumstances of his birth and gives him a false place in the family. He grows up surrounded by people who do not explain who he is. While others move through the town's ordinary business, Aureliano spends long hours among books and the mysterious papers left by Melquíades. The house, like the town, grows emptier. Old relatives die. Some go away and do not return. Others come home only after the lives they hoped to build elsewhere have disappointed them.

One of Fernanda's sons, José Arcadio, returns from Europe and finds money that Úrsula had hidden. He briefly lives a life of pleasure before being murdered for the treasure. Fernanda herself dies, and the house falls deeper into ruin. The old servant Santa Sofía de la Piedad, who maintained the family through many years, finally leaves without ceremony. The town no longer has the energy that brought travelers and companies to Macondo. The world seems to be withdrawing from the place that once tried to welcome every marvel.

Amaranta Úrsula, Fernanda's youngest daughter, comes back from Europe with her husband, Gaston. She brings plans to restore the family house and revive the town. The adult Aureliano is there, and the two develop a relationship. They fall in love and become lovers even though they are related; neither initially understands the full family history that would make their kinship unmistakable. Gaston eventually stays away, and the relationship takes over their lives. The house briefly fills with the possibility of a beginning, though its walls and gardens are collapsing around them.

Amaranta Úrsula becomes pregnant and gives birth to a boy. The child has a pig's tail, the very feature that Úrsula and José Arcadio Buendía had feared when they married generations earlier. The mother dies after the birth. Aureliano is overwhelmed by grief and loses track of the infant while he wanders through the remains of his life. When he returns, he finds that ants have carried away and killed the child. The last baby of the Buendía line has died before anyone could build a future for him.

Alone, Aureliano finally breaks the secret of Melquíades's manuscripts. The writings describe the family history, including events that seemed impossible to know when the manuscripts were made. They have waited for the moment when a reader would exist who could decipher them. Aureliano reads his own story and the story of the people who came before him. The papers do not offer a route out. As he reaches the final lines, a violent wind tears through Macondo. The town is destroyed, and the history it contained reaches its end.

We have now followed the founders who built Macondo beside a river, their children and grandchildren, the Colonel's wars, the banana company, the years of rain, and the last two lovers in the decaying house. The names have repeated because each generation belongs to the same family, though the people bearing them have made different choices. The narrative closes when the final reader discovers that the family's existence and its destruction have been written in the same papers. No Buendía remains to begin the story again.
