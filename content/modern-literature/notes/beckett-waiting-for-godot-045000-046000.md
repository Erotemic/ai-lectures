## Editorial intent and listening order

A complete, spoiler-containing retelling of the two-act play precedes nine interpretive lectures. The act retellings explain what spectators actually see, rather than presenting the play as a story about a secret identity that is ultimately revealed. The analyses investigate time and repetition, physical comedy, dialogue, master/servant power, religion and negative certainty, memory, stage craft, bilingual authorial versions, and documented influence. The course treats stage directions and silence as essential evidence. These are original explanatory manuscripts, not performances or reproductions of the copyrighted play.

## Facts and distinctions to protect

- The French play was composed October 1948–January 1949, printed in French in 1952, and premiered in Paris on 5 January 1953 with Roger Blin directing. Beckett translated it into English himself; Grove Press published the English script in 1954; London’s Peter Hall production opened in August 1955; the first UK Faber edition appeared in 1956. These are *different events* (composition, original-language print, premiere, translation, English print, English production, UK print).
- The UK publication was subjected to censorship-related modifications; later revised/unexpurgated English texts differ. Beckett’s 1975 Berlin production produced a further documentary layer of rehearsal notes. A comparison must identify which edition/production it considers; the lecture does **not** claim to have collated a French first edition and multiple English editions line by line.
- Act I: Estragon struggles with a boot, he and Vladimir wait, Pozzo drives Lucky by rope and speaks of selling him, Lucky is ordered to dance and think, the boy postpones Godot’s arrival, and the men decide to leave but do not move.
- Act II: leaves appear, Pozzo is blind, Lucky is mute, the men fall while attempting help, the boy disputes being the previous visitor, and the final proposed departure is not executed. The elapsed time, causes of the physical alterations, and whether the boy is the *same person* are not independently settled by the stage.
- Godot **does not appear within the play**. The characters expect him; the play does **not** explicitly confirm that he will never come. A religious reading has textual grounds, including the thieves and sheep/goats, but Godot's identity is not established as God. The boy’s goat/sheep report should not be turned into a solved Biblical code. The play’s theological, political and existential readings remain interpretations rather than documentary statements of Beckett’s intentions.
- Pozzo's impairment and Lucky's muteness are not presented as deserved divine punishments. Lucky's thinking speech is coerced, not a neatly decipherable theorem; treating it as authentic religious belief or scholarly fact would misrepresent the scene. The second act does not provide a liberation ending for Lucky.
- The phrase “theatre of the absurd” belongs to a later critical vocabulary especially associated with Martin Esslin’s 1961 book. Do not imply that every playwright grouped under this label signed onto one program, or that Beckett alone invented all postwar experimental drama.

## Source provenance and access limitations

The companion source catalog documents plot guides, Cambridge publisher-visible scholarship, research projects, library and museum archives, archival correspondence indexes, and edition metadata. They were checked for the statements cited. No claim is made that a complete French original or a complete 1954 American / 1956 British / 1965 UK text was directly collated for this source review. The full play and detailed theatrical notebook remain copyrighted, and no long script passages are reproduced. Fine textual claims should be verified against a *specified edition* before publication as definitive line-level criticism.

Particularly useful sources: Lawrence Graver for the French/English relationship; Ronan McDonald for documented religious readings and postwar interpretive context; University of Reading for the two premiere histories; Washington University and Emory for the 1975 production and holdings; the Morgan Library and DePaul Special Collections for the 1957 San Quentin performance and Rick Cluchey; and Emilie Morin for the limits of the category 'Theatre of the Absurd'. The plot guides support event sequence but are secondary and are not treated as a critical edition. All source IDs and claim anchors are in the eleven `metadata/<ID>.json` sidecars and `metadata/sources.json`.

## Practical production guidance

Keep the act narratives before criticism; the scene sequence remains comprehensible without reading the play first. Do not put URLs inside the spoken markers. For sound synthesis, pronounce Didi, Gogo, Pozzo and En attendant Godot naturally; verify pronunciation if an engine produces unnatural syllabification. The close-reading workshop is an imagined rehearsal, not an instruction to perform any copyrighted dialogue. The production notes should be outside the narrated transcript. No audio synthesis, binary assets, shared server modifications, or fixed target durations were introduced.
