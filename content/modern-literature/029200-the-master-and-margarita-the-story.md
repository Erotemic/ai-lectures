# The Master and Margarita — The Story

It is a warm evening in Moscow. Two literary men sit beside the ponds in a district where writers, editors, housing officials, and theatrical managers live by a complicated mixture of professional ambition and caution. One man is Berlioz, an influential editor. The other is a younger poet called Ivan Bezdomny. They are discussing a poem about Jesus and whether the figure in the poem should be presented as historically real. The conversation has the self-assurance of men who believe the official rules of explanation are complete.

A stranger joins them. He appears foreign, well dressed, peculiar, and serenely confident. He calls himself a consultant in black magic. He argues with their certainty and claims to have been present when Pontius Pilate interrogated a prisoner in ancient Jerusalem. When Berlioz refuses to be intimidated, the stranger predicts a bizarrely specific death. Berlioz will be decapitated, and the circumstances will involve a woman and spilled oil.

A little later Berlioz hurries away, slips near a tram, and is killed exactly as the stranger predicted. Ivan, horrified, pursues the foreigner and his astonishing companions. One is a tall figure with a theatrical manner. Another resembles an enormous black cat that can stand, talk, and behave like a mischievous human. The scene is so impossible that every attempt Ivan makes to explain it sounds to others like evidence of a disturbed mind. His chase through Moscow ends with him taken to a psychiatric clinic.

The stranger's name is Woland. As the city will discover, he is no ordinary conjurer. His companions include Koroviev, an extravagant master of deception; Behemoth, the huge irreverent cat; and Azazello, whose directness can be more frightening than the others' jokes. They move through the institutions of Moscow as though every locked door, administrative title, and social convention were a temporary theatrical prop. People who have learned to manipulate rules for personal advantage find themselves suddenly vulnerable to a power that is not impressed by their official importance.

But before we follow Woland, we need to understand the other city that appears in the novel. The stranger has begun telling a story about Jerusalem under Roman rule, and it will keep returning until it changes the fate of the people in Moscow.

## The procurator and the prisoner

Pontius Pilate is the Roman governor of Judea. He is physically miserable, suffering from a severe headache, and tired of the burdens of judgment. A prisoner has been brought before him: Yeshua Ha-Nozri, a wandering teacher accused of challenging established authority. Pilate's first instinct is to treat the case as another administrative obligation. The man before him, however, seems unafraid to speak simply and without calculation. Yeshua notices the governor's pain and speaks about people in a way that Pilate finds both irritating and strangely comforting.

Yeshua does not appear as an all-powerful figure who is about to rescue himself through a miracle. He is vulnerable to the decisions of officers, informers, and priests. His words have been reported and distorted by others. Pilate begins to think that the prisoner is harmless, perhaps even someone he would like to keep alive. Yet Yeshua has also spoken in a way the Roman authorities may construe as a threat to Caesar's power. Pilate fears the consequences of being seen to protect such a man.

There is an opportunity to release a prisoner in connection with the festival. Pilate wants Yeshua chosen. The local religious authorities insist instead on the release of another condemned man. Pilate yields. His official act sends Yeshua to execution. Even as he performs it, he knows that he has chosen his own safety over what he believes to be just. That knowledge will be harder for him to bear than the immediate political risk he avoided.

We do not hear the Jerusalem story in one uninterrupted stretch. Sometimes Woland speaks of it. Sometimes Ivan dreams it. Sometimes it appears as pages written by a character we have not yet met. The interruptions are part of the book's design. Moscow's inhabitants are trying to live in a world where the past can be managed by professional language, and a story of a frightened administrator keeps entering that world through other doors.

## The visitors take an apartment

After Berlioz's death, Woland and his companions establish themselves in a Moscow apartment whose previous occupants have disappeared or been removed under suspicious circumstances. Its rooms become the center of a chain of scandals. The apartment has a reputation that already makes neighbors nervous. Now it becomes an impossible address: people are transported away, documents change meaning, visitors leave without remembering what happened, and official investigations produce contradictions that no one wants to report too openly.

The theatrical world gives Woland another stage. His entourage arranges a show at the Variety Theatre, where a packed audience expects a demonstration of black magic that will eventually be explained as tricks. Instead money rains into people's hands, fine clothing appears, and performers invite the crowd to embrace sudden luxury. Later the gifts become worthless or vanish at embarrassing moments. People who enjoyed the spectacle are left exposed, undressed, frightened, or eager to deny they ever believed in it.

The show is funny, cruel, and not always fair. Some people punished by Woland's party are petty opportunists who have profited by deception. Others are frightened clerks or bystanders with no obvious crime proportionate to the humiliation inflicted on them. The spectacle demonstrates how readily public confidence can turn into panic when the terms of reality change. It also shows that the supernatural visitors take pleasure in disturbance. We should not confuse their exposure of hypocrisy with consistent justice.

Ordinary Moscow machinery works hard to restore explanations. Theater officials must file reports. Housing administrators must identify who has permission to occupy which room. Police seek witnesses. The witnesses either describe impossibilities or revise their recollections so that they sound employable. Soviet Moscow, as depicted here, is a place where professional security often depends on saying the expected thing and avoiding a subject that might bring investigation. Woland's entourage makes the mechanisms visible by treating everyone as an actor whose part can suddenly be changed.

In the psychiatric clinic, Ivan meets another patient. This man has no name he wants to give. He calls himself the Master.

## The writer and the woman who believed him

The Master tells Ivan that he wrote a novel about Pontius Pilate. He had been living a relatively quiet life when an unexpected sum of money allowed him to leave his job, rent basement rooms, and devote himself to writing. He met a woman named Margarita, who was married to another man but felt her ordinary life had become empty. They fell in love. Margarita recognized the importance of the book long before anyone in the literary establishment was willing to praise it. She gave the writer the name by which we now know him.

When the Master submitted his manuscript for publication, it met rejection, hostile criticism, and ridicule. The objections were not merely aesthetic disagreements. In the Moscow world of the novel, the act of writing about a religious figure, moral courage, and a Roman official's betrayal of conscience carries political and cultural risks. Reviewers found ways to attack the author and his intentions. One critic, Latunsky, became especially hateful to Margarita because of the harm she saw done to the man she loved.

The Master's confidence collapsed. He grew afraid of the dark and of the people who might come for him. At a low point he burned his manuscript. Margarita saved what pages she could, but could not restore his willingness to live and write. Through betrayal, fear, and institutional pressure he lost the safety of his rooms. He entered the clinic and withdrew from the world. Margarita was left without a clear explanation of his disappearance. She wanted to find him and feared that he had died.

The Master is not an embodiment of indestructible artistic courage. He gave up. Nor is Margarita merely a reader standing behind a more important genius. The story turns on her capacity to act when the writer himself can no longer imagine a path forward. She will take risks that he does not choose and that no ordinary social rule could prepare her for.

Meanwhile the Master tells Ivan about his writing and the life he lost. Ivan, who began the book as a poet eager to prove himself in approved literary circles, discovers a different possibility for literature: a manuscript written because a subject mattered, not because an editor had ordered a particular kind of poem. He also begins to understand why the stranger's story of Pilate feels more real than his former certainty can comfortably allow.

## The execution and the work of concealment

We return to Jerusalem after Yeshua has been condemned. Soldiers take the prisoners to be executed. The heat is oppressive. The day's work is treated by officials as a sequence of tasks to complete, while the people near the condemned suffer in ways that official language cannot describe. One follower, Levi Matvei, wants desperately to rescue Yeshua or at least spare him prolonged suffering. He cannot. He is left to witness the consequences of a sentence issued in another room.

Pilate cannot undo what he has ordered. He calls upon the officer who manages secret affairs, Aphranius, and gives instructions that result in the killing of Judas of Kiriath, the informer whose role helped bring Yeshua before the authorities. Pilate may feel that punishing the informer is a form of redress. But the murder cannot transform the governor's earlier act into courage. His official ingenuity is considerable; his moral failure remains.

In his dreams Pilate tries to imagine a conversation continuing with Yeshua, as though the day had ended differently and the two men could walk together along a path of moonlight. The dream offers relief by revising the one fact he cannot face. He wanted justice and chose self-preservation. The desire to rewrite the consequence is as human as it is futile. This buried story of a powerful man who cannot bear his own choice will return at the book's end.

## Margarita refuses to stay home

Margarita, meanwhile, is still in Moscow. She has a comfortable material position and a husband who is not presented simply as a villain. Yet she cannot accept the disappearance of the Master as a settled fact. She walks through the city thinking about him. Azazello approaches her with an offer. He knows things about her life that a stranger should not know. He gives her a cream and instructions that promise the possibility of seeing the Master again.

She applies it. The transformation is immediate and fantastic. Freed from ordinary physical limits, she can fly. She leaves the life in which respectable women are supposed to remain safely enclosed and moves above the city. Her flight is exhilarating, but it is also angry. She visits the apartment of the critic Latunsky and wrecks it, acting on her resentment of a man who helped destroy the Master's confidence. The episode carries the pleasure of revenge without pretending that breaking furniture can repair the years of suffering. Margarita has gained powers, not moral infallibility.

Her journey leads her into the strange company of Woland. A great ball is being prepared. It will require a hostess. Margarita accepts the position because she believes it may win her the return of the Master. The ball draws a parade of dead people associated with acts of violence, betrayal, and cruelty. The visitors come through in a punishing succession, and Margarita must meet and greet them with extraordinary endurance. What began as a private search for a lost lover becomes an encounter with suffering on an enormous scale.

Among the guests is a woman named Frieda, condemned to a recurring torment connected with the killing of her infant. Margarita is disturbed by her plight. The offense is grave, but the spectacle of unending punishment also presses a question about whether anything can ever end for someone who has done wrong. Margarita has no easy answer. What she does have is the impulse to intervene when the opportunity arises.

When the ball is over, Woland offers Margarita a reward. Her first request is not for the Master. She asks that Frieda be released from her punishment. Woland allows the wish and then grants Margarita another opportunity to speak for herself. She asks for the Master, and he appears. He is frightened, worn down, and skeptical that anything this strange can be real. Margarita does not wait for him to become confident. She holds to the fact that he is there.

Woland returns the manuscript that the Master burned. The restored book proves that the work has not ceased to exist merely because its author destroyed the pages he controlled. The statement carries a special weight in a novel whose author struggled with censorship and whose text itself was not published in his lifetime. For the characters in this scene, though, the immediate event is intimate: the life the Master believed finished has opened again because Margarita would not accept his disappearance as the final word.

They return to the basement rooms where their happiness had once existed. But the story does not end in a safe domestic restoration. The city has been altered, the authorities are investigating, and the supernatural visitors have other business to complete.

## The final ride

Moscow's investigators close in on the strange apartment. Woland's companions answer the siege with further absurdities and destruction. The cat's behavior becomes more outrageous, fires break out, and official explanations grow increasingly desperate. The city wants the visit to be a temporary criminal disturbance that will fit into a manageable report. The visitors are already preparing to leave.

Levi Matvei comes to Woland as a messenger from Yeshua. He asks for the Master and Margarita to receive peace. The request distinguishes peace from the higher reward of light, but the novel does not turn that distinction into a fully explained map of the afterlife. Whatever judgment is being made, it involves both the writer's suffering and his limits. Woland agrees to carry it out.

Azazello visits the lovers with wine. They drink, and their earthly lives come to an end. The sequence moves through the supernatural logic that has governed Woland's appearances; the lovers' departure is not described as an ordinary medical death followed by a documented journey. They join Woland and his companions, who ride out of Moscow as a storm approaches. The absurd disguises of the entourage fall away, revealing figures less like city pranksters and more like presences from another order of existence.

They travel to a place where Pontius Pilate remains, tormented by the memory of the day he condemned Yeshua. His faithful dog Banga stays beside him. For what seems an immeasurable time, Pilate has wanted to complete the conversation he abandoned through fear. Margarita sees his anguish and wants him freed. The Master is given the chance to finish the story he wrote. He releases Pilate, who can finally move toward the figure he could not defend while alive. The long-delayed act of mercy does not erase the original condemnation, but it ends Pilate's imprisonment in the same moment of cowardice.

The Master and Margarita are not sent along Pilate's road. They are given a quiet resting place together. It is not the same as returning to their old Moscow apartment, and not the triumphant public recognition a writer might have dreamed of. It is rest beyond the reach of the arguments, reviewers, administrators, and fear that shattered their earthly life. The image is tender without being entirely transparent. A reader can ask whether this is justice, mercy, limitation, or all three.

## The city after the visitors

The epilogue returns to Moscow. Officials explain the strange days through accounts of hypnotists, criminal tricks, and mass confusion. Many people resume something like normal life, although they are altered by experiences they cannot fully admit. Some retain humiliating memories. Some change jobs. The city has survived, but its explanations do not seem equal to what has taken place.

Ivan has become a scholar. He no longer pursues the literary career with which the book began. He can speak in the language of conventional explanation, yet each spring, when the moon is full, he is troubled by dreams. In those dreams the stories of Jerusalem and Moscow return. Pilate is no longer cut off forever from the man he condemned. The Master and Margarita appear, carrying a peace that Ivan can feel but cannot turn into a public proof.

The novel began with two literary men certain that the world had already been explained. It closes with a man who has learned a profession devoted to explaining the past, and who still receives a dream he cannot file away. In between, a writer lost his manuscript and recovered it; a woman risked her place in the world for love and showed mercy to someone she did not know; a Roman official was trapped by the memory of an act he could not undo; and Moscow discovered that authority, cleverness, and official documents are not the same thing as understanding.

That is the whole story's movement. It is at once a comedy of city life, a love story, a story of political fear, and a meditation on forgiveness. Its final resting place is not a solution that makes those strands identical. It is a place where the novel allows some of its wounded people to stop running from what they have done and what has been done to them.

## Sources and editorial distinctions

This is an original plot orientation to Mikhail Bulgakov's novel, composed mainly between the late 1920s and 1940 and published posthumously beginning in censored journal installments in 1966–67. Particular episodes and the manuscript's complex textual history vary in minor details across editions; the narration follows the central plot without inventing a definitive textual version.
