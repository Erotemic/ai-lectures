# The Lord of the Rings: How Tolkien Made a Modern Myth

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

Imagine beginning a sequel to a successful children's book and discovering, years later, that the sequel has become a historical romance of entire civilizations. The writer has to invent not only events but reasons why people remember events differently, languages that have changed across centuries, a chronology in which separate groups can plausibly reach distant places, and an ending that does not betray the emotional weight of the journey. That is one way to understand Tolkien's long labor on The Lord of the Rings.

The Hobbit appeared in 1937. Its success led the publisher to ask for more about hobbits. Tolkien began another story that year, but the manuscript gradually absorbed material from the far larger mythology he had been developing independently. It took many years, with interruptions and extensive rewriting, to reach a publishable form. The Fellowship of the Ring and The Two Towers appeared in 1954, and The Return of the King in 1955. These were three published volumes of a single long novel, internally organized into six books. The three-volume format was a practical publishing decision, not evidence that Tolkien conceived three tidy, self-contained plots.

That distinction helps us see the novel as an artistic structure. Its first movement travels outward from one hobbit's house. Its middle separates characters, sometimes following one set of travelers for many chapters before returning to another. Its later movement places military spectacle alongside the obscure suffering of two individuals moving through Mordor. Its conclusion looks beyond the destruction of the Ring into the difficulty of living after victory. The form changes the scale on which the word hero can be understood.

## The manuscripts are evidence of labor

The Tolkien Estate's account of the book's composition describes draft after draft, revisions in several colors, complicated chronologies, and maps revised repeatedly to match the travels of characters. The original manuscripts are held in substantial part by Marquette University, where researchers can examine the history of drafts through the Anduin system. The documents matter because they challenge a comforting myth about worldbuilding: that a fully worked-out fictional universe arrives whole in its creator's imagination and the novel is merely a tour through it.

Tolkien made choices, changed them, revised names, tested dates, and rethought the relations among characters. The sense of deep historical consistency did not appear without painstaking editorial work. He also used techniques that allowed history to remain suggestive rather than fully explained. His appendices record chronologies, alphabets, genealogies and later histories, but his main plot often lets a ruin or a song imply more than a formal explanation could convey.

His invented languages were central to this imaginative process. Carl Hostetter explains that Tolkien had been developing Elvish languages for decades before The Lord of the Rings appeared. The evolution of words and names helped generate the fictional peoples and histories that would give those words meaning. This reverses the assumption that fictional names are always ornaments pasted onto an already plotted adventure. For Tolkien, language could be one of the sources from which the story grew.

## A novel that pretends to be a recovered book

The Lord of the Rings has a frame that readers can easily overlook. It presents itself as a translation of a record left by its participants, associated with Bilbo, Frodo and Sam and later preserved and transmitted. The idea of the Red Book allows the novel to behave at once like an old chronicle and like a self-conscious literary artifact. The presence of appendices, calendars and language notes reinforces that impression, even though the frame is part of the fiction.

The device shapes the reader's trust. We are invited to believe the narrated world has records, gaps, variants and inherited names. It also makes the story's ending a transition in authorship. Frodo writes, and Sam is given a role in preserving and completing the account. History is not merely what kings announce after the battle. It is also what a gardener who survived the journey remembers and carries forward.

Tolkien shifts register across the narrative. Shire dialogue can be comic and practical. The speech in Rohan may sound close to heroic verse. The language associated with Mordor can be harsh and threatening. Moments of grief can become lyrical, while scenes of battle sometimes assume the distant perspective of a chronicle. The shifts give different peoples their own relations to memory and public life. A criticism that calls the work simply archaic misses how often it is deliberately plain, domestic and funny.

## The art of interlacing separate lives

Once the Fellowship breaks, Tolkien does not constantly cross-cut in the way a modern screen drama might. Large stretches follow Aragorn, Merry and Pippin; then another book turns to Frodo and Sam. The effect is to make information unevenly distributed. A reader knows that Gandalf has returned while Frodo does not. A captain in Gondor may act on evidence that is incomplete or misleading. Hope and despair are not merely states of mind; they arise from limited access to the whole story.

The critic John R. Holmes, in the 2022 *Companion to J. R. R. Tolkien*, identifies this intricate interlace structure as an important technical achievement. The novel uses sequence not only to control suspense but to create a moral experience of partial knowledge. The characters must act without the reassurance of being given the correct final chapter beforehand. Aragorn's march to the Black Gate is meaningful because he cannot verify Frodo's survival. Sam's decision to take the Ring is meaningful because he believes Frodo has died. Knowledge arrives late, and sometimes the decision cannot be undone.

The book also asks for pauses. Literary scholar Vincent Ferré has argued that its landscapes, contemplations and apparent delays are central rather than incidental to its adventure. Lothlórien cannot be reduced to the gift of useful equipment. The Old Forest is not only a detour around the riders. Songs are not just illustrations of history; they are occasions when people reckon with beauty, grief and endurance. Remove those scenes and you can preserve a chain of events while losing much of the novel's point.

## Fantasy before and after Tolkien

Tolkien did not invent the use of imaginary worlds, magic, quests, mythic creatures, or heroic romance. Earlier traditions and writers had already made important work in these forms. His achievement was to bring linguistic invention, imagined historical depth, travel narrative, war, fellowship, and a particular moral imagination into a single sustained work that became a major reference point for later fantasy. To call Tolkien the inventor of fantasy would erase the predecessors whose work made his achievement possible. His influence is better understood as a transformation of existing traditions than as a creation from nothing.

The work's enormous later influence also creates a trap for readers meeting it now. Many of its techniques have become familiar through novels, role-playing games, films and video games that borrowed or transformed them. An invented map, a company of travelers with different abilities, ancient swords with histories, a dark ruler, and a decisive journey can appear conventional because generations of successors have made these devices conventional. We should not mistake the experience of encountering a source after its descendants for evidence that the source lacked originality.

Nor should we measure Tolkien only by whether modern fantasy has preserved every one of his assumptions. The tradition has also changed in response. Later writers have questioned inherited monarchy, racialized species, the arrangement of gender roles, and the treatment of ordinary labor. They have explored worlds in which the old sources of moral legitimacy are uncertain. Tolkien's importance includes the arguments his work made possible, not simply the stories that imitate its surface features.

## Tolkien and allegory

Tolkien explicitly resisted the idea that the Ring must stand for one object in twentieth-century history and that the story was composed as a coded account of a particular war. He preferred to leave readers room to find applicability, rather than placing a single controlling allegorical interpretation over the work. We should take that preference seriously without treating the author's statement as a ban on historical or political reading. A writer may dislike direct allegory and still be shaped by war, technology, religion, grief and the society in which he lives.

The book's attention to industrial devastation can resonate with readers concerned about ecological damage. Its depiction of coercion can resonate with readers who have lived under authoritarian power. Its homecoming can speak to people who return from war unable to feel at home. These are legitimate conversations between a text and a reader's world. They become weaker when presented as proof that each character or machine was intended to decode into a single real person or event.

Tolkien's moral language also resists a simple secular-versus-religious division. He created a mythology in which mercy, providence, temptation, mortality and hope have recognizable religious depth. Yet the novel rarely turns these into explicit instruction. People encounter moral demands through relationships and choices. A reader with no interest in accepting Tolkien's theological commitments can still understand the narrative significance of a hero who reaches the limit of endurance and is saved through consequences of earlier mercy.

## The inheritance of a book

Tolkien's enormous influence is a reason to examine his work closely, not to shelter it from criticism. The text contains choices about who speaks, which cultures are foregrounded, and what forms of authority appear natural. It deserves to be read alongside critical scholarship about race, gender, class and environmental imagination, not sheltered from those questions. The 2022 *Companion* addresses several such lines of study. A work becomes richer when its strengths are examined with the same care as its difficulties.

One lasting lesson for storytelling is the difference between a catalog of imagined facts and a world with a past. Tolkien can tell us the name of a sword, but the name matters when someone carries it into a battle shaped by earlier losses. He can invent a language, but it matters when a character hears a name with affection or grief. He can create kingdoms, but their flags matter when the people beneath them make decisions with inadequate information. His achievement is not that he filled the most pages with fictional details. It is that he made details capable of bearing human consequence.

And one lasting lesson for endings is that the victory does not end the story's responsibilities. The king returns. The enemy is defeated. The land is damaged. Some people come home and some cannot. The book closes not on the claim that history has been completed, but on the renewed life of those who must go on living in a world that remains fragile. This is a modern myth not because it has solved the world, but because it has given durable imaginative form to the difficulty of saving what can be saved.
