# The Lord of the Rings: Kingship, Women, Peoples, and Unequal Voices

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

The Lord of the Rings invites us to cheer the return of a king. That ending can feel either satisfying or uncomfortable, depending on what we think political legitimacy should require. Aragorn is courageous, patient, and protective of those under his care. He also inherits a dynastic claim. The story treats the joining of those qualities as the recovery of a rightly ordered world. But the phrase rightly ordered raises a question: ordered for whom, and who gets to say?

Tolkien wrote within traditions of romance, medieval heroic literature, Christian moral imagination, and British social experience. These traditions give his novel strengths that are inseparable from its limitations. The book can make service more important than display and still imagine society through inherited rank. It can give Éowyn one of its most memorable acts of courage and still leave much of its public history to men. It can show sympathy for an enemy soldier's humanity while drawing some enemy peoples in broad, alienating strokes. A serious reading has room for all three observations.

## Aragorn's claim is not enough by itself

Aragorn's bloodline connects him with an old royal order, but the novel withholds his public kingship until he has repeatedly acted as a protector. He guides the hobbits in danger, refuses to exploit the Ring, honors Boromir, organizes the pursuit of Merry and Pippin, accepts help from those not under his command, and risks his life at the Black Gate. After battle he becomes a healer. These deeds do not erase the importance of descent in Tolkien's fiction, but they keep legitimate authority from becoming merely the discovery of the right family tree.

The comparison with Denethor clarifies the issue. Denethor has a public office, intelligence, and longstanding sacrifice on behalf of Gondor. He is not a fraud who has never earned respect. Under strain, however, he comes to see the city increasingly as an extension of his own will. When he cannot determine its fate, he tries to determine the fate of his son. His despair becomes domination. Aragorn's better leadership is partly the capacity to imagine a future that other people can inhabit without owing every action to him.

Still, a reader need not accept hereditary kingship as the natural institutional answer to political disorder. Tolkien is presenting a mythic romance, not a constitutional design study. The question worth asking is what the novel's symbols accomplish. The returning king represents a restoration of remembered obligations, a reconnection of divided realms, and a kind of authority that ought to heal. We can recognize those literary meanings while retaining skepticism about political systems that depend on the personal virtue of a ruler.

## Éowyn is not a single slogan

Éowyn's story illustrates the danger of reading characters only as evidence for a thesis about the author. She is proud, brave, angry, and grieving. She is assigned care work in a household debilitated by Wormtongue and by Théoden's despair. When the king prepares for war, the same world that celebrates courage in his riders expects her to wait behind. Her desire for action is therefore not merely personal impatience. It arises from unequal access to the public forms in which her society recognizes honor.

Her decision to ride in disguise and face the Witch-king is a genuine act of agency. She saves Théoden from further humiliation and takes a risk that seasoned soldiers have reason to fear. The text does not simply punish her for crossing a boundary: the enemy is defeated through her courage and Merry's. Yet the experience nearly kills her. In the Houses of Healing she moves toward a different future with Faramir, one in which she wants to help lives flourish rather than pursue death in battle.

Two simplifications are tempting. One is to say the ending returns a rebellious woman to a safely domestic role and therefore cancels her earlier courage. The other is to say her arc is perfectly liberating because she was permitted a great military deed. Neither accounts for the full pattern. Éowyn's choice to heal can be meaningful without settling whether the culture that constrained her was fair. Her rejection of death-seeking is not the same as submission to every expectation. But the novel does not present a comprehensive transformation of gender relations, and we should not invent one to make a favored book satisfy us.

Galadriel and Arwen create different problems for interpretation. Galadriel is an ancient ruler whose refusal of the Ring is one of the work's decisive acts. She has authority, memory and an agency that does not depend on a male protagonist's permission. Arwen's choice to marry Aragorn and accept mortality is profoundly consequential, but the main quest narrative gives her relatively little direct speech or action. The appendices offer a fuller account of her history and loss. Readers can recognize the seriousness of her choice while questioning why the main narrative grants the woman who helps shape its future kingdom comparatively little dramatic presence.

## Whose voices reach us?

The fellowship crosses several cultures and makes the overcoming of inherited distrust a moral achievement. Legolas and Gimli begin amid an old Elf–Dwarf antagonism. By the end, each can see beauty in the other's homeland. Their friendship pushes back against the collective labels that fill the story: Elves, Men, Dwarves, Orcs, Southrons, Easterlings, Rohirrim. Collective names can help create a large map quickly, but they can also disguise the individual lives contained within them.

Tolkien occasionally interrupts that flattening. In Ithilien, Sam considers a fallen Southron soldier and wonders what could have brought him to war, and whether he really wanted to be there. The thought is brief but morally consequential. The soldier is no longer only a body in an enemy formation. He has become someone with a possible home, a history and a set of constraints that the protagonists do not know. On the ride to Minas Tirith, the Drúedain under Ghân-buri-Ghân provide essential guidance through routes the Rohirrim cannot find themselves. They are not simply another faction to be conquered or a picturesque obstacle. Their knowledge changes the outcome.

Other aspects remain troubling. Enemy forces are often described in ways that use bodily appearance, direction of origin and inherited enmity as shortcuts to moral legibility. The Orcs in particular raise a persistent question. If they are thinking and speaking beings, capable of complaint, loyalty and fear, how do they fit a moral world that usually treats them as a category of enemies rather than as individuals with recoverable futures? Tolkien struggled with the imagined origins and moral condition of Orcs across his writings, and later materials do not offer a single simple answer that settles the published novel.

Recent scholarship has taken gender, race and diversity in Tolkien seriously; this is not a topic invented by hostile readers. But the presence of such scholarship does not mean every criticism has been proven. We should distinguish what the text directly does, what it leaves out, what readers infer from its patterns, and what the author is documented as having believed. A metaphor about East and West is not automatically a direct manifesto on a modern nation or ethnicity. At the same time, a work's invented setting does not make its patterns of representation immune to scrutiny.

## Care, status, and a hierarchy worth questioning

Some of the novel's most moving relationships run across status differences. Sam is Frodo's gardener, yet repeatedly saves his life. The small hobbits become indispensable to kings and captains. Healing offers Aragorn a way to exercise royal authority that is intimate rather than theatrical. But the society remains shaped by inherited distinctions. It often values the honorable servant and the wise master rather than asking whether people should be born into fixed roles of service.

A modern reader can find admirable material here without accepting the arrangement in full. Service can be freely chosen. Care work can be the foundation of public life. Humility can restrain power. Those insights do not require class relations to remain unchallenged. Tolkien's fictional world gives us powerful representations of loyalty inside hierarchy and moments when that hierarchy becomes morally inadequate to the people living within it.

The same holds for royal restoration. Aragorn's public conduct makes him worthy within the book's moral vocabulary, but the question of how a society ensures good leadership after a good individual dies remains largely outside the story. The return of the king is a satisfying ending for a romance built around loss and continuity; it is not a substitute for debating institutions, representation and accountability in actual political life.

If we love a novel, criticism can feel like an attempt to take it away. If we dislike some of its assumptions, admiration can feel like an attempt to excuse them. Neither reaction is necessary. The Lord of the Rings has endured partly because it imagines goodness with great force, and it is important enough to invite questions about the people its imagination centers. Reading both its generosity and its limits is a way of taking the work more seriously, not less.
