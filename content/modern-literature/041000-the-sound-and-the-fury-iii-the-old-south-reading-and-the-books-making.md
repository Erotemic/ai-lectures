# The Sound and the Fury III: the old South, reading, and the book’s making

## Faulkner: What Exactly Is Falling Apart in the Old South?

The Sound and the Fury is often described as the story of a once-great Southern family falling into decay. That is accurate as a starting point and inadequate as an ending. It accepts too quickly the family's own scale of value. What made the Compsons great? Whose labor supported their property? Why does the loss of a pasture, a marriage prospect, or a place at Harvard feel to the family like the loss of a civilization? William Faulkner builds a family tragedy whose language of decline is inseparable from the history of race, class, and ownership in the American South.

The Compsons live in Jefferson, Mississippi, within the fictional Yoknapatawpha County. Faulkner invented a county with recurring families, roads, land disputes, memories, and institutions, allowing different works to illuminate one another. The Sound and the Fury appeared in nineteen twenty-nine, after the American Civil War and Reconstruction were long past but while their aftermath remained intensely present. The family claims standing through ancestry in a region where the old plantation order had relied on enslaved Black labor. The loss of white aristocratic influence is not the same thing as the disappearance of an innocent golden age.

The property transactions are central. Land has to be sold to support Caddy's wedding and Quentin's Harvard education. Those expenses are supposed to protect or elevate the family. They are the costs of trying to reproduce a status no longer backed by sufficient income. The pasture that Benjy remembers becomes a golf course. A place once known by bodily habits is transformed by a commercial use directed toward a different class of people. From one perspective it is progress in land development; from another it is a direct loss; from the family's public language it is evidence of decline. Faulkner lets those values collide.

Quentin's education is especially revealing. The family invests in a prestigious Northern institution as though entry into its cultural world will secure the value of the Compson name. But Quentin carries the household's ideology with him. His crisis makes the limits of education brutally visible: admission, tuition, and geography do not by themselves undo inherited beliefs about female sexuality and family honor. Money purchases access to a future, not freedom from the ideas that make that future uninhabitable for him.

Jason believes the family's problem is that he has been denied his rightful share. He is resentful about a banking opportunity linked to Caddy's failed marriage. He is employed in a store, speculates in cotton, and takes money meant for Caddy's daughter. He thinks of himself as efficient and persecuted; the reader sees a man clinging to class entitlement while adopting a brutally acquisitive ethic. His behavior is not simply a continuation of old aristocracy, nor is it honest adjustment to a modern economy. It is a mixture of the worst claims of both systems: inherited superiority without corresponding responsibility, and the pursuit of cash without ethical restraint.

Mrs. Compson imagines that the family's honor resides in her suffering and the maintenance of certain names. Her contempt for the daughter who has shamed her is sustained even while she is deceived about money that daughter sends. The mother values the outward sign of refusing contamination more than the reality of supporting a child. The family's conventions are therefore not merely cruel; they are dangerously ineffective as standards of judgment. Their language allows them to call exploitation respectability.

Jason’s complaints also open the book beyond Jefferson. He follows cotton prices through the financial communications of a national market, then blames the losses on distant institutions and people he does not understand. His anger includes antisemitic and racist stereotypes. Those claims belong to the character, not to an accurate account of market behavior or to facts we should repeat as explanations. His car, the traveling performers, and the telegraph connect a town that he wants to imagine as fully knowable with a larger economy he cannot control. Professor Wai Chee Dimock, in her Yale lectures, reads that expanding scale as a defining feature of Jason’s section. It makes him more historically intelligible without excusing his treatment of the people under his power.

The family's sense of tragedy also assigns different worth to different bodies. Benjy is renamed when his disability threatens the meaning attached to a prestigious family name. His movement, sexuality, and living arrangements are controlled by people who imagine that respectable households have the right to manage such matters. Caddy is expelled because her sexual history cannot be reconciled with that same respectability. Her daughter is kept, policed, and denied control over money that was sent for her support. Quentin's male anguish is treated as evidence of the family's moral seriousness; the women's constrained choices are treated as problems to be managed.

Now consider the Gibson family. Dilsey and her relatives are not owners of the house. They are Black workers sustaining its daily life. The white family performs ancestry and grievance while the Black family performs the care without which the estate would cease to function even on its diminished scale. This contrast is one way Faulkner tests the family's definition of greatness. If honor is measured by faithful responsibility rather than inherited rank, the people with the least formal authority may have the strongest claim to it.

The Confederate monument in the town square enters the novel's closing movement as part of the route by which Benjy orients himself. Its public meaning cannot be separated from the history of a region that commemorates a defeated Confederate cause. It is not necessary to pretend that Benjy understands a political symbol as a historian does. The point is that a place can be an ordinary sensory landmark for one person while carrying a highly charged public account of the past for others. Memory is never housed only inside individuals; it is also placed into streets, property boundaries, and civic monuments.

The novel also resists the idea of a single unified South. Jefferson contains wealth and poverty, white authority and Black communal institutions, the local store and the traveling show, family property and commercial leisure. The Harvard campus appears as another world, but it is linked financially and morally to the Mississippi estate. The story crosses those spaces to show that modernization does not arrive as one simple force replacing another. Old forms of power survive within new economic practices, and people learn to translate inherited privilege into contemporary grievance.

Faulkner published the novel just before the stock market crash in October nineteen twenty-nine. It is tempting to read the Compsons' failure as an allegory of the Great Depression, but their central narrative takes place in nineteen ten and nineteen twenty-eight. The story was written before the Depression became the national crisis it later was. The historical relation is more precise if we see a pre-crash Southern order already strained by older structures of class, racial hierarchy, family property, and new economic demands. Later readers may recognize resonances with the Depression, but these should not be retroactively inserted as the novel's originating context.

Nor does the book give a complete political account of the South. Faulkner's treatment of Black characters is limited, and the family history is filtered disproportionately through privileged white interior lives. Those limitations themselves belong to the history of American modernism. A novel may reveal contradictions in racial power without escaping every assumption of its own cultural setting. The result should sharpen interpretation rather than relieve the reader of the responsibility to compare the fiction with historical evidence.

What, finally, has fallen apart? Some property has been lost. A son has died. A sister has been banished. Household finances have been stolen and mismanaged. But the title's borrowed sense of futility does not require us to mourn the restoration of aristocratic authority. We can read the family's collapse as an exposure of the fragility of its ideals: a system designed to preserve honor that cannot care for its members, preserve truth, or respect the people whose labor sustains it.

That is why The Sound and the Fury remains a major historical novel even while its form seems preoccupied with private consciousness. The most intimate sensory details—the scent of Caddy, the hands of a watch, the contents of a cash box—are connected to property, prestige, gender rules, and the long aftermath of slavery. Faulkner's family tragedy becomes a historical one when we stop treating the Compsons' version of greatness as the final authority on the world they have lost.

## How to Read The Sound and the Fury Without Flattening It

The most helpful way to approach The Sound and the Fury is not to pretend that it will become easy once every date has been deciphered. A chronology is useful; a list of family members is useful; a map of Jefferson is useful. But the novel's real work begins when we ask why Faulkner lets each person construct such a different kind of account. We can practice that skill by returning to six scenes and testing what each scene tells us, what it leaves uncertain, and how its form changes its meaning.

First, consider the opening fence. A man calls to a caddie on a golf course. Benjy Compson hears something that recalls his sister Caddy. The chain of association is immediate, but the reader may not yet know the connection. We must keep two times active: the Saturday on which Benjy hears the call and the earlier years when Caddy belonged to his daily world. The golf course adds a third dimension: we learn that the family sold land that Benjy once knew as pasture. One sound thus joins wordplay, personal attachment, and economic history. Nothing in the scene announces all three. They become available as the novel supplies more evidence.

Try reading that opening as if it were an ordinary plot summary. You might say that Benjy and Luster walk by a fence and a golfer shouts. You would have captured the immediate event and missed most of its force. A more productive question is: what does the narrator's attention reveal about what matters to him? Benjy's attention is governed by a name and a lost relationship. The structure tells us that before it gives us an explanation. Form is carrying character information.

Second, return to the scene of Caddy climbing the tree while a death is hidden inside the family house. We can describe the action clearly: children outside, a girl climbing, boys below, adults withholding news. We can also trace distinct interpretations. For Benjy, Caddy's movement belongs to a network of affectionate memory. For Quentin, the sight anticipates his anxiety about a sister whose body he thinks he must regulate. For Jason, informing on other people is already part of his relation to power. Which meanings are directly established by the childhood scene, and which emerge because we have read the adult sections? A good close reading keeps that distinction visible.

The tree also lets us ask who gets to look. Caddy wants access to information the adults deny. She moves into the place from which she can see. The boys remain below and see her instead. A scene about a family death becomes a scene about knowledge and the gaze. It would be reductive to call it merely a prediction of future sexual shame. It is also a demonstration that the girl can act in a way the boys do not, even though the novel will give the boys the central narrative voices.

Third, look at Quentin's watch. He breaks off the hands and still hears its mechanism. We could label the object a symbol of time, then stop. But that would not explain what the symbol does. Quentin is physically able to damage the display of a machine while remaining unable to change the passing day. His mind keeps revisiting Caddy's past even as he approaches his own planned death. The contradiction between visible control and actual power appears at the scale of a small object. What other objects in the section carry that kind of double meaning? Consider water, the heavy flat-irons, the places where he crosses paths with strangers, and the clothes he arranges before the end.

Fourth, listen to Jason's accounting. He explains the checks Caddy sends for her daughter as though he is an honest executor of the family's judgment upon a disgraced woman. Then follow the transaction instead of the accusation. Who sent the money? Who was supposed to benefit? Who actually took control of it? What did Mrs. Compson believe she was burning? The answers expose a deception beneath the apparently straightforward voice. Here the hardest work is not placing scenes in time; it is refusing to be guided by the narrator's categories of innocence and guilt.

The point generalizes. A narrator who knows what day it is can still misunderstand a person. Jason's confident syntax makes him easier to follow than Benjy, but it can make him more dangerous to believe. A strong reader distinguishes reliability about events from reliability about motives. Jason can accurately report that his niece left the house and still be profoundly wrong about why the conflict exists and who has been harmed. We need not deny every fact he states to reject the moral structure he builds around those facts.

Fifth, turn to Dilsey preparing the house on Easter morning, and then to the sermon at the Black church. What changes when narration leaves the brothers' first-person consciousness? We see an elderly woman moving through practical tasks and a community gathering through words, song, and bodily response. We also see a third-person narrator selecting what to show and where to turn our attention. This is a widening of perspective, not a transparent window into every member of the congregation. Notice whose labor is described, whose private motives remain offstage, and what the service offers that no one in the Compson house has been able to offer.

One can read the Easter service as the book's answer to despair. That is a defensible reading if supported by the language of resurrection and Dilsey's reaction. It is not defensible to conclude that the service eliminates the family's economic conflicts or the segregation in which the church exists. Interpretation becomes stronger when we specify the level of a claim. The service changes a way of understanding suffering. It does not enact a redistribution of land or wages. A critic can value the spiritual scene while asking what the novel leaves socially unresolved.

Sixth, return to the final carriage. Luster drives Benjy by an unfamiliar route around the town square. Benjy cries until Jason restores the expected direction. The immediate experience is one of distress followed by relief. A reading of the family's fortunes might call this a temporary restoration of order. A reading of public history might attend to the monument and the Southern past placed at the square's center. A reading of care might ask who chose the route, who responded to Benjy, and whose authority made that response possible. None of these interpretations invalidates the others automatically. They require different kinds of evidence.

Now bring the scenes into a single method. Begin with what happens in the text, in language that does not yet assume a motive. Then identify whose viewpoint organizes that account. Then ask what details are repeated elsewhere under a different description. Finally, distinguish the interpretation you infer from the fact the text establishes. You can keep a rough chart of dates, characters, locations, and recurrent objects, but the chart is an aid to thinking, not the thing being studied.

For an audio listener, one practical strategy is to hear the novel's three named voices as three methods of selection. Benjy selects through sensory association. Quentin selects through emotional obsession and disrupted language. Jason selects through accusation and account-keeping. The fourth section gives a wider narrative frame in which work and public events become visible. The divisions are not watertight—each section contains more than one tendency—but they prevent the difficult beginning from seeming arbitrary. The author is not hiding a simple novel under a maze. He is making us confront the effects of different minds and social positions upon the same past.

You can use digital scholarship to test chronology without outsourcing interpretation. The University of Virginia's Digital Yoknapatawpha project records events and maps from Faulkner's fiction, while explaining that some temporal placements involve scholarly judgment. A reader who uses such a resource should ask not only whether an event has been dated but what textual evidence supports the date. It is possible to learn the calendar more exactly and become less dogmatic about a narrator's meaning.

After these six scenes, the novel should feel less like a locked puzzle and more like a set of competing claims. The task is not to achieve one final, panoramic voice that makes all the others obsolete. It is to understand how affection, fear, shame, money, labor, and historical memory become different stories according to the person allowed to tell them. When Faulkner changes narrative method, he changes what the reader must do to hear the world.

## The Sound and the Fury: How the Book Was Made and Why It Matters

The Sound and the Fury was published in New York in October nineteen twenty-nine, just before the stock market crash. It did not immediately become an easy commercial success. The novel asked readers to begin inside the sensory experience of Benjy Compson, then move through Quentin's fragmented last day, Jason's relentless accusations, and a final third-person section centered in part on Dilsey. Its difficulty was not the result of a careless failure to put chapters into order. The four-part construction was the form through which Faulkner made the Compson family's history into several competing experiences of reality.

The novel's title comes from Shakespeare's Macbeth. In the final act, Macbeth speaks of life as a story of noise and anguish that seems to lack enduring significance. Faulkner takes a phrase from that speech, but his novel is not a demonstration that human experience is meaningless. Its first narrative consciousness, Benjy's, is an explicit challenge to the assumption that literary significance depends upon a narrator's capacity for conventional explanation. The reader can perceive love, grief, and injustice through a speaker who does not know how to formulate those abstract categories.

Faulkner later described the book's origin in an image of children outside the house while a family death is concealed from them. Caddy climbs a tree to look through a window; the brothers below see her. The anecdote is illuminating because the eventual novel does not settle for the child's view of a single incident. It returns to a network of family relationships through increasingly different formal approaches. The story grows not through the accumulation of more sensational events but through changes in who is able to tell what. A writer dissatisfied with one perspective does not simply correct it; he lets a different kind of consciousness expose what the first could not.

The book's text has an important publication history. Faulkner wanted temporal transitions in Benjy's section marked more richly than printing economics permitted. In letters and later public discussion he described a wish to use different colors of ink. The original edition made use of typography, including italics, but the precise relation between such signals and changes of time is not mechanical. In a much later Folio Society edition, scholars produced a fourteen-color arrangement for Benjy's section. That edition is a valuable experiment in making chronology visible. It is not an author-produced color manuscript recovered from nineteen twenty-nine, nor proof that Faulkner finally settled on precisely fourteen time layers.

This distinction is part of textual scholarship. A reader may encounter the nineteen twenty-nine first-edition text, later corrected texts, and editions with scholarly apparatus. Noel Polk's editorial work has been important in the history of restoring and explaining the novel's text. Modern critical editions can help readers navigate questions of variant wording, punctuation, context, and racial language. But no editorial system should pretend to abolish interpretive uncertainty. The temptation to make a difficult book mechanically clear can produce a different book.

One particular later addition deserves separate treatment. In nineteen forty-five Faulkner wrote a Compson family appendix for Malcolm Cowley's anthology The Portable Faulkner, which appeared the following year. The appendix elaborates a genealogy and the family's history across centuries, with information beyond the original novel's ending. It has been printed before or after the novel in some editions and omitted from others. The appendix may illuminate the author's later thinking, but it should not be silently folded into a plot account of the original work. What the reader of nineteen twenty-nine knew at the final carriage scene is not identical to what a reader learns from the appendix.

The importance of the anthology belongs to a larger critical history. Faulkner had admirers well before Cowley edited The Portable Faulkner, and his reputation did not depend upon one intervention alone. But the anthology helped new readers perceive the connections among works set in Yoknapatawpha County and assisted the revival of serious attention to Faulkner after a period of uneven availability and reception. In the years after the Second World War, literary critics increasingly studied his formal experiments in detail. The Sound and the Fury became a central object of American literary scholarship, not merely a notorious novel said to be hard to read.

Faulkner received the Nobel Prize in Literature for nineteen forty-nine, with the award presented in nineteen fifty. The sequence matters when sources describe him as a nineteen forty-nine laureate and report a Stockholm speech delivered in December nineteen fifty. The honor was not awarded for one book alone, and it should not be used as a substitute for literary analysis. It does show that an author whose early formal experiments had resisted broad immediate popularity became an internationally recognized figure. His influence extended well beyond Southern regional fiction.

What, concretely, made this novel influential? One feature is the way it uses different consciousnesses without granting a final authoritative explanation. Another is its radical juxtaposition of temporal layers, permitting remembered events to appear as present sensation. A third is its ethical challenge: readers must recognize that clear language does not ensure truthful judgment, and that an absent or marginalized character can be more central than those who possess narrative control. These are formal resources that later writers and critics could use in many settings. Claims of direct influence on a particular author, however, require evidence of actual reading or reception, not mere similarity.

Faulkner belongs in a modernist conversation with writers such as James Joyce and Virginia Woolf. All three explored ways of representing consciousness and the limits of straightforward chronology. Yet similarity does not mean that each technique was borrowed from one identifiable source. Literary history has to distinguish a movement's shared problems from documented lines of transmission. The Southern history of Faulkner's fiction also differs in decisive ways from Joyce's Dublin and Woolf's England. His technique is not an imported style floating free of place; it is bound to land, racial hierarchy, family names, and the aftermath of slavery in his fictional Mississippi.

The novel also generated arguments about what responsible reading should value. One school emphasizes the intricacy of its form and the aesthetic accomplishment of combining conflicting accounts. Other approaches ask what the same form reveals about white Southern power, Black labor, disability, masculinity, and women's exclusion from narrative authority. These are not necessarily rival disciplines. The book's narrative method determines which lives we enter and which claims we are tempted to believe. Formal analysis and ethical criticism meet in the decision to give Caddy no narrative section and to reserve the major Black-centered episode for a third-person account.

There is also something to learn from the author's own dissatisfaction. Faulkner sometimes described The Sound and the Fury as an attempt he could not quite get right, and he returned to the Compson material in later writing. Such remarks should not be treated as a final verdict on the novel's value, any more than later praise should make its limitations invisible. They suggest a writer for whom form was a persistent problem of telling, not a puzzle assembled once and for all. A novel can become a landmark partly because the solution it offers is imperfect and generative.

Finally, consider why the book belongs in a course on the most consequential twentieth-century literature. Its reputation does not rest simply on the novelty of four dated sections. It asks readers to become active in reconstructing events and cautious in assigning moral authority. It makes a family's private memories inseparable from social arrangements of race, gender, labor, property, and power. It shows that the ability to narrate one's experience is neither evenly distributed nor a reliable sign of wisdom. If you finish its final page with a chronology and nothing more, you have solved only one of its problems. If you can hear the four sections as different instruments sounding against one another, you have begun to recognize the literary discovery that made this work endure.
