# Ulysses II: changing language, epic form, kinship, and women

## Ulysses: How a Novel Can Think in More Than One Language

Imagine telling the story of a man who goes to work, attends a funeral, eats lunch, visits friends, and finally returns to his wife. You could describe what he does. You could explain what he thinks. You could alternate dialogue and description and hope your readers came to know him. James Joyce does all this in Ulysses, but he discovers another possibility. Instead of using a stable narrative voice to report a whole day, he allows the forms of language encountered in that day to alter the form of the novel itself.

Ulysses is often introduced as the great novel of stream of consciousness, as though one technique could account for the whole work. That phrase names a genuine part of its technique, but it is not a sufficient description of the entire work. As Pericles Lewis observes in Yale's Modernism Lab, Joyce's initial exploration of interior monologue develops into a far wider array of styles. The novel is not one unbroken transcript from inside a skull. It is a changing experiment in how inner life, public discourse, literary tradition, and bodily experience interact.

## The mind is not a polished speech

At the beginning, Stephen Dedalus's thoughts move through quotation, memory, perception, philosophy, and resentment. When he walks on Sandymount Strand, the boundary between what he sees and what he thinks is sometimes unmistakable and sometimes difficult to locate. To read the passage well, we need not translate every line into a neat proposition. We can ask what causes a thought to change direction. A sound recalls a word. A sight recalls an argument. An argument reopens a grievance.

Bloom's thoughts are different. He notices how things work. A shop front suggests an improvement in advertising. A passing face suggests a story about someone's family. A smell becomes appetite or nausea. He can think about a kitchen and a dead child in close succession without explaining the movement to himself. A traditional narrator might announce that the domestic detail symbolizes mourning. Joyce makes us experience the adjacency before asking us to interpret it.

This technique can be called interior monologue, but even that term requires care. The words on the page are not a tape recording of private thought. They are selected and arranged by Joyce. Sometimes a narrator's sentence slips toward a character's diction without introducing a quotation. Sometimes a bodily sensation breaks into a passage of formal commentary. Sometimes the prose is deliberately ambiguous about whose vocabulary we hear. Literary form does not vanish when the narrator stops explaining; it becomes more difficult to recognize.

## The newspaper and the trap of a headline

Consider Aeolus, the episode in which Bloom negotiates advertising and Stephen discusses rhetoric at a newspaper office. The text is interrupted by headlines. A headline promises to identify the news, give it importance, and package it for circulation. By imposing headline form on scenes that can scarcely bear the weight, Joyce demonstrates how public language can inflate, redirect, or trivialize what happened.

A newspaper can make anything seem official by printing it in a certain way. Yet headlines are not always lies. They are conventions that compress events for a reader's attention. Joyce's satire works because this useful convention can also substitute a ready-made emphasis for understanding. The chapter invites us to distinguish the event from the terms in which institutions make it consumable.

In Wandering Rocks, by contrast, the problem is not compression but distribution. Nineteen street movements give us pieces of overlapping life. The reader becomes a tentative citywide observer but never acquires total omniscience. Snippets of one itinerary appear in another. A reader who notices the interference can begin to reconstruct chronology, but the effect is not simply a crossword. It is the experience of a society in which each person's important story is background to another person's afternoon.

## Music without a score

Sirens starts with fragments that sound detached from meaningful narrative. Later their sounds recur in a bar full of songs and listeners. Reading the chapter is like hearing motifs introduced before one knows where they belong. A sound can correspond to a word, a voice, an action, or a memory. It can be funny until its return makes it painful.

The technique is often compared with musical fugue. The comparison is illuminating because of repetition and counterpoint, but we should not pretend the novel is a fully notated fugue whose formal rules can be checked like a conservatory exercise. The musical analogy guides attention to recurrence and variation. Joyce is exploring how a novel might make us listen to language as time rather than only extract propositions from it.

The result suits Bloom's situation. He hears songs that other customers enjoy together, but he also hears the private history of Molly, Rudy, and betrayal. Public music becomes the medium of solitude. No additional paragraph announcing his sadness would reproduce quite the same effect.

## A narrator who makes hostility sound ordinary

Cyclops offers a more political demonstration. An unnamed, sarcastic narrator tells us about the Citizen and Bloom in a Dublin pub. His language comes already loaded with judgments about who is annoying, who is generous, who belongs, and who deserves to be mocked. Readers who accept the narrator's tone without examination risk accepting his contempt. Meanwhile the episode erupts into inflated imitations of legal, biblical, heroic, and journalistic registers.

The little biscuit tin thrown at Bloom is treated with the verbal magnificence suitable to an enormous epic battle. We know the scale is ridiculous. Yet the question of who gets to control the telling is serious. Satirical language can expose a nationalist's prejudice; prejudiced language can also disguise violence as a public joke. The chapter makes us experience both processes at once.

It is helpful here to separate narratorial reliability from the moral views of a character. The unnamed speaker's account is partial and hostile. The hyperbolic passages are not neutral footnotes correcting him. They are further performances of public discourse. Our task is not to find a single plain narrator hiding behind them but to measure the distance between the actions and the tones used to describe them.

## What happens when the genre changes altogether?

In Oxen of the Sun the language moves through historical literary styles while a woman gives birth and young men debate and drink. We hear English prose aging toward the present. Why use linguistic history for a maternity scene? Because a style, like a language community, does not emerge fully formed. Every voice has been born from prior voices. The analogy is productive but also ironic. The male speakers' virtuosity does not mean they understand the laboring woman whose body supplies the occasion.

Circe goes further. Its form is a drama script with fantastically elaborate directions. A courtroom materializes out of Bloom's anxiety. Figures transform. Stephen's dead mother appears. The style of theatre allows events normally hidden within consciousness to be shown as if they occupied the same stage as actual policemen and brothel furniture. There is no need to decide that every hallucination is a supernatural fact. The visible convention of stage direction lets the work dramatize inward forces while keeping practical consequences in play.

Ithaca does something apparently opposite. It adopts the language of systematic questions and answers. After Nighttown's uncontrolled transformations, the catechism promises exhaustive knowledge. Exactly what happened? What was present? How can the relations of two people be classified? Yet its precision becomes absurdly excessive. The more it lists, the more visible the gap between factual inventory and mutual understanding. Knowing the motion of stars cannot tell us what Stephen means to Bloom.

Finally, Penelope allows Molly Bloom a long, lightly punctuated inward monologue. The ending is often celebrated as the restoration of an authentic female voice after so many male interpretations. It does give Molly a kind of authority the novel has long withheld from her. But nothing about a lack of periods makes words unmediated nature. Joyce has constructed this voice carefully, and Molly's memories, conventions, prejudices, and wishes remain her own partial world.

## Style is an ethical problem

The succession of styles is not simply a display of James Joyce's verbal genius. It repeatedly asks who gets described by whom, what language makes visible, and what it prevents others from seeing. The newspaper makes events saleable. The pub makes violence heroic. The hospital makes birth a literary performance. The catechism makes domestic intimacy measurable. Molly's monologue answers some of the representations imposed upon her, but it also reveals another consciousness that cannot be exhausted by a label.

The structural risk is obvious. An elaborate technique might become a game that leaves people behind. Joyce repeatedly returns from that risk to the bodies and losses underneath it: a dead child, a funeral, the desire to be welcomed, a stranger struck by a soldier, a woman lying awake in bed. His novel is experimental because he suspects a single narrative language would falsify the amount of life one day contains.

To understand Ulysses is not to identify every technique by name. It is to recognize what each technique does to our relation with the people and events it presents. That is why the style keeps changing. No voice, not even an extraordinarily intelligent one, can claim a city to itself.

## Ulysses: An Epic Hidden in an Ordinary Day

Why does a novel about a Dublin advertising salesman bear the title Ulysses? Ulysses is the Latin name of Odysseus, the Greek traveler who spends ten years struggling to reach home after the Trojan War. Homer's Odyssey describes monsters, gods, seafaring, hospitality, fatherhood, and the long-awaited return to Ithaca. Joyce offers a man carrying an advertisement through newspaper offices, a young teacher with nowhere to sleep, and a singer lying awake in a bed. The comparison is neither a joke that can be dismissed nor a mechanical key that unlocks every sentence.

Understanding the relationship requires us to ask what is gained and lost when an epic travels from a heroic age into a modern city.

## The correspondence is real, but flexible

Joyce planned Ulysses with an elaborate system of correspondences. His surviving schemes assign episodes Homeric counterparts, particular times, organs, arts, colors, symbols, and techniques. The Morgan Library's exhibition of the author's schemes establishes that these were part of the work's development and its explanation to selected readers. But Joyce's Homeric plans became more elaborate during composition, and he later had doubts about the way public circulation of the schemes could distort reading.

Joyce did design these Homeric correspondences; they were not simply imposed by later professors. But the chart cannot substitute for the novel. If every scene must perfectly reproduce an episode from Homer, we will be tempted to force the text into an answer sheet. Joyce is exploring what happens when a familiar cultural structure is stretched across material that resists it.

Stephen's morning forms a Telemachiad, recalling Odysseus's son Telemachus searching for news of his father. Stephen has a living father, Simon, but the relationship is difficult. He also struggles with a dead mother and the question of spiritual lineage. To call Stephen a fatherless son is therefore a useful but incomplete metaphor. We should not erase Simon or confuse symbolic deprivation with literal orphanhood.

Bloom resembles Odysseus in wandering through the city and returning home. Molly has a Penelope-like position as the woman at home, but the analogy is deliberately unsettled. Homer's Penelope is famous for resisting suitors during the wanderer's absence. Molly has sex with Boylan during Bloom's day away. This is not proof that she is simply the anti-Penelope, either. Her final recollection of Bloom's proposal complicates fidelity, memory, and the possibility of choosing someone while failing them.

## Hospitality matters more than monsters

Many correspondences are visible in comic miniatures. Homeric giants who devour sailors become a lunchtime chapter filled with appetite. The alluring Sirens become the singers and sounds of a Dublin bar. The one-eyed Cyclops becomes the Citizen, who can see his nation through only one idea of belonging and throws a biscuit tin after Bloom's carriage. Circe's dangerous transformations become a red-light district staged as extravagant hallucination.

These substitutions do not merely make ancient stories smaller. They test the moral vocabulary of epic. What counts as courage when there is no monster large enough to strike with a sword? Bloom argues against persecution in a room whose people can make him afraid. He refuses to abandon Stephen after the young man is knocked down. He visits a dying acquaintance's funeral with attention to the survivors' practical needs. These actions are not grand military achievements, but they resist the daily indifference that can harm people as effectively as an openly cruel act.

Notice that the Odyssey is also a story about hospitality. Odysseus survives because strangers offer shelter, and he also meets hosts who violate the rules of welcome. In Ulysses, a late-night cabman's shelter and Bloom's kitchen become sites where hospitality can occur without ceremony. Bloom does not have to be Stephen's father to offer cocoa. Stephen does not have to accept permanent residence to acknowledge the offer.

The names Eumaeus and Ithaca invite us to evaluate the quality of a homecoming rather than congratulate ourselves for recognizing a parallel. What has Bloom gained at the end of his travels? A chance to care for a young man and to return to his own domestic life. What has he not gained? Certainty about Molly, restoration of Rudy, or a marriage unscarred by loss. The epic ending has been broken into a smaller and more difficult form of persistence.

## Home is not the same as ownership

Stephen starts the day in a tower, but does not feel entitled to stay. Bloom has a house, but its emotional arrangement is uncertain. Molly has remained in the house, yet the novel's long silence about her inward life shows how easily a woman at home can become an object of other people's interpretation. Joyce repeatedly separates the idea of home from possession of property.

In Homer's poem, the returner's authority may be restored through the defeat of enemies and the reordering of the household. Joyce offers no such cleansing violence. Boylan is not killed or driven from Ireland. The marriage is not legally judged. Stephen is not ceremonially adopted. The house is entered, a cup of cocoa prepared, and a man goes to bed. A home is made, if at all, through acts that remain open to failure.

This difference has political force. Dublin in 1904 belonged to a country under British rule. The right to claim a home, a nation, a language, or a lineage was not straightforward. The Citizen's violent possessiveness of Ireland shows the danger of treating belonging as an exclusive title. Bloom's family history makes him suspect to some people who have never asked what his acts of citizenship and solidarity look like. In this setting, epic ownership must be examined rather than automatically admired.

## An epic of things usually left out

Traditional heroic poetry often records kings, battle, divine intervention, and the destinies of communities. Ulysses records digestion, advertisements, desire, female labor, bodily functions, misread notes, and unpaid bills. These are not presented to mock ordinary people for failing to be warriors. They are material from which the substance of any life is made.

Joyce's parallel with Homer can give a lunch immense resonance. But the lunch is not dignified only because it secretly resembles a myth. It also insists on its own importance: the body must be fed, the appetite is subject to taste and revulsion, and a person thinks while chewing. If we see the epic correspondence as the only source of value, we recreate the very hierarchy Joyce's novel is working to overturn.

The changing prose styles reinforce this argument. The epic has become not one polished song but a contest of voices: the journalist's headline, the nationalist's insult, the medical student's joke, the catechist's question, the singer's refrain. Heroism cannot simply belong to whoever has the most commanding language. One of the grandest-speaking men, the Citizen, is also one of the most dangerous.

## What the Homeric frame cannot settle

Some literary interpretations assign an almost sacred father-son reunion to Bloom and Stephen. It is tempting because Bloom's son died and Stephen's family relationships are troubled. But the two men's meeting ends with separation, not a new family contract. The point is not that the Homeric analogy has failed. It is that the analogy enables us to see the pathos of an incomplete encounter. The novel's characters remain more particular than the roles we borrow to describe them.

It is equally tempting to identify Molly with female earth, nature, or eternal sexuality and stop listening to her. The Penelope parallel can become another form of silencing if the reader treats her final monologue as a symbol rather than the speech of a specific woman with personal memories, grievances, and choices. The final voice is full of particulars that refuse a clean diagram.

A fruitful use of the Homeric system is therefore comparative. Ask what the named correspondence makes visible. Ask which differences are deliberate. Then return to the prose and to the needs of the character. The schema is a reading instrument, not a verdict.

Ulysses makes one of modern literature's strongest arguments about scale. If the ancient world could recognize a human life through the adventures of a returning warrior, a modern reader might learn to recognize it in a man trying to remain kind while the city makes him lonely. The epic has not vanished. It has been redistributed among people whom epic poetry might once have left in the background.

## Ulysses: Bloom, Stephen, and the Family That Cannot Quite Form

One of the pleasures of Ulysses is that James Joyce allows a young intellectual and a middle-aged advertising salesman to inhabit the same city without turning one into a mere lesson for the other. Stephen Dedalus is twenty-two, strikingly learned, short of money, and nearly helpless in certain ordinary affairs. Leopold Bloom is thirty-eight, curious about countless practical things, socially awkward, and capable of a kind of attention that Dublin's talkative men often mistake for weakness. Near the end, Bloom helps Stephen after a drunken confrontation with soldiers, brings him home, and offers him cocoa. Their meeting suggests a father and son, but it never becomes the complete reconciliation that the analogy seems to promise.

Why is this incompleteness important? Because the novel wants to know how people care for one another when resemblance does not erase difference.

## The young man who can explain everything

Stephen knows how to turn an event into an argument. He has read philosophical systems, theatrical traditions, and Catholic theology. In the tower he interprets Buck Mulligan's mockery as an injury to his mother's memory. At school he turns a history lesson into a judgment about human destiny. On the strand he thinks about the foundations of perception. In the library he builds a theory of Shakespeare's life and work.

His intelligence is real. Joyce does not treat literary education as a fraud. But Stephen's brilliance is inseparable from a fear of dependence. He needs a salary but despises the headmaster who pays it. He wants friendship but resents the laughing company that offers it on terms he cannot bear. He is alienated from his father yet cannot stop thinking in familial patterns. He rejects a religious duty at his mother's bedside and remains haunted by her final request.

The idea of being free from social authority is attractive to him precisely because so many authorities have claims upon him. He wants to become a writer, which means deciding what the past may demand from a living artist. But people do not become independent by declaration alone. Stephen still needs food and shelter; he still needs a person who will come back for him when he is injured.

## The older man who notices the small thing

Bloom's habits look less impressive. He worries about advertising space, funeral subscriptions, bills, social misunderstandings, and what people will think of his appearance. He is capable of technical speculation that is half practical invention, half daydream. He is also unusually alert to the needs of people who are absent from the scene. At Paddy Dignam's funeral he considers what the widow and children will require. At the maternity hospital he thinks about the woman giving birth. In Nighttown he follows a drunk man whose companions have not undertaken to protect him.

Care is not the same as moral perfection. Bloom conducts a pseudonymous flirtation with Martha Clifford. On the beach he masturbates while watching Gerty MacDowell. He can objectify women, indulge fantasy, resent Molly, and avoid open communication. Joyce's achievement is not to present a flawless humanitarian. It is to make a human being's ethical strengths visible without erasing his evasions and appetites.

Bloom's goodness appears in small choices he might easily have made otherwise. He might have remained silent when the Citizen baited him. He might have left Stephen to the police. He might have treated a widow's material situation as someone else's problem. The day shows him choosing attention more than once, alongside many occasions when he withdraws or hesitates.

## Two forms of fatherlessness

Stephen's mother has died. His father Simon lives but is an unstable source of security. Bloom's own father died by suicide, and Bloom carries the stigma and grief of that death. His son Rudy died as an infant. The two men meet at an intersection of different losses: one has a difficult father and a dead mother, the other a dead father and a dead son. The pattern is powerful, but it is not symmetrical.

It is tempting to say that Stephen is the son Bloom needs and Bloom the father Stephen needs. Joyce provides just enough suggestive detail to make the hypothesis emotionally persuasive. The young man's vulnerability reminds Bloom of lost parenthood. Bloom's attention supplies Stephen, briefly, with something other than mockery or instruction. But to make either man wholly satisfy the other's need would be to violate what the novel actually records. Stephen declines Bloom's invitation to stay and leaves the house.

That refusal is not simply a failure to recognize a perfect solution. It may protect Stephen's independence, or express his unease with Bloom, or reflect exhaustion and habitual restlessness. The narrative does not give an omniscient verdict. Bloom has offered hospitality. Stephen has not made a promise. We can value the encounter without pretending the future has been settled.

## The city keeps measuring people incorrectly

Other people habitually describe Stephen and Bloom by ready-made categories. Stephen is a clever young man who should get himself into print, a drunk who needs controlling, a possible genius to be teased. Bloom is an advertiser with peculiar interests, a husband whose wife's conduct can be whispered about, a person marked as Jewish, an object of suspicious jokes.

These categories may contain partial truths, but they are often used to prevent further knowledge. The Citizen's prejudice is an extreme form of the same reduction that appears in milder social comedy. Stephen's own theories can also reduce people to roles within an argument. He can use Shakespeare and his family as elements of an interpretive machine, just as others use Stephen as the designated young intellectual of a conversation.

The novel's changing styles resist such a conclusion. We are not allowed to view either man through just one set of social terms. Bloom's breakfast is as much part of him as his defense of love against hatred. Stephen's tired and frightened body is as much part of him as his learned Shakespeare theory. The meaning of each life grows from accumulating relations, not from one decisive label.

## Is Bloom really the hero?

The word hero can mislead if it implies mastery, victory, and superiority. Bloom loses arguments, avoids conflict, forgets items, suffers humiliation, and goes home to an uncertain marriage. Yet in a novel whose mythic framework invokes Odysseus, he has a claim to the title because he keeps responding to human need. His acts are limited. He cannot make Stephen stay. He cannot undo his son's death. He cannot know the future of his marriage. He can feed a cat, attend a funeral, intervene on a street, and make cocoa.

Stephen can also be understood generously. His refusal to kneel at his mother's deathbed may be a defense of conscience that remains painful precisely because the person he loved asked him to do otherwise. His intellectual suspicion can expose pretension and historical violence. To say he needs kindness is not to say he must surrender his right to judge.

The central relation thus does not divide the novel into cleverness and goodness. Both men are capable of wit, perception, fantasy, error, and sympathy; they practice these capacities differently. Their differences give the meeting substance. A perfectly matched pair could become a symbol. Two awkward men asking each other questions over cocoa remain people.

## The home that waits beyond the encounter

There is a third person essential to this account: Molly Bloom. A reading focused on Bloom and Stephen can turn Molly into the place to which the older man returns, or the unfaithful woman whose behavior makes his kindness heroic. The final monologue refuses that use of her. Molly is neither prize nor obstacle. She remembers, desires, resents, and makes judgments in a voice that the two men cannot fully control.

That ending also alters our view of Bloom's longing for a restored family. The dead Rudy cannot be replaced, the living Milly is becoming independent, and Molly's private life will not become transparent because her husband wishes it. Family is not an equation in which one missing member can be canceled by a new variable. It is a set of relationships whose pleasures and injuries cannot be neatly totaled.

The gift Bloom gives Stephen is temporary and concrete. He offers his time when the young man most needs someone to stay nearby. The gift Stephen gives Bloom is harder to describe: for a night, Bloom can direct some of his paternal attention toward a living person. It does not heal the original loss. But an act need not cure grief to be worthwhile.

That is one way to understand the novel's humanism. Not as a theory that every person is good underneath, and not as a demand to forgive every injury. It is a commitment to seeing what a person does and suffers when the city's preferred story about him is incomplete. Two people can matter to one another without becoming each other's destiny.

## Ulysses: Molly, Gerty, and the Problem of Speaking for Women

For much of Ulysses, Molly Bloom is a person known through what other people say and imagine about her. We hear that she is a singer. We see Leopold Bloom bring her breakfast. We learn that Blazes Boylan is expected to visit her and that Bloom fears the two will have sex. We hear bits of men's talk about her attractiveness and the arrangement of her household. Yet she is absent from many of the scenes through which the narrative builds her importance. She becomes a woman inside her husband's thoughts long before she becomes a woman whose own thoughts we can hear.

When Joyce finally gives her the last episode, Penelope, the shift is structural. Molly becomes a speaking mind rather than merely the object of another mind. That does not automatically make the novel a complete or unproblematic representation of women. It makes the problem of who speaks for whom impossible to ignore.

## Molly is more than the faithless Penelope

In the Odyssey, Penelope is celebrated for resisting suitors while her husband is away. Molly's sexual relationship with Boylan destabilizes the classical parallel. A crude reading would turn her into the opposite of a faithful wife and finish there. But Joyce gives her a history that neither chastity nor infidelity can summarize.

She has been a child in Gibraltar, a singer with professional ambitions, a partner in a marriage, a mother of two children, and a woman whose surviving child is moving toward adulthood. Rudy's death belongs to her history no less than Bloom's. Her grief, though expressed differently, cannot be omitted from the marriage merely because the book spends so many pages within the husband's mind.

Molly also judges Bloom. Sometimes she admires his kindness and capacities; sometimes she thinks him awkward, inadequate, or exasperating. Her judgments move with desire, irritation, bodily sensation, and memory. We need not decide that every statement is fair in order to recognize her as a substantial consciousness. Characters are not granted personhood by being reliably right.

In remembering the marriage proposal, Molly recalls not being acquired or simply accepted but choosing for herself. The novel's last yes belongs to a woman remembering a moment of mutual desire. That memory does not provide a contract guaranteeing the couple's future. It does demonstrate that the wife whose actions have troubled the hero has her own past and her own account of how the marriage began.

## What does the absence of punctuation mean?

Penelope is famous for its extended, minimally punctuated language. It moves through eight long paragraphs or sentences, depending on editorial description, with few of the stops a reader expects. The experience can feel astonishingly intimate: one thought leads to another without the narrator announcing that we have entered a character's consciousness.

There is a trap in calling this unfiltered female nature. Women do not think in one uniform style, any more than men do. Molly's voice is a literary construction by a male author who is making specific artistic choices. The Morgan Library's account of Molly's style places the episode alongside Nora Barnacle's letters, noting similarities in the movement of speech. The comparison illuminates Joyce's craft without making Nora and Molly interchangeable: the fictional monologue is not a transcript of a real woman's private consciousness.

Joyce does something powerful by denying the reader a familiar narrator's explanation of Molly. He also makes a claim on the power to imagine what a woman thinks. Readers can admire the technical achievement and still ask where the imagination is perceptive, where it is stereotyped, and what the author cannot know. The two responses are not mutually exclusive.

## Gerty and the seductive voice of advertising

Another woman's inward language has already mattered in Nausicaa. Gerty MacDowell imagines romance through phrases borrowed from sentimental fiction and popular culture. Her sense of beauty is shaped by clothes, marriage prospects, and a consuming wish to be seen. The narrator imitates that style until its sentimentality becomes almost unbearable.

Then Bloom's presence creates an erotic encounter of looking. Gerty reveals her legs; Bloom masturbates at a distance. It is important to state the action plainly because turning the scene into harmless flirtation erases the public sexual behavior and the questions it raises. Equally, declaring with certainty what Gerty has consented to would assign transparent intentions to a scene constructed from layers of sentimental idiom and a spectator's gaze.

When Gerty leaves, Bloom notices that she limps. The delayed fact changes the frame. It exposes how beauty has been constructed by selection and omission, as much in Bloom's gaze as in the romance-novel style. But a disability is not a moral lesson attached to a woman's body. Gerty has a life beyond the instant at which the male observer revises his fantasy, and the novel only partially makes that life visible.

The episode is especially valuable for discussing the dangers of literary technique. Joyce can imitate the language that shapes a young woman's expectations and expose its artificiality. But a satire of sentimental language can become another way for a sophisticated reader to feel superior to the person caught within it. Reading responsibly means recognizing both the power of the convention and the person who has learned to desire through it.

## A birth the men narrate without performing

Oxen of the Sun offers another problem. Mina Purefoy gives birth while men in the hospital discuss sex, medicine, reproduction, and the histories of language. Joyce has designed the prose to evolve through literary periods, as if English itself were being born. The formal analogy is clever, and the result is often extremely funny. But the central bodily labor is largely elsewhere.

This gap deserves examination. A novel capable of staging the development of English prose can still show a room full of men who do not understand what the woman in labor is enduring. We should not mistake their talk for her experience. Nor should we assume the author approves of every attitude that passes through the room. Joyce's style frequently lets voices expose themselves through the disproportion between their words and the event they describe.

The chapter suggests that reproductive labor sustains social life while public language can turn it into entertainment or professional display. That reading arises from the juxtaposition of the birth and the speeches, not from a statement by Mina herself. Her relative silence is part of what the interpretation must acknowledge.

## Bodies without sanctimony

Joyce writes frankly about menstruation, urination, childbirth, masturbation, appetite, and fatigue. The frankness was one reason the novel encountered obscenity prosecution. But the significance of bodily detail cannot be reduced to a celebration of freedom from censorship. A body can be described in a way that grants it presence, or in a way that makes its owner a spectacle. Ulysses does both, and reading it means distinguishing the occasions.

Bloom has genuine interest in women's practical needs, yet he also turns women into fantasies. Stephen's intellectual speculation is often dominated by fathers and authors, even when a mother's death supplies the emotional pressure. Molly's final voice answers some earlier representations without settling all the questions they raise. We should resist any single neat verdict about whether the novel is simply progressive or simply misogynistic. More exact criticism asks what each episode permits a woman to say, who benefits from the conventions used to tell it, and what remains unspoken.

## A last word is not the whole word

The end of Ulysses places Molly's memory and affirmation after an entire day of other people's storytelling. In one sense this is a formal act of justice: a woman who has been imagined by others finally gets the closing voice. In another sense it exposes the limits of the earlier novel, because we discover how much our understanding of her was mediated by an anxious husband and a public culture ready to define a woman by her sexual conduct.

Molly's yes is not a solution to the ethics of representation. It is an invitation to reread the preceding seventeen episodes with greater caution. When Bloom worries about her, what does he actually know? When men joke about her, what have they earned the right to say? When a narrator gives us Gerty's fantasies, can we hear a person beneath the fashionable vocabulary? And when the book ends, can we let Molly remain complicated rather than forcing her into a symbol of the eternal feminine?

The achievement of Penelope is not that Joyce finally tells us what women are. It is that the novel ends by giving a specific woman more room to be contradictory than any single account of her had previously allowed.

- Stephen / Telemachus: spiritual and paternal estrangement, not a claim of literal fatherlessness
- Bloom / Odysseus: ordinary wandering, hospitality, and homecoming without restored sovereignty
- Molly / Penelope: analogy destabilized by her affair and her independent final voice
- Citizen / Cyclops: partial vision and exaggerated violence, in the biscuit-tin episode
- Eumaeus / Ithaca: shelter, conversation, hospitality, and unresolved departure
