# Ceremony — The Story

A continuous, spoiler-containing narration of Leslie Marmon Silko's *Ceremony* (1977). It recounts the main plot and ending in original prose. The novel moves across time and interweaves distinct narratives; this orientation gathers the human sequence into a path a first-time listener can follow without suggesting that the original is told in this order.

## Coming home does not mean the war has ended

When Tayo comes home to New Mexico after the Second World War, the desert does not look like a place he can safely inhabit. He is sick, sometimes unable to hold food down, sometimes overwhelmed by memories that arrive without warning. He has spent time in a veterans' hospital, where the people trying to help him can name his symptoms but cannot make his past stop happening inside him. The war is over in the calendar. For Tayo, the war continues in the way a face can turn into another face, or rain can become a threat, or a dead man can seem suddenly present.

He returns to the Laguna Pueblo community where he grew up, but home is not simple either. His mother, who left him in childhood, was his Auntie's sister. She had a relationship with a white man, and Tayo carries the shame that some relatives attach to his mixed ancestry. Auntie raised him in her household while never quite letting him forget that he was not her son Rocky. Rocky seemed to be heading toward the kind of success the schools promised: athletics, education, a future outside the old life. Tayo was loved by his uncle Josiah and by the family's grandmother, but he grew up with the feeling that some kinds of belonging were conditional.

Rocky and Tayo enlisted together. They were sent far across the Pacific. In the Philippines, under the pressure of defeat and captivity, Rocky was badly wounded and died. Tayo could not save him. During the fighting, a Japanese prisoner or enemy soldier reminded Tayo of Josiah. The resemblance broke down the distance he was supposed to maintain between an enemy and a beloved elder. After the soldiers ordered a killing, his mind could not keep the images separate. The horror of the war became mixed with the fear that he had somehow witnessed Josiah's death as well.

In the jungle he had begged the rain to stop. Rain seemed to belong to exhaustion, disease, bodies that would not dry, and the suffering of men who could no longer find shelter. But when Tayo returns to New Mexico, the land is dry. Drought has troubled the region, and he begins to fear that his desperate words overseas contributed to it. The feeling does not make sense as ordinary weather science. It makes sense as the burden of a man whose memories, responsibilities, and grief have fused. He carries not only the death of Rocky and the news of Josiah's death but an impossible sense that his own voice has injured the world.

Others have come home damaged. Harley and Leroy drink heavily. Emo, another veteran, fills bars with stories about the war and the excitement of power. He enjoys repeating the humiliations and violence he could inflict on others while wearing a uniform. To Tayo, these gatherings are dangerous. The men can briefly recognize one another's dislocation, but the drinking does not restore what they lost. Emo especially knows how to touch Tayo's shame about his ancestry and his wartime breakdown. An argument turns violent; Tayo is capable of frightening rage, and that frightens him as much as Emo does.

In the family home, Auntie is grieving for Rocky, the son whose hopes she trusted. She must also live beside the nephew who survived. The unfairness between the two men is almost too painful to name. Robert, Auntie's husband, tries to help. Grandma watches and understands more than people think. But the house cannot simply absorb Tayo's suffering by waiting for it to pass.

## The losses that began before the war

To understand the cattle Tayo will later seek, we have to go back to Josiah. Josiah believed there was a future in cattle that could endure the difficult country rather than merely conform to the habits of ranchers whose animals struggled with the land. He acquired a herd of spotted cattle descended from Mexican stock. Their coats and behavior made them different from the animals the white ranchers preferred. They moved where the vegetation and water might sustain them, crossing boundaries human beings had marked as property.

Josiah also loved a woman called Night Swan, who had traveled, danced, and endured the judgments attached to a woman perceived as different. She recognizes in Tayo something of the life that people refuse to understand when they insist on putting everyone into a neat category. On an errand connected with Josiah, Tayo meets her and they become lovers. This encounter happens before the war, yet it returns to his memory afterward because Night Swan's acceptance does not fit the shame Auntie has taught him to expect. She offers another way of imagining his mixed heritage: not a defect to apologize for, but a fact of who he is.

Then Josiah dies while the young men are away. His cattle scatter. When Tayo comes back, the animals have become an unfinished responsibility as well as a link with the man who believed in them. To find them would be to act again in a landscape that has seemed hostile and impossible, and to carry forward something Josiah left unfinished. At first Tayo cannot even imagine undertaking such a journey. He is trapped in repetition, unable to separate a remembered danger from a present one.

An elderly healer named Ku'oosh comes to help. He listens, performs a ceremony, and offers a language for damage that the military hospital did not provide. But the suffering Tayo brings from the Pacific, and the strange new forces that have entered the country, do not yield to that treatment. Ku'oosh recognizes that a different response may be necessary. He directs Tayo toward Betonie, a healer who lives near Gallup and has been thinking about how ceremonies must meet a changed world.

Betonie lives amid objects and places that do not fit an outsider's idea of traditional purity. There are ordinary contemporary things around him; there is the city; there are the effects of contact between peoples and histories. Tayo is wary. Why should a healer associated with old knowledge work in a place like this? Betonie answers, in effect, that the world has not remained still. A ceremony is not protected by pretending that nothing new has happened. It must recognize the circumstances it is trying to change.

Betonie speaks of stories that reach far beyond one veteran's suffering. He tells Tayo about destructive patterns in which people are divided, made fearful, and persuaded to destroy one another and the land. The stories do not give Tayo a simple enemy he can defeat. They show that the most dangerous response may be the one that reproduces the very pattern he is trying to escape. His recovery will have to include the power to refuse a certain kind of action when that action feels most justified.

Betonie performs his ceremony and tells Tayo that its completion will require more. There will be signs and encounters: cattle, a mountain, a woman, and a pattern Tayo must learn to recognize by living through it. Tayo leaves without receiving a tidy promise that his pain will vanish. He has, instead, a direction.

## The cattle and the mountain

On the road home, the old routines try to claim Tayo again. He encounters Harley and Leroy, their drinking, their exhausted jokes, and the familiar temptation to let the day dissolve. But he eventually turns away. He begins looking for Josiah's cattle, tracing the places where the animals might have gone. The search takes him into the mountains and toward land that white ranchers have fenced. The fence represents a claim of ownership that does not correspond to the cattle's life or to older relationships between the land and the people who moved across it.

Tayo finds the spotted cattle behind a rancher's barrier. They are not conveniently waiting in a corral for someone to claim them. They have followed the country. He cuts the fence and attempts to drive them back. The work is exhausting and uncertain. A man can know that a task is right for him and still be unable to perform it easily. Cattle scatter; the terrain changes; the threat of discovery is real.

During the search he meets a woman named Ts'eh. She has a house in the mountains and seems to know things about the land and the cattle before he tells her. Their meeting is intimate without the novel explaining her as only a romantic figure. She helps him in ways at once practical and hard to account for. A hunter also appears, and people and animals seem to move through a pattern Tayo has been learning to see. He does not suddenly acquire omniscience. He begins to feel that his actions are no longer separate from the land and the lives around him.

Tayo and Ts'eh spend time together. The cattle are recovered and cared for. When Robert comes to help bring the animals home, Tayo can show something real for the journey: the herd is no longer simply a source of guilt. Josiah's hope has been carried into action. The recovery does not erase Rocky's death or make the war unreal. It gives Tayo a present tense in which his body can work, care, and respond rather than only recoil.

He eventually returns to family life. For a period he feels stronger, though the drought and the unrest in the community do not simply disappear. When he spends a summer with Ts'eh, their life has a quieter rhythm of gathering plants, tending animals, and noticing the changes in the country. She is connected with the signs Betonie described, but she is also a person with whom Tayo can share ordinary warmth. The novel leaves room for both kinds of presence.

Then danger returns. Emo has been telling stories about Tayo, encouraging the belief that he is violent, unstable, or dangerous. White authorities may be brought into the hunt for him. Ts'eh warns him to leave and tells him where to go. Her instructions matter because he is entering a situation in which the wrong choice could undo the change he has made.

## The friends who are no longer friends

Tayo evades the men looking for him, but encounters Harley and Leroy. Their familiarity offers the old comfort of company. For a moment he is drawn back into drinking and the hope that they will treat him as one of their own. Then he becomes aware that they are not taking him to safety. They have become part of Emo's effort to capture him. In a moment of dreadful clarity, Tayo understands that he cannot depend on the bonds of shared service if the men have accepted the hatred and despair that now organize Emo's life.

He escapes and reaches an abandoned uranium mine. The place is a wound cut into the ground. What was taken from the earth has become part of a history reaching from New Mexico to the war in the Pacific. Tayo's memories of the destruction of Japanese cities, the injury to the land near home, and the men who still try to make power out of humiliation no longer appear as unrelated things. The mine is not merely a convenient place to hide. It is where the different strands of his experience converge.

Emo and others arrive. They have Harley with them. Tayo remains concealed while Emo torments and tortures the man who betrayed him. Tayo has a sharp tool—a screwdriver—and can imagine rushing out to kill Emo. The temptation is powerful. He wants to end Harley's suffering and punish the man causing it. It would be possible to tell that action as an act of rescue or revenge, a decisive moment when the victim becomes strong enough to strike back.

But Tayo sees another possibility. If he answers cruelty by joining its pattern, Emo will have drawn him into the story Emo understands: power exists in the ability to hurt, and the only answer to violence is stronger violence. Tayo cannot promise that his restraint will save Harley. In fact, he must witness suffering he cannot prevent. His refusal is terrible rather than easy. He does not kill Emo. He survives the night without completing the destructive pattern that others have prepared for him.

This choice cannot repair every loss. Harley dies; other men who followed Emo are also lost. Emo himself does not turn into a repentant friend. Tayo's act matters because it stops one further repetition of the violence, not because it produces an instant reconciliation in which everyone is healed.

He returns to the places Ts'eh has directed him toward, completes the remaining acts of care, and goes back to the community. He tells Ku'oosh and the elders what happened. The story can now be heard by other people. His experience is no longer an isolated private torment with no place among the community's accounts of its own survival. Those who listen recognize Ts'eh's connection to older stories and the larger pattern in which Tayo has been moving. The ceremony reaches its completion in the telling as well as in the acts.

The possibility of rain returns. Tayo's fear that he had destroyed the world by asking the jungle rain to stop has been answered not by proving that he controls the weather, but by finding a way to act within a world whose relationships exceed him. He can remember Rocky and Josiah without making their deaths the whole explanation of his existence. He can recognize the force of a destructive story without becoming its next instrument.

## The shape of the ending

Silko does not give us the kind of ending in which a veteran goes home, receives an explanation of his illness, and is cured by a single conversation. Nor does she give us a simple battle in which goodness eliminates evil. Tayo has to travel, make mistakes, listen, retrieve the cattle, choose whom to trust, and refuse the most tempting act of revenge. Each event answers something from his life before the war while also changing what he can do now.

And he is not the only teller. The novel carries other stories through and around his history, including stories of drought, departure, return, and the dangers of losing a relationship with the sources of life. They do not function as optional illustrations added to a realistic war story. They are part of the world in which his experience becomes intelligible. A chronological account like this one helps us keep the people and events straight, but it cannot reproduce the complete effect of how Silko braids those narratives together.

At the end, an old grandmother suggests that what has happened sounds like a story she has heard before, though the names keep changing. There is humor and recognition in that observation. Tayo's suffering was real, specific, and not interchangeable with anyone else's. Yet the community has lived through recurring contests over how to remember, how to belong, and how to resist destruction. A story becomes available again because someone has lived it differently.

For a listener encountering *Ceremony* for the first time, this is the movement to keep in view: a man comes home unable to live with the war, finds that the explanations offered to him cannot contain what he has experienced, follows an unfinished family responsibility into the mountains, meets people who change what healing means, and finally refuses to become the violent answer to a violent world. His return is not to the home he left. It is to a home he can participate in again.
