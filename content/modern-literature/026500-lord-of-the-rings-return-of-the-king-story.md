# The Lord of the Rings — The Story, Part Three: The Return of the King

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

The last volume begins with two journeys that do not know enough about each other. In the west, armies are moving toward the decisive siege of Gondor. In the east, Sam Gamgee is outside an enemy fortress, carrying the Ring he took from a friend he believed was dead. The commanders who must defend the city do not know how close the small travelers have come to Mordor. The travelers do not know how many lives are being risked to keep the enemy looking elsewhere.

Gandalf rides to Minas Tirith with Pippin. The city stands in layers of stone beneath a high mountain, beautiful and formidable but worn by generations of siege and loss. The Steward Denethor rules it in the absence of a king. He is intelligent, proud, and exhausted by years of seeing his people pushed back. He also knows that Boromir, his favored elder son, has died. Pippin, who has survived partly through the courage of Boromir and the other members of the Fellowship, offers service to Denethor's household in repayment.

The Steward's remaining son, Faramir, is defending the outer approaches. He has not brought the Ring home from Ithilien. His father's bitterness about that choice runs beneath their conversations. Denethor believes that the rulers of Gondor must make hard decisions while others enjoy the luxury of moral purity. Faramir believes that some means of defending a city will destroy what makes the city worth defending. Their conflict takes place while enemy armies approach, and neither of them can leave his responsibilities to settle it.

## The city under threat

In Rohan, King Théoden prepares to ride to Gondor's aid. He has survived Saruman's invasion and knows the new war may demand everything his people have. Merry, who has come to love the king, wants to accompany the riders. Théoden sends him away from the battle. Éowyn, the king's niece, is likewise commanded to remain behind. She has lived with duty and loss for years, and the prospect of being left to wait for news is intolerable. Disguised as a young rider named Dernhelm, she takes Merry with her and joins the cavalry without the king's knowledge.

The riders must approach through dangerous country and avoid enemy patrols. They receive help from Ghân-buri-Ghân, a leader of the Drúedain, whose people know hidden routes through the forest. The aid is practical and decisive. The Rohirrim reach Gondor by a path the enemy has not properly guarded. They arrive after much of the outer land has been ravaged, just as the city is beginning to lose hope.

Inside Minas Tirith, Faramir has returned with the survivors of a terrible retreat. Denethor sends him again to hold an exposed position. Faramir is wounded and carried back gravely ill. Despair overcomes Denethor. He believes the enemy has already won, and that no power remains capable of resisting the forces he has seen. He prepares to burn himself and his unconscious son rather than wait for defeat. Pippin, horrified, seeks Gandalf. Gandalf prevents Faramir's death, but Denethor refuses rescue and perishes in the fire. The city is left without its ruler just as its greatest battle begins.

The attackers bring enormous engines against the gates. The chief of the Ringwraiths, often called the Witch-king, advances amid terror. The defenders are battered and disorganized. Then horns sound from the fields: the Rohirrim are arriving. Théoden leads a mounted charge that breaks into the enemy's ranks, a moment of hope purchased at immense risk. The battle changes again as new enemies enter, including great beasts bearing fighting towers. Théoden is struck down, his horse falling upon him. The Witch-king approaches to finish what his attack began.

Éowyn stands beside the fallen king. She reveals herself and refuses to retreat. Merry, small and terrified, strikes at the Witch-king from behind with the blade taken long ago from the barrow. Forged long ago to oppose such powers, the blade helps break the spell protecting the enemy leader. Éowyn then delivers the blow that ends him. Victory does not feel triumphant. Théoden dies, the woman who defended him falls gravely injured, and Merry is nearly overcome by the shadow of the encounter. Courage has not exempted them from loss.

## The road of the returning king

Before taking the mountain road, Aragorn has revealed himself more openly to Sauron through the seeing-stone recovered at Orthanc. He uses an instrument with a dangerous connection to the enemy, but not as a means of controlling every will. The gesture is a calculated challenge. By showing the sword and the identity of Isildur's heir, he encourages Sauron to fear that a powerful claimant may now possess the Ring. This risk is intended to draw attention toward war in the west. His choice also makes a return to obscurity impossible. The Ranger who guided frightened hobbits has stepped into public history.

Far from the main road, Aragorn has taken a different path. He is heir to an ancient line of kings, but his claim will be proved by what he can do for the living. He travels with Legolas, Gimli, Rangers from the North, and the sons of Elrond through a mountain passage feared by ordinary travelers. Beyond it lies the place where dead men remain bound by an oath they broke in an earlier age. Aragorn summons them to fulfill their ancient promise, and they follow him south. The company looks less like a rescuing army than a procession out of legend.

The dead help Aragorn break the power of the Corsairs threatening the southern coast. He releases them after their oath has been fulfilled. He does not bring an army of ghosts to fight across the Pelennor Fields. Instead he gathers living soldiers from the southern regions of Gondor and sails upriver in the captured ships. When the black-sailed fleet first appears before Minas Tirith, its arrival seems to announce another defeat. Then Aragorn's standard is raised. The ships are carrying help.

With the combined efforts of Gondor, Rohan, and the newcomers, the battle turns. The enemy is driven from the fields. Aragorn moves among the wounded, and his knowledge of healing plants and his care for sufferers become as meaningful as his ancestry. He tends Faramir, Éowyn, and Merry, whose injuries are not merely cuts and broken bones but afflictions of the spirit as well as the body. Faramir begins to recover. Éowyn, after the collapse of her imagined future, remains in the Houses of Healing. There she comes to know Faramir. Their friendship becomes love, a future neither could have planned while the battle was still being fought.

The victory at the Pelennor Fields has brought enormous relief, but it is not the defeat of Mordor. Gondor's outer lands remain scarred, and its defenders have lost too many people to mount a campaign that could conquer the enemy on equal terms. Aragorn does not use the wounded city's triumph as proof that he has become invincible. The captains gather to ask what sort of action can still help the absent Ring-bearer. Their plan is an attempt to create a window, not to win another glorious battle for its own sake.

The victors are under no illusion. Sauron still commands greater armies than they can defeat in a conventional campaign. The Ring remains somewhere in the east, and Gandalf does not know whether Frodo or Sam is alive. For Pippin the campaign is especially fearful. He has been enlisted in Gondor's defense and is smaller than most soldiers around him. At the Black Gate he will be drawn into fighting that dwarfs the battles of the Shire, struck down beneath a fallen troll while he tries to protect a friend. When the darkness breaks after the Ring's destruction, he is still lying under the weight. The visible military victory has almost killed one of the hobbits who never went near Mount Doom, another way of showing how the divided fellowship's fate remains shared.

Aragorn, Gandalf and the captains decide to march on the Black Gate. Their movement is an act of deliberate misdirection. They will invite Sauron to believe that an overconfident claimant of the Ring is challenging him and draw his attention away from the interior of Mordor. They may die doing it. They proceed because the only possible success depends on the Ring-bearers reaching Mount Doom while the eye of the enemy is turned outward.

## Sam at the tower

On the eastern road, Sam has been watching the Orcs carry Frodo into the tower above Cirith Ungol. The fortress is full of soldiers serving rival commanders. They begin quarreling over possessions, prisoners, and orders, and their argument becomes a deadly fight. Sam climbs through the confusion. The Ring hangs at his neck. Wearing it makes him hard for enemies to see, but it also offers him images of himself as a mighty hero able to put right the whole ruined land. He recognizes the temptation in part because his actual desire is so modest: he wants to rescue Frodo and, if possible, return one day to a garden.

He reaches Frodo, who is alive but stripped, wounded, and frightened. Their reunion is joyous and tense. Sam has taken the Ring for a good reason. Frodo, exhausted by its absence and its claim upon him, reacts sharply when Sam offers it back. Their friendship survives the moment, but Sam sees how close the burden has come to consuming Frodo's ability to trust. They take what clothes and supplies they can find and escape into Mordor.

Mordor is not a convenient stretch of empty land. It is crossed by roads, watched by guards, and filled with soldiers under pressure from the great war in the west. Frodo and Sam are forced to move by stealth, disguise, and desperate calculation. They join a marching Orc column long enough to escape scrutiny, then slip away when they can. Their food runs low. Water becomes the greatest need. Heat, ash, dust, and exhaustion reduce their world to the few steps they can take before resting.

The Ring grows heavier for Frodo, not because the metal is physically larger but because its demand has become almost inseparable from his consciousness. Sam does what he can: he finds water, gives away portions of food, thinks of where the next slope may provide cover, and finally carries Frodo himself when his friend can no longer stand. He cannot carry the Ring for Frodo without risking the same claim to power; he can carry his friend. Their movement toward the mountain becomes a series of small acts that no historian of great battles would know how to celebrate.

Gollum has followed them through the land. He is reduced by hunger and obsession, yet still cunning. Near the mountain he attacks, then slips away. Frodo and Sam reach the last ascent. The armies of the west are now gathered at the Black Gate, struggling to endure a battle they cannot hope to win by force. Sauron has turned his attention toward the apparent challenge. The road to the fire is open enough for two hobbits to take it.

## At the fire

At the place where the Ring was made, Frodo does not perform the act for which everyone has suffered. His will has been worn down over months, and at the very center of the Ring's power he claims it as his own. It is a terrible reversal, but not an arbitrary trick. Frodo has resisted with everything he had, and now he can resist no longer. The Ring has finally reached the part of his desire that does not want to surrender.

Sauron becomes aware of what has happened. For the first time the danger that has escaped his calculations is plain: someone stands at the fire where the Ring can be destroyed. He sends the Ringwraiths toward the mountain, but the race is almost over. Gollum attacks Frodo, bites the Ring from his finger, and rejoices at recovering it. In his frenzy he loses his footing and falls into the fire, taking the Ring with him.

The Ring is destroyed. Its maker's power collapses, and the structures held up by that power begin to fail. The Ringwraiths perish. The armies at the Black Gate, which had seemed certain to crush the western captains, lose their organizing will. On the mountain, Frodo and Sam have no path to safety. They stand amid ash and burning rock with the knowledge that the quest is finished but perhaps not their lives. Eagles come with Gandalf and carry them away before the destruction closes over them.

The survivors celebrate in the field of Cormallen. Frodo and Sam are honored far beyond what either imagined when leaving the Shire. The friends separated beside the river are reunited. Yet the story does not treat this celebration as payment that erases everything that happened. Boromir is dead. Théoden is dead. Many unnamed fighters and civilians will not return. The Elves know that the destruction of the Ring has altered their own ability to preserve places such as Lothlórien. A victorious world is not the same world that existed before the war.

Aragorn enters Minas Tirith as king, accepting a role tied to service and responsibility rather than merely to bloodline. Arwen, Elrond's daughter, comes to marry him, choosing a mortal life and its eventual separation from her Elven kin. Faramir and Éowyn move toward a future together. The company spends time among friends and slowly begins its journey home. Their route takes them back through places where the marks of the war remain visible. The larger world is changing, even where the weapons have been laid down.

## The Shire is not untouched

The returning hobbits expect to find the familiar country preserved because they left it. Instead they find barriers, petty rules, confiscated goods, and men hired to intimidate their neighbors. Much of the landscape has been damaged. Trees have been cut down, mills altered, and the habits of free local life replaced by bureaucratic control and bullying. The troubles began under a hobbit called Lotho Sackville-Baggins, who gained wealth and control by dealing with outsiders, but they have passed beyond him. A figure known as Sharkey has taken charge.

Merry and Pippin have learned to organize people under pressure. Sam knows the place and people well enough to help restore their confidence. Frodo, who has survived the Ring, resists the desire to settle every wrong through revenge. Together they rouse the Shire and defeat the hired ruffians in the Battle of Bywater. It is a smaller battle than the siege of Gondor, yet it matters no less to those defending their homes. Hobbits who never walked to Mordor must choose whether to remain passive or protect their neighbors.

Sharkey is Saruman. He has come to the Shire after the destruction of his power and sought revenge by spoiling a place that mattered to the people who helped defeat him. He cannot rebuild his former empire, but he can still injure a community through spite. Frodo refuses to kill him and orders him to leave. Saruman is accompanied by Wormtongue, whom he humiliates even in defeat. When the truth of another murder emerges, Wormtongue finally turns upon Saruman and kills him. Wormtongue is himself slain by hobbit archers. The scene ends not in the restoration of perfect innocence but in the grim knowledge of what prolonged domination can make of people.

The Shire begins to recover. Sam uses the gift of soil from Lothlórien to help heal damaged gardens, and a new tree grows where an old landmark was destroyed. Merry and Pippin find public roles in the society they helped defend. Sam marries Rosie Cotton, and their home fills with ordinary domestic life. Their happiness is not a betrayal of the travelers who died or the friends who must leave. It is part of what the entire struggle was meant to protect.

Frodo does not recover in the same way. Old wounds trouble him on their anniversaries. The burden of the Ring has left a pain for which the Shire has no cure. He has returned to the place he wanted to save, but he cannot wholly inhabit the peace it now offers. He writes the account of their journey and leaves its completion to Sam. Eventually he travels west with Bilbo, Gandalf, Elrond and Galadriel, departing from the Grey Havens. His voyage is not a promotion to a throne or a simple reward for courage. It is an admission that a life can be saved and yet be too wounded to return unchanged.

Sam goes back to the Shire, to Rosie and their children, to the work of living. He has helped save a world and has not lost the ability to love the particular one awaiting him. The great novel ends at a door, with a family, with the ordinary evening still available to someone. The Ring has vanished, the armies have dispersed, the king has returned, and the remaining heroes must decide how to go on. Tolkien leaves the reader with a victory that is real, costly, shared, and incomplete.
