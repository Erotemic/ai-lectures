# Slaughterhouse-Five - The Story

An original, complete plot orientation to Kurt Vonnegut's 1969 novel, including the narrator's opening frame, Billy Pilgrim's wartime experience, the Tralfamadorians, and his foretold death. The novel's nonlinear ordering and the reality of Billy's time travel remain open to interpretation. Analysis follows at `020000`.

## A war book that cannot begin

A man wants to tell a story about a city that was destroyed. He has been trying to write it for years. He knows he was there. He was a prisoner of war when Allied bombers devastated Dresden, Germany, in February 1945. He survived beneath a building that had once been used for slaughtering animals, while much of the city above him burned. He has carried the memory home, and he has found that remembering a catastrophe does not give him a straightforward way to tell it.

This speaker resembles Kurt Vonnegut, who actually survived the Dresden bombing as an American prisoner. He visits an old army companion, Bernard O'Hare, hoping to recover memories and give the book a shape. But the past is stubborn. The men remember fragments, people, impressions, and the limits of what they understood at the time. At O'Hare's home, his wife Mary objects to the possibility of a handsome, heroic war story. These soldiers were young and frightened; she does not want a novel that makes their suffering look like a magnificent adventure. The narrator takes her objection seriously.

Eventually he chooses to tell the story of Billy Pilgrim. Billy is not a glorious warrior. He is a young American with little aptitude for fighting and no romantic wish to become a hero. He becomes a soldier because a war is happening and men are being sent into it. Around this man, Vonnegut builds a book that moves back and forth in time. The war keeps returning among scenes of suburban life, an encounter with aliens, and an anticipated future death.

To understand the events, it helps to meet Billy in the order in which much of his life takes place. But we should remember that the novel itself is not satisfied with a straight chronology. Billy experiences moments from different years as if he has lost the ability to stay in one place in time. Whether this is a literal gift, a way of speaking about a damaged memory, or something that refuses either explanation will remain part of the story.

## A boy who is drafted

Billy grows up in the invented New York city of Ilium. He is awkward, passive, and often pushed toward experiences he has not chosen. His father wants him to become tougher, but the lessons do not make him into a confident man. When World War II reaches him, Billy trains to serve as a chaplain's assistant. He is poorly prepared for combat. His father dies in an accident before Billy's service takes him into the European fighting.

During the Battle of the Bulge, Billy becomes separated from his unit behind German lines. Other American soldiers find themselves moving through the same winter landscape. Two scouts have some ability to survive in the field. Roland Weary, a heavily equipped and aggressive young soldier, has a fantasy of himself as a formidable warrior. He wants the men around him to take part in a story of military friendship and spectacular courage. Billy, cold and exhausted, cannot perform the role Weary assigns him.

Weary's resentment becomes violence. He attacks Billy for seeming weak and for interfering with his image of the group. The scouts depart, unwilling to be trapped by Weary's quarrels. German soldiers capture Billy and Weary. The men who have been taught to imagine themselves as powerful fighters are now prisoners who must obey instructions they can scarcely understand.

They are moved by rail, under miserable conditions, toward camps holding Allied prisoners. Weary's feet are damaged by the poor condition of his footwear. Gangrene develops. Before dying, he blames Billy for the events that led to his capture and suffering. This accusation is passed to Paul Lazzaro, another American prisoner. Lazzaro is small, vicious, and fascinated by the idea of revenge. He promises to punish Billy someday for Weary's death.

Billy is too preoccupied with simply surviving to know what form that threat might eventually take. The accusation travels farther into the future than Weary does.

## Captivity and Dresden

At one stage the Americans encounter British prisoners who have been held long enough to organize their camp into a strange display of order and abundance. The British have food, rehearsed performances, and rituals that make captivity seem almost manageable. The newly captured Americans, dirty, starving, and unprepared, are a different sort of spectacle. The encounter is comic in its contrast, but the comedy depends on the desperate reality that these men are not in charge of their lives.

Among the Americans Billy meets Edgar Derby, a middle-aged former schoolteacher who has volunteered to serve. Derby has a steadiness and decency that contrast with Weary's theatrical toughness and Lazzaro's appetite for retaliation. No extraordinary military ability makes Derby admirable. He remains a person capable of concern for others even when circumstances offer little reward for it.

The captives are eventually taken to Dresden, a German city of art, public buildings, and civilian life that has not yet suffered the kind of destruction seen elsewhere in the war. The Americans are housed in a former slaughterhouse, building number five. For them the name is simply the address of their confinement. They work under guard, including at a factory producing a vitamin-enriched syrup intended for pregnant women. The work is one of the novel's disorienting juxtapositions: men who might die tomorrow prepare nourishment for unborn children in a city that is about to burn.

Billy's wartime days are full of humiliations and accidents. His body and clothes do not fit anybody's idea of a proper soldier. Germans and Allied prisoners alike attempt to explain events through slogans about honor, military virtue, or national character. The people who actually have to endure the conditions are much less orderly than the stories told about them.

On the night of February 13, 1945, Allied bombers attack Dresden. Explosions and incendiaries transform the city. The prisoners survive in a deep storage space beneath the slaughterhouse. When they emerge, they find a devastated landscape. Places that had seemed permanent have been reduced to rubble, ash, and the remains of people. The work afterward is terrible. Survivors are made to search for bodies and help dispose of the dead. The scale exceeds what the men can comprehend through an ordinary sequence of events.

In the midst of this ruin, Edgar Derby is accused of taking a teapot from the wreckage and is executed. His death is not an epic sacrifice in battle. A man who survived the firebombing is killed for a small act involving an object that, against the destruction surrounding it, seems grotesquely insignificant. Derby was not beyond fault or beyond history. But to Billy, and to the story as the narrator tells it, his execution shows how little sense official violence can make when placed beside actual human need.

The war ends. Billy goes home. The city and its dead do not remain safely behind him.

## A respectable life

After the war, Billy is hospitalized for emotional difficulties and becomes acquainted with Eliot Rosewater, another veteran. Rosewater introduces him to the science-fiction stories of a writer named Kilgore Trout. Trout writes about distant planets, strange technologies, and the ridiculous failures of humanity. These stories are not necessarily better explanations of the world than history books. They give Billy ways of imagining experience that ordinary social language has not supplied.

Billy trains as an optometrist. He marries Valencia Merble, whose father has an established optometry business, and becomes prosperous in Ilium. They have two children, Robert and Barbara. From the outside, Billy has followed a familiar American path: profession, marriage, house, family, financial security. The accomplishments are real, but they do not make the years of war disappear. He can sit in a comfortable room and find himself somewhere else in his mind, or in the novel's telling, somewhere else in time.

Valencia loves Billy and worries about him. He is often distant. Their marriage contains affection, misunderstanding, domestic habits, and silences. Billy seems to move through it with less resistance than most people expect a grown man to show. Even his prosperity feels as if it has happened to him, rather than being a future he has deliberately built.

Their son Robert becomes involved in trouble as a young man and later joins the military, serving in Vietnam. Their daughter Barbara becomes increasingly frustrated by her father's behavior. For her, the family must function in an ordinary world of appointments and responsibilities, even when Billy insists that the world has a very different structure.

Billy's years after Dresden also include a series of visits and encounters with Kilgore Trout. Trout's bizarre stories are full of societies and creatures that resemble humanity just enough to expose its evasions. The science fiction Billy reads begins to matter as much to the reader as his medical career. Another language for his experience has entered the house.

## The planet outside ordinary time

Billy claims that he is unstuck in time. He moves without choosing from one moment of his life to another. He can be a frightened prisoner walking through snow, then an older husband lying in bed, then a child under the care of his parents. Nothing he does provides reliable control over the order. Events are not recalled as ordinary flashbacks. In the novel's account, they appear as places he inhabits.

He also reports that extraterrestrial beings called Tralfamadorians abduct him on the night of Barbara's wedding. The aliens are very small, physically strange, and not concerned with human expectations of dignity. They bring Billy to their planet and place him on display in a transparent enclosure, like an animal in a zoo. Visitors can observe him as an example of human life. For a man whose time in the army repeatedly stripped him of authority over his own body, the new confinement is both fantastical and disturbingly familiar.

The Tralfamadorians explain that they see time differently from humans. To them, different moments do not vanish one after the other. Events exist together, and a being capable of perceiving time in more than one dimension can see a person's life as a structure rather than a story moving from beginning to end. A death is a terrible moment, but it is not the only moment in which that person exists. Elsewhere in the structure, the person is still living.

Their account also denies the freedom human beings believe they possess. The universe's events are already there. The aliens even know that one of their own will someday destroy the universe in an experiment. They do not propose to stop the event. It is simply part of the structure they can see.

For Billy, this offers relief from an unbearable question: why did these things happen, and why could nobody prevent them? He learns to move his attention away from suffering and toward the moments he finds pleasant. If time is a landscape already laid out, he does not have to face the exhausting burden of imagining a different result.

The Tralfamadorians also bring an Earth woman, the movie performer Montana Wildhack, to Billy's enclosure. She and Billy live together under the gaze of their captors, become lovers, and have a child. They are described as a domestic pair within an exhibition. The circumstances make the intimacy both oddly comforting and deeply compromised. Neither person chose to be there. Even in a story that moves between stars, the question of who controls a body does not vanish.

## Death already known

In 1968 Billy is involved in a plane crash while traveling to an optometrists' convention. He survives with severe injuries. Valencia, rushing to reach him at the hospital, crashes her car. She ultimately dies of carbon monoxide poisoning while trying to get help. Billy's survival and his wife's death become another pair of events that resist any satisfying moral explanation.

He recovers in a hospital and encounters Bertram Rumfoord, a well-connected scholar studying wartime air operations. Rumfoord initially doubts Billy's account of having witnessed the Dresden bombing. The old distinction between those who experienced an event and those permitted to explain it officially returns. Billy insists that he was there. Rumfoord must decide what to do with the testimony of a man who does not fit his expectations of a credible witness.

Afterward Billy becomes more public about the Tralfamadorians and his movement through time. He travels to New York, speaks on a radio program, and writes about what he believes he knows. Barbara is angry and frightened for him. To her, a father who claims to have been kept in an alien zoo may be unwell or confused. To Billy, the knowledge he is trying to share has become the only explanation that gives his life a coherent shape.

He says that he has already witnessed his own death. In a future year, 1976, he will speak publicly in Chicago after the city has suffered destruction of its own. An assassin sent on behalf of Paul Lazzaro will shoot him, fulfilling the revenge promised decades before when Roland Weary died in captivity. Billy speaks of this death without the expected terror. If he has already lived the moment and will continue to inhabit others, it is not the final disappearance that ordinary chronology would make it.

Whether this death is the future of the fictional world or Billy's anticipated story about himself is not explained by an external witness who settles the question. In the novel, it is part of the pattern of experiences Billy tells us he can see.

## Where the telling finally goes

The narrator returns again and again to the effort to write about Dresden. The city cannot simply be made into a backdrop for a clever plot. Ordinary narrative rewards feel suspect. If all the suffering had to happen so a survivor could grow wiser, the dead would have been used as material for somebody else's success story. The war resists that form of consolation.

Vonnegut sometimes lets the narrator appear inside Billy's wartime world, not as a commanding hero but as another young American caught in circumstances larger than any one soldier. The book can be fantastical and self-conscious, yet it keeps returning to the remembered slaughterhouse. The alien planet, the prosperous optometry practice, the future death, the frozen marches, and the ruined city are all parts of one work because a life can contain them without arranging them into a moral lesson.

Near the end we return to the aftermath of Dresden. There are searches through ruins, the work of dealing with bodies, and the unanswerable question of what one person can say when language has failed. When human explanation finally runs out, the ending turns to the sound of a bird. The book does not conclude with a general's triumph, a victorious soldier's speech, or an instruction to make peace with everything that has occurred.

The title refers to a real place within the fiction, the place that sheltered the prisoners while the city above them was destroyed. The subtitle reminds us that many of the soldiers called upon to endure this war were young, and that the habits of telling war as a magnificent adventure do not make their suffering easier to see.

Billy Pilgrim's life has ranged across decades and, perhaps, across planets. We have followed an awkward soldier through captivity and a bombed city, watched him become a wealthy professional, seen him lose his wife, and heard his account of aliens who tell him that no moment ever truly goes away. The book gives us no simple way of choosing which parts of his account are a literal cosmology and which are a way of surviving memory.

What it gives us instead is the persistence of a terrible event. Dresden survives in the story not as a lesson neatly mastered but as something the narrator must repeatedly approach, leave, and approach again. The lives continue, and so does the task of telling what happened to people who were never prepared to be characters in a war story.
