# Beloved — The Story

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

Before we arrive at the haunted house in Toni Morrison's Beloved, we need to know where its inhabitants came from. The book does not tell their history from beginning to end. Memories emerge in fragments, and some events are disclosed long after we have seen their effects. For this telling, we will follow the lives that lead to the house first, then return to the events of 1873. Everything that follows reveals major parts of the novel, including the death at its center.

Sethe is enslaved at a Kentucky farm called Sweet Home. The name sounds pleasant, and for a time its owner, Mr. Garner, prides himself on treating the enslaved men who work there as if they possess adult dignity. But his supposed kindness cannot change the fact that he owns people, controls their work, and decides their futures. Among the men are Paul A, Paul F, Paul D, Sixo, and Halle. Halle works to buy freedom for his mother, Baby Suggs, and eventually marries Sethe. Sethe has children, and for a time she knows a kind of domestic life in the midst of captivity. Nothing about that security can be relied upon.

After Mr. Garner dies, a new authority arrives: a man whom the enslaved people call schoolteacher. He has nephews who work alongside him, and he studies the people he has been given power over as if they were specimens to be measured, classified, and trained. Sethe becomes the object of their scrutiny. She discovers them writing down supposed human and animal traits. The men also take the breast milk she has been saving for her baby. When Sethe tells Mrs. Garner what happened, the men whip her so severely that her back is left covered with scars. She is pregnant. Her children have already begun the journey toward freedom, and she is desperate to catch up with them.

The people who plan the escape have very different ideas of how much they can risk. Some have been imagining a place beyond Sweet Home for years; others have made private bargains with hope that they barely dare speak aloud. Sethe's husband Halle has used his labor to buy Baby Suggs's freedom, and Sethe believes he will come with her when the time arrives. Sethe's hope that Halle will come with her makes the escape a plan to reunite a family, not merely to save herself. The people they mean to leave behind are armed, organized, and accustomed to treating any attempt at freedom as theft.

An escape has been planned. Not everyone makes it. Sixo is captured and killed; his death is terrible, and the people carrying out the killing believe they can do it without moral consequence. Paul D is captured and sent into a brutal chain of ownership and punishment. Halle does not join Sethe as promised. Later, Paul D tells her that Halle had seen what happened to her and had been so broken by it that he appeared to lose his mind. Sethe has lived for years without knowing whether her husband was dead or alive. She must leave without him.

She moves toward the Ohio River, pregnant, injured, her feet damaged beyond the comfort of ordinary walking. On the road she encounters Amy Denver, a poor young white woman escaping an oppressive situation of her own. Amy is not a magical rescuer. She is young, talkative, and unaware of much that Sethe has suffered. But she stops to help. She attends to Sethe's wounds and remains beside her as the baby is born in a small boat near the river. Sethe names her new daughter Denver after the girl who helped deliver her. The child's name will preserve an encounter whose importance the two women could not have anticipated.

On the other side of the river, a man named Stamp Paid helps carry Sethe and the baby toward Cincinnati. People who have escaped slavery and people who organize escape routes support one another, though they live under constant threat. Sethe reaches the house at 124 Bluestone Road. Baby Suggs, already free, is there. So are Sethe's other children. For the first time, the mother can hold her family in a place where she hopes nobody has the right to claim them. Baby Suggs welcomes her, bathes her, and helps her heal.

Baby Suggs has become a source of strength for people in the surrounding Black community. In a wooded place called the Clearing, she leads gatherings where people are encouraged to cry, laugh, move, and care for bodies that slavery taught them to despise. She does not have a church building or a formal office. She has her own presence, her experience, and the trust of neighbors who have survived things difficult to recount. At one point a large celebration at her house leaves some neighbors feeling envious of the abundance. Feelings among the community are not simple. People support one another and also hurt one another.

Sethe spends twenty-eight days with her children in freedom. She learns the pleasures of working for wages, of choosing where to sleep, of believing she has a future beside them. She has arrived exhausted and battered, but she has arrived alive. The peace is brief. Schoolteacher knows where she has gone. He is coming to the house with men who intend to take Sethe and her children back to Kentucky. The next part of the story begins when their arrival turns twenty-eight days of hope into the terrible memory that will inhabit the house for years.

Then the terror that Sethe has fled reaches the house in Cincinnati.

Schoolteacher and his party arrive in Cincinnati to reclaim Sethe and her children. The news does not reach the house in time. Some neighbors have been hurt by the ostentatious feast at Baby Suggs's home, and the community that once acted as a web of warning and protection does not intervene quickly enough. Sethe sees the men who intend to return her family to slavery. She gets the children into a woodshed. Before they can be taken, she tries to kill them. The oldest daughter, a little girl who has already begun to crawl, dies from a wound to her throat. Sethe is stopped before she can kill the others. The surviving children are hurt and frightened; the baby Denver is still in her arms.

For Sethe, the act follows an unbearable conviction: the children will be better dead than owned. Other people around her do not accept that conviction as a simple answer. The enslavers, suddenly confronted with the aftermath, decide the children are of no further use to them. Sethe is arrested and imprisoned with Denver. People sympathetic to her cause help secure her release. When she comes back to 124, the life she had begun there has ended. Baby Suggs withdraws from the world. Neighbors avoid the family. The oldest daughter's grave receives a stone on which Sethe manages to have a single word carved: Beloved.

Years pass. The house develops a furious haunting. Objects move and sounds break out without visible cause. Sethe's sons, Howard and Buglar, eventually leave. Baby Suggs dies. Sethe continues to work, and Denver grows into a young woman who has never managed to become part of the town around her. She knows that something happened to her older sister, but pieces of the story reach her in different ways. Her mother's attachment to the dead child is woven into their daily lives. In 1873, Sethe and Denver are the only people living at 124, unless one counts the ghost.

Then Paul D comes to the door. He is a man Sethe knew at Sweet Home, someone who remembers the life she had before the river. His journey has been long and painful. After capture he was sold and eventually imprisoned on a Georgia chain gang. The men there were locked into degrading routines and forced labor, chained together until a violent storm helped create an opportunity to escape. He has made his way through several lives since then, keeping the most painful parts of himself closed away. When he sees Sethe, he recognizes her, and she recognizes someone who can say the names of people she believed she had lost.

Paul D enters the house and confronts its supernatural disturbance. The ghost erupts into an upheaval of noise and shaking, and then the house becomes quiet. He begins to imagine staying. Sethe is tempted by the possibility that ordinary companionship might return. Denver is more guarded. The ghost, whatever it was, had been familiar to her, and Paul D is a stranger claiming room in a household that has always centered on her mother and the dead sister. Still, the three of them go to a carnival. They spend an afternoon among people rather than in isolation, and for a moment the future does not look as narrow as it had before.

When they return to 124, a young woman is sitting outside near the house, exhausted and strangely helpless. She says that her name is Beloved. She gives few clear answers about where she has come from. Her skin and behavior suggest an adult, but her speech, appetite, and demands often seem childlike. Sethe and Denver take her in. Denver is immediately drawn to her. Beloved seems to know things she should not know, and she asks questions that call up Sethe's buried memories. Her interest in the past is insistent, almost hungry.

Beloved asks Sethe about earrings, family memories, and the things a baby might have heard long ago. She delights in receiving descriptions of the life before she arrived. Denver tells her about the river and Amy Denver, drawing on the birth story her mother has repeated. At first these exchanges give Denver something she has almost never had: a person her own age who seems willing to listen. But the more the women talk, the more Denver understands that Beloved's desires are directed above all toward Sethe. In a family already shaped around one dead child, a living daughter has again become an observer.

The house that had grown quieter fills again with disturbance, this time in human form. Beloved attaches herself to Sethe and wants stories, attention, and closeness. Denver wants Beloved for herself, and Beloved often treats Denver as an obstacle rather than a friend. Paul D feels pushed from one room to another as if the house itself were rejecting him. Eventually Beloved pressures him into a sexual encounter in a small outbuilding. He is ashamed and troubled, unable to explain what has happened to him. His relationship with Sethe weakens.

At the same time, people outside the house have begun talking again about the old story. Stamp Paid carries a newspaper clipping about Sethe's act and shows it to Paul D. Paul D struggles to believe what he is told, but the truth cannot be dismissed. He asks Sethe about it. She tries to explain why the arrival of schoolteacher left her believing she had only one choice. Paul D hears both what she says and the terrible thing she did. He leaves.

Sethe is now left with her daughter, the mysterious young woman, and the memory of the dead child. She begins to recognize Beloved not simply as a guest but as the return of someone she once lost. Whether the young woman is literally the murdered daughter, an embodiment of that daughter, or something the novel refuses to settle entirely, the people in 124 begin living as though a dead child's demand for her mother has come back into the room. The balance of the household is about to turn dangerous.

Beloved has altered the balance of the house, and the consequences now fall on each of its residents.

With Paul D gone, Sethe's attention narrows around Beloved. The young woman asks questions about the past, expects care, and becomes furious when she does not receive exactly what she wants. Sethe increasingly treats her as the daughter she killed. She tries to explain the woodshed and the years of grief that followed. She wants Beloved to know that the terrible act was meant, in her mind, to protect a child from being owned. Beloved does not become comforted by the explanation. The more Sethe gives, the more Beloved demands.

Sethe starts missing work and then loses it. The household's resources diminish. The two women move into an exhausting cycle of pleading, accusation, closeness, and resentment. Sethe grows thin and ill while Beloved appears to swell with the attention and food she receives. Denver watches her mother disappear into a relationship from which she seems unable to escape. Until now, Denver has been frightened of strangers, ashamed of her family's history, and dependent on the enclosed world of 124. She understands that remaining in the house may kill her mother.

So Denver walks out. She seeks help from Lady Jones, a woman who once taught her and who has remained outside the family's isolation. She explains enough of the situation for Lady Jones to understand that there is real need. Food begins to arrive, sometimes left without fanfare for Denver to find. Other neighbors take notice. The women who had once failed Sethe, avoided her, or simply lost touch with the house begin acting together. Denver looks for work, speaks to people she has not approached in years, and discovers that she can have a life not wholly defined by what occurred before she was old enough to remember.

News of Beloved spreads. Some townspeople connect the young woman's appearance to the dead child; others hear about Sethe's decline and want to intervene. Ella, who helped formerly enslaved people escape and knows that the past can impose terrible claims on survivors, gathers a group of women. They approach 124 together. Their action is not a trial and not a private conversation with Sethe. They stand outside the house and raise their voices. The sounds of their singing and prayer fill a place that has been cut off from the community for years.

At the same time, a white man named Mr. Bodwin comes toward 124. He is an abolitionist who has helped the family in the past, and he has arranged employment for Denver. Sethe sees him approaching while the women are assembled outside. The sight of a white man moving toward the house collapses the distance between 1873 and the day schoolteacher arrived. She believes that the danger has returned and that she must stop it. This time she rushes toward the man with an ice pick rather than turning on her children. The women restrain her before she can kill him.

In the confusion, Beloved disappears. The novel does not provide a tidy report of where she goes. The people who saw her disagree about what they witnessed. A story circulates that she was pregnant; other accounts are hazier. No one brings her back to 124. The haunting that had consumed Sethe's life begins to recede, though the loss remains.

Sethe is exhausted, bedridden, and devastated by Beloved's absence. Denver has found a place in the world and work to do. Paul D returns to the house and finds Sethe lying where Baby Suggs once withdrew from life. He sits beside her. Their conversation does not undo the murder, her years of isolation, or the suffering he endured. He asks her to think of herself as a person who still matters, rather than as someone whose entire worth depended upon the child she lost. It is not a command that transforms her instantly. It is an invitation to remain alive and to imagine a life after what has happened.

The novel closes with the town's memories of Beloved beginning to fade, even as the text resists making her into an ordinary ghost story that can be neatly told and finished. For our purposes, the events are these: Sethe escaped slavery, lost her daughter to her own desperate act, lived for years with the dead child's presence, accepted a woman who appeared to embody that presence, nearly died trying to satisfy her, and was helped back toward life when Denver asked for help and the community answered. The novel's shape is more broken and recursive than this telling. Its story has now reached its end, and the analysis can return to all the ways Morrison makes these events felt and remembered.
