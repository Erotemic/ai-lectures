# The Hour of the Star: Who Has the Right to Tell a Life?

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

What has been achieved when Macabéa's life is finally told? A reader has spent time with someone who would otherwise have been easy to ignore. But she has been represented by Rodrigo S. M., a narrator who makes his difficulty in representing her almost as conspicuous as her suffering. He can name the cruelty of her circumstances. He also makes her the object of his aesthetic experiment. *The Hour of the Star* refuses to let us settle comfortably on the idea that telling an overlooked person's story is always a straightforward act of justice.

This is a novella by the Brazilian writer Clarice Lispector, published in Portuguese in 1977 as *A Hora da Estrela*, the last of her books published in her lifetime. Its apparent plot is simple: a poor young woman from the northeast migrates to Rio de Janeiro, works as a typist, hopes for affection and a better future, and dies after being struck by a car. Its artistic structure is anything but simple. The fictional narrator's attempt to tell this story becomes a second story, with its own anxieties, vanities, evasions, and ending.

Understanding that second story changes how we hear the first.

## The fiction of an unadorned voice

Rodrigo repeatedly suggests that he wants to write plainly. He distrusts elaborate literary ornament when his subject is a girl who cannot afford dinner. But his promised simplicity is itself an elaborate performance. He comments on the decision to write, on the sound and shape of his sentences, on the difficulty of knowing Macabéa, and on the moral discomfort of using her life as material.

He is capable of saying things about her that sound contemptuous. Then he appears to rebuke himself. Perhaps the rebuke is sincere; perhaps it is also a way to recover the reader's admiration. The novel does not supply an innocent narrator standing outside these contradictions. It makes the contradictions audible.

There is an important difference between the author's choice and Rodrigo's self-description. Lispector has written the entire arrangement, including the persona of a male narrator, his failures, and the moments when the narrative seems to escape his control. Rodrigo is not a transparent mouthpiece for her opinions. His inability to give an untroubled account of Macabéa is one of the novel's subjects.

The first page's many possible titles, and the book's repeated hesitations about beginning, give this difficulty a formal shape. A title ordinarily tells us what a work is. Here even that first claim of authority is multiplied and destabilized. Critical writing about the novella has long noticed that the narrated life, the act of its narration, and the narrator's own self-examination coexist. We are not asked to choose just one of these as the real book.

## Poverty as a structure of missed possibilities

Macabéa lacks money, but the novel is not merely a catalogue of deprivation. We see how deprivation enters into language, gestures, medical care, employment, sexuality, and public attention. She makes mistakes at the office because she has inadequate training and little social standing. The doctor can tell her what she ought to eat without placing that food within her reach. The radio brings her an apparently limitless supply of information while offering little help in interpreting it. Images of feminine glamour circulate everywhere; she can imitate lipstick and admire a star, but she cannot cross the economic distance that separates their lives.

These are not isolated examples of personal inadequacy. They are social arrangements in which information, pleasure, work, and prestige are available in radically different forms to different people. Macabéa's inability to describe this system in political terms does not absolve it. An educated observer can see an injustice that the person living through it cannot yet name, although the observer may be wrong about many other things.

Her northeastern origin is relevant. Brazil's internal migrations, regional hierarchies, and imagined difference between a modern urban southeast and an impoverished northeast help make Macabéa a stranger in a city that is nominally her own country. But the novel should not be taken as a full sociological account of northeastern migrants. Macabéa is a particular fictional character, and much of what we learn about her is mediated by a narrator who has difficulty seeing past his categories.

That mediation matters especially when he describes her simplicity. We may be tempted to turn her into a symbol of absolute innocence. But innocence can become another way of erasing a person. Macabéa likes things. She wants to keep her job; she enjoys small treats; she imagines a different appearance; she takes a lover's attention seriously. These may be fragile choices, but they are choices. A story about her need not take away her remaining agency in order to make the reader care.

## The social theater of looking valuable

Olímpico is not rich, educated, or powerful. His ambition is to move upward, and he understands enough about the local hierarchy to look for people who might help him. When he abandons Macabéa for Glória, his calculation is crude but legible. Glória's confidence, urban credentials, and manner make her appear more useful to the future he dreams about. The boyfriend's cruelty is therefore continuous with the larger economy of social recognition.

Yet Olímpico is not simply an allegory of evil. A man made insecure by class standing can seek the feeling of superiority through the humiliation of someone more vulnerable. The result does not become excusable because the mechanism is understandable. It becomes more disturbing: the hierarchy can reproduce itself among those whom it has also injured.

Glória too is a product of this world, but she is not identical with Olímpico. She acts disloyally, then tries to help the woman she has wronged. The mixture resists a morality play in which every character receives one fixed quality. Her gestures may be inadequate, but their inadequacy is part of the novel's account of compassion in unequal circumstances.

Even Rodrigo is involved in this theater. If Macabéa is ordinary and almost invisible, his ability to notice her may make him seem especially profound. His pity risks becoming an assertion of his own distinction. Lispector gives the reader no safe position from which to congratulate the act of reading while remaining untouched by its inequalities.

## The narrator as a problem of literary ethics

Suppose we asked a practical question: who can tell a life with accuracy? Direct participants possess memories and sensations that outsiders may never learn. Outsiders sometimes see institutional patterns that those participants have no time or resources to examine. Neither position guarantees full understanding. To tell another person's story is to make selections and to incur a responsibility for those selections.

Rodrigo's situation intensifies the problem because Macabéa is his invention within the fiction. She cannot correct him. He controls her opportunities to speak. His claims that he is suffering alongside her may be entirely genuine within his imagined experience, but the power relation remains. The narrator's compassion does not make his authority disappear.

The fact that Lispector chose a male persona is important without requiring a single explanation. It creates additional distance between author, speaking voice, and protagonist. The man can announce his authority, collapse into embarrassment, and describe female experience with an obtuseness the reader is free to recognize. The writer has arranged a situation in which a voice may reveal its limitations at the very moment it claims responsibility.

This is not a demand that literature only portray people exactly like their authors. It is a test of how portrayal works. If a narrator admits that he cannot know everything about his subject, does the admission make him more reliable? Sometimes. But if he continually discusses his own sensitivity, that discussion may also consume the space in which the subject ought to appear. The novella holds these possibilities in tension instead of issuing a rule.

## Clock time and the promised future

Time is more than a sequence of incidents in this book. The radio announces hours, facts and products. The office requires punctual labor. These forms of time belong to a city that can measure a person without necessarily attending to her life. Macabéa survives day by day inside its schedules.

Madame Carlota offers another kind of time: the future as transformation. Her prediction finally invites Macabéa to imagine life on the other side of the present. She does not receive an explanation of how money, romance, and protection will be acquired. She receives a narrative. For someone who has lived with so few imagined alternatives, a narrative of possibility feels like something almost material.

Then the car arrives. We could call the ending a savage joke about credulity, but that reading would make Macabéa the butt of the joke. The scene's force comes from the real happiness she feels for a moment and from the social violence that the joke would hide. The wealthy object that hits her gives the collision a class meaning, although the novel does not reduce death to a mechanical punishment delivered by a car brand.

The title's star has contradictory suggestions. It recalls the film celebrity she admired. It recalls the occasion when strangers finally gather around her. And it recalls the awful concentration of narrative attention on the moment of death. None of these makes her suffering worthwhile. A violent end is not redeemed simply because it produces a striking literary image.

## What the ending does to the reader

Many novels try to make a death intelligible by assigning it a lesson. Perhaps a character was brave; perhaps a social system was corrupt; perhaps the ending demonstrates that life is indifferent. Lispector's novel permits all of these reflections without allowing any one of them to close the case.

Macabéa dies, and the narrator remains alive to report it. That asymmetry is the final test. He can finish the book, but she cannot speak after his final account. The reader should resist the desire to make his language do more than language can do. It can give attention; it can identify injuries; it can challenge habits of indifference. It cannot reverse an accident or provide a poor worker with the life she did not receive.

There is also a danger in celebrating the novel's self-conscious technique without remembering the person at its center. A perfectly elegant discussion of narration might make Macabéa disappear all over again. Conversely, an account that treats her only as a pitiful object misses the critical intelligence of the form. The book's accomplishment lies in holding both demands together: look at the person, and look at the means by which you are being invited to look.
