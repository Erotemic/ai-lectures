# Catch-22 - The Story

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

A young American bombardier lies in a hospital on an island in the Mediterranean. His name is John Yossarian. He is not gravely ill. He has discovered that a low-grade complaint of the liver can keep him away from the airplanes, and he would like to stay away from them for as long as possible. The hospital is not safe in any absolute sense. Patients die; the war has its ways of entering every room. But there are beds, meals, and a chance of getting through a day without being ordered into the air. For Yossarian, that is almost enough to make it a sanctuary.

We are in the Second World War, among American airmen based on the Italian island of Pianosa. Yossarian flies combat missions over enemy territory. The men are supposed to finish a required number of missions before they can be sent home. Their commanding officer, Colonel Cathcart, keeps increasing that number. Reach the threshold and it moves. The war is fought against Germany, but much of Yossarian's daily fear is attached to the officers who decide how often he must return to a sky full of antiaircraft fire.

Joseph Heller's novel refuses to tell this story in a tidy sequence. It circles among episodes, tells a joke, repeats it, and later returns to reveal a dead man behind the punch line. I am going to give you a way through the events and the people. The novel itself makes you discover what happened in a much less comfortable order.

## The island and its rules

Yossarian's fellow airmen have distinct methods of surviving, or of appearing not to notice what is happening. Hungry Joe is tormented by nightmares when he thinks he might finally be sent home. Orr, Yossarian's tentmate, spends time tinkering with small devices and repeatedly crashes aircraft. Clevinger believes that sensible argument ought to work on people in authority, a conviction that encounters military hearings in which no answer can save him from being accused. The chaplain, a shy man who wants to do right, finds that almost any kindness can be made suspicious. Doc Daneeka, the flight surgeon, knows perfectly well that danger is real, but is no more able than the others to escape the machinery around him.

The machinery has a name: Catch-22. A pilot can supposedly be taken off combat duty if he is insane. But if he asks to be grounded because the missions are dangerous, that request demonstrates that he has a rational desire to survive. A rational man is not insane. If he is too mentally unwell to ask for help, he keeps flying. If he knows enough to ask, he keeps flying. The rule pretends to offer an exit, and the act of trying to use the exit closes it.

Yossarian is not interested in being a model of martial virtue. He wants to live. He fakes ailments, argues, delays, and makes schemes. Sometimes he is funny. Sometimes he is infuriating. The men around him can be vain or cruel or absurd, but they are also human beings placed in situations where a small administrative decision can mean death. To understand Yossarian's terror, we must remember that he has been into the sky and has seen what happens there.

There is a young man named Snowden whom Yossarian could not save on an earlier mission. That event will emerge slowly. For now it remains lodged in him as something that no officer's assurance can explain away.

## Officers who compete, officers who profit

Cathcart wants promotion and imagines that heroic-looking achievements will get him noticed. His colonel's decisions about mission quotas therefore have less to do with the lives of crews than with the career he dreams of. Colonel Korn helps calculate the bureaucratic advantage in every maneuver. Generals compete with one another over status. One commander wants bombs dropped in impressive patterns for photographs. The target, the danger, and the soldiers on the planes are secondary to the image of an orderly military success.

Major Major Major Major, burdened with a name that makes his advancement into a running joke, becomes an officer who can be visited only when he is absent. This is the sort of military policy that the men are expected to obey without asking how anyone could ever obey it. The absurdity is not an escape from the war. It is part of the way the war is administered.

One of the most successful men on Pianosa is Milo Minderbinder, the mess officer. Milo begins with food, trade, and the promise that he can get better supplies for everybody. Soon he manages a vast syndicate that buys and sells across borders and loyalties. Aircraft and crews become delivery services. He can turn almost any material into a profitable transaction, or make it seem that he can. He assures the men that everyone benefits because everybody has a share. The books that would demonstrate the benefit do not become any easier to read.

At one point, Milo's commercial arrangements even lead him to cooperate with forces that are supposed to be the enemy. He arranges for the bombing of his own base as a business transaction. Men are killed. Yet Milo can describe the attack through accounting, as though a profitable balance sheet might change what has happened on the ground. The spectacle makes no sense as patriotism. It makes a particular, chilling sense as a system that allows any atrocity to be reclassified as efficiency.

## Missions that refuse to end

The men fly missions over places such as Bologna, Ferrara, and Avignon. The routes change; the danger does not. Yossarian sometimes tries to affect where the bombers will go. There are arguments about maps and target lines, attempts to avoid dangerous operations, panics under flak, and desperate hopes that another flight will count as the last one required.

On a mission at Ferrara, Yossarian has a chance to release his bombs, but the pass goes wrong and another run is ordered. Danger that might have ended becomes danger renewed. He receives an award for his conduct, a bureaucratic recognition that does not restore the dead or make the next sortie less frightening. Promotions and medals are remarkably good at changing the official meaning of events.

McWatt, a pilot who can seem cheerful even when others are terrified, performs reckless low flying. In one terrible incident his aircraft strikes and kills a man named Kid Sampson, who is standing on the beach. McWatt, unable to bear what he has done, flies his plane into a mountain. Doc Daneeka's name is recorded on that flight's list, although he was not actually aboard. The records declare him dead, and the military starts treating him as dead. His protest that he is alive does not outweigh a document saying otherwise. Letters of condolence reach his wife, who eventually begins a life organized around his official death. A living man is not permitted to recover his identity from a clerical fact.

There is even a man who remains a nuisance because he is dead. Lieutenant Mudd arrived as a replacement and was sent on a mission before he could formally report to the squadron. He died almost immediately. His belongings were left in Yossarian's tent, but the orderly room cannot process his absence, because the paperwork says he was never assigned to the unit in the first place. No one who handles the case needs to deny that a human being was killed. The system simply has no box in which to record what happened to him. Yossarian lives beside the personal effects of a man the records insist was never there.

Other men disappear from the squadron by degrees. Clevinger is lost. Dunbar, a man who tries to make time feel longer by filling it with boredom, is made to vanish through the system's opaque decisions. The hospital acquires a soldier whose name and identity no one can establish. Some losses are brutal and visible. Others arrive as paperwork, silence, or an empty place that nobody explains.

Yossarian meanwhile goes to Rome on leave. For the American officers it offers alcohol, sex, escape, and what seems to be a world with ordinary human variety. But Rome is occupied and damaged, and the people he meets are not simply background figures for soldiers on holiday. He has encounters that begin in comedy and end in bitterness. Luciana, a young Italian woman, briefly offers the possibility of a real attachment; his failures of judgment and fear help spoil it. His friend Nately becomes devoted to a young sex worker, repeatedly trying to persuade her to leave her work and be with him. His idealism is at once sincere, possessive, and blind to the conditions of her life. The woman and her younger sister have struggles that cannot be solved by an American airman's romantic fantasies.

The men return to duty. Cathcart keeps raising the required number of missions. That number eventually reaches eighty. A supposed finish line has become a way of ensuring that no finish line exists.

## Nately, Rome, and the cost of a joke

Nately dies on a mission. He had finally begun to imagine a future with the woman he loved, and now that future closes. When Yossarian tells her of his death, she blames the messenger. Her grief becomes an obsessive attempt to attack Yossarian, an almost slapstick pursuit that cannot conceal its misery. The novel sometimes makes violence and comedy share a sentence, but nothing about Nately's death becomes less final because the next scene is funny.

Yossarian returns to Rome later and finds it transformed into a place of menace. People suffer openly while officials enforce arbitrary rules. Children are vulnerable; women are exploited. A military policeman can find grounds to arrest an American officer whose pass is missing while far graver wrongdoing around him proceeds without consequence. Aarfy, another officer, rapes and kills a young Italian woman who had been working as a maid. Yossarian is appalled, but the military police arrest him for being in Rome without authorization. Aarfy is not the one they take away. The city's suffering and the officials' attention to the wrong offense make a brutal ending to what once seemed a comic leave from Pianosa. The city is no longer a comic interval away from Pianosa. It shows what happens to people without the protection of a uniform, a position, or a document.

Yossarian refuses to fly. He has flown the missions, watched men die, and had the demanded total repeatedly moved away from him. His refusal is not a secret illness or another attempt to manipulate a flight plan. It is an open break with the orders being given to him.

Cathcart and Korn propose a solution. They will send him home, give him the appearance of an honorable discharge, and let him become a useful public example. The price is that he must endorse them and the policy that will keep the other airmen flying. If he accepts, he may survive. He will also be lending his name to a system he knows is killing people for the sake of careers. If he refuses, they can prosecute him.

Yossarian is at first willing to take the offer. He is exhausted and frightened; he has spent the whole book trying to save himself. Why should that not be enough? But the bargain does not deliver him effortlessly out of the novel. Nately's grieving lover, still pursuing him, attacks and wounds him. He is taken back to the hospital, and there the question of his promised endorsement remains waiting for him. His injury does not prove that staying on the island is safe; it makes the prospect of being sent home more tempting still.

## The secret of Snowden

At the center of these events is the memory Yossarian has never been able to leave behind. During the mission over Avignon, Snowden was wounded in the aircraft. Yossarian went to help him, dealing first with an injury that seemed possible to treat. He tried to comfort the younger man and control the bleeding. Then he discovered that Snowden had suffered a devastating wound to his torso. The body's interior had been torn open. There was no possible medical intervention Yossarian could give him in that plane. Snowden died while Yossarian held him.

This memory changes the meaning of Yossarian's apparent cowardice. He is not frightened by an abstraction called battle. He knows what military language can conceal when it says a man was a casualty. Death is not merely the number on a mission report. It is a person's body destroyed in front of another person who desperately wants him to live.

The novel brings us to the scene repeatedly, withholding its full reality until late. Once you know it, you hear the earlier jokes differently. An officer's ambition and a mission quota are no longer just silly. Every decision has an underside of broken bodies that the paperwork and the laughter can temporarily keep out of sight.

Yossarian considers the officers' bargain and recognizes that his escape would be purchased with an endorsement of further danger for others. He does not suddenly become fearless. He does begin to see that personal survival and moral surrender are not the same thing.

Then he learns something about Orr. His tentmate's repeated incompetence and crashes may not have been incompetence at all. Orr has apparently made it to neutral Sweden by a route he prepared through practice and determination. What had looked like a series of absurd misfortunes becomes evidence of a practical plan. A man has found a way out that neither requires dying obediently nor praising the men who send others to die.

With help and encouragement from the chaplain and others who refuse to treat the officers' bargain as the only reality, Yossarian decides to run. He will try to reach Sweden. He cannot know whether he will succeed. The novel ends with him going, not with a safe arrival or a vindicating ceremony. An enemy may still kill him; the military may catch him; the route may fail. But for the first time the future is not entirely defined by a quota that someone else can increase.

That is the story. A man trying to avoid death in a comic war novel discovers that survival cannot be separated from the lives of others. The book begins with the private invention of hospital symptoms and ends with an actual decision whose risks he accepts. To understand why that movement matters, we need to look more closely at Heller's form, at the way his jokes work, and at the extraordinary number of institutions that learn to say absurd things with perfectly serious faces.

## Reading and source note

This is an original prose orientation to Joseph Heller's 1961 novel, not an audiobook of its copyrighted text. The story has been presented in a more legible sequence than the novel's intentionally recursive chronology. Several plot events in Heller's narrative are ambiguous or surreal; the narration does not present the officers' paperwork as a realistic description of every Allied air unit.
