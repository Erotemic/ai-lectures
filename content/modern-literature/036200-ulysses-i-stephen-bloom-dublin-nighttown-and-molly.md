# Ulysses I: Stephen, Bloom, Dublin, Nighttown, and Molly

## Ulysses — The Story, Part One: Stephen’s Morning

On the morning of June sixteenth, nineteen hundred and four, a young man stands in a stone tower beside Dublin Bay. He is twenty-two. He has been living with two other men, he has very little money, and he does not know where he is going to sleep that night. His name is Stephen Dedalus. He has studied philosophy, literature, theology, and languages; he can make an argument out of almost anything. He is also grieving his mother, who has recently died, and his intelligence is doing a poor job of protecting him from the pain.

Elsewhere in Dublin, a middle-aged advertising salesman named Leopold Bloom will get out of bed and make breakfast. Stephen and Bloom are strangers at the start of the day. They will end it together for a little while, and then they will part. Between those points James Joyce's Ulysses will follow two men walking through the same city while memories, advertisements, songs, grievances, meals, and entire historical traditions compete for their attention. The novel ends not with either man's verdict on life but with the thoughts of Bloom's wife, Molly.

The novel's day is ordinary, but the telling is not. Joyce sometimes records thought with scarcely any preparation, sometimes changes genres entirely, and sometimes lets a phrase from the street interrupt a private recollection. What we need first is the story beneath those methods. The eighteen episodes have traditional names taken from Homer's Odyssey, but those names are chiefly supplied by the explanatory schemes surrounding the novel, not printed as chapter titles in its first edition. We can use them as landmarks without turning the narrative into a quiz about Homer.

## Telemachus: a tower that is not home

Stephen shares the Martello tower with Buck Mulligan, a medical student whose wit is fast, cruel, theatrical, and often genuinely funny. Buck opens the morning by parodying the Catholic Mass with a shaving bowl and a performance of priestly ceremony. He treats Stephen's solemnity as material for comedy. His ease is seductive: he seems able to survive embarrassment by laughing first. Stephen cannot.

The third resident is Haines, an English visitor interested in Ireland and its language. The men discuss a disturbing dream Haines has had, breakfast, debts, the possibility of earning money from Stephen's talents, and the Irish language. Beneath the chatter, Stephen feels dispossessed. The tower is rented through Mulligan, and Stephen has helped pay for it. Yet it is not a space in which he feels at ease, especially after Haines's frightening nighttime behavior. The men pass around Stephen's key as though authority over the building were simply settled by whoever talks loudest.

A woman arrives to deliver milk. Haines addresses her in Irish, which she does not understand. Stephen notices the distance between an idealized national language and the actual speech of an older Dublin woman earning her living. The encounter makes his sense of Ireland no more straightforward than his relation to England: the English visitor can possess a cultivated interest in Irish identity, while an Irish worker may not have access to what that visitor wants to hear.

Then comes the real injury. Stephen's mother died after asking him to kneel and pray by her bedside. He refused. Mulligan has referred to her in language Stephen finds intolerably disrespectful. Stephen is haunted not only by the death but by uncertainty over his refusal. Did he protect his freedom of conscience, or did he fail a woman who needed comfort? The thought cannot be argued away. His mother's image appears again and again, especially when he is tired or drunk. He can be brilliant about Hamlet and theology; he cannot make grief tidy.

Stephen goes bathing neither with Mulligan nor with Haines. He leaves while the others are enjoying the day. He has surrendered the key and, in his own mind, the tower. A man who resists religious, imperial, and domestic authority can still be forced out of a house by a roommate's jokes and a practical disagreement about who holds a key.

## Nestor: teaching history without escaping it

Stephen teaches at a boys' school run by Mr. Deasy. In class he questions the students about ancient history. There is a correct answer and a school exercise to finish, but there are also his private thoughts about what history does to the people forced to inhabit it. The past may be recited as a sequence of rulers and battles. It is harder to explain what it means to be trapped inside inherited institutions.

After class Stephen helps a student, Cyril Sargent, with mathematics. Sargent is awkward, not particularly gifted, and exposed to the impatience of other boys. Stephen sees, fleetingly, how a mother's care can make an unpromising child precious. Here the subject of maternal love returns without warning. A mathematical exercise has become a reminder of what his own mother once gave him, and of his unease at the manner of her death.

Deasy pays Stephen, offers advice about thrift and responsibility, and asks him to deliver a letter about foot-and-mouth disease to the newspapers. Deasy thinks the situation in Ireland can be explained by failures of financial discipline and by the supposed conspiracies of Jews. He voices antisemitic falsehoods while presenting himself as a practical man with historical knowledge. Stephen does not endorse the accusation. Deasy's pronouncements expose how easily confident instruction can become prejudice when history is spoken as certainty rather than examined as evidence.

Stephen accepts the money and letter. He has little choice about the money; he is broke. He can mock the headmaster's confidence, but he still needs wages and has agreed to carry the document into the city. His contempt for practical authority does not release him from dependence upon it. This is a recurring predicament. Stephen wants to make himself into an artist, yet schools, families, churches, and governments continue to supply the circumstances in which his independence must be enacted.

## Proteus: walking by the sea

Stephen walks along Sandymount Strand. Much of what follows occurs in his head. He sees wet sand, shore birds, dogs, human traces, shells, water, and the carcass of a dog. He is also thinking about Aristotle, the relation between sight and sound, fathers and sons, his years in Paris, his mother's death, his neglected appearance, and his own ambitions. There is no single topic because there is no single governing narrator clearing a path through everything that enters a mind.

Some of Stephen's thoughts are grand. He is trying to understand perception itself: how the world reaches us through the senses, how a thing can remain itself while changing appearance, and how language makes a pattern from what we see. Some thoughts are petty. He worries about his clothes, his money, and the people he knows. The movement between registers is essential. A human being does not become incapable of discomfort merely because he can discuss philosophy.

Stephen remembers family distress and his return from Paris when his mother was dying. He thinks of his father, Simon Dedalus, and of a household in decline. One association leads him toward a book, another toward a bodily sensation, another toward a myth. Joyce does not guarantee that every association will be explained. The reader sometimes has to listen as one listens to a stranger's half-remembered remark: by attending to its direction, not demanding that every proper noun immediately become familiar.

There is a small but revealing difference between Stephen's thoughts and the place where he stands. The strand is crowded with life and death indifferent to his theories. Dogs sniff and run. People move at a distance. The sea works upon the shore whether or not the young philosopher is ready to define it. Even the writing changes texture as his consciousness brushes against what he cannot control. He leaves traces of his own in the sand and moves away.

For a long while this has been a novel about a young artist who wants to break from his circumstances. Now Joyce is ready to bring in a very different hero. The point of following Stephen first is not that Stephen will turn out to be the book's secret center. It is that we have met someone intensely skilled at interpreting the world, yet not particularly skilled at living in it. The man who becomes the central wanderer is less impressive in conversation, more practical in his sympathies, and more openly interested in the needs of bodies. His name is Leopold Bloom.

## A morning without a resolution

Nothing in Stephen's morning is resolved. His mother remains dead. The dispute with Mulligan has not been repaired. His employment is precarious. His family's poverty remains. His literary aspirations are not yet a livelihood. And the man whose path will briefly cross his has not yet met him in the unfolding action.

What changes is the way the novel asks us to attend. A tower breakfast, a school lesson, and a walk by the sea can hold a crisis of belief, history's machinery, and the repeated question of whether the young artist has a home. Joyce does not offer a speech announcing the theme. He gives us a man who thinks in borrowed texts and arguments as he moves through material facts. Even the most educated mind must wake somewhere, earn something, and carry a body along a shore.

## Ulysses — The Story, Part Two: Bloom’s Day

Leopold Bloom's morning begins at home, at seven Eccles Street. He is thirty-eight, married to a singer named Marion Bloom, called Molly, and employed selling newspaper advertisements. He moves through his kitchen attending to details that a grand epic might leave outside its field of vision. A cat wants feeding. The stove and breakfast require attention. Bloom buys a pork kidney at a butcher's shop and cooks it for himself. He brings Molly her breakfast in bed. We discover his character not by hearing a declaration of principles but by watching how he handles a dish, a door, a letter, an animal, a person.

This is the beginning of the fourth episode, usually called Calypso. We have left Stephen Dedalus, the brilliant, grieving young teacher, and entered a mind with a different rhythm. Bloom notices prices, smells, foods, streets, advertisements, the habits of people around him, and possible improvements to nearly everything he sees. His thoughts are not less complex than Stephen's. They are less committed to sounding important.

## Calypso: what is known at breakfast

A letter arrives from Milly, the Blooms' daughter, who is staying away from home and learning photography. She is young enough that Bloom's pride in her emerging independence is mixed with worry about the men beginning to notice her. The letter recalls a family whose ordinary intimacies have been changed by a child's departure.

Another loss is older and much harder to name. Bloom and Molly once had a son, Rudy, who died as an infant. Their marriage has not recovered the uncomplicated physical intimacy it had before his death. Joyce gives the reader no single medical or psychological key that makes everything afterward inevitable. The child's absence persists as a possibility unfulfilled: Bloom imagines the boy growing, walking with him, becoming someone whose future could have been known.

There is also a more immediate difficulty. Molly has arranged to see her concert manager, Hugh 'Blazes' Boylan, later that day. Bloom believes that the visit will include sex. He can think about the appointment, imagine it, resent it, and try to distract himself, but he does not confront Molly directly that morning. Their marriage contains care, shared history, guarded private lives, and an anticipated betrayal. It is not adequately described by any single one of those terms.

Bloom leaves home carrying funeral arrangements in mind. An acquaintance, Paddy Dignam, has died, and Bloom will attend the burial. Before doing so, he moves across the city on minor errands, entering a Dublin that will continually address him through other people's demands and expectations.

## Lotus Eaters: the pleasures of postponement

Bloom collects a letter at the post office under the name Henry Flower. He has been exchanging flirtatious correspondence with a woman named Martha Clifford. The alias sounds romantic and a little ridiculous; the emotional impulse behind it is recognizable. Bloom is wounded by what he believes Molly will do, yet he is also searching for a private form of attention elsewhere. Neither action licenses a simple account of innocent husband and guilty wife.

He reads the letter, thinks about its language and how far to carry the exchange, buys a cake of soap, and visits a church. The service interests him as a sequence of gestures, objects, perfumes, repeated words, and effects on the congregation. He is not simply mocking belief. He is curious about how belief works inside a physical setting. Later he goes to a public bath, anticipating the comfort of warm water and the body's temporary release from pressure.

The Homeric title, Lotus Eaters, suggests forgetfulness and seductive rest, but Bloom is not intoxicated on an enchanted island. He is postponing uncomfortable things through errands, ritual, flirtation, and a bath. That is one of Joyce's central transformations: the great danger of losing one's way can look like a series of agreeable interruptions before a funeral.

## Hades: a city of the living carrying its dead

Bloom travels to Glasnevin Cemetery in a carriage with men who knew the deceased. Martin Cunningham is capable of practical kindness. Jack Power speaks with the assurance of a familiar social circle. Simon Dedalus, Stephen's father, is among them. The carriage passes through Dublin, giving its occupants opportunities to name streets, remember people, speculate about the dead, and judge the living. Their conversation is sociable, careless, and sometimes cruel.

For Bloom, death opens several doors at once. He thinks of Paddy Dignam's widow and children and what financial arrangements might protect them. He thinks of his father, who died by suicide. He thinks of Rudy. These are different kinds of loss: an acquaintance's funeral, a parent's death weighted by stigma, and the child who should have grown up. They converge without becoming the same event.

Bloom's manner sets him apart. The other men sometimes find his questions and practical comments odd. He thinks about cemetery systems, the means of burial, the persistence of memory, and the less elevated bodily processes involved in death. He is not indifferent to the deceased because he notices the physical details. On the contrary, he remembers that the dead person leaves people behind with rent and food to think about. He has a capacity for sympathy that can sound unceremonious because it refuses to stay within a formal language of mourning.

The funeral also reveals how a city carries its dead into everyday social relations. Men who have behaved badly become objects of affectionate remembrance, while people still alive can be judged as if they were already fixed characters. Bloom, who is treated as an outsider for reasons connected to his family background and Jewishness, hears such talk without being entirely protected from it. After the service the men return to the city. An epic descent into the land of the dead has become a burial attended by people who will still need lunch.

## Aeolus: the newspaper as a machine for talk

At the newspaper offices Bloom works at arranging an advertisement. He is not a great executive and has no power to command the people around him. He knows how an advertisement must connect a business, a paper, and a reader; he asks whether a particular design can be executed. The editor, Myles Crawford, and his associates spend much of their time talking about politics, rhetoric, old Dublin journalism, and notable speeches. Stephen arrives with Deasy's letter about foot-and-mouth disease and becomes part of the conversation.

The men's eloquence is attractive, but it can also be a way of avoiding work. They speak as though the past were a storehouse of powerful sentences to be quoted or improved upon. Joyce alters the chapter's very appearance: newspaper-like headlines interrupt the action, as if the conventions of the newspaper were taking over the way the novel reports its own events. The city produces language, and language in turn produces an image of the city.

Stephen tells a story involving two elderly women climbing a Dublin monument. The anecdote shows an emerging writer trying to make art from the ordinary materials of his city, rather than solving any of his immediate problems. Bloom is present in the same institution, yet the two men have not met in the meaningful way toward which the day's design is moving. They share a building before they share a conversation.

## Lestrygonians: what appetite reveals

Lunch arrives, and Joyce makes hunger another instrument of observation. Bloom looks into one eating establishment and is put off by the sight of people consuming food. He chooses a quieter alternative, Davy Byrne's pub, where he eats a Gorgonzola sandwich and drinks wine. His mind continues to work while he eats. He watches passing people, thinks of advertisements and charities, and remembers the little adjustments by which a person keeps life comfortable.

He also attempts to avoid Boylan, whom he knows or suspects is on his way toward Molly. Bloom's problem is almost painfully ordinary. He cannot stop the appointment merely by seeing its arranger. He does not want a public confrontation. He shifts his route through the city as though a change of street could modify what will occur inside his house.

The name Lestrygonians recalls the cannibal giants who destroy part of Odysseus's fleet. Here their appetite appears in crowded eating rooms and the body's demand for sustenance. Joyce need not claim that lunch is literally violence. He lets an ancient image sharpen the reader's recognition of how animal, social, and moral life overlap. Bloom needs food and dislikes watching others eat; he envies intimacy and avoids its risks; he wants a home and spends much of the day moving away from it.

## A day with an approaching hour

By early afternoon the two central lives are still on different tracks. Stephen has left a tower, tried to teach, and begun to circulate among Dublin's men of letters. Bloom has fed his family, attended a funeral, pursued work, collected a private letter, and eaten lunch. He has also spent hours approaching the time when his wife expects Boylan.

None of this is filler around a heroic adventure. The incidental business is the adventure, and it gives the future encounter between the men its meaning. When Bloom offers someone kindness later in the night, he will not suddenly have become a hero. The day's errands, his attention to grieving families, and his respect for people whom others mock will have prepared us to recognize the act. The larger story is still moving, carried by a man's effort to keep living amid a grief he cannot repair and a betrayal he cannot yet bear to face.

## Ulysses — The Story, Part Three: The City Speaks

By afternoon, Stephen Dedalus and Leopold Bloom have spent hours moving through Dublin without yet forming a relationship. Stephen has been to a school and a newspaper office. Bloom has cooked a kidney, attended a funeral, negotiated an advertisement, and tried to avoid thinking about his wife's planned meeting with Blazes Boylan. Now the story begins to behave less and less like a novel told by one reliable voice. A library becomes a debate about how a poet writes. A network of streets becomes a map of intersecting lives. A bar becomes music. Another bar becomes a quarrel whose storytellers cannot be trusted to keep the scale of an event in proportion.

The plot is still simple enough to follow. But the city no longer seems to have a single way of describing itself.

## Scylla and Charybdis: an argument that cannot become a home

Stephen visits the National Library and discusses Shakespeare with a group of literary acquaintances. His theory begins with a provocative question: what relation did Shakespeare's personal life bear to the story of Hamlet? He speculates about fathers, sons, marriage, betrayal, and the playwright's emotional history. Much of the argument concerns the tension between an author who creates a work and the same author who disappears inside it.

Stephen is witty and inventive. His listeners are not always convinced, and they do not have to be. At points his confident argument seems to expose what he is himself struggling to understand: a dead mother, a difficult father, a desire to create without simply repeating family inheritance. He speaks about another writer's marriage and child partly because his own life's questions will not leave him alone.

Buck Mulligan arrives and subjects the proceeding to his habitual mockery. Stephen's intellectual self-display is vulnerable to ridicule, and he knows it. Toward the end he even says he does not believe his own theory. The admission does not mean the debate was pointless. It tells us something about the difference between an interpretation that is illuminating and one that must be defended as a biographical fact. A theory can reveal the speaker's obsessions without being the final truth of Shakespeare's intentions.

Bloom is also at the library, attending to an errand. Paths cross through rooms and passages; the structure brings characters together spatially before they understand how to approach one another. Stephen is imagining fathers and sons on a literary scale. Bloom has lost a son and will later find himself caring for a younger man, without that man becoming a replacement for the child. The parallel is available to us before it is available to them.

## Wandering Rocks: nineteen passages through one city

The novel now moves among people rather than following one consciousness. Father Conmee, a Jesuit priest, travels across town. The Dedalus sisters struggle in an impoverished household. A one-legged sailor begs in the streets. Men pass shops, recall debts, look for companions, collect parcels, perform their business, and overhear one another. A person mentioned in one fragment may appear in another, sometimes at almost the same moment from a new angle.

The episode is divided into nineteen movements. It opens with Father Conmee and ends with the progress of the viceroy's carriage through Dublin. Between these two figures of ecclesiastical and imperial authority, the city is full of people whose journeys never become official events. The geography is both precise and disorienting. We are never standing high enough above Dublin to make it completely transparent.

Joyce often lets a snippet from somewhere else intrude into a scene. An event seems to have arrived before its explanation. A familiar name suddenly belongs to another location. One can treat this as a puzzle to solve on a map, and maps are genuinely useful. Yet the more important experience is that the city exceeds any person walking through it. Each inhabitant has an itinerary; nobody possesses all the itineraries.

Boylan appears in this network, proceeding toward his appointment with Molly. Bloom and Stephen appear too. But the book does not close in upon the adulterous meeting as a conventional domestic novel might. Its perspective keeps circulating. Even the action that torments Bloom is one thread among the errands of an entire population. This is not a claim that Bloom's distress is trivial. It is a reminder that the world does not stop to recognize a private emergency.

## Sirens: the room plays its visitors

At the Ormond Hotel, two barmaids, Miss Douce and Miss Kennedy, stand at the center of talk and sexual attention. People gather to drink, flirt, and listen to singing. Music moves through the bar. Simon Dedalus, Stephen's father, sings. Ben Dollard sings too. Bloom enters and takes a meal while sound, memory, and expectation run together in his head.

The episode begins with broken verbal fragments that seem, at first, hardly to belong to a story. Then the sounds return in contexts where they acquire shape. Repetition works like a musical motif: a phrase can be comic in one place and painful in another, and an apparently idle noise becomes part of an elaborate pattern. Joyce is not simply describing musicians. He is making the novel's language imitate the experience of music without becoming a score.

Bloom hears and watches while aware that Boylan is bound for Eccles Street. In the bar he makes notes and thinks about his correspondence with Martha Clifford. One private flirtation travels through his mind while another man's visit to Molly hangs over the day. The voices of singers, the sounds of glasses, the hotel staff, passing traffic, and the words Bloom does not want to say form a kind of competition for his attention.

Music can gather people into a temporary public feeling. It can also leave a listener startlingly alone. A song performed for everyone reaches Bloom through the particular losses he carries: Rudy, his father, Molly, and the imagined future he can no longer simply expect. Joyce's technical bravura does not remove these losses; it gives them another shape. The same sound that seems to be filling a room can make one person feel everything the room does not know about him.

## Cyclops: the public house and the single eye

The twelfth episode takes place in and around Barney Kiernan's pub. The main storyteller is an unnamed man with his own prejudices and resentments. His language is sharp, impatient, and full of innuendo. He makes Bloom appear irritating before we can consider Bloom's conduct independently. Other voices interrupt with extravagant imitations of journalism, legal record, scripture, genealogy, and heroic narrative. Ordinary events are described as ceremonies of national significance, then reduced again to gossip.

A nationalist known as the Citizen dominates the bar. He is furious about Ireland's subordination to Britain and speaks of national belonging as if it were an exclusive possession. Ireland's political injuries are real; that does not make the Citizen's definition of who counts as Irish correct. His anger turns repeatedly into antisemitic attacks on Bloom. The Citizen demands proofs of belonging while refusing the possibility that a person may have more than one inheritance.

Bloom's family history is complicated. His father came to Ireland from continental Europe and had Jewish roots. Bloom has been baptized, and his religious practice does not fit a simple stereotype. None of these details stops his neighbors from treating Jewishness as something to weaponize against him. The scene is not a neutral dispute over immigration. It shows a man being forced to defend his personhood against a group whose shared jokes are becoming threats.

Bloom argues that persecution and hatred cannot constitute a healthy national ideal. He speaks of love as something other than submission to prejudice. It would be sentimental to pretend that this settles the quarrel. His appeal is mocked. The Citizen grows more belligerent, and Martin Cunningham helps Bloom get away. As the carriage departs, Bloom retorts that Jesus was Jewish. Enraged, the Citizen hurls a biscuit tin after him and misses.

In Homer, the one-eyed Cyclops throws great stones after Odysseus's ship. Joyce reduces the projectile to a tin, but the narrative voice blows up its significance into an absurd spectacle. That absurdity has two targets. The Citizen is dangerous because he can see only a narrow idea of national identity. The storyteller is dangerous in another way: he makes the small and ugly event grand, as though violent prejudice could be dignified by sufficiently impressive language.

## Voices that do not agree

Notice what has happened between the library and the pub. Stephen's talk about Shakespeare asks how a person's private life enters an artwork. The wandering streets show how a hundred partial lives can coexist without one controlling narrative. The hotel makes private memory answer to public music. The pub shows collective storytelling capable of magnifying anger and concealing cruelty.

Bloom has not defeated the Citizen in any lasting political sense. He has escaped an ugly scene. Stephen has not solved the problem of fathers and sons by delivering an argument. The city has not made their paths converge into a friendship. And Molly's meeting with Boylan is not shown to us directly at the moment it most troubles Bloom.

An epic usually promises that the hero's encounter with a monster will clarify who he is. Here we recognize Bloom through what he refuses: he will not accept that national loyalty requires hatred of people whom a nationalist wishes to exclude. The act is small and vulnerable; his opponent is ridiculous as well as menacing. That mixture is Joyce's way of making moral life visible in a bar at the end of an ordinary afternoon.

## Ulysses — The Story, Part Four: Desire and Nighttown

The afternoon quarrel at Barney Kiernan's pub ends with Leopold Bloom leaving under a shower of insults and an ineffectual flying biscuit tin. He has not resolved what will happen at home. He has not yet formed a bond with Stephen Dedalus. He is simply moving on through Dublin, carrying fatigue, curiosity, grief, and the uneasy knowledge that Molly has probably received Blazes Boylan. The novel now enters a stretch in which desire and its representations become impossible to separate.

There are three episodes: Nausicaa, Oxen of the Sun, and Circe. The first puts Bloom on a strand watching a young woman. The second takes him to a maternity hospital where men drink and joke during a woman's labor. The third turns an actual Dublin district into a stage on which desires and fears appear as apparitions. Throughout, Joyce makes the reader ask what belongs to the visible world, what is imagined by a character, and what is produced by the novel's altered style.

## Nausicaa: a romantic view from the other side

At Sandymount Strand, near the shore that Stephen walked in the morning, three young women are looking after children. One is Gerty MacDowell. Gerty thinks about clothes, marriage, beauty, disappointment, and the kind of suitor a popular romance would promise. Her mental language is filled with the expressions and emotional conventions of advertisements and sentimental fiction. We are allowed to occupy her imaginative world, but that occupation is complicated. Is this Gerty's inward voice, the language her culture has taught her, a narrator imitating it, or a mixture of all three? Joyce does not label the layers for us.

Gerty becomes aware of Bloom at a distance. There is an exchange of looking, and she gradually poses so that he can see more of her body. Fireworks rise from a nearby celebration. Bloom masturbates while watching her, reaching climax as the fireworks burst. In a sanitized account of the novel, the episode might be called a moment of connection and stop there. It would miss both the bodily action and the difficulty of describing what consent, desire, power, and mutual knowledge mean when two strangers communicate almost entirely through looking.

When Gerty gets up and leaves, Bloom notices that she limps. The detail changes the reader's understanding of the opening sentimental portrait. Her disability has not been a defining fact in the prose's earlier account of her appearance, and Bloom must revise his impression. His reaction is mixed, containing observation, sympathy, and assumptions that are not especially generous. Joyce offers no certificate of enlightened conduct. Bloom can be tender in one scene and troubling in another.

After the women depart, Bloom stays near the shore thinking about Molly, Boylan, his daughter, female bodies, reproduction, and the events of the day. The suspense concerning his marriage has not vanished. If anything, the sexual encounter on the beach has exposed how easily private unhappiness can be redirected into an encounter that provides no durable companionship.

## Oxen of the Sun: language is born and grows distorted

At the National Maternity Hospital, Mina Purefoy is in labor with another child. Bloom visits and finds a group of younger men, including medical students, drinking, joking, discussing sex, and behaving with an apparent distance from the physical ordeal taking place nearby. Stephen is among them, and so is Buck Mulligan. The sound of childbirth and the talk of young men belong to the same institution, yet they receive strikingly different kinds of attention.

The chapter's form is part of the story. Its prose passes through a sequence of styles that recall the history of English writing, from early forms toward contemporary slang and advertising. The changing language is often compared with the development of a fetus, a metaphor of growth toward birth. But the passage is more than an ingenious literary exercise. It makes every way of speaking appear historically made. A male gathering can convert a laboring woman's experience into jokes, learned analogies, medical remarks, and displays of verbal virtuosity without adequately sharing her experience at all.

Bloom is not immune to the culture of the room, but he pays attention to the risks to mother and child. He has known both fatherhood and infant death. His concern about Stephen is now becoming specific. Stephen is drunk, surrounded by people who may enjoy his talents without taking responsibility for his welfare. Bloom recognizes the vulnerability underneath the performance.

News of the birth comes, and the young men go out toward another drinking establishment. One child's arrival into the world has been surrounded by an extraordinary excess of language. The event itself remains simple, bodily, serious. Bloom follows, increasingly less willing to leave Stephen to the night's hazards.

## Circe: the district becomes a theatre

In Dublin's red-light district, often called Nighttown, the novel abandons ordinary narrative for the form of a play. Speakers are named. Stage directions expand. Objects speak. People change sex, social rank, or identity. Laws are pronounced in instant grotesque trials. The dead return. Time behaves erratically. This is not a record of supernatural occurrences that every inhabitant of Dublin sees. Much of it is dramatic rendering of fear, fantasy, guilt, cultural stereotypes, and passing thought. Yet real actions continue inside the theatrical hallucination.

Bloom enters the district looking for Stephen. Along the way imagined accusations attack him from many directions. He becomes, in fantasy, a public reformer, a criminal, a laughingstock, an object of female and male attention, and a person whose sexual anxieties have been exposed before a courtroom. His life has supplied all these contradictory selves: he longs to be recognized as decent while fearing that others see only his humiliations.

At Bella Cohen's brothel, the hallucinations become more openly sexual and coercive. Bella is transformed within the fantasy into a domineering male figure called Bello; Bloom is placed in a submissive position. The sequence can be disturbing and comic at once. The theater is not a transparent guide to what Bloom literally desires. It stages desires alongside shame, caricatures, and imagined punishment. One must resist turning an invented trial into a psychological diagnosis.

Stephen is drunk and increasingly unable to keep his mother's death outside his mind. Her apparition appears in terrifying form, calling him toward religious repentance. Stephen resists and strikes at the vision, smashing a chandelier with his walking stick. His intellectual rebellion has become an action with material consequences. The brothel's staff demand compensation. The hallucinatory scene and the practical trouble are happening together, but they are not the same thing.

Outside, Stephen gets into a confrontation with British soldiers. The conflict draws on political hostility, personal insult, masculine pride, and Stephen's drunken recklessness. A soldier strikes him and he falls. The police appear. Bloom intervenes, attempts to keep Stephen from being arrested or further harmed, and takes responsibility for getting him away. Nothing in Bloom's day has prepared him to enjoy a fight, and he has little authority in the district. Nevertheless, he stays.

Near the end Bloom sees an apparition of Rudy, his dead son, imagined as the child might have appeared had he lived and grown older. The vision is not an objective resurrection. It is one of the novel's clearest meetings of care and grief. Bloom has just protected another man's son, or at least a young man whose own father is absent from the scene. In the act of caring for Stephen he has not recovered Rudy. The imagined boy remains lost.

## What has actually changed?

The events of the night can be summarized plainly: Bloom sought out Stephen, found him drunk in a dangerous district, witnessed disturbances and violence, and helped him avoid further harm. But a bare summary leaves out the emotional ordeal through which Joyce makes the rescue possible. In the space of one episode Joyce has made shame, desire, nationalism, religion, parental loss, and the fear of social punishment appear in bodies on a theatrical stage.

Bloom may not be a heroic warrior, yet he acts when another person is exposed. Stephen may be brilliant, yet he has become defenseless after a mixture of drink, grief, and bravado. Their relationship is not the fulfillment of a grand destiny. It begins in a vulnerable moment when the older man chooses to follow rather than walk away.

The rest of the night will take them from spectacle into exhaustion, then from exhaustion toward a house. After so much elaborate language, the next questions will be remarkably simple. Can they find a quiet room? Can Stephen get something to eat or drink? Will he accept Bloom's hospitality? And what does it mean for Bloom to go home to the marriage he has spent the day circling?

## Ulysses — The Story, Part Five: Home and Molly

After the hallucinations and violence of Nighttown, Stephen Dedalus is exhausted and drunk. Leopold Bloom has followed him, kept him out of further trouble, and now tries to get him somewhere safer. The extraordinary stage-play language of the red-light district gives way to talk that is tired, circuitous, and imperfectly informed. Joyce calls the episode Eumaeus, after the hospitable swineherd who shelters Odysseus. The allusion is grander than the place, but the hospitality is real.

## Eumaeus: not much to say, and much to be avoided

Bloom and Stephen reach a cabman's shelter. They meet other late-night men, including a sailor whose stories of distant places may be partly invented. In the small hours, people can tell stories because their listeners are too tired to demand perfect evidence. Bloom talks with Stephen about nationality, work, politics, music, and the difficulties of making a living. Stephen answers with a mixture of acuity and detachment. Neither man has suddenly discovered a perfect counterpart in the other.

The narration itself seems worn down. Sentences trail into clichés and confused explanations. Details are supplied at such length that the point is sometimes lost. The style is important because it denies the expectation that a great meeting between two protagonists must sound grand. An older man and a younger one are making an awkward attempt to communicate while desperately in need of rest. Their ideas cross and separate. Bloom's sympathy can become overattentive and patronizing; Stephen's independence can become mere resistance to accepting help.

The shelter is also full of uncertain information. The sailor's accounts, the men's discussions, and the narrator's own phrases do not authorize us to reconstruct every biographical fact as if it were an official record. Joyce has shown how a newspaper can speak in headlines and a pub in national boasts. Now he shows how fatigue can make language both generous and unreliable.

Bloom eventually asks Stephen to come home with him. The invitation is practical. It offers a roof, something warm to drink, and a temporary place outside the city's nighttime dangers. For a long day Bloom has been moving away from his own house. Now he is willing to open it to a stranger whose concerns he cannot fully understand. Stephen accepts the invitation to visit, not the role of a permanent substitute son.

## Ithaca: return in questions and answers

They reach seven Eccles Street. Bloom must let them into the house after discovering that he does not have his front-door key. The journey has brought him back to the very place whose domestic crisis gave the day its private suspense. Molly is upstairs. Boylan has already visited. The house is familiar, but homecoming does not restore the morning's condition.

In the kitchen Bloom and Stephen prepare cocoa and talk. They compare languages, beliefs, habits, and memories; the differences between them do not disappear. Stephen's education has equipped him with a dense culture of texts, while Bloom's knowledge is mixed with inventions, calculations, and practical experimentation. Each may recognize in the other a kind of possibility. The older man has lost an infant son. The younger one is estranged from his father and troubled by his mother's death. Their encounter opens a possibility of tenderness without literally replacing the missing members of either man's family.

Joyce tells this episode almost entirely as a catechism: a sequence of questions and answers, often in the language of scientific report, mathematics, or official inventory. It is an extraordinary choice for a scene of quiet, awkward companionship. How was the water drawn? What objects were in a room? What observations could be made of the stars? The catechism can ask what a character's feelings were, but it answers through external arrangements or apparently complete classifications that may tell us less than a silence would.

The form creates a strange experience of precision. A sentence can look like the output of a perfect measuring instrument while describing matters no instrument could settle. The elaborate list becomes comic when placed beside cocoa, washing up, the history of household objects, and two men struggling to find words for their lives. Sometimes the questions become so minute that the reader sees how demanding a full record of an ordinary night would be. We might know the temperature of water and remain uncertain what one person means to another.

Bloom offers Stephen a place to stay. Stephen refuses. This refusal is not an artistic rejection of kindness so much as the continuation of a life that cannot be solved by an invitation. They go outside. Under the stars, both men urinate, a bodily action that the catechism describes with its characteristic seriousness. After the sweep of cosmic observation, the body returns. Stephen leaves, and Bloom watches him go.

There is no adoption, no permanent union, and no speech that closes the older man's grief. For a brief interval each has offered the other a form of attention missing from the rest of the day. That is enough to matter. The novel is much more honest for allowing the connection to end without transforming it into a solution.

Bloom returns inside, notices traces of the day's household activity, and goes upstairs. He climbs into bed with Molly. He has to decide what to say about his day. The report he gives her is selective, just as each of their private accounts has been selective. He asks about breakfast, speaks of work, and adjusts himself to the knowledge that the bed and the marriage are not untouched by someone else's presence. He is exhausted. The day of wandering ends in a room shared with another person whom he does not entirely know.

## Penelope: the wife has a mind of her own

Most of the novel has shown Molly as Bloom imagines her: singer, wife, lover, mother, desired body, source of tenderness and jealousy. The final episode changes the authority of the whole book. Bloom falls asleep, and Molly's thoughts take over. We enter a voice that moves in long stretches with almost no conventional punctuation. She remembers her lovers, the day's sex with Boylan, her marriage, her daughter Milly, her lost child Rudy, the complicated people of Dublin, and her childhood in Gibraltar.

This is not a confession made to the reader, a courtroom statement, or a speech to her husband. It is the restless movement of a mind through its own associations. Molly can be vain, affectionate, furious, shrewd, ungenerous, sensuous, and tired within a short passage. She makes judgments, contradicts herself, enjoys memories, revises them, and attends to her body. Neither Bloom's worry nor Boylan's desire has told us all there is to know about her.

As in Bloom's breakfast, physical detail is part of the mind Joyce brings onto the page. Molly's thoughts include menstruation, urination, sexual experience, clothing, and bodily discomfort. Joyce refuses to reserve the dignity of consciousness for a character when the character is thinking lofty thoughts. But neither does he make frankness a guarantee of wisdom. A thought that arrives without a period is still shaped by prejudice, wishes, and memory. Molly's voice belongs to Molly as a fictional consciousness, while the written form is the result of deliberate literary artistry.

Some memories carry her far from the bed in Dublin to the landscape and sunlight of Gibraltar, where she grew up. Others return her to the history of her marriage. She recalls Bloom asking her to marry him. At the close of the novel, her memory centers on her acceptance, on the bodily closeness and affection of the moment. The word yes recurs, and the book ends with it.

The ending is not evidence that Molly has decided to renounce Boylan or that the Blooms' marriage will now proceed without pain. She has not delivered a judgment about the future. Her mind has returned to a moment when she actively chose the man now sleeping beside her. The closing affirmation is real, yet it exists alongside the day's betrayals and the loss of Rudy. This is the life Joyce asks us to recognize: a person may hold contradictions that do not disappear merely because the final word is affirmative.

## A whole day, and no grand victory

We have now reached the end of the novel's outward action. Stephen began in a tower and did not find a secure home. Bloom began at home and spent the day wandering in the shadow of a feared appointment. He attended a funeral, faced prejudice, watched a young woman on a beach, and protected a drunken stranger. Molly lived a day largely outside the book's direct view until the final hours, when the novel gave her consciousness the last word.

An ancient epic is said to end when the traveler comes home and restores his household. Joyce offers a smaller, harder form of return. Bloom comes home, but his marriage is changed and remains unresolved. Stephen has accepted kindness but not a new father. Molly remembers an earlier yes, but not as an uncomplicated cure for the present. The day's victories are partial: someone was protected from harm; a room was shared; the dead were remembered; an ordinary life continued.

That is the full story of Ulysses in one sense. In another, the story is inseparable from how it has been told. The next work for a reader is to understand why a novel about an advertisement canvasser's day becomes an experiment in the very possibilities of narration: how the same city can be heard through newspapers, music, hallucinations, scientific questions, and the unpunctuated language of an inward voice. Its form is not decoration around a hidden conventional plot. It is Joyce's way of asking what it would take to tell any life fully.
