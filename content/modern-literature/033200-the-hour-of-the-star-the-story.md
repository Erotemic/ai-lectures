# The Hour of the Star — The Story

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

Imagine being asked to tell the life of a person who has spent almost all her life escaping notice. There are familiar ways to begin a story. We might identify her parents, put her in a house, give her a problem, and watch her find a way through it. Clarice Lispector's *The Hour of the Star* does something more disquieting. Before we can meet its protagonist, we must listen to the man who is trying to tell her story. He does not know whether he has the right words. More troublingly, he is not sure whether he has the right to speak for her at all.

His name is Rodrigo S. M. He is a fictional narrator, not simply the author speaking under another name. The book opens with a series of possible titles and a writer who keeps approaching the point of beginning, then turning away. He talks about his method, his discomfort, the rawness of the story he wants to tell. He claims he will use plain language. Yet every declaration of simplicity gives him more room to examine himself. The young woman about whom he means to write has scarcely entered the book, and already we know a great deal about the man who proposes to make her visible.

The novel is short, but this delay is one of its central events. The reader waits while a writer considers how to represent someone whose circumstances are radically different from his own. He eventually gives her a name: Macabéa.

## The young woman in the city

Macabéa is nineteen. She was born in Alagoas in Brazil's northeast, a region whose economic inequality and migration into the wealthier southeastern cities shape the world she inhabits. Her parents died when she was young. An aunt raised her with harsh discipline and little tenderness. When the aunt dies, Macabéa is left to make her way in Rio de Janeiro, a city she does not understand and that has no reason to notice her.

She works as a typist, though she is a poor one. Her pages are full of mistakes; she is not properly trained for the job. Her boss grows impatient and considers dismissing her. She asks to stay, apologizing, and eventually he relents. The concession preserves her income, but it does not give her security. She has neither savings nor influential friends nor the kind of professional polish that might protect her in a competitive city.

Her room is crowded, shared with other young women. Her meals are cheap and inadequate. She lives on small pleasures: a hot dog, a cup of coffee, the sensation of being briefly alone. When she has a little time to herself, she may listen to the radio, which tells the hour and delivers snippets of information and advertisements. She repeats what she hears, sometimes without understanding it. The world enters her life as names of products, fragments of facts, and promises made to people other than her.

Macabéa is fascinated by movie stars and glossy images of beauty. Marilyn Monroe represents a radiance to which she has no obvious access. She is not shown making a coherent plan to become famous. The picture matters because she wants, in a small and inarticulate way, to know what it would feel like to be a person whom others see.

She is frequently tired and unwell. A more confident character might complain that her employer mistreats her or that she cannot afford food. Macabéa rarely possesses the language for such a complaint. Rodrigo tells us she does not understand the causes of her discomfort. We should notice the distinction: this is how her narrator describes her, and he can make even her bewilderment sound like a failing. She knows some things by sensation, by longing, and by daily repetition. The fact that she cannot formulate a social analysis of her poverty does not make the poverty unreal.

Rodrigo sometimes interrupts to tell us what he thinks of her body, her slowness, her innocence, or his own suffering as her narrator. At other moments he seems moved by her bare persistence. His tone can be cruel and protective within the space of a paragraph. So the question of Macabéa's life is braided together with another question: can someone give a neglected person narrative attention without turning that person into a monument to the narrator's sensitivity?

## A boyfriend who wants to rise

Macabéa meets Olímpico de Jesus, another migrant from the northeast. Their initial bond is simple: they recognize something of their origin in one another. But Olímpico wants much more than companionship. He is a factory worker who dreams of rising in the world, acquiring money and importance, perhaps entering politics. He talks as though success will be an act of personal will. He is quick to recognize humiliation in his own life and much slower to recognize it when he inflicts it on others.

Their courtship consists of walks and awkward conversations. Macabéa passes along strange little items she has heard on the radio. Olímpico finds them tiresome. He asks questions that expose how little she knows of herself. Sometimes she answers vaguely because she cannot think of an answer; sometimes she is simply pleased that they are together. They do not create a shared language. She imagines he is her boyfriend. He examines what association with her might mean for his ambitions and finds little to recommend it.

Their relationship is not a dramatic romance spoiled by one terrible mistake. It is a succession of small occasions on which a person with a little power makes another person feel smaller. There are brief pleasures, but almost no mutual curiosity. Even the compliments Macabéa hopes to receive are filtered through Olímpico's interest in rank and advancement. He does not have the kind of wealth or status that commands the city. Yet with Macabéa he can rehearse the part of somebody superior.

At Macabéa's office works Glória. She is more comfortable with the social codes of Rio, more assured in her appearance and in conversations with men. She too has limits and disappointments, but she knows how to present herself as someone entitled to be noticed. Olímpico encounters her and rapidly redirects his attention. He leaves Macabéa and begins courting Glória, whom he considers a better prospect.

Macabéa is hurt, though she has difficulty making a scene out of hurt. She tries bright lipstick, imitating the glamour she has admired in photographs. The gesture does not transform her. It reveals the distance between the available image of womanhood and the life she actually leads. Her attempt to put herself together meets a world that has already decided what sort of woman deserves admiration.

Glória is not only Macabéa's successful rival. She feels a measure of guilt. She offers kindness, advice, and small material assistance, although she cannot undo what has happened. The two women do not become equal through this gesture; Macabéa remains dependent on a kindness she cannot demand. Still, it is one of the few moments in the story when another person looks at her with something resembling concern.

## Doctors and predictions

Macabéa visits a doctor. Her food and health are poor, and the medical encounter exposes another gap between an institution and the person it is supposed to serve. The doctor is impatient, and even his practical advice assumes resources she does not possess. A person cannot simply acquire a better diet by being told she ought to have one. She leaves with little changed. The consultation has given a name to her vulnerability without giving her the means to escape it.

Then Glória encourages her to visit a fortune-teller. The woman is called Madame Carlota, a former sex worker and madam who has remade herself as someone selling a view of the future. Macabéa has scarcely traveled beyond the narrow circuits of her life; the trip itself feels momentous. Carlota talks energetically, occupies space, and takes command of the conversation. After hearing of Macabéa's past and present, she reacts with horror at how little happiness the girl has known.

But the fortune-teller does not stop there. She offers Macabéa a magnificent future. Happiness will arrive. There will be prosperity, romance, and a wealthy foreign man who will care for her. Details matter less than the decisive change in tense. For once, someone speaks to Macabéa as if her life contains possibilities. She is no longer just the girl who makes typing mistakes and loses a boyfriend. She is the center of a story about what might happen next.

Macabéa emerges from the appointment almost intoxicated by hope. It is perhaps the most concentrated happiness of her life. Nothing in the city has actually changed. Her wages are the same. The room, the office, the class divisions, and the indifference of passersby are the same. But she has been told a different future, and in that brief interval the story of herself seems to open.

Then a car strikes her.

She has stepped into the street and is hit by a Mercedes. The luxury of the vehicle is not incidental to the way the event feels. A girl with almost nothing has just been promised abundance; a conspicuous instrument of abundance knocks her down. The prophecy and the collision come together so quickly that there is no time for the usual fictional bridge between hope and outcome.

Macabéa lies injured near the curb. People gather. The person whose life has drawn almost no attention now becomes an object around which strangers momentarily assemble. The novel lingers over her body's struggle and over the way she apprehends, or cannot apprehend, what is happening. Rodrigo cannot settle easily into the role of reporter. He has imagined her life and now must imagine its ending. At times he seems to bargain against the event he has narrated, as though he might still revise it if he delays the last word.

Macabéa dies. Her promised beginning has become her end.

## After the star

Rodrigo calls this her hour of the star, but not because she has achieved fame or experienced the luxurious life the fortune-teller predicted. The phrase is bitter, unstable, and painfully close to the vocabulary of the films she admired. At the instant her life ends, an audience finally surrounds her. We cannot be sure what such attention is worth to someone who cannot receive it.

The narrator also reaches an ending, though he will go on living outside the life he has represented. He cannot make Macabéa less dead by describing her more beautifully. Nor can he finish the story as though her death closes the questions that brought her into view. His effort to write her has exposed his power: he decides when she appears, how she is explained, and when the tale is complete. He is moved, perhaps, but he remains the person in control of the words.

One might summarize the plot in a few sentences: a poor migrant lives in Rio, works badly for little pay, loses an unkind boyfriend, receives a dazzling prediction, and is killed in the street. But such a summary leaves out the novel's second motion: the uneasy voice that knows it is making a story out of a person whom society has barely recognized.

The hour of the star is therefore neither the happy ending Macabéa desired nor a simple demonstration that hope is foolish. Her hope is real, even if its promised object is a fraud. Her modest pleasures are real. Her vulnerability is produced by specific social arrangements, not merely by an abstract human fate. And the narrator's words cannot restore to her the life that those arrangements have made so precarious.

At the end we are left with two forms of silence. One belongs to a young woman who never acquired a commanding public voice. The other is the silence that should follow when a writer, having used all his eloquence, has to admit that eloquence cannot save her.
