# To Kill a Mockingbird — The Story

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

If you have never read Harper Lee's *To Kill a Mockingbird*, or if you read it years ago and remember only a lawyer, a trial, and a mysterious neighbor, this is a place to begin. The book has more than one story moving through it. Before its most famous courtroom scenes, it spends a long stretch of time with children who are learning the geography of a town and the habits of its people. Those seemingly ordinary childhood years are part of what happens, not an interruption before the important events.

The town is Maycomb, Alabama, during the Great Depression. It is an old county seat, not a place where life moves quickly or money circulates easily. Families know one another's names, or believe they do. They also know which families own land, which have lost it, which are considered respectable, and which are spoken about in whispers. Segregation shapes everyday life. The adults have explanations for how things work, although those explanations do not always agree with what the children actually see.

Our guide is Jean Louise Finch, known as Scout. She tells the story later in life, looking back on the years when she was a small girl. She lives with her older brother, Jem, and their father, Atticus Finch. Their mother died when Scout was too young to remember her clearly. Atticus is a lawyer, a widower, and a father whose age and quiet manner sometimes make his children worry that he cannot do the impressive things other fathers can. Calpurnia, a Black woman who works in their household, cares for the children, enforces rules, and is one of the adults who knows how to stop Scout when Scout decides to argue with the world.

Jem is older, already beginning to enjoy the privileges of being the one who knows how a game ought to be played. Scout wants to be included in everything and resents being told that a girl must behave differently. Their home is both affectionate and full of small disagreements. Atticus tends to explain, rather than shout. The children do not always welcome an explanation, especially when it asks them to admit that someone else might have a point.

One summer a boy called Dill arrives to stay with his aunt, who lives near the Finches. Dill is smaller than Jem, but astonishingly capable of invention. He brings elaborate stories and an appetite for adventures. The three children spend their days creating games and dares, and the most compelling mystery on their street is the house occupied by the Radley family.

Arthur Radley, whom the neighborhood calls Boo, has not been seen outside in years. The children know him almost entirely through other people's tales. They have heard that he was troublesome when he was young, that his family shut him away, and that terrible things may have happened behind the windows. Some of the claims are plainly impossible. That does not make them less entertaining. The house becomes the place they run past, the place they stare at, and the place they dare one another to approach.

Jem runs up and touches it. The children invent plays in which one of them performs the imagined disasters of the Radley household. They try to make the unseen man appear at a window. When Scout asks their neighbor Miss Maudie about him, she receives a more ordinary account: Arthur is a human being living under circumstances the children do not understand. The story they prefer, however, is the frightening one. It gives every shutter and shadow a dramatic explanation.

Then Scout begins finding things in the knothole of a tree beside the Radley property. At first there is chewing gum, then old coins, then other small objects. The gifts are not expensive, but they are strangely well chosen for children. When Jem and Scout discover two little figures carved from soap to resemble them, it becomes difficult to think of the objects as random rubbish. Someone who knows their daily movements has taken the trouble to leave them presents.

The children's interest in the Radley house also gets them into trouble. One night they sneak onto the property to look through a window. A shot rings out. They flee in confusion, and Jem's trousers catch on a fence. He leaves them behind rather than be caught. He is terrified of what Atticus will say if the truth comes out. Later, Jem returns alone to recover them. He finds the trousers placed where he can reach them, with their torn fabric clumsily mended. Someone has been there before him, and that person has chosen to help rather than expose him.

For a while the gifts continue. Jem and Scout decide to leave a thank-you note in the tree. Before they can deliver it, the knothole is filled with cement. Nathan Radley, Arthur's brother, says the tree was dying. Atticus looks at it and sees no sign of illness. The children have lost a small but important line of communication with a person they have never properly met. Jem, who is getting old enough to recognize deliberate adult interference, takes the loss hard.

In the middle of these summers, Scout begins school. She is eager to go, but her first teacher does not appreciate that she can already read. Scout has learned by sitting beside Atticus and watching him read, not through the prescribed sequence of classroom lessons. She feels punished for knowing something she was supposed to learn later. When she comes home angry and ready to abandon school, Atticus asks her to consider what the new teacher might have been facing: a room full of children, and a town whose customs she has not yet learned.

One of those customs concerns Walter Cunningham, a boy whose family has very little money. He refuses an offer of lunch money because he could not pay it back. Scout understands the reason; her teacher does not. When Walter comes home with the Finches for a meal, Scout makes a fuss over the way he eats. Calpurnia sends her away and makes her understand that a guest deserves respect even when his habits are unfamiliar. Scout is discovering that being right about a fact does not give her permission to humiliate a person.

There are other children whose circumstances the school accommodates in a very different way. The Ewells live in poverty near the town dump, and their children scarcely attend school. The adults have long since reached a compromise with the family, even though that compromise means ordinary rules are not applied to everyone. Scout encounters these decisions first as schoolroom puzzles, not yet as pieces of the larger conflict to come.

That winter, Maycomb experiences cold and snow severe enough to astonish the children. They build an improvised snowman with more mud than snow. Then a fire destroys Miss Maudie's house. The neighbors gather outdoors in the freezing dark to watch and help. Scout stands near the Radley place, absorbed by the emergency. Later she discovers a blanket around her shoulders. She has no memory of anyone putting it there. Atticus and Jem work out what must have happened: someone from the Radley house came close enough to cover her while she was watching the flames. The man the children had spent months describing as a menace has apparently been taking care of them.

Around this time, another matter moves from adult conversations into the children's lives. Atticus has agreed to defend Tom Robinson, a Black man accused of assaulting a white woman. Other children begin repeating insults they have heard at home. Scout fights when she thinks someone has insulted her father; Atticus urges her not to answer every taunt with her fists. At a family Christmas gathering, her cousin Francis repeats ugly things about Atticus, and the quarrel ends with Scout striking him. She knows very little about the accusation itself. What she understands is that her father's decision has made the family the subject of angry talk.

Atticus remains quiet about his own abilities. When a rabid dog is seen coming down the street, the sheriff needs someone capable of shooting it before it reaches the houses. To Scout and Jem's astonishment, Atticus takes the rifle and kills it with one shot. They learn he was once renowned for his marksmanship. He never thought the talent was something to show off. For the children, the incident changes the picture of their apparently unremarkable father, but Atticus returns to being Atticus rather than accepting the role of a neighborhood hero.

There is also Mrs. Dubose, an elderly neighbor who speaks viciously about Atticus's defense of Tom Robinson. Jem becomes furious and destroys her camellia bushes. As punishment, Atticus makes him read aloud to her for a period each day. The sessions are miserable. She is ill-tempered, in pain, and often seems scarcely able to listen. Then she dies. Atticus tells Jem that she had been struggling to free herself from dependence on morphine before her death. The reading helped her endure the hours of withdrawal. She leaves Jem a flower from the plants he damaged. Her cruelty does not vanish because he now knows more of her story, but the boy has learned a fact about her life that he could not have guessed from their quarrels.

The prosecution of Tom Robinson now draws the children away from their games and into the legal and racial order that governs the town.

Tom Robinson is a Black man accused of raping Mayella Ewell, a white woman. In Maycomb's segregated society, the charge is already dangerous before a witness takes the stand. Many white townspeople assume Tom's guilt because of the races of the people involved. Atticus is subjected to abuse for taking the defense seriously. His children hear that abuse at school, at family gatherings, and in the streets.

For the children, life continues in ways that seem almost independent of the case. Jem grows older and more private. Scout dislikes having to ask his permission to join games and resents being told to act more like a young lady. Calpurnia takes the children to the Black church she attends while Atticus is away. Scout sees a congregation that raises money for Tom Robinson's family, who have lost his income while he is imprisoned. Many people welcome the children; one woman challenges their presence. Calpurnia belongs to a world beyond the Finch kitchen, with relationships and responsibilities the children have scarcely imagined.

Aunt Alexandra, Atticus's sister, comes to stay with the family. She is intensely concerned with family history, manners, and Scout's behavior. She wants Scout to become the sort of girl Maycomb's social rules approve of, and she is much less comfortable than Atticus with Calpurnia's position in the household. Her arrival makes the Finch home more crowded with expectations. Atticus refuses to dismiss Calpurnia. Scout continues to resist the prescribed version of girlhood. Jem, trying to sound grown up, sometimes speaks as though he has joined the adults on the other side of the argument.

Dill, the visiting boy who has shared the children's summers, reappears after running away from his new circumstances at home. He is eventually allowed to stay with the Finches. The reunion brings something of the old childhood world back. The three friends can still wander together, but the town is no longer simply a place for dares and stories about the Radley house.

One evening, Atticus leaves home and goes to the jail where Tom Robinson is being held. Jem, Scout, and Dill secretly follow him. A group of men arrives at the building. They are not there to hear evidence. They mean to take Tom out of the jail, and Atticus is standing in their way. The children do not grasp the full danger when they step forward into the gathering. Jem refuses Atticus's order to go home. Scout notices Mr. Cunningham, the father of Walter, the boy she knows from school. She talks to him as though they were meeting during an ordinary day and asks about his son. Her questions are awkward and innocent. They also force the man to respond as the father of a child she knows, in front of people who have arrived as a threatening crowd. He tells the others to leave. The men disperse, and Tom remains in the jail.

The next day the courtroom fills with spectators. Scout, Jem, and Dill make their way to the balcony, where Maycomb's Black residents are seated. Reverend Sykes, whom they met at church, helps them find places. From above they can see the judge, the lawyers, the witnesses, and the white jury. Atticus is below at the defense table. For hours the children watch a version of adult life that no family quarrel or school lesson has prepared them to understand.

The prosecution's story begins with Bob Ewell, Mayella's father. The Ewells live close to the town dump in a neglected house. Bob does little to care for the family. When Mayella accuses Tom, he supports her account, claiming to have seen the assault. The sheriff, Heck Tate, describes being called to the Ewell home and finding Mayella injured. Under Atticus's questioning, it emerges that no doctor was summoned to examine her. Her injuries were reported on the right side of her face. When Bob is asked to write his name, he uses his left hand. In the defense's account, a left-handed attacker might have inflicted the injuries in the way described.

Mayella takes the witness stand next. She is young, frightened, and quick to become angry. Atticus addresses her politely, but even ordinary signs of respect seem to make her suspicious. She is unused to being treated courteously. She insists that Tom came into the house and attacked her. Yet the details of her account falter when Atticus presses her to describe exactly what happened and who else was present. She lives in an isolated household with several younger children, a father who drinks, and very little evidence of friendship or support. None of that resolves the accusation; it simply becomes part of what the courtroom hears about her life.

Then Tom Robinson gives his account. He has passed the Ewell property on his way to and from work. Mayella has asked him to help with chores at various times, and he has stopped to help her without payment. On the day in question, he says, she invited him inside to do some work. He noticed that the younger children were not there. She had sent them away. According to Tom, Mayella tried to kiss him. Her father saw what happened from outside, became enraged, and shouted threats at her. Tom ran.

Tom's left arm is badly disabled. It was caught in a cotton gin when he was a boy and has never worked properly since. The injury makes the prosecution's description of the physical struggle difficult to reconcile with what the court can plainly see. Atticus has also given the jurors reasons to doubt Bob Ewell's reliability and reasons to consider whether Mayella was hurt by someone in her own household. Tom cannot prove every movement that occurred in the Ewell house that day. He can explain why he was there and what he says happened, and the prosecution has offered little independent evidence to settle the charge.

There is a moment during questioning when Tom says that he felt sorry for Mayella. The prosecutor seizes on it. In this courtroom, a Black man's pity for a white woman is treated not as an ordinary human feeling but as an unacceptable reversal of rank. Tom is pressed and spoken to harshly. Dill, listening from above, becomes distressed by the prosecutor's treatment of him. He has heard adults speak unpleasantly to people before, but now the cruelty seems too obvious to ignore. Scout takes him outside to recover.

Beyond the courtroom they encounter Dolphus Raymond, a white man who lives with a Black woman and their children and is regarded by much of Maycomb with contempt. He carries a bottle in a paper bag and lets townspeople assume that he is drunk. He reveals to the children that the bottle contains an ordinary soft drink. The supposed drunkenness gives other white people an explanation for his way of life, one they can accept more easily than his deliberate choice to live with the people he loves. The children return to the trial with yet another example of how a town can prefer a story about someone to the person's own account.

Atticus speaks to the jury at the end of the evidence. He draws attention to the contradictions and the lack of proof, asks the jurors not to decide the case according to racial prejudice, and reminds them that a court is supposed to judge evidence. Jem listens intently. For all his growing doubts about Maycomb, he still believes that a convincing defense ought to produce an acquittal. He has watched Atticus expose the weakness of the charge. To Jem the outcome now seems almost certain.

The jury leaves to deliberate. The wait stretches into the evening. The children grow tired. When the jurors finally return, they find Tom guilty.

There is no dramatic reversal. The court's procedures have been followed, people have testified, and the verdict has come back against the man whose defense seemed so persuasive to Jem. Atticus collects his papers and leaves the courtroom. In the balcony, the Black spectators rise as he passes beneath them. Scout has to be urged to stand as well. She does not immediately understand why they are doing it, but she recognizes their respect for her father and the loss that has just occurred.

The guilty verdict is not the end of what happens to Tom Robinson or the Finch family.

The children now have to go back to ordinary days in Maycomb. They eat, quarrel, attend school, visit neighbors, and listen to the adults explain what happened. But Jem's idea of the town has been shaken. He expected a jury to consider evidence and return the verdict that followed from it. Instead, the court has convicted Tom. The people who made that decision can return to their homes without appearing any different from the neighbors he knew before.

The Black community sends gifts of food to Atticus's house, an expression of gratitude that moves him, though the families contributing can scarcely afford the expense. Miss Maudie invites Jem, Scout, and Dill for something to eat and speaks about the people who did try to help Tom. She is trying to offer the children a reason not to surrender to despair. It is a difficult consolation. Tom remains convicted, and the family whose life is at stake cannot solve the problem by saying that a few people acted decently.

There is an appeal to be pursued, but Bob Ewell, Mayella's father, is not content with the verdict. Although the court accepted his family's accusation, Atticus exposed contradictions in his testimony and made him look foolish in public. Bob is angry and wants Atticus to suffer for humiliating him. He confronts Atticus in town, spits at him, and threatens him. Atticus refuses to fight. He wants to believe that Ewell's anger will spend itself without further harm. The people who know Bob are not all so confident.

Meanwhile, Scout tries to return to the concerns of school and family. Aunt Alexandra, her father's sister, continues to live at the Finch house and tries to teach Scout the manners and obligations expected of a respectable young woman. Scout is most comfortable in overalls and dislikes the idea that polite behavior requires pretending not to notice things. Yet she also begins to recognize that Alexandra worries about the family. Even the adults who seem opposed in principle must share the same rooms when there is danger or grief.

Tom Robinson never receives the freedom the children hoped a just verdict would give him. News arrives that he has been shot and killed while trying to escape from prison. The reported number of shots is seventeen. Atticus goes with Calpurnia to tell Helen Robinson, Tom's wife. The news reaches Scout indirectly as adult voices break into her everyday world. There is no possibility of an appeal that can bring Tom back. The trial has ended in a conviction and now in a death, leaving Helen and the children to carry the loss.

Some townspeople speak of the killing as the unsurprising result of Tom's attempting to flee. Others recognize how little chance he had been given. Mr. Underwood, who runs the local newspaper, writes an editorial about the killing. Atticus had tried to persuade the court to give Tom a fair hearing; now the town is left with statements and reactions rather than a living man whose case could still be contested.

The Ewell family is not finished with the people connected to the trial. Helen Robinson, trying to support her children, takes work with Link Deas, Tom's former employer. Bob Ewell begins frightening her on her way to work. Link Deas confronts him and orders him to stop. There is also an incident at the home of Judge Taylor, who presided over the trial, suggesting that someone is trying to get inside. The threats have a recognizable direction: people who contradicted or embarrassed Bob Ewell are being made to feel vulnerable.

Not everything that happens in Maycomb is announced as part of the conflict. Some of it appears in the small, disorienting things Scout hears at school. Her teacher, Miss Gates, condemns the persecution of Jewish people in Nazi Germany. Scout agrees that such cruelty is wrong. But she has also heard the teacher express contempt for Black people in Maycomb after Tom's trial. Scout cannot easily reconcile the two attitudes. The teacher condemns injustice at a distance and accepts something closely related in her own community. Scout asks Jem about the contradiction, but the trial remains too painful for him to discuss. He wants her to leave the subject alone.

As autumn arrives, the school prepares a Halloween pageant. Scout will take part wearing a costume shaped like a ham, made bulky by wire and fabric. She is meant to appear onstage at the right moment but falls asleep and misses her cue, an embarrassment that feels enormous at the time. After the festivities, she and Jem start home on foot in the dark. Scout is still wearing the awkward costume. Jem, nearly a teenager now, accompanies her along the familiar road.

Their walk should be ordinary. The trees and houses belong to a neighborhood they have crossed for years. But they begin hearing footsteps behind them. At first it might be someone playing a trick. Then the sounds come closer. Jem becomes concerned. Before they can get home, a man attacks them. The children cannot see clearly what is happening. Scout's costume restricts her movement and her view. Jem struggles with the attacker and is badly hurt. Scout hears frightening noises, stumbles, and tries to make sense of bodies moving in the dark. Another figure enters the struggle. The violence stops.

Someone carries Jem toward the Finch house. Scout follows, shaken and unsure who has rescued them. At home a doctor is called. Jem's arm is broken, and there are other injuries to examine. Atticus is frightened for both children. The sheriff, Heck Tate, comes to investigate. Outside, a man has been found dead. It is Bob Ewell. The violence that followed the trial has reached the Finch children at last.

Scout sees a pale, quiet man standing back in the room, almost hidden from attention. For a moment she does not understand who he is. Then she recognizes Arthur Radley. Boo Radley, the neighbor she and Jem once described as a monster, has come out of the house. He heard or saw the attack and intervened. He carried Jem home. The children had spent years inventing terrifying explanations for the life inside the Radley place. In the end, the man from that house protected them when an actual danger appeared on their street.

Atticus and Heck Tate disagree about how to account for Bob Ewell's death. Atticus initially fears that Jem may have killed Ewell while defending himself and Scout. He does not want a special exception made for his son. But Tate has examined the scene and knows what happened well enough to understand that Arthur Radley was the rescuer. The sheriff insists that Ewell fell on his own knife. He is not prepared to drag Arthur into a public inquiry that would turn a private, frightened man into the center of town attention. Atticus slowly realizes that Tate is determined to protect the person who saved his children.

Scout understands something of that determination too. For years she had wished Arthur would emerge so she could satisfy her curiosity. Now that he is standing in front of her, she no longer asks him to explain himself or perform the part she invented for him. She speaks to him gently and guides him to Jem's room. He looks at the sleeping boy, then asks to go home. Scout takes him by the arm and walks with him down the street, allowing him the dignity of looking as though he is escorting her rather than being escorted.

At the Radley porch she pauses. From there, she can see the Finch home and the route she and Jem have taken so many times. She can imagine the children's games on the street, the changing seasons, the night of the fire, the gifts left in the tree, and the recent walk in the darkness, all as events a neighbor might have watched from that side of the road. She returns home alone. She has finally met Arthur Radley and learned that the most frightening stories about him were wrong.

Atticus is still awake. Jem is recovering, and Scout is exhausted. Her father stays with the children, and the ordinary intimacy of the house resumes: a parent with a book, a child struggling to remain awake, the knowledge that tomorrow will bring another day. The book closes with Scout's childhood world both restored and permanently altered. Her brother is alive because Arthur came to their aid. Tom Robinson is dead, and nothing that happens on the Radley porch can reverse his death. The town remains Maycomb, full of the same people and houses. Scout's understanding of those people will never again be quite so simple.
