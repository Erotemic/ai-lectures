# Wide Sargasso Sea: Who Owns the Woman in the Attic?

Critical analysis following `013200`, the complete story of Jean Rhys's novel.

## What changes when a character receives a past?

In Charlotte Brontë's *Jane Eyre*, the woman imprisoned in the attic is part of the crisis that confronts Jane and Rochester. In Jean Rhys's *Wide Sargasso Sea*, published in 1966, that woman becomes Antoinette Cosway, a person whose childhood, desires, memories, and fears precede the marriage through which another story has chiefly known her. The later writer does not simply reveal one missing fact. She changes what the original narrative's arrangement had made it possible for readers to ignore.

This is a useful place to begin because a retelling can be deceptively conservative. It might take a familiar character, add scenes of suffering, and ask us to feel sorry for her without disturbing the categories that first defined her. Rhys does something more exacting. She changes the distribution of voice. Antoinette narrates substantial portions of the novel, but she is not its sole authority. Her husband narrates a central part, and the book briefly permits Grace Poole's practical perspective. The reader has to live with the mismatch between what these speakers know, what they fear, and what their words do to one another.

The result is neither a correction that makes Antoinette perfectly innocent nor a psychological case study that explains her to the satisfaction of an outside observer. It is a novel about the damage done when another person's explanation becomes law, property, diagnosis, and destiny.

## The Caribbean after emancipation is not a backdrop

The story begins in Jamaica after the British abolition of slavery. The Cosway family's decline is bound to the collapse of the old plantation economy. That economy depended on the forced labor and dehumanization of enslaved people. Its former beneficiaries do not lose the history of their power when their wealth becomes insecure, and the formerly enslaved do not suddenly receive equal resources and freedom from racist hierarchy. Rhys sets Antoinette's early life inside a society in which emancipation has changed the legal order without making the consequences of slavery disappear.

This creates an uncomfortable relation between Antoinette's vulnerability and her family's history. She is mocked, isolated, and ultimately harmed. But she is not harmed in a society that had previously been innocent. The neighbors' anger toward the old estate cannot be understood merely as irrational hostility toward a lonely child. At the same time, the child did not personally create the plantation order that shapes how others see her. The novel holds both facts in view. We cannot explain the first fire by saying only that a family was persecuted; we also cannot explain away the terror of a child by saying that her family's past makes her pain unimportant.

The word Creole has changed meaning across places, periods, and speakers. In the novel Antoinette is a white Creole, born in the Caribbean and not securely accepted by either an imagined English home culture or the Black community around her. Calling her a colonial outsider should not lead us to identify her position with that of Black Caribbean people who experienced slavery and its aftermath differently. Rhys builds a society of unequal differences, not a single generalized feeling of not belonging.

The places are also specific. Coulibri is in Jamaica; Granbois is on Dominica; the final locked room is in England. The movements between them are movements between distinct social and geographical relationships. Antoinette's husband treats the Caribbean as one disorienting whole. The novel allows us to observe his incomprehension, but need not authorize his perspective. A place that appears excessive to an uneasy traveler may be legible to someone who has lived within its languages and histories.

## Who is allowed to tell the marriage?

The middle section's English husband is unnamed in Rhys's text. Readers of *Jane Eyre* are invited to recognize Rochester, but the name's absence matters. The reader cannot rely on the authority granted him by his position in Brontë's novel. We hear a man who is unsettled, proud, financially interested, and repeatedly convinced that misunderstanding is evidence against other people.

His narrative is not worthless. He has fears; some of the events he reports are disturbing; Antoinette's actions can harm him. But his observations are shaped by a structure that gives him enormous power and little incentive to examine himself. He has married into money. He can control movement and property in ways his wife cannot. He can leave the Caribbean and carry her away. And when he receives Daniel Cosway's letter, he is poised to turn rumor into knowledge because it fits the story he is already beginning to believe.

Daniel's allegations are not a neutral dossier assembled by an independent investigator. They arrive through a person with interests and resentments of his own. The husband does not discover that his wife has a family history; he already knows that much. He begins treating that history as a hereditary verdict. The evidence becomes less important than the interpretive frame into which it is placed. Antoinette's attempt to explain herself is repeatedly made into fresh proof that she cannot be trusted to explain herself.

A familiar fictional device is the unreliable narrator, but *Wide Sargasso Sea* complicates it. The book does not grant one all-knowing authority from which a reader can mark every statement true or false. It makes the power to judge unevenly distributed. When one speaker's interpretation can determine whether another is confined, unreliability is not only a problem for a reader solving the plot. It is a problem of material power.

This is why the husband's decision to call Antoinette Bertha is so consequential. It converts an act of interpretation into an act of possession. He does not have to persuade her that she is Bertha; he can arrange a life in which that is the name that controls how other people refer to her. Naming becomes part of an infrastructure of domination, alongside money, marriage law, travel, and the locked room.

## Christophine's refusal

Christophine is among the few people who openly tell the husband that his conduct is wrong. She is a Black Caribbean woman who has cared for Antoinette since childhood, and her life cannot be separated from unequal relationships of work, class, and colonial authority. She has practical knowledge, confidence, and a capacity to refuse the husband's account of events. Yet her freedom is constrained by the threat that colonial law can be used against her practice of obeah.

We should resist two neat but inadequate readings. One is to make Christophine a magical helper who exists solely to move Antoinette's emotional story along. The other is to make her the book's uncomplicated spokesperson for all Black Caribbean perspectives. She is a particular character, with judgments, anger, limits, and a history we receive only partially. Her decision to advise Antoinette to protect herself is practical, not merely supernatural. Her confrontation with the husband exposes how little his authority depends on being morally right.

But her confrontation does not remove his authority. The risk of punishment and his control of Antoinette's situation prevent Christophine from delivering an easy rescue. That failure is not proof of her weakness as a character. It is evidence of the kind of power the novel is examining. A strong voice may still be made ineffective by the law and the household that decide whose words matter.

The treatment of Tia and the people who set Coulibri alight raises another limit. Rhys's novel can give Antoinette an interior life that Brontë's plot largely denied her while still leaving Black characters with less narrative room than its white Creole protagonist. Postcolonial criticism should not turn that rescue into a declaration that all silenced people have now been heard. We can value Rhys's intervention and ask what voices remain difficult to access through it.

## Mental suffering, diagnosis, and coercion

The novel depicts disturbing psychological experiences. Annette's grief becomes overwhelming after the loss of her home and son. Antoinette is frightened by dreams, experiences confusion, and later cannot reliably account for all of her actions in England. It would be dishonest to insist that the novel treats suffering as purely a false label imposed by bad men. The suffering is real.

But the opposite conclusion, that the women's suffering proves that the men's treatment was justified, is just as inadequate. Annette's distress follows calamity and is intensified by her vulnerability to abuse. Antoinette's distress develops within a marriage that removes her authority over her own identity and moves her into confinement. The text gives the reader evidence of conditions under which a person's mind can become less secure. It refuses the comfortable sequence in which an allegedly unstable woman is first identified and only then, regrettably, restrained for everyone's good.

Rhys's novel is not a modern diagnostic case history. Its language of madness belongs to characters, institutions, fears, and inherited ideas about families. A contemporary reader may recognize trauma, depression, dissociation, or coercive control, but attaching a diagnosis should not replace reading what the novel actually makes happen. The question of how much suffering precedes confinement and how much confinement produces or intensifies suffering remains important. So does the fact that the legal and social power to make decisions rests elsewhere.

A published study on the historicization of madness in *Wide Sargasso Sea* emphasizes that Rhys's representation of mental disturbance extends beyond Antoinette alone. That wider depiction challenges the assumption that the unstable woman is the only person whose behavior requires explanation. Fear, obsession, and possessiveness in an English husband may be treated as rational agency, while similar intensity in a wife may be used to remove her agency. The double standard is part of the story's architecture.

## How the ending changes Jane Eyre

If you know Brontë's novel, you recognize what Antoinette's candle points toward. If you do not, you can still understand Rhys's ending. The child who survived the fire at Coulibri has been confined in a house controlled by the man who renamed her. Her recurring dreams merge the earlier destruction with the place where she now lives. She wakes and decides to act.

Rhys does not stage the fire as a finished spectacle viewed from a safe distance. In *Jane Eyre* the figure in the attic becomes publicly visible through the fire and its consequences. In *Wide Sargasso Sea*, the closing movement belongs to the woman carrying the candle. The difference shifts where the reader is asked to stand. We do not watch an incident that clears a path for another heroine's future. We remain inside a consciousness attempting to find a way out of confinement.

That does not make the action morally simple. Burning a house can endanger others; a dream of escape may be inseparable from destruction. The novel's sympathy does not require that every imagined choice be praised. Instead it insists on the chain of events by which a woman arrives at the threshold of this choice. The novel converts an event once known largely by its effects on others into an action embedded in a person's history.

We can describe the relationship between the two books as a response, revision, and counter-narrative. None of these requires us to pretend that Brontë and Rhys wrote within the same historical understanding. *Jane Eyre* appeared in 1847; *Wide Sargasso Sea* appeared more than a century later, after changes in literary form, Caribbean politics, feminist thought, and ideas about empire. Rhys writes back to an earlier text, but she also writes a novel that can be read as its own work of conflict, place, and voice.

## The historical work of rewriting

The timing of Rhys's book matters. Published in 1966, it entered a world in which Caribbean societies were confronting colonial histories and new political possibilities. Its story is set much earlier, in the aftermath of emancipation. The distance between the nineteenth-century setting and the twentieth-century writing makes it possible to see inherited categories being investigated from a later vantage point. Yet it is important not to imply that a single literary work expresses one uniform Caribbean position. Rhys's own identity and the whiteness of Antoinette's family are part of the situation, not details that disappear because the novel criticizes English colonial authority.

The novel's formal achievement is to make the right to explain another person a central part of the plot. The husband can speak eloquently about what frightens him. His eloquence does not give him ownership of his wife's meaning. Antoinette can tell the story of her childhood. Her vulnerability does not make her testimony omniscient about all the people around her. Christophine can judge the marriage sharply. Her moral clarity does not free her from the power of the law. The book forces the voices to coexist, without an independent narrator announcing which one has resolved every question.

And the geographical movement gives the conflict its physical form. Coulibri is the site of a family's old power and a child's loss. Granbois is where love becomes mistrust as one spouse refuses to recognize the other's home. England is the place imagined as the center of legitimate culture but experienced by Antoinette as near-total dispossession. The journey toward the supposed center ends in a room without a view.

The key question to carry into other literature is not simply whether a famous fictional villain might have been misunderstood. It is broader: whose past has a narrative been allowed to treat as irrelevant? What happens when that past receives voice without becoming a perfect excuse? And how does the answer change when voice is connected to property, law, race, and the ability to leave or remain in a place?

*Wide Sargasso Sea* gives a person a history, but it does not promise that being heard will save her. Its fierce accomplishment is to make forgetting that history more difficult. Once Antoinette has spoken, the room in the attic is no longer just a device in somebody else's plot. It has a world behind its locked door.
