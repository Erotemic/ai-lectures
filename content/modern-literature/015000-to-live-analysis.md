# To Live: History, Endurance, and the Refusal of Consolation

Critical lecture following `014200`, the full story of Yu Hua's *To Live*.

## Does survival have to mean victory?

At the end of Yu Hua's *To Live*, Fugui has outlived the people who made his family. The man who once squandered a rich inheritance is an elderly farmer plowing beside an ox with his own name. He talks. He remembers. He continues. The novel could have framed this as an absolute defeat or an inspirational victory. Instead it leaves the reader facing a more difficult question: what does it mean to live when life has not rewarded virtue, effort, or love in any proportion we would call just?

The novel was published in book form in 1993, after earlier magazine publication. Its story passes through China's civil war, the transformation of rural property, the Great Leap Forward and its famine, and the Cultural Revolution. These historical events matter enormously. But Yu Hua does not principally tell their story through the speeches of leaders, the procedures of committees, or a textbook account of political institutions. He gives us a man who has to obtain food, protect children, look after an ill wife, and find work. The large event reaches him as a change in what ordinary acts now cost.

That perspective helps explain both the book's power and the difficulty of interpreting it. A reader can see how a policy enters a family's kitchen without therefore possessing a comprehensive history of the period. Fugui's account is literary testimony from a fictional person. It is not a representative sample from all rural communities and not a neutral chronicle of the People's Republic. Its truth is organized through particular people, changing relationships, and the persistence of a voice.

## The first death is an old social position

Fugui begins the book as an irresponsible son of a rich landowning household. He behaves cruelly toward Jiazhen, wastes money, and gambles away property that he did not create. The novel does not begin by presenting him as an innocent victim of history. His earliest disasters have unmistakable connections to his own conduct. He injures the people who love him while pretending that wealth frees him from consequences.

Then Long Er wins the land. The family becomes poor, and Fugui's father dies. For a time the loss appears to have one stable moral meaning: the foolish heir ruined his household. But the political revolution changes what it means to be a landowner. Long Er is later condemned and executed as a landlord. The man whose victory in the gambling house looked like Fugui's destruction has acquired the property that makes him vulnerable to state punishment. Fugui, the loser, remains alive.

That reversal is one of the novel's most chilling jokes. It is not a lesson that gambling was prudent. Nor is it a defense of political executions. It dramatizes the instability of conclusions drawn from one moment in a life. A choice can be morally reckless and nevertheless have effects different from those anyone intended. A possession can move from a sign of security to a source of mortal danger when the rules of public power change.

A conventional moral fable might organize suffering as punishment and later happiness as reward. Yu Hua keeps interrupting that arrangement. Fugui learns to love his family better; disaster does not stop. Political campaigns claim to remake society; vulnerable people still die. The old man's endurance is therefore not proof that events were just. It is evidence that his life has continued despite their injustice and contingency.

## Who gets to narrate the century?

*To Live* begins with a traveler collecting folk songs. The collector is not the central protagonist. The collector arrives with a project of his own: finding cultural expression he can gather and preserve. That expectation frames the story Fugui is about to tell. Fugui, however, gives him a life story. The old man takes over the narrative space, and the book becomes an encounter between an educated visitor's expectations and a rural speaker's account of what has mattered.

The difference resembles the distance between an official history and what someone tells at the edge of a field. Political names and dates may be accurate but insufficient to explain why a man's child is absent from a family meal, why a worker never returns from a construction site, or why an elderly husband remembers how his wife walked when she was healthy. Fugui's memory makes events legible at the scale where a life is lived.

James A. Winship, writing about the novel for *Education About Asia*, notes Yu Hua's movement away from the violent experimentation of some of his early fiction toward gentler but no less devastating first-person storytelling. That observation is useful as a description of form, not an instruction to imagine Yu Hua as having abandoned criticism. The novel's violence can become more disturbing because it is reported in a voice that is often plain, amused, or matter-of-fact. The prose does not always announce an atrocity with the emotional force a reader expects. Sometimes the gap between what happens and how Fugui says it is precisely where grief becomes audible.

The collector's presence also raises a question about the act of listening. Why is Fugui willing to tell a stranger everything? We might assume he would rather avoid his memories. Yet remembering seems to give him pleasure. He can speak the names and habits of people who are no longer alive. His story is not an archive in which the dead are stored away; it is an activity through which a relationship remains possible for the duration of the telling.

The final ox deepens this idea. Fugui's companion bears his name. The old man calls to it in a manner that sounds as if many animals are present. Names no longer correspond to a bustling household, but they still make a world. We should not treat this as an easy substitution in which the animal compensates for his family. The point is that care has not become impossible. He can still share labor with another living creature. Even reduced to its simplest visible form, his life contains relation.

## Historical structures and preventable deaths

Several deaths in the novel arise under specific systems of authority. Youqing dies because medical staff take too much blood to help the wife of a powerful official. The event is not an abstract reminder that everyone is mortal. It exposes a hierarchy in which the life of a poor child becomes expendable when another life is treated as politically important. The fact that the official is Chunsheng, a man Fugui once knew as a companion in danger, intensifies the contrast between solidarity and rank.

Fengxia dies in childbirth when proper medical care is unavailable in a context where political campaigns have damaged the authority and operation of health institutions. The novel makes the cost of ideological upheaval visible through a young woman whose ability to communicate is already limited by childhood illness. She has found love with Erxi, built a marriage, and is about to become a mother. Her death is not introduced only to illustrate a political thesis; the thesis becomes urgent because we have been asked to care about a person whose future has been interrupted.

Erxi dies in a construction accident. Kugen dies after an ordinary act of feeding goes wrong. Jiazhen dies after prolonged illness and grief. The deaths share the family's grief but not a single cause. An interpretation that assigns every loss directly to one regime would distort the narrative. So would an interpretation that calls all the deaths random fate and ignores the institutional failures that made some of them preventable. The book moves among individual responsibility, structural violence, illness, labor danger, and chance. That movement is one reason a single comforting moral cannot govern it.

The historical sequence also resists a simplified distinction between a wholly cruel past and a wholly compassionate new order. Fugui's old family wealth was unequal and his conduct was selfish. The revolution changes property and status, but the new authority can itself produce coercion and scarcity. The Great Leap Forward's failures enter through hunger, exhausting labor, and the reorganization of village life. The Cultural Revolution turns yesterday's official into today's persecuted person. The novel does not ask readers to ignore historical differences between these episodes; it shows what happens when people's security is made contingent on successive claims about who should be protected or punished.

## Jiazhen and the work of forgiveness

One of the easiest mistakes is to describe *To Live* solely as a man's transformation. Fugui does change. But Jiazhen lives alongside him, carrying a great deal of the labor that makes his survival possible. She returns after his humiliation of her. She cares for the children during his wartime absence. She perseveres through sickness, hunger, and the deaths of Youqing and Fengxia. Her capacity for care should not be made into a natural resource available to a man who has finally learned to appreciate it.

The novel gives Fugui an opportunity to recognize what he once took for granted. He learns to see his wife not as the person who is obliged to wait for him, but as someone whose devotion required choices and endurance. That recognition is part of his moral growth. Yet it would be too simple to call Jiazhen merely a redemptive force whose work exists to improve a male protagonist. She has losses he cannot entirely understand, and her response to Chunsheng after Youqing's death is her own ethical action.

When Chunsheng later faces persecution, Jiazhen does not say that the death of her son no longer matters. She insists that Chunsheng should live despite what has happened. The distinction is crucial. Forgiveness, in this scene, is not the revision of history to make injury disappear. It is the refusal to demand an additional death as the price of acknowledgment. The scene has a resonance with other novels in this course, including *Ceremony*, where a protagonist must refuse the destructive invitation to settle suffering by repeating violence. The histories are not interchangeable, and neither writer is giving an abstract law. But both dramatize the difference between recognizing a wrong and making destruction the only imaginable answer.

Jiazhen dies after Fugui has become a more loving husband. Her death does not cancel the recognition; nor does it give the marriage a neat ending in which past cruelty is balanced by later devotion. What remains is the fact that they lived long enough for the relationship to change, and not long enough to be protected from further loss.

## The novel and the film do not tell the same ending

Many listeners will know *To Live* from Zhang Yimou's 1994 film. It is important not to use the adaptation as a shortcut to the novel's plot. The film relocates and transforms important circumstances, including the family's setting and the professions and fates of particular characters. Most decisively, the film does not end with Fugui as the sole surviving human member of his household in the same way the novel does. It offers a different balance of survival, loss, and future possibility.

The change is not necessarily a betrayal of the source. Adaptations make aesthetic and practical choices, and a film can achieve something powerful through objects, faces, and visual sequences that a novel does not use in the same way. James A. Winship's comparison in *Education About Asia* is helpful precisely because it treats the works as differently organized stories rather than assuming that the film simply illustrates the book. When discussing the original novel, we must let its ending remain severe.

The frame with the ox would lose much of its force if Fugui merely appeared after an ordinary chain of hardship with a family still around him. Here, he has survived the people whose names and stories fill his speech. The final image does not announce that history has forgiven them. It asks us to consider what one person may continue to do when the expected structures of continuity have collapsed.

## Translation and the limits of a universal lesson

The English translation by Michael Berry was published in 2003, a decade after the novel's book publication in Chinese. The translation made the work available to many readers who could not encounter Yu Hua's prose in the original language. That is an achievement involving choices of rhythm, humor, register, and the sound of speech. A reader hearing English narration should remember that the qualities of the voice arrive through this additional act of literary work. We can discuss Fugui's conversational simplicity, but should not pretend that every English verbal choice is a transparent record of the Chinese original.

In a postscript, Yu Hua relates that an American song called *Old Black Joe* helped inspire his thinking about a person who survives long suffering with kindness. The connection is documented in the National Endowment for the Arts' discussion of the novel. It would be misleading, however, to conclude that different histories of enslavement and Chinese rural life are therefore interchangeable. An inspiration can cross languages and cultures without making the structures of violence equivalent. Yu Hua was moved by a human possibility; his novel gives that possibility a specifically Chinese historical life.

A universalizing reading is tempting because so much of the story can be described in ordinary terms: a man wastes his inheritance, learns to care, loses loved ones, and keeps living. But the circumstances of those losses—the civil war, agrarian reform, state campaigns, famine, local rank, disrupted medical practice—are not detachable decorations. The book can speak across cultures because it is so particular, not because we can strip away its world and be left with a generic fable about perseverance.

This also keeps us from converting Fugui into a model whom every suffering person should imitate. People who endure coercion do not owe an audience inspiration. The novel gives us one fictional speaker who, at the time we encounter him, is willing to recount his past without presenting it as a case against the world. His manner is not a mandate to forgive every institution responsible for harm. It is a mode of living that belongs to his story.

## Why the title cannot be shortened to hope

What is Fugui's achievement? He does not defeat the forces that took his loved ones. He does not prove that his improvement caused a happier life. He does not even receive the reward of dying beside his family. He learns to love and care under conditions that do not promise protection, and he keeps making room for a relation with the world after each loss.

If we call that simply hope, we risk making the novel sound as though it has guaranteed a better future. If we call it simply resignation, we risk missing the humor, tenderness, and active care that persist in Fugui's voice. The two descriptions do not resolve the tension. He recognizes what he cannot control while still doing what is within his reach. He walks with the ox. He tells a stranger his story. He can still notice the day's labor and the living creature beside him.

The historian may ask which political processes shaped rural China during the decades Fugui remembers. The novel gives the historian reasons to pursue that question in other records. The literary reader can also ask what changes when that history is carried by a single aging voice. What can a story preserve that a policy chronology cannot? How does the pacing of family scenes make public decisions legible? Why does the narrative leave us with someone speaking rather than with a final verdict?

*To Live* is not a proof that suffering makes people better. Sometimes suffering destroys people. It is not a denial that institutions bear responsibility for preventable harm. Some harms in Fugui's life are preventable, and the narrative makes that plain. The book asks what remains possible when neither moral desert nor historical progress can promise that love will be rewarded. Yu Hua's answer is austere and alive: a person remembers, works, cares where he still can, and continues to speak in a world that has not explained itself to his satisfaction.
