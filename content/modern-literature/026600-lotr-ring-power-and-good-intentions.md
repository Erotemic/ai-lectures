# The Lord of the Rings: The Ring, Power, and the Danger of Good Intentions

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

Imagine that a country is under attack, and someone offers its defenders a weapon powerful enough to end the war. There is no reason to doubt the invasion is real. The people who may die are not imaginary. The weapon exists. The catch is that using it would gradually turn its wielder into the kind of ruler whose power made the invasion possible. Would refusing it be integrity, or would it be an irresponsible luxury purchased with other people's lives?

This is not a hypothetical imported into The Lord of the Rings from a modern political philosophy course. It is the predicament through which Boromir understands the One Ring. He comes from Gondor, where a war long deferred has become ordinary life. He has seen his people pay for the defenses that allow others to remain safe. At the Council of Elrond he is not primarily fascinated by wealth or personal grandeur. He imagines a weapon that might save his city. His tragedy is that the Ring can make that argument more persuasive each time he repeats it to himself.

Sauron's Ring is an instrument of domination. It was made within a system of other rings and carries the maker's power and purposes. In the story it has concrete effects, including making a hobbit invisible to ordinary sight under many conditions. But the novel refuses to reduce it to equipment with useful features and unfortunate side effects. It alters the bearer's imagination of what ought to be done. It offers command to people who fear helplessness, order to people who fear chaos, and a heroic self-image to people afraid that their ordinary abilities will never be enough.

## Boromir is not the only test

When we read Boromir's attempt to seize the Ring, we can recognize his error without making his motives simple. Suppose he had been indifferent to Gondor. Then his offer would have no moral weight. The temptation works precisely because there is something worth protecting. His mistake is to imagine that taking possession of absolute power can be treated as a local military adjustment. He separates the goal of defending his people from the moral and political character of the means he would employ. Under pressure, that separation collapses.

Gandalf offers a different response. He has immense knowledge and power already, but knows that adding the Ring would not simply let him remain Gandalf with better tools. He could begin as a guardian and end by compelling everyone to accept his account of their own good. A benevolent tyrant can do real damage while describing the damage as necessary care. Gandalf's refusal is therefore not an admission that he lacks the courage to fight. He chooses kinds of action that preserve the possibility of other people's judgment, even when a more spectacular solution is available.

Galadriel's temptation has an aesthetic and political quality of its own. She has defended a place of beauty against time and darkness, and she knows its survival is endangered even if the quest succeeds. Imagine being offered the ability to prevent loss, to secure a world against decay, and to make every doubtful person see its beauty as you see it. The offer is not simply to become Sauron wearing a different face. It is to become a ruler who can call domination love, and make resistance appear ugly or perverse. Her refusal means accepting limits upon what she may preserve.

Faramir gives perhaps the clearest practical alternative. He is a captain, not a retired philosopher, and his father can accuse him of neglecting military necessity. He has reason to want a victory. Yet he lets Frodo go. He does not announce that armies or rulers are illegitimate; he continues to fight. What he rejects is the conversion of a military obligation into permission to use any power available. Faramir's choice shows that Tolkien does not reject public authority altogether. The book honors leaders who defend, heal and make room for others to flourish. It asks where their authority comes from and what it is for.

We can see the same distinction in Aragorn's use of the palantír. He risks revealing himself to Sauron and using an ancient artifact for strategic information. But this is not identical to putting on the Ring and attempting to command every will around him. Aragorn's legitimacy in the novel is assembled from service, healing, loyalty, and a claim to an old inheritance. A modern reader may question the assumptions about hereditary kingship while still recognizing that Tolkien contrasts rightful rule with the fantasy of making all other minds extensions of one's own.

## The temptation of a small garden

The most revealing Ring temptation may belong to Sam. When he carries it briefly in Mordor, it offers him an enlarged version of his deepest desire. He could become a mighty figure who transforms a wasteland into a flourishing garden. Unlike a would-be conqueror, Sam already knows how to nurture something alive. His motive seems innocent, and that is what makes the moment so instructive. The Ring would magnify the scale of his dream until other people's freedom disappeared inside his certainty about what a garden should be.

Sam resists partly because he knows the difference between tending plants and commanding a world. He can take responsibility for a plot of ground. He cannot responsibly claim the right to remake every landscape and life according to a design no one else may refuse. In that small distinction the novel gives its answer to an enormous political question. A good end does not turn unlimited power into a good institution. We do not have to be hostile to gardens, or to ambitious public works, to understand why a garden imposed by absolute command is no longer the same good as a garden cared for in relationship with other people.

Saruman provides the inverse case. He speaks as a strategist who understands what history now requires. Rather than accepting the weakness of the free peoples, he plans to become indispensable to the victorious side and eventually supplant it. He turns Isengard into a factory and Rohan into a field of influence. His language of realism becomes an excuse for treating others as materials. He is not defeated only because he chose the wrong alliance. He has already begun to inhabit a world where friendship, free conversation, and living landscapes are obstacles to be managed.

Denethor presents another failure of imagination. His despair is founded on real evidence: armies gathering, losses sustained, and the apparent inferiority of Gondor's resources. Yet he interprets the evidence as a proof that no future remains except a humiliation he must forestall. The palantír has shown him selected truths, not simply illusions. A mind can be crushed by true information when it refuses to recognize what the information does not include. Denethor's need to control the terms of defeat becomes fatal to himself and almost fatal to Faramir.

## Is Tolkien saying power always corrupts?

It would be convenient, but inaccurate, to end with that slogan. The novel depends upon power exercised well. Éomer leads riders. Théoden chooses to act. Faramir commands soldiers. Gandalf exercises authority when he must. Galadriel protects Lothlórien. Aragorn assumes a throne, and the book presents healing and restoration under his reign as goods. The distinction is not between all power and no power. It is between power answerable to a good beyond the ruler, and power that makes the ruler's will the definition of the good.

Even this formulation requires scrutiny. Tolkien's preferred symbols of legitimate authority include kingship, ancient bloodlines and inherited obligations. Those symbols may not persuade a reader committed to democratic accountability. We can acknowledge that tension without flattening the novel into either a defense of monarchy or a manifesto of anti-government individualism. The story explores forms of authority in a mythic political universe, where character and lineage are foregrounded in ways that differ from the institutions of a modern state.

There is a further ambiguity. The Ring appears to amplify desire, but characters also arrive at their choices through history, temperament, and circumstance. Boromir is not simply hypnotized into doing something that has no connection with his own convictions. Sam's refusal is not just immunity granted by being small. These choices become legible because the people have histories. The supernatural object intensifies moral possibilities that were already present.

That is why Tolkien's solution is not a better master of the Ring. The quest succeeds when the instrument of domination is removed from the political field altogether. This does not end political conflict, nor does it prevent ordinary corruption. The Shire will still require neighbors willing to stand up to bullies, and Gondor will still need rulers who listen. What disappears is the dream that one superior will can finally make the world's other wills unnecessary.

The distinction is worth carrying outside fantasy, cautiously. Not every powerful tool is a Ring, and not every emergency measure is tyranny. But emergencies are especially good at making a concentration of power seem temporary and virtuous. Tolkien gives that pressure a memorable narrative form. The burden of proof falls not only on what a weapon can accomplish, but on what kind of people and relationships its use will create.
