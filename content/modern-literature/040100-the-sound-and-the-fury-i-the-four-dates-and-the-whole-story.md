# The Sound and the Fury I: the four dates and the whole story

## The Sound and the Fury — The Story, Part One: Benjy’s Saturday

Begin beside a fence. Someone on the other side is hitting a little white ball. A man calls to a caddie, and the sound of that word reaches a listener for whom it is not a job title. It is a name. It sounds like Caddy, his sister, and the sound releases a remembered world. This is how William Faulkner begins The Sound and the Fury. He gives us neither the family's pedigree nor a convenient explanation of who is speaking. He gives us Benjy Compson, walking by the fence on a Saturday in Mississippi, and he allows a word to draw thirty years of family life into the present.

The date at the head of the section is April seventh, nineteen twenty-eight. It is the day before Easter and Benjy's thirty-third birthday. Benjy cannot speak in conventional language. He cries, moans, moves toward things he recognizes, and recoils from changes that disturb him. His consciousness, however, is presented to us in sentences. The writing is not a transcript of speech he could deliver. It is Faulkner's effort to invent an experience of perception before the explanatory story most narrators would supply. Benjy is not having the past recalled as a clearly labeled historical account. He encounters a sight or a sound, and suddenly another time is present.

Luster, a younger member of the Black Gibson family who work in the Compson household, is taking Benjy around the grounds. Luster is searching for a lost quarter. He wants money to attend the traveling show that has arrived in town. Benjy wants no such thing. He wants familiar routes, familiar faces, the security of things that once stayed where they belonged. Luster's practical irritation and Benjy's grief share a single landscape, but they do not amount to the same problem. The family for which the Gibsons labor is not simply unhappy; its money and household authority have been deteriorating for decades. The golf course beyond the fence lies on land that used to belong to the Compsons.

The name Caddy sends Benjy to the childhood he shared with four Compson children: Quentin, the eldest; Caddy, his sister; Jason, another brother; and Benjy himself. For part of that childhood he was called Maury, after his mother's brother. When the family decided that he did not meet their expectations of what a son should be, his mother insisted on changing his name to Benjamin. His body and his needs did not change with the name. What changed was the family's language for its embarrassment. They wanted a family name rescued from a child whom they judged incapable of carrying it.

In one of the novel's earliest remembered scenes, the children play outside while their grandmother is dying. The adults withhold what is happening in the house. Caddy climbs a tree to look into a window. From below, the boys see her muddy underclothes as she climbs. Quentin is disturbed, Jason reports, and Benjy sees a movement that matters because it is Caddy's. The scene will acquire meanings the children cannot yet comprehend. There is death hidden inside the house, and there is a young girl's freedom to climb and see what the adults will not tell her. The boys' different reactions anticipate the possessiveness, resentment, and fear that will shape the adult household.

Caddy is the child who most consistently cares for Benjy. She leads him, comforts him, and notices his distress. He recognizes her through her smell, sometimes associated with trees and open air. The bond is intimate and ordinary. For a child denied the explanations other people receive, a scent, a path, a touch, or a voice can give the world stability. But Caddy is a person growing older, not a permanent piece of the garden. As she begins relationships beyond the home, Benjy registers unfamiliar scents and departures. He cries when her familiar presence changes. He cannot tell us that she has a right to a life of her own; neither can the rest of the household easily accept it, though they possess words with which to justify their demands.

Around the house stand a father and mother who do not reliably shelter the children. Mr. Compson is intelligent, worn down by drinking and resignation. He offers aphorisms instead of an adequate response to pain. Mrs. Compson is absorbed in sickness, family reputation, and her grievances. She imagines her own suffering as the center of the family's story. The actual work of feeding, tending, escorting, and protecting is done largely by Dilsey Gibson and her relatives. When Benjy's memories move among childhood rooms, outdoors, and the kitchen, it is often a Gibson family member who physically makes a day possible.

We learn what happens to Caddy by fragments. She grows up, becomes sexually active, and becomes pregnant. A marriage is arranged with Herbert Head, a man whose wealth seems to offer escape from disgrace. He discovers that the child is not his and abandons the marriage. Caddy is excluded from the Compson home, but her daughter is raised there. The child is also named Quentin, after Caddy's brother. Thus there are two people named Quentin in this novel: the brother who dies in nineteen ten, and the daughter whose turbulent teenage life is unfolding in nineteen twenty-eight. Benjy's sensory memory does not sort their names for us. The listener must.

Caddy's wedding produces one of the most piercing absences in Benjy's section. She is dressed for departure; he is dressed and moved through a ceremony whose social meaning he cannot grasp. To him, it is enough that the person who steadied his world is going away. The novel does not give Benjy a clear declaration about abandonment. It lets the ceremony, the voices, and his distress reveal it. He also remembers that the family sold its pasture, the very space linked to his ordinary pleasures, in order to fund Quentin's education at Harvard and to support Caddy's wedding. A sacrifice described by others as family advancement becomes, for Benjy, a loss of ground.

Other memories carry a harsher story. At a gate near passing schoolgirls, Benjy runs toward one of them. Frightened adults interpret the encounter as a sexual danger, and the family responds with a devastating bodily intervention: he is castrated. Faulkner does not invite us to treat the family's judgment as a medical diagnosis or to imagine that Benjy has consented. The scene belongs to a world quick to exert control over someone who has little recognized power to explain himself. The possibilities of institutional confinement hover over his life as well.

There are also fragments of Quentin, the older brother, who was sent north to Harvard and never returned. The young man's suicide in nineteen ten reaches Benjy not as an orderly cause-and-effect report but through conversations and changes in the household. Mr. Compson eventually dies; the mother withdraws further into complaint; Jason becomes the adult son who holds the household's money and weaponizes its humiliations. Benjy continues to inhabit the house where the family he knew has disappeared.

Listen to the present-day day beneath the memories. Luster wants his quarter. Golfers call across the fence. Benjy has moments of contentment, then cries. He follows a route around a property no longer wholly his family's. He has a candle-lit birthday cake with candles; his mother handles his presence as both a worry and an affront to her vanity. The Gibson family supplies the attention and patience the household's supposed owners largely fail to offer. Each ordinary task can bring a sensory return to Caddy, and every return contains the renewed fact that she is gone.

A conventional narrator might say: this family has suffered a tragedy, and here is its sequence. Benjy cannot do that. Yet his account discloses something that a polished family history might conceal. The people who speak loudly about honor can be negligent; the sister branded a disgrace was a source of tenderness; and the land called a family possession was also a place where a vulnerable person lived in bodily safety. Benjy's world is more than an elaborate puzzle to decode. Its disorienting form places a demand on us: before judging him or solving him, attend to what he senses, what he loves, and what has been taken away.

By the time his Saturday ends, almost nothing in Benjy's situation is resolved. He has not recovered Caddy, and the family's fortunes have not returned. Faulkner gives us a first account of loss that cannot become a tidy explanation, because the person experiencing it has no stable distance from the losses. The past does not sit behind him. It keeps arriving with the word Caddy, the smell of trees, a place in the yard, and the movement of a body that remembers a sister's care.

## The Sound and the Fury — The Story, Part Two: Quentin’s Last Day

The second part of The Sound and the Fury begins not after Benjy's Saturday but nearly eighteen years before it. The heading reads June second, nineteen ten. We are in Massachusetts with Quentin Compson, the eldest Compson son, near Harvard University. By the time this day ends he will be dead. The novel lets us accompany him for hours before the whole significance of his actions becomes clear. It is not the suspense of whether a hero will escape a threat. It is the distress of being near someone who is preparing to end his life while thinking continually about what he cannot repair.

Quentin wakes to a watch that belonged to his grandfather. His father has given it to him with a bitter account of time and futility. Quentin breaks off its hands, but the movement does not stop. The watch becomes one of the day's governing objects. Measuring time is supposed to make a day manageable. For Quentin it makes the distance between what happened, what he remembers, and what he intends almost impossible to bear. A clock can be deprived of its visible hands without relieving the mind that hears it.

It helps to know the central event of his recent family life. His sister Caddy has had sexual relationships and become pregnant. The family has arranged a marriage with Herbert Head, a man who does not know the full circumstances and whose social standing seems capable of protecting the Compson name. Quentin has confused his sister's autonomy with the collapse of all order. He loves her; he also cannot bear the thought that she has a life independent of the identity he has assigned to her. These feelings are entangled in his mind, where jealousy, fear, affection, shame, and fantasies of rescue continually exchange places.

At one point Quentin tells his father that he and Caddy have committed incest. The claim is false. It is an attempt to make the unbearable sexual history of his sister into a story in which he can occupy a central, tragic role. His father does not accept the confession. Instead of recognizing a son in crisis, Mr. Compson responds with a weary skepticism about purity, desire, honor, and the importance of anything people pursue. Quentin hears these ideas without being freed by them. His father's cynicism collides with his own rigid ideal of an unchanging sister. Neither offers the living Caddy room to be herself.

Quentin also remembers Dalton Ames, associated with Caddy's sexual history. These memories are not straightforward hearings of evidence. Quentin's imagination returns to scenes of confrontation, to questions asked and answers anticipated, to the possibility of violence, and to the humiliating possibility that no declaration by him will change what Caddy chooses. In his distress he turns a woman's experience into an accusation against himself. That is why the most emotionally charged passages are not simply descriptions of something Caddy has done. They are records of Quentin's failing effort to make the world obey an inherited moral language.

In the present-day streets around Cambridge and Boston, he tries to move through ordinary actions. He walks, rides, looks into shops, meets fellow students, pays attention to a river, and makes purchases. The route is not random. Quietly, he obtains heavy flat-irons, objects associated with his plan to drown himself. He encounters water over and over. The Charles River is both a place in a real city and the dark destination toward which his thoughts keep turning. Nothing in a shop window or a morning's errand can lift that private direction from his mind.

There is a strange, moving episode with a young Italian girl whom Quentin cannot easily communicate with. He tries to help her find where she belongs, and she follows him. Adults begin to read the situation differently. A brother or guardian arrives; Quentin is suspected of taking the child; there is police involvement before the misunderstanding is cleared up. This episode matters partly because it puts Quentin's bewilderment into an external, social form. He believes himself to be acting harmlessly; other people see a suspicious man escorting a girl. His own certainty about his intentions gives him no control over what others conclude.

In another part of the day he moves among university acquaintances, including Gerald Bland, whose easy social confidence and manner with women become intolerable to him. Quentin gets into a fight. His body is injured; the encounter leaves embarrassment and confusion, not heroic resolution. The campus world that should represent his education and future has become another setting in which he measures himself against an ideal of masculine authority he cannot inhabit. The Compsons sold land to finance his attendance at Harvard. Quentin will not complete his first year.

His relation to time is built from more than clocks. He remembers Caddy as a child in the stream and as a young woman at the edge of departure. He hears conversations out of order. Names and phrases repeat until they seem to detach from their first occasions. His consciousness can move from a bench or a shop to the Compson household with no formal transition. Sometimes words fracture and conventional punctuation disappears. The language is not a puzzle that will yield a single tidy equation once every flashback is dated. It is a mind trying to escape its own chronology and failing.

Notice the other Quentin. In the later events of the novel, Caddy has a daughter given this very name. The choice memorializes the brother after his death, but it also makes the family repeat a name while refusing to face what the first bearer suffered. The younger Quentin, the girl, will have her own conflicts with Jason, the surviving brother. She is not present to tell this Harvard story. At this point in the novel the name belongs to a young man whose world has contracted around an idea of disgrace.

Quentin's suicide is not presented as a conclusive philosophical insight. Nothing his sister has done makes his death inevitable or justified. The novel shows how an ideology can deepen distress: an ideal of chastity that cannot account for another person's freedom; a code of family honor that places a disproportionate burden on women; a father's intellectual detachment that lacks care; and Quentin's inability to distinguish emotional pain from an unalterable sentence. These pressures do not exhaust the meaning of his mental state. They help us see why the thought of a future is disappearing from him.

He prepares himself carefully. Clothing, objects, and small arrangements matter to him at the end because they offer a semblance of control. The day continues to unfold in scraps of observed life even as his intention becomes unmistakable. Later sections and the larger family history establish that he drowns himself in the Charles River. The first-person account stops short of becoming a reporter's description of the death. No one can narrate what Quentin does not survive to tell.

One important difference between Benjy and Quentin emerges here. Benjy's memories arrive through sensory association without a clear past-tense boundary. Quentin knows about clocks and the prestige of abstract explanations; that knowledge is no protection against obsessive returns. He tries to abolish time by removing the watch's hands, while the watch continues ticking. He tries to abolish the difference between Caddy's choices and his own by claiming to be responsible for her sexual history. Both efforts fail. The world outside his mind persists.

The section leaves us with an appalling contrast. In one place, the family has spent its land and its hopes to send a son into elite education. In another, a son cannot imagine inhabiting the future that the money was meant to purchase. It is tempting to call Quentin the victim of the family's decay, but that would ignore the ways his own possessive beliefs reproduce the family's damage. Faulkner refuses a single sentence that excuses or dismisses him. We are asked to witness both his suffering and the forms of power he imagines having over someone else.

## The Sound and the Fury — The Story, Part Three: Jason’s Good Friday

The third section of The Sound and the Fury opens on Good Friday, April sixth, nineteen twenty-eight. It is one day before Benjy's Saturday, but the story has traveled forward from Quentin's last day in nineteen ten. Now the man telling us about the Compson household is Jason, the remaining adult brother who manages its money. He is not difficult to follow in the way Benjy and Quentin are. He speaks in sentences whose targets are immediately clear. He complains, calculates, mocks, and blames. If the earlier sections force us to work out what a speaker cannot explain, this one requires a different effort: we must decide how much to believe a speaker who explains everything to his own advantage.

Jason begins in a state of anger that seems to have no resting point. His mother is ill, or says she is ill; Benjy makes demands he finds intolerable; servants do not obey him quickly enough; and his teenage niece, whom the family calls Miss Quentin, repeatedly escapes his supervision. She is Caddy's daughter. The name was taken from the Harvard student who died before she was born. Jason treats her as living evidence that his sister ruined his prospects. In his account she is not a young person whose circumstances deserve attention. She is an expense, a danger, a liar, and an insult to his authority.

That authority is more fragile than it sounds. Jason works in a farm-supply store and depends on a household whose social standing is a residue of its past. The Compsons are not prosperous aristocrats in possession of a functioning estate. They are people who talk as though a lost place in society entitles them to obedience. Jason knows how little is left, and he measures every injury in money. When he looks back to Caddy's marriage with Herbert Head, he remembers a promised bank job. Herbert withdrew the offer after the marriage failed. Jason has turned that disappointment into a story in which Caddy owes him the life he imagines he would have had.

The most consequential thing Jason does with money is deliberately hidden from his mother. Caddy has been sending funds to help support her daughter. Mrs. Compson does not want to accept her disgraced daughter's assistance, and Jason pretends to enforce the refusal. He shows his mother fraudulent substitutes for the checks and allows her to burn them in a performance of injured family honor. Meanwhile, he keeps the real proceeds. The deception allows him to steal from both the mother and the girl while telling himself he is the one person who works and suffers. Money, in his mind, becomes proof that he is right; control of the money becomes permission to punish.

The niece is not docile. Miss Quentin slips out of the house, meets people her uncle dislikes, and refuses his moral lectures. A traveling show is in Jefferson, and Jason grows alarmed that she is meeting one of its performers. He pursues her through town, interrogates people, and watches her with an intensity that he regards as vigilance. The reader sees coercion. The more he polices her, the more she seeks ways around him. The fact that she resists does not make every choice safe; it does make Jason's claim to be her selfless guardian untenable. He is largely uninterested in her welfare apart from what it can do to his own reputation and authority.

Jason's language reveals his world as clearly as any formal flashback. He insults women, Black employees, religious figures, customers, and his own family. He talks as if exploitation were an ordinary privilege of the competent and complaint a mark of inferior people. His self-pity is constant even when he is in control. Faulkner makes this voice energetic and sometimes darkly comic, but the energy must not be confused with moral credibility. Jason is capable of reporting accurate facts. He is also capable of arranging those facts into a case in which he is always the wronged party.

A commercial side of his life makes the self-deception more visible. He follows cotton prices and speculates in the market, imagining quick gains that will compensate him for what the family has cost. His decisions do not deliver the mastery he boasts of. He becomes enraged by losses and suspicious of strangers, while finding other people to blame for his calculations. The old family estate has been reduced by years of expenditure and bad judgment; his own response is not to rebuild it through honest work but to gamble, hoard, and embezzle. He can see figures on a page much better than he can see an ethical obligation.

At home his mother continually reinforces the family's mythology. She praises the supposedly dutiful Jason and laments the other children. The love she extends him is not the kind that asks him to become more generous. It is an alliance in grievance. He agrees to protect her from unpleasant facts, and in doing so gains her support for cruelty and concealment. The mother who refuses Caddy's money on principle is being deceived by the son she trusts. It is a miserable domestic bargain: she keeps her self-image, he keeps the money, and Miss Quentin is denied what her mother has tried to provide.

Dilsey, the aging Black cook, is present throughout these conflicts as the person expected to make the household function. She and her family have looked after the Compson children for years. Jason orders and insults them; they answer, comply where necessary, or quietly protect others. The narrator is not interested in their history except where it intersects with his complaints. Yet even in his version we can detect how much the house depends on the labor he refuses to respect. He imagines his money makes him master of events, but he could not manage the household's most basic needs alone.

Benjy also figures in Jason's anger. He is a brother with continuing needs, not a profitable asset. Jason speaks about him with contempt and treats his presence as a threat to social respectability. An adult man who has not been given authority over his own body and who has lost the sister who understood him now lives under the control of a brother who values almost nothing about him. Jason's version of practical reason is therefore an especially severe judgment upon the family. It counts resources and obscures the people whose lives those resources sustain.

As Good Friday continues, Jason moves between the store, the house, and the streets where he suspects Miss Quentin has gone. He watches the performer's movements, invents accusations, and returns to the familiar rhythm of argument. Faulkner does not give him a sudden confession. He has little reason to doubt that his resentment is justified, and it has become easier for him to preserve the structure of his life than to consider the harm he does. The narrative's relative clarity is itself an irony. Unlike the brothers whose minds shift through time, Jason can tell a continuous story. But he is least willing to allow a competing account any weight.

The catastrophe of Easter morning has not happened yet. Miss Quentin has not yet taken the stored money and escaped. By ending Jason's section on Friday, before Saturday and Sunday gather the consequences, the novel lets us occupy his justification before we see its failure. He believes he owns his niece's future and her mother's financial help. He believes he alone is being cheated. What he cannot imagine is that she might refuse the role of permanently indebted prisoner that he has written for her.

Faulkner's structure has now given us three different responses to the same family history. Benjy responds to absence with uncomprehending grief. Quentin responded to his sister's adulthood with impossible demands upon time, purity, and himself. Jason responds with bills, threats, and theft. The apparent maturity of his language is no guarantee that he understands more. Indeed, the precision of his accusations may help him avoid knowing the person he has become.

## The Sound and the Fury — The Story, Part Four: Dilsey and Easter Sunday

On Easter morning, April eighth, nineteen twenty-eight, The Sound and the Fury changes its narrative voice. The three Compson brothers have each spoken through a version of their own consciousness. Now the story moves into third person. The first sustained attention falls not on the family that owns the house but on Dilsey Gibson, the Black woman who has spent much of her life caring for the family. She wakes into a cold, difficult morning, a house full of uncooperative people, and work that cannot be left undone because the people who depend on her would suffer.

Dilsey is aging. Her body does not move without cost. Her daughter Frony and grandson Luster belong to the household's extended world of labor, and each has a life whose concerns the Compsons often treat as secondary. The kitchen, meals, clothes, and Benjy's care require immediate attention. Mrs. Compson complains and makes demands; the authority she displays is disproportionate to what she contributes. Jason's movements are tense and impatient. He is about to discover that the money he has been keeping has gone missing. Dilsey does not receive a day off from other people's emergencies simply because it is Easter.

In the household there are signs that Miss Quentin has escaped. She has climbed down from her room and left. Jason's hidden cash is gone. The girl has found the money he has diverted from her mother's support, and she leaves with a man connected to the traveling show. To Jason, the theft is proof of her wickedness. To the reader, who knows how he obtained much of the money, the situation is more complicated. The girl is taking money he has concealed from its intended use, though her method and destination carry risks. Her escape has a consequence for Jason that he cannot publicly explain without exposing his own dishonesty.

Jason's anger is enormous because he has lost more than a hoard. He has lost the appearance of total control. He seeks help from the local sheriff, who has little reason to assist him in the way he demands. Jason cannot provide a simple account of rightful ownership without questions about why he was holding so much cash and where it came from. He then drives off in pursuit of the traveling performers. His fury takes him beyond the household, but the pursuit does not restore his power. He fails to bring Miss Quentin back. His attempts to force an encounter culminate in humiliation and a physical rebuff rather than a clean triumph.

Meanwhile Dilsey takes Benjy to church with members of her family. This is one of the novel's most important changes in setting. The Compson house has been full of claim and counterclaim, illness, shame, profanity, and resentment. The church is not untouched by the surrounding society's segregation and hardship. It is a Black congregation whose collective ritual gives voices and bodies a place that the white family's household rarely allows them. The movement from kitchen to church brings us into a community with its own relationships, customs, forms of authority, and account of suffering.

At the service a visiting preacher, Reverend Shegog, begins in a way that does not at once impress his listeners. As his sermon develops, his voice and language change. He draws the congregation into the story of Christ's suffering and resurrection. Easter becomes not merely a date in the calendar but a form of time that reaches beyond a family's ruin. The preacher invokes grief, endurance, death, and a promise that death is not the only word. People respond bodily and aloud. Their participation is not a decorative spectacle inserted to make the Compsons' trouble seem smaller. It is an expression of a community's worship and of the resources its members have made within a world that denies them equal standing.

Dilsey is moved to tears. The sermon connects with her own long knowledge of the Compsons, of children who grew up, of those who died, and of a house that seems to be ending while daily duties continue. Her sense of having witnessed both beginnings and endings has special force because she has spent years bearing other people's burdens. She sees the house's long decline more clearly than the household's owners. Yet she is not freed from her work by an epiphany. When the service is over, there will still be food to prepare and Benjy to attend. The spiritual dignity of the episode coexists with an economic and racial structure that remains unchanged.

The third-person narration matters here. Dilsey does not speak a full first-person monologue like the brothers. We come close to her through movements, conversations, the attention she gives to others, and the narrative's description of her labor. This is a real widening of the book's perspective; it is not the same as allowing her to control the whole story. Faulkner still mediates her voice. A reader should not take the section's reverence for her endurance as a complete account of what such a person might desire outside the household that depends on her.

When Dilsey returns to the Compson home, Jason is absent and angry, the niece is gone, and the familiar routines must be resumed. Benjy continues to be cared for, but his presence generates another sharp ending. Luster eventually takes him out in a carriage, driving through the town square. He chooses a route that differs from the one Benjy expects. Benjy becomes intensely distressed. It is a small change of direction to another person and a disorienting disruption to him. Jason appears, reacts angrily, and takes control of the reins. When the carriage resumes the familiar way around the monument, Benjy becomes calm.

The final carriage passage has often been read as a restoration of order. It does restore a local sensory order for Benjy: the horse, the square, and the expected direction align once more. But the household has not recovered its dead, Caddy has not returned, the lost money is not found, and the system of domination under which Dilsey works is not repaired. The novel ends with Benjy's experience of a familiar pattern, not with a political settlement or a family's moral cure. The precision of this distinction keeps the ending from becoming sentimental.

What has happened to Caddy's daughter? She has escaped the house and Jason's control; the novel does not provide a trustworthy report of her long future. Jason fears she is leaving with a man from the show, and the narrative supports that immediate departure, but we are not given permission to declare her freedom secure or to condemn her to a fate the text does not narrate. Her disappearance is a boundary for the family's power and for the reader's knowledge. The mother, Caddy, remains excluded. Her daughter's flight repeats an exit but does not repeat the exact life.

The Easter setting offers competing meanings of endurance. In the Compson family, endurance can mean stubborn preservation of a reputation, repeated accusation, or a refusal to release an old injury. Dilsey's endurance is different: she repeatedly helps people meet material needs even when they scarcely recognize the cost. The sermon describes survival in yet another register, through the language of death and resurrection. Neither the family's ruin nor Dilsey's labor is magically undone, but the novel suggests that the world contains forms of community and time unavailable to Jason's angry accounting.

The last section also makes us think again about who has been speaking. The family possesses a rich account of its own grievances because the three sons have been granted the interior stage. Caddy has never had a section to herself. Dilsey, although central to the last movement, has not received the same kind of first-person access. At the end we know more, but we have not heard every life on its own terms. That limitation belongs to the work's ethical and artistic design, not merely to a missing page.

A carriage turns. A man who has been crying grows quiet. It is not a grand conclusion, but it gathers the book's question into a physical act. How much of the world must remain stable for someone to live in it? Who keeps that stability in place? Who has the authority to change a route, a name, a marriage, a body, a life? The final Sunday makes those questions more acute than a simple story about a once-great family's fall could ever have done.

## The Sound and the Fury — The Whole Compson Story in Time

If you have followed The Sound and the Fury in the order William Faulkner wrote it, you have been asked to assemble a family history from four different experiences of time. The first part begins in nineteen twenty-eight and leaps back through Benjy's memories. The second returns to nineteen ten and stays near Quentin's last day while the past intrudes upon it. The third comes back to nineteen twenty-eight but begins one day before the first. The fourth reaches Easter Sunday. Now we can put the main events into an approximate chronological order without pretending that chronology is the whole meaning of the novel.

The Compsons are a white Mississippi family with an established name and a fading hold on property, money, and social authority. Their fictional home is in Jefferson, within the region Faulkner calls Yoknapatawpha County. The family draws status from a Southern order that survived the Civil War as a set of claims about ancestry and rank, even as its material basis eroded. Its life is supported by the work of the Black Gibson family. That division of labor is not a footnote to the Compsons' decline. It is a continuing condition of their domestic life.

There are four Compson children. The eldest son, Quentin, bears the name of an older family line and grows into a fiercely self-conscious young man. Candace, called Caddy, is adventurous, affectionate, and increasingly determined to live on her own terms. Jason, the younger brother, watches the world with suspicion and grows into a man who turns slight into grievance. Benjy, originally called Maury, has a profound intellectual disability and cannot communicate his experience in ordinary speech. He depends greatly on Caddy's care. Their father drinks and retreats into bitter philosophy. Their mother is absorbed in her health and the family's perceived humiliations.

Around the end of the nineteenth century, the children's grandmother dies. The children are kept away from the house's adult rituals. Caddy climbs a tree to look into a window, and the boys look up at her. The image matters because the children are young enough not to understand the death behind the glass, but old enough to begin policing one another's bodies and actions. Later the moment becomes emblematic of how Caddy can see and act while her brothers project meanings upon her. Benjy's attachment to her is rooted in care rather than a theory of family honor, but even his need can become a demand that she never change.

The children grow. Caddy spends time with boys and then young men. Quentin is disturbed by what he understands as her loss of sexual purity. His distress is not simply protective affection; it becomes an attempt to make his sister's choices a verdict upon his own identity. Benjy becomes distressed by signs that Caddy smells different or is moving away from their old life. Jason, already resentful, learns to treat family scandal as an injury he can later charge to another person's account. Meanwhile Caddy herself is navigating the restricted choices available to a young Southern woman whose family values its reputation more consistently than her needs.

As the financial situation worsens, the Compsons sell part of their land. One major sale helps fund Quentin's Harvard education and Caddy's wedding. The lost pasture is not an abstraction. Benjy has known it as a place where he could move within familiar boundaries; later golfers occupy land that once belonged to the estate. To the adults the transaction is a way of preserving status by selling property. To the dependent son it is an everyday world changed without his consent. To the student it is a great investment in a future he does not survive.

Caddy becomes pregnant. She marries Herbert Head, but he discovers that the child was conceived before the marriage and is not his. The marriage collapses. Jason had hoped to obtain a job through Herbert, and he never forgives his sister for the opportunity that disappears. Caddy is effectively barred from the Compson home. Her child, a girl named Quentin in memory of the dead brother, is raised among the people who have cast out her mother. The arrangement separates a mother from her daughter while allowing the family to preserve its narrative of moral superiority.

Before that child grows up, Quentin the elder dies. On June second, nineteen ten, while studying at Harvard, he walks through Cambridge and the surrounding area in a state of severe psychological distress. He obsessively returns to Caddy's sexual history, the family's idea of honor, and his father's hopeless philosophy. He tries to confess a sexual act with his sister that did not occur. He breaks the hands off a watch that continues to tick. He acquires heavy objects connected with his suicide plan, and he ultimately drowns himself in the Charles River. The novel does not make his death a necessary consequence of Caddy's choices. It shows a mind in crisis within a destructive structure of belief.

Mr. Compson later dies. The mother remains in the household, now increasingly dependent on Dilsey while speaking of the family's wrongs as though they are principally injuries to herself. Jason becomes the son who supervises affairs. Benjy remains in need of assistance, and his body has already been subjected to punishment and control when frightened adults interpret an incident at the gate as sexual aggression. His treatment exposes the discrepancy between a family's claim to respectability and its willingness to override a vulnerable member's bodily autonomy.

Caddy attempts to support her daughter through money sent to the household. Jason diverts much of it. His mother believes he is rejecting the assistance; in reality he keeps the proceeds while presenting fraudulent checks for burning. Miss Quentin grows into an unhappy teenager who resists his authority. Jason monitors her movements, condemns her sexuality, and demands compliance from someone he has helped deprive of both a mother and financial support. The conflict is both intimate and economic. He controls the terms on which the family will describe her behavior and tries to control the money that might help her act independently.

By Good Friday of nineteen twenty-eight, the traveling show has come to Jefferson. Jason believes his niece is seeing one of the performers. He searches for her and returns again and again to the story of how his life was ruined by Caddy. Saturday is Benjy's birthday. He walks with Luster beside a fence enclosing land that used to be his family's pasture. The calls of golfers, the smell of the day, and the movements of people draw his childhood into the present. The reader has heard Quentin's voice from eighteen years earlier and Jason's voice from yesterday. All three brothers are still trying, in different ways, to live with a sister who has gone.

On Sunday morning Miss Quentin escapes through a window and leaves with money Jason has concealed. She departs in the company of the traveling show. Jason's pursuit is futile and angry. His inability to explain publicly the cash he had accumulated compromises his position. While he tries to recover the money and his authority, Dilsey takes Benjy to Easter services in a Black church. Reverend Shegog's sermon gives suffering and survival a language the Compson household does not possess. Dilsey returns to work. Later Benjy cries when a carriage is driven along an unfamiliar route, then quiets when it moves through the square as he expects.

That is the practical sequence, but several limits matter. Not every remembered scene can be assigned one undisputed calendar date. Quentin's feverish recollections do not establish every claim he makes about Caddy's private life. Miss Quentin's future is not known simply because she leaves town. The later Compson genealogy Faulkner wrote in the nineteen forties is an additional text, not part of the original nineteen twenty-nine ending. We can use all of these materials to orient ourselves without forcing the novel into a dossier of certain facts.

Think, finally, about the family from the outside. A household says it is a tragedy because it lost land, a Harvard student, a marriage, and its social prestige. But another account observes a disabled son's denied choices, a woman's expulsion, a girl's stolen funds, and the unrecognized labor of a Black family that kept the house functioning. The order of events will not tell us which of these stories deserves primacy. It gives us the groundwork to hear how Faulkner made them compete. The Sound and the Fury is not difficult simply because its chapters are out of order. It is difficult because the people who claim to explain the past are often the least able to understand what they have done to others.
