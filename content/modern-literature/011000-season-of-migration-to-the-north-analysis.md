# Season of Migration to the North: Who Returns, Who Speaks, Who Is Left Behind

## The novel begins with a false relief

A young Sudanese scholar returns from England and believes he is home. Tayeb Salih permits him a beautiful interval of confidence: the river is familiar, the village knows him, his grandfather lives, the land seems continuous with his childhood. The stranger Mustafa Sa'eed interrupts that confidence. A man who looks like a settled farmer suddenly reveals that he has also inhabited the world of English education and metropolitan ambition. An apparent return to origins becomes an encounter with a double.

It is tempting to tell the rest of the novel as the discovery that colonialism has damaged both men. That is true but insufficient. Colonial history is not a substance that affects everyone in the same way. The narrator's education has given him one set of opportunities; Mustafa's has given him a stage on which to perform another identity; Hosna's position in the village makes her vulnerable to forms of coercion that neither man's academic success has abolished. The novel continually asks us to move our attention among these unequal lives.

Salih published the Arabic novel in 1966, and Denys Johnson-Davies's English translation appeared in 1969. It comes from a Sudanese literary world whose modern Arabic writing cannot simply be filed under the English-language African novel. That distinction is part of the point of studying it. English literary education is one of the things its characters experience, not the only language in which their experience can be told. The book does not invite us to identify 'African' culture with a timeless village and 'European' culture with a single modern city. Its social worlds are more complicated than that.

We will attend to five things: the machinery of the doubled narrator, the theatrical fantasies of empire, the women whose lives are recounted or suppressed, the village's own violence, and the ending in the river. Each reveals a limit in the idea that returning to one's birthplace necessarily means returning to oneself.

## A voice inside a voice

Most of the book is told by a man whose name we never learn. He is not an anonymous neutral camera. He is educated, proud of his achievements, attached to family, susceptible to nostalgia, and sometimes late in seeing what is happening around him. His account has its own desires. When he says the village feels unaltered, we should hear the reassurance in that statement as well as the geography it names.

Mustafa's confession enters this first-person account and changes the authority structure. We hear one man's reconstruction of another man's self-presentation. Sometimes Mustafa speaks in extended recollection. Elsewhere the narrator receives information from other witnesses, the story of a trial, and eventually an arrangement of objects and documents in a locked room. The result is not a complete forensic record. It is a mosaic of testimonies selected, remembered, and situated by the younger man who cannot stop thinking about the older one.

The nested narration gives Mustafa control over the story of his European years, and his most dramatic claims are also claims about his own power. He presents himself as capable of bringing death and ruin to English women. He portrays his career as a kind of counter-conquest. To repeat those declarations as the transparent message of the novel would give the murderer the authority he wants. The more careful reading asks why the book puts so much of its information inside someone who is skilled at manufacturing an identity.

The unnamed narrator has a parallel weakness. He can distinguish the difference between sophisticated argument and evidence in professional life, but his behavior toward Hosna reveals an inability to translate knowledge into help. The novel doesn't make him morally identical to Mustafa. It makes identification with Mustafa both a threat and a temptation. One man has acted spectacularly and destructively; the other can still fail people through hesitation. The doubling is a technique for testing similarity without erasing difference.

## The north as a stage

The title describes movement in a direction, not an uncomplicated progression into civilization. The colonial educational system carries Mustafa from Sudan into English institutions. Once there, he finds that even brilliant achievement does not free him from being viewed through fantasies. He is expected to represent a remote, dangerous, erotically charged elsewhere. Rather than rejecting those expectations, he learns to exploit them.

He furnishes his life as a performance. His London rooms, manners, and stories can be interpreted as props on a stage built partly by European Orientalism and partly by his own willingness to embody it. He knows that his listeners may prefer a thrilling imaginary version of Sudan to the practical details of a Sudanese man's education. The performance gives him an advantage in particular encounters. It also lets him avoid being known as a person who must answer for ordinary actions.

There is a profound danger in describing this as revenge for empire. Imperial domination made certain fantasies available; Mustafa manipulates them; some of the women involved pursue fantasies of their own. But women dying by suicide are not emblems in a historical game, and Jean Morris is not a symbolic European territory he is entitled to occupy. Murder does not become resistance because its perpetrator frames it in the vocabulary of conquest. The book is strong precisely where the language of historical retaliation becomes morally unreliable.

Scholars have debated this relation between gender and empire. Waïl S. Hassan emphasizes the way colonial, racial, and patriarchal systems create unstable and mutually reinforcing constructions of masculinity. That approach helps us resist the idea that Mustafa's conduct can be evaluated only at the level of Africa versus Europe. The individual women do not stop being people when he makes them represent continents. Nor does Mustafa become free from domination by reenacting the forms of domination he condemns.

The narrative's relationship to Joseph Conrad's *Heart of Darkness* is often noted. Conrad sends a European traveler into the Congo and makes the encounter an occasion for European uncertainty and horror. Salih sends an educated Sudanese man north and lets England become a site of projection and self-estrangement. The reversal is illuminating, but it is not a neat reply in which a formerly subordinated speaker takes complete narrative control. Salih's narrator is also bewildered, and the double he encounters does not possess a stable counter-truth. The Sudanese river is as central to Salih's novel as the London rooms where Mustafa constructed his legend.

## The narrator's first blindness: nostalgia

When the narrator first arrives home, the Nile and his grandfather represent continuity. These are real sources of attachment. It would be unkind and inaccurate to call his affection merely false. The grandfather has lived through changes and is a person whom the young man loves. Village life includes care, humor, cooperation, work, and remembered relationships. The narrative makes us feel why a return might be joyful.

Yet nostalgia can change what a person notices. The narrator returns as someone whose opportunities were shaped by institutions available only to certain people. He likes to think of himself as deeply rooted, but he also stands at a social distance from villagers who have not traveled or studied as he has. His freedom to move between village and professional employment becomes part of his power. When a dispute is uncomfortable, he can leave.

Mustafa first disrupts the narrator's self-image through resemblance. Hosna's fate disrupts it through responsibility. The contrast is essential. It is one thing to discover a disturbing secret about a man who resembles you. It is another to recognize that your village, which you trust as the ground beneath your feet, can deny a woman the right to refuse marriage. One form of knowledge concerns the past of a double. The other concerns something being decided in the present, while intervention may still be possible.

The first discovery fascinates the narrator. The second demands action. His hesitation shows that these are different kinds of difficulty. A scholar can become a practiced interpreter of culture without developing the courage or practical understanding to interrupt a wrong that everyone nearby knows is occurring.

## Hosna is not an appendix to Mustafa

A reading concentrated entirely on Mustafa and the unnamed narrator risks reenacting one of the novel's injuries. Hosna appears initially as the wife left behind by an enigmatic man. The reader might imagine that her narrative function is to prove that Mustafa was capable of an ordinary domestic life. But she has desires and refuses the marriage proposed by Wad Rayyes. Her refusal is the clearest moral fact in that part of the story.

The older man's sexual entitlement is not merely an antique custom imposed by abstract tradition. He is a particular person who expects his wish to govern a much younger woman's life. Families and neighbors make or tolerate decisions that help him. The narrator's position is uncomfortable because he may have connections and authority that could be used differently. He is asked to help and does not do enough.

The resulting murder and suicide are not a poetic resolution of a love triangle. They are the outcome of coerced marriage, attempted sexual violence, isolation, and a community's willingness to discount a woman's declared will. Critics may disagree about how far the novel gives Hosna an interior voice of her own. That is itself a reason to resist converting her into an illustration of the male narrator's awakening. Her life is not justified by the lesson it teaches him.

The comparison with the London section becomes sharp. Mustafa makes women into instruments of his self-construction. In the village, men make a woman into an object through which marriage and status can be settled. These are not historically identical structures, and there is no reason to describe the village as simply England in miniature. But the novel does not permit colonial critique to excuse local patriarchy. It shows how easily a person can denounce one form of domination while failing to interrupt another.

If we ask which of the two men has understood the experience of moving between worlds, we may be asking the wrong question. The life most urgently at stake in the novel's second half belongs to a woman who does not need the narrator's seven years of higher education to understand that she does not consent.

## The locked room and the archive of a self

After Mustafa's disappearance the room remains. It contains a version of England transported to the Nile village through books and objects. It is tempting to call the room the truth concealed beneath Mustafa's outer life. But collections are curated, and Mustafa has curated this one. An interior decorated to preserve a particular identity can be another kind of performance.

The narrator enters in search of an answer. He encounters writing, portraits, possessions, the material residue of a vanished career. The mirror in which he briefly sees himself as Mustafa turns the room from evidence of a stranger's life into a threat to his own boundaries. The effect is almost gothic. The missing man seems to have arranged a future scene in which someone else becomes him by looking too long at his remains.

Notice the distinction between records and lives. An archive of books and letters might help reconstruct what a person did, but it can also preserve that person's preferred story about the people he harmed. Without the testimony of those absent, the archive is not self-correcting. The narrator's fascination threatens to make him the final editor of Mustafa's mythology. To become the keeper of another man's secrets is not the same as becoming accountable to that man's survivors.

The room's presence inside the village undermines a simple geography of north and south. The north has not vanished when Mustafa comes home; it sits locked inside a Sudanese house. Likewise, the narrator carried his education into the village even before Mustafa revealed his past. The two geographical directions are lived within the same persons. But the book also insists that these internal migrations do not erase the reality of village households, fields, children, and unequal obligations.

## The river and the open ending

The novel begins with the Nile as a sign of the narrator's secure return. It ends when he enters its water and struggles to come back to shore. Mustafa may have died there during flooding. The unnamed man, swimming later, approaches the possibility of disappearing in a similar way. The structure makes repetition feel available. He could complete the pattern by letting himself go.

Instead he chooses to cry for help. The act has sometimes been read as an affirmation of life against the nihilism associated with Mustafa. That reading has force. The narrator's cry is directed toward others, rather than toward the enclosed theater of one man's exceptional suffering. Yet we do not receive a guaranteed rescue. It would be inaccurate to say that the book ends with the narrator safely back among his family, resolved to behave better. It ends with a call whose answer is not narrated.

His decision to live is an ethical beginning, not an absolution. It cannot undo his failure toward Hosna. Neither can it recover the women Mustafa harmed or the friend whose life might have been saved by different action. What it can do is break the movement toward silence and repetition. It is a beginning, not a verdict of innocence.

Recent scholarship has also attended to the novel's environmental and material energies: the river, seasonal cycles, agricultural work, heat, and the economies connecting the countryside with modern institutions. Charis Olszok, for example, reads the novel's climatic and energy structures as more than scenery. This perspective helps keep the river from becoming only a convenient symbol for psychological depth. The Nile sustains life, destroys land during floods, shapes work, and exists beyond the dramas men wish to see reflected in it.

## What the book changes in this series

Many of the novels we have read ask how a person recovers a past. *Season of Migration to the North* makes the problem sharper by separating possession of a story from responsibility for the people affected by it. The narrator learns an extraordinary amount about Mustafa. He does not thereby become a good guardian to Mustafa's wife. Interpretation and care are not the same act.

The novel also dislodges a familiar literary map. It is written in Arabic by a Sudanese author, it engages European literature without belonging to Europe, and it treats the village not as a static cultural specimen but as a changing social world whose people have conflicting interests. Its formal doubleness is not an ornament. A narrator who believes he has come home must discover how many stories can coexist inside one place, including stories he did not want to know.

At the end, the cry across the river asks whether another relation is possible. It does not offer an abstract synthesis of Europe and Africa, traditional and modern, self and other. It asks for an answer from someone alive on the bank. A man who has spent much of the novel listening to another man's legend finally speaks in a way that makes him vulnerable to other people. Whether they hear him is the question on which Salih stops.

## Critical reading and sources (not spoken)

The distinctions between colonial critique and the ethical status of Mustafa's crimes, and between the narrator's self-knowledge and his obligation to Hosna, are interpretive arguments. The novel is originally Arabic; do not conflate it with an English original. Relevant reading includes Waïl S. Hassan on masculinity and empire, Charis Olszok on environment and energy, and NYRB's edition with the Denys Johnson-Davies translation and Laila Lalami's introduction. Plot orientation: `010200`.
