# The Dispossessed — The Story

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

Shevek grows up on Anarres, a dry world settled by people who rejected the private ownership and class structures of the neighboring planet Urras. Their movement was inspired by Odo, a revolutionary thinker whose followers chose to build a society without government in the familiar sense. They organize work through syndicates, share resources, and teach their children that no person should own another. The settlement is more than a century old when Shevek is born. It has survived hardship, shortages, and the continuing need to trade with Urras across a spaceport whose wall marks the boundary between the worlds.

Shevek's father, Palat, cares about him. His mother, Rulag, accepts an important work posting far away, and Shevek grows up with a sense of absence he does not fully understand. He attends schools where children learn how to cooperate, but also how to avoid seeming superior to others. Shevek is exceptionally gifted at mathematics. When he tries to explain ideas other pupils do not understand, teachers sometimes accuse him of drawing attention to himself. He wants to share what he sees, but his delight in discovery does not fit easily into a social world suspicious of individual distinction.

Shevek and his friends also hear the stories of Odo's revolution and the departure from Urras. The people who came to Anarres were not seeking a more efficient government. They wanted to live without the coercive relations of owning and being owned. For children born generations later, however, the old world's objects and institutions can sound almost fictional. The young sometimes respond to prison, money, and property as puzzles rather than living memories. This distance from the founders' struggle becomes important when they grow old enough to notice how their own society uses shame and exclusion.

The children are taught about the oppression of the old world. They learn that property once meant people could lock up things, land, and even other people. A group of boys becomes curious about prisons and decides to create one as an experiment. They confine another child for long enough to frighten and humiliate him. The incident reveals to Shevek how quickly the power to control another body can be exercised, even in a community founded on opposition to coercion. His friends are not monsters. They are children discovering a terrible possibility.

As a young man, Shevek studies physics. The elderly teacher Mitis recognizes his ability and sends his work to the capital, Abbenay, where a senior scientist named Sabul is influential. Shevek goes to the Central Institute of the Sciences expecting to find colleagues interested in the questions that have occupied him for years. Sabul has access to books and correspondence from Urras, where physicists have pursued related research. He becomes Shevek's gatekeeper. Shevek learns the language of Urras so that he can read the necessary papers, but Sabul often insists that correspondence pass through him and that Shevek's work carry his own name as well.

A woman physicist, Gvarab, offers Shevek an alternative intellectual companionship. She is less esteemed than Sabul, yet she has pursued questions of time that interest Shevek deeply. When her health declines and she dies, Shevek loses one of the people who genuinely understood his project. The research increasingly consumes him. He falls ill and is visited in the clinic by Rulag, the mother he scarcely knows. They try to speak about the years of separation, but the conversation cannot make the past simple. Shevek refuses the easy reconciliation she seems to hope for.

He reconnects with his friend Bedap, who has grown critical of Anarresti society. Bedap believes that the institutions meant to coordinate labor are beginning to command people instead, and that social pressure has become a substitute for the laws and police their ancestors rejected. Other friends have suffered for expressing ideas the community finds distasteful. Their conversations force Shevek to reconsider whether Anarres always lives by the freedom it claims to defend.

On an outing with friends he spends time with Takver, a woman who studies living organisms and whose interests differ greatly from his own. They fall in love and choose to live together. Their partnership is warm, practical, and mutually important. They know that their society discourages treating people as possessions, but they also know they want to share a life rather than be scattered whenever work allocations change. As Shevek struggles with his theory of time, he and Takver begin to imagine a family. Their plans will soon run into a drought that strains the whole planet and into professional barriers that make Shevek wonder whether an anarchist society can still build walls around new ideas.

The pressures of Anarres’s institutions grow sharper when drought makes ordinary survival difficult.

Anarres enters a severe drought. The world was never easy to live on, but the shortage demands new sacrifices. Work assignments become urgent, food is rationed, and people are moved to places where their labor is needed most. Shevek and Takver have a child, Sadik, and try to remain together while the crisis unfolds. Their attachment becomes another practical problem for an organization that assigns workers by need. Shevek is posted far away. Takver is posted elsewhere. Letters take time, and the family remains separated much longer than either wanted.

Shevek spends harsh stretches of time doing difficult labor away from the scientific institute. He is not physically well suited to all of it, but he works as best he can. When he returns toward academic life, Sabul has made it clear that the Institute does not welcome the path Shevek wants to pursue. Publication and correspondence remain under informal control. Shevek sees a system without legal property in which valuable information and prestige can nevertheless be treated as possessions by the people who occupy important positions.

Eventually Shevek and Takver reunite. Their family grows, and their friends continue discussing how to renew the founding commitments of Anarresti society. Bedap and other dissatisfied people help organize a Syndicate of Initiative, an effort to create new channels of communication, especially with the people of Urras. The plan is controversial. Anarres was settled by revolutionaries leaving Urras behind. Many citizens fear that renewing contact will expose their society to the money, rivalry, and domination they escaped. Others believe that refusing to speak with outsiders is itself a form of imprisonment.

The idea at the center of his work cannot be completed by simply conducting one experiment. Shevek has been trying to reconcile different ways of thinking about succession and simultaneity, ideas that he believes belong in a single account of time. He requires correspondence with other physicists and access to questions that scholars on Urras have explored. The restrictions Sabul placed around letters and books are therefore not minor inconveniences. They change which research can be done, who is permitted to take credit, and whether an important discovery can leave the room in which it was made.

Shevek's research has advanced toward a general theory of time. He believes it may make instantaneous communication across vast distances possible. He wants the work to circulate rather than become the private advantage of any country. But Anarresti authorities and social opinion obstruct his efforts to publish and correspond freely. His daughter notices that other children sometimes treat the family as suspect. Takver sees the costs of the controversy in daily life, while also understanding why Shevek cannot simply abandon the work.

He decides to travel to Urras. It will be a dangerous act of personal initiative, not a diplomatic visit officially approved by everyone on Anarres. He may never be allowed to return. Takver encourages him to go, despite what his departure means for their family. She believes in the project and in Shevek's freedom to pursue it, even when that freedom takes him away from her. They cannot know whether the journey will repair the relationship between the worlds or break their own life apart.

At the port, protestors gather as the ship prepares to leave. Some believe that a man going to Urras is betraying the revolution. Others want to protect his right to cross. Rocks are thrown, and violence erupts. Shevek reaches the ship. For the first time in his life he is leaving the world on which he was born, the world whose people taught him that freedom begins with refusing ownership.

The crossing brings illness as he receives vaccinations. A doctor looks after him, and he begins encountering the social manners of a society organized around paid service, wealth, and institutional rank. When he arrives on Urras, reporters photograph him as a celebrated visitor from Anarres. University officials and politicians welcome him. The planet is green, cultivated, and beautiful in ways he has rarely seen at home. He is given a luxurious private room and a servant who attends to his needs. The contrast with Anarres is overwhelming.

At first the visit seems full of promise. Urrasti physicists can discuss questions he has pursued almost alone, and the university gives him time and materials. Yet he begins to notice who is absent from the conversations: women have no equal place in elite science, ordinary workers remain distant from the privileged campus, and the officials who praise his brilliance also ask when he will finish the theory that brought him here. Shevek has crossed the wall to escape one kind of confinement. He will soon learn what the other world expects in return for its hospitality.

On Urras, the scientific freedom Shevek hoped to find has become entangled with another form of political control.

Shevek lives as a distinguished guest at a university on Urras, the rich and beautiful planet whose people have never stopped regarding the anarchist moon Anarres with suspicion. He can read new scientific work, meet other researchers, and devote himself to the theory of time he has pursued for years. But his hosts want an answer they can possess. A government that controls military power and industrial wealth sees enormous value in a discovery that could permit instantaneous communication across interstellar distances.

The physicist Chifoilisk warns Shevek that the political struggle on Urras extends into the laboratory. The governments of A-Io and Thu are rivals, and neither will treat a powerful theory as a gift to everyone. Other colleagues make the danger clearer in less direct ways. Some praise the superiority of their own civilization and assume that an outsider from Anarres must be grateful to be included in it. Shevek meets people who are kind to him, and people whose friendship is mixed with ambition. He begins to realize that the luxury surrounding him is also a means of keeping him separated from ordinary Urrasti life.

Shevek wants to see how the rest of the planet lives. He speaks with Efor, the servant assigned to him, and gradually hears more about the poor, about people denied the choices that seem effortless to the wealthy, and about the labor that sustains the gardens and rooms he admires. He also becomes aware of a war developing around the state of Benbili. The great powers are prepared to fight by supporting different factions there, far from their own comfortable capitals. The news helps Shevek understand that his scientific discovery would enter a world already skilled at turning ideas into instruments of competition.

As his research approaches completion, his movements become more restricted. Shevek discovers that the government has been treating him less like a free visitor than a valuable resource. He cannot easily leave the university, and the officials who appear concerned for his safety watch whom he meets. His theory is no longer merely something he wants to finish. He must decide who will receive it, and whether a person working inside a state institution can keep such a discovery from becoming state property.

The warning signs come even in social visits. Wealthy families expect servants to remain nearly invisible. Women who are highly intelligent have few opportunities to take part in public scientific life, and Shevek is surprised by the assumptions men and women make about one another. At a party he drinks too much and behaves badly, an experience that shames him when he wakes. Urras is beautiful and generous to someone with his public reputation, but the generosity exists alongside habits of exclusion that become clearer the longer he stays.

He makes contact with people outside the protected university world. Political opponents of the government invite him to speak. Shevek enters a huge public demonstration where workers and activists gather to demand change. He speaks about solidarity across borders and the possibility that people need not accept the institutions that divide them. The authorities move against the crowd. Soldiers fire on protestors, and the scene turns into a massacre. Shevek escapes, injured and shaken, knowing that the government which welcomed him as a famous scientist is capable of slaughtering people demanding the freedom to speak.

He finds refuge at the embassy of Terra, a world linked to others through a wider interstellar civilization. The ambassador, Keng, listens to him. Shevek explains that he came to Urras hoping to build a bridge between divided societies and that he has learned his discovery cannot safely belong to one government. He offers his completed theory freely, so that knowledge of instantaneous communication can circulate beyond the rival states of Urras. The gift is meant to prevent any one authority from owning the future of communication between worlds.

His journey is not finished. Shevek wants to return to Anarres and to Takver, Sadik, and the family he left behind. The Terrans help him arrange passage on a ship not controlled by the governments whose interests he has offended. A Hainish man named Ketho hopes to accompany him, curious to see the anarchist society for himself. Shevek cannot promise that the people at home will welcome them. He knows there may be renewed hostility at the port; the wall has not disappeared because one person crossed it.

The novel ends with the ship approaching Anarres. Shevek has not returned with wealth or victory over the people who opposed him. He returns with a theory shared freely, knowledge of Urras, and the willingness to face the community he criticized. His wife and children are waiting somewhere on the world below. Whether the port will open peacefully is not decided for us. The journey began when Shevek left home to make contact possible; it closes with the hope of coming home without turning that home into a prison.
