# Ficciones I: invented worlds, authors, dreams, chance, libraries, and paths

## Ficciones: Tlön, Uqbar, Orbis Tertius — An Encyclopedia Replaces the World

Suppose that you open an encyclopedia, find an account of a country, and then discover that every other copy of the same encyclopedia has no such country. The ordinary explanations are a printing error, a deliberate fraud, or a prank. In a story by Jorge Luis Borges, the explanation grows so large that the existence of the familiar world begins to seem precarious.

The story is called Tlön, Uqbar, Orbis Tertius. Its narrator is a figure called Borges, a fictionalized version of the author, who is speaking with his friend Adolfo Bioy Casares. They have been discussing mirrors and an observation attributed to a writer from a remote country named Uqbar. The remark is tantalizing. So is the source. They search an encyclopedia and cannot find Uqbar. Then they find a copy with extra pages, in which an article describes the country and says that its literature concerns two imaginary places, including Tlön. The pages cite books and authors that stubbornly refuse to turn up elsewhere. Research, which ought to settle a question, has produced additional questions.

The narrator is fascinated less by the mystery of geography than by the consistency of the invented literary world. Here is a country whose writers write about a place that might be more unreal than their own. Soon another object turns up: a volume of a vast encyclopedia devoted to Tlön. It belonged to Herbert Ashe, an acquaintance who has died. This book does not merely sketch a far-off nation. It supplies a coherent account of a civilization whose languages, philosophy, sciences, and habits of thought have been organized around premises startlingly unlike those of ordinary physical common sense.

In Tlön, reality is treated as mental experience. The inhabitants' languages resist the easy assumption that stable objects exist independently of what people perceive or say about them. One invented language favors impersonal verbs; another assembles qualities rather than naming enduring things. The exact grammatical constructions are deliberately strange. Each language makes certain descriptions feel natural, while its alternatives can expose the assumptions hidden in our own. The narrator reports Tlön's different schools of idealism, and we encounter a world where the philosophical argument is no longer confined to a university classroom.

The encyclopedia relates phenomena in which lost things reappear as duplicates, or in which expectation seems to produce objects. Borges calls such duplicates by a specialized term, hronir. These are not presented as laboratory results established in our world. They belong to a deliberately fabricated order described inside the story. And yet the encyclopedia's precision performs a peculiar magic on its reader. The more details a writer invents, the more those details resemble the documentary evidence by which we ordinarily identify something as real.

A later account, presented as a postscript and dated beyond the initial narration, changes the scale of the affair. A secret intellectual undertaking has reportedly been trying over generations to fabricate an entire alternative world. The original project of an imaginary country becomes a collective creation of Tlön; the extraordinary encyclopedia is a coordinated artifact rather than a miraculous chance discovery. Its planners do not merely invent marvelous myths. They assign their specialists the labor of producing a convincing history, physics, grammar, metaphysics, and mathematics. The result has enough internal connections to compete with any account of the existing world.

Then comes the invasion. Objects associated with Tlön appear in the narrator's surroundings. The world begins to use the invented language and admire the invented coherence. Schooling and scholarship turn toward the new encyclopedia. The narrator imagines a future in which the familiar historical record will be revised or displaced. People are susceptible not simply to a lie but to the relief of a beautifully ordered explanation. The dazzling completeness of Tlön offers an escape from a contingent, untidy reality.

And what does the narrator do? He does not lead a campaign to defend the facts. Near the end, he describes continuing an obscure translation of an English writer. This response can sound like resignation, aesthetic withdrawal, or a modest resistance to a universal program. We are not given a final, external voice that certifies exactly what has invaded what. We have the narrator's account, its documents, its dated postscript, and the peculiar experience of becoming unsure where his fiction stops.

To read the story only as a clever parable about idealism is to miss its political pressure. A coordinated institution manufactures an apparently self-sufficient picture of reality, and people find it more compelling than imperfect evidence. To read it only as propaganda allegory is equally limiting. Borges also asks us to notice the work a writer and a reader perform together: a few improbable pages inside a book can reorganize how we imagine everything outside it.

The story leaves us with a question more difficult than whether Tlön is true. If reality depends partly on the systems in which we describe it, how can we recognize a system that has begun to replace rather than explain the world?

## Ficciones: Pierre Menard — A Book Written Twice

Imagine reading a memorial essay about a recently deceased writer. The essayist wants to restore the writer's reputation, rescue neglected work from vulgar misunderstandings, and establish the greatness of an almost impossibly ambitious project. The title promises an author of Don Quixote. But the author's name is not Miguel de Cervantes. It is Pierre Menard.

Borges constructs the entire story as an account of Menard's intellectual career. The narrator catalogs the visible publications: scholarly articles, experiments in versification, and learned contributions that imply an active, cultivated literary life. Another observer has supposedly misrepresented the list. The tone is that of the jealous defender of a literary reputation, complete with social names and carefully judged praise. This is already comic, because the narrator's conviction that he is being scrupulously fair makes his snobbery increasingly visible.

Then he turns from the public works to the private undertaking. Menard has been writing passages of Don Quixote. That statement might suggest a modern translation, an adaptation, a literary hoax, or even a transcription. The narrator rejects all of these. Menard's aim is to create certain passages that are verbally identical to Cervantes's original. He does not wish to copy them as an exercise or to simulate a lost manuscript. He wants to arrive at exactly the same words through a separate act of authorship, as a French writer living centuries after Cervantes.

There are two possible methods. One is to become Cervantes by reconstructing his language, worldview, and historical conditions. This seems promising in theory and nearly impossible in practice. More important, Menard rejects it as too easy, a kind of theatrical impersonation. The harder method is to remain Pierre Menard: to have his own life, to inhabit the modern world, and nevertheless to write a portion of the seventeenth-century novel word for word. The project's apparent absurdity is also its precision. Menard is not trying to improve the words. He is testing what it would mean for the same sentence to be produced by two different histories.

The narrator then performs the experiment in reading. He places the two versions side by side and insists that the modern one is richer. The letters have not changed. What has changed is the entire field of associations the narrator brings to them. An attitude to history that seems unsurprising in Cervantes is suddenly interpreted as startling when attributed to a twentieth-century writer familiar with modern philosophy. Another passage acquires an ironic or philosophical weight from the fact that it appears to be the deliberate choice of a later author. The comedy comes from a reviewer whose confidence increases as his evidence for textual difference dwindles to zero.

The joke is not that words mean nothing. It is that the same material words can enter a different conversation. A reader who knows the supposed date, country, and intellectual history of an author reads differently from a reader who knows none of those things. The narrator's excesses can be ridiculous; the underlying question remains serious. We rarely encounter a book as a naked string of sentences. We meet it inside a story about its making, its author, and its audience.

At the end, the narrator recommends an audacious technique: reading an earlier book as though a later writer had written it. The technique invents relations between works that chronology seems to prohibit. It can be an exercise in insight or a mechanism of wishful projection. Borges does not rescue us by providing a sober critic who settles the dispute. The ridiculous admirer has discovered a genuine pressure point in literary scholarship.

The story appeared in 1939, and its form mattered. Instead of giving Menard a conventional dramatic scene, Borges writes a scholarly obituary for an invented achievement and trusts that the apparent apparatus of serious criticism will make the fiction possible. We can laugh at its absurdity without ignoring what it teaches. The act of writing may end, but a text does not finish acquiring histories when its first author puts down the pen.

## Ficciones: The Circular Ruins — The Dreamer Who Is Dreamed

A man arrives by water at an abandoned circular temple. He has barely any past that matters to him. His intention is clear and immense: he wants to dream another person into existence. Not imagine a character in the familiar sense, and not merely see a face in sleep. He wants a being who can walk and be mistaken for flesh and blood.

The temple is ruined, remote, and associated with an old divinity. Local people bring the stranger food. He spends his days sleeping and trying to control his dreams. The first dreams take the form of a classroom. He lectures to a multitude of students, searching for someone sufficiently independent to become the center of his work. He favors those who answer with intelligence rather than merely repeating the teacher's thoughts. At last he chooses one figure, dismisses the rest, and tries to make the selected dreamer grow into a complete person.

The effort fails. A period of insomnia ruins his project. He must begin again, this time working slowly and physically. He dreams a heart, then the rest of a body, attending to detail until the young man seems fully composed. Yet the body cannot quite be awakened by the dreamer's power alone. In a dream the old deity of the ruined temple appears and identifies himself with fire. The god promises to animate the created person so that the people of the world will take him for a real man. Only the fire god and the creator will know the secret.

There is a condition: the new human being must be instructed in the god's rites and eventually sent to a distant temple. The dreamer obeys. For years he teaches the youth, privately grieving the separation he must one day arrange. Before sending him away he removes his awareness of the origin of his life. This looks at first like an act of kindness. To discover that one's father had made one in a dream might be a terrible humiliation. But the erasure also gives the creator control over what his creation can know.

Time passes. The man is told of a remarkable person at another temple, someone who can walk through flames without injury. He understands that the report concerns his imagined son. He is afraid that this immunity will reveal the truth. The son could learn not merely that he is protected by fire but that his place in reality differs from everyone else's. The secret the creator most fears is the one he has built his entire life around keeping.

Then fire reaches the creator's own ruins. He expects destruction. Instead, the flames leave him untouched. He understands, in a moment of relief and terror, that he too exists as another being's dream. The revelation reverses the relationship that organized the story. His power over a dream was real within his experience, but his understanding of its source was incomplete. What he thought made him different from his son has proven to be a difference of position, not of kind.

The ending offers a temptation: imagine an infinite chain of dreamers, one created by another without a final beginning. But the story itself need not prove that the regress is infinite. It tells us the creator has discovered a predecessor and leaves the rest open. Borges makes an ontological problem personal. To know oneself as made is not only a logical embarrassment. It changes the meaning of memory, pride, the wish to protect another, and the fear of being exposed.

There is also a literary mirror. Every fictional person exists in a story imagined by someone else. But this observation alone does not exhaust the plot. If we treat the young man's distress as an empty joke because he is fictional, we would have to treat the dreamer's distress in the same way, and perhaps our own feelings for them. The story's force depends on our granting a character enough reality to care whether his reality can be taken away.

The circular shape becomes apt at last: a maker and a made being turn out to occupy the same structure. A god who can dream a person is also a person in someone else's dream. Borges makes the final sentence change the meaning of everything preceding it, including the reader's confidence in the voice that told the story.

## Ficciones: The Lottery in Babylon and Herbert Quain — Chance and Invented Books

Two stories in the first half of Ficciones begin with forms that are not supposed to behave like ordinary fiction. One is a report about the social history of a lottery. The other is a critical survey of a dead novelist's works. In both, the real plot is hidden in the logic of the system being described.

In The Lottery in Babylon, a speaker describes a city where almost every status and event may be assigned by chance. It was not always so. The lottery began as a familiar gamble, offering prizes in exchange for tickets. That seemed too weak to compel the attention of an entire society. Penalties entered the drawing: not every ticket would bring money; some might bring loss, disgrace, or punishment. At first the punishments were enforceable in recognizable ways. Later the machinery became more elaborate. A mysterious body called the Company gained authority over outcomes and began to determine, or appear to determine, more and more of what happened.

The narrator describes lotteries nested inside other lotteries. One drawing might select the sort of consequence that a later drawing would specify. An unfavorable result could itself be modified by another random procedure. At some point the difference between an extraordinary coincidence and an officially planned event becomes impossible to prove. Citizens no longer know whether a promotion, an arrest, a romantic encounter, or an accident is part of the lottery. The Company grows more secret as its alleged reach grows more complete.

Does the Company exist? The narrator does not offer evidence strong enough to settle the question. It might be an administrative power whose decisions shape every life. It might be a name people use to give senseless events a secret author. There may be no intelligible center at all. The story is disturbing because random outcomes and conspiratorial explanations begin to do the same work. Both can relieve a person of the burden of asking who made a decision, and both can make responsibility disappear.

The next object is a book review, or rather a review of several books. An Examination of the Work of Herbert Quain describes a novelist whose formally adventurous works appear to exist only inside Borges's invented criticism. One novel is a detective puzzle whose solution seems to embarrass the reader, since the apparent answer may conceal a better one. Another experiments with branching narrative in reverse. The reader begins at a consequence and moves backward into mutually incompatible pasts. A theatrical experiment uses a layered structure in which what counts as drama or reality changes. A book of short pieces offers material that another writer might borrow.

The fictional critic takes the works seriously enough to explain their structures and judge their shortcomings. Yet the reader cannot go to a shelf and check whether Quain's achievement has been represented fairly. We must reconstruct the nonexistent books from the critic's report. What at first seems like a shortcut around writing a novel becomes a demanding exercise for the imagination. We are asked to make books out of the descriptions of books.

The two pieces are especially revealing when paired. Babylon imagines a social order in which every event can be attributed to a hidden system. Quain imagines books in which the reader's search for form becomes part of the construction of the work. One turns chance into authority; the other turns literary authority into a possibility that never becomes a finished object. Both ask what happens when the institutions that supposedly explain our experience become opaque.

Borges also refuses to confuse sophistication with benevolence. A labyrinthine system can be elegant and cruel. A branching story can be ingenious and emotionally empty. When the narrator of the Quain survey gives a verdict, the verdict remains an opinion, not a law of aesthetics. We can admire a fictional novelist's inventions and still ask whether their designs make us see any person more clearly.

These are not plot summaries of books Borges secretly wrote and later hid. The books are fictional artifacts. Their absence is part of the technique. By offering what looks like reference material, Borges makes the reader an involuntary collaborator in a library that does not exist. And by describing a lottery that seems to allocate all reality, he asks us to consider whether the desire for a complete explanation can be more oppressive than mere chance.

## Ficciones: The Library of Babel — Every Book and No Answer

Picture a universe made of rooms. Each room is hexagonal, with shelves of books on several walls, a passage to neighboring spaces, and the stairways by which inhabitants travel between levels. To its people this building is not a library somewhere in the world. It is the world. The narrator who describes it has spent a lifetime moving through its galleries and trying to understand its order.

The books look uniform in size. Each contains a fixed number of pages, lines, and characters. The alphabet of possible printed signs is small. But the arrangement of those signs varies. Most volumes appear to consist of unbroken nonsense; others contain a fragment of a sentence, a word, or an apparent sequence of meaningful statements before falling back into meaningless combinations. The great proposition is that the collection contains every possible book that can be formed from its finite alphabet at the prescribed length.

There is a mathematical distinction worth making. The number of distinct possible texts of a fixed length over a finite alphabet is enormous but finite. A library that stretches without spatial end might contain endlessly repeated copies of those possible texts. Borges's narrator speculates about infinity, periodic order, and the distribution of books without having a vantage point outside the building. The story's anguish depends less on the geometry than on the collision between a complete combinatorial inventory and the human desire for the right page.

If every possible book exists, the library contains a book that accurately predicts your life, or seems to. It also contains more books falsely predicting the same life, books denying the accurate prediction, books differing from the truth by one letter, and books written in languages no living person has learned. A true sentence cannot identify itself merely by appearing on a page. Without a way to distinguish reliable evidence from arbitrary combinations, possession of all possible statements does not amount to knowledge.

The inhabitants respond with different kinds of hope and violence. Some seek the catalogs that will identify the books worth reading. Others believe there must be a volume that justifies every person's existence. Some search for a single master book, or a reader who has found it. Groups pursue rival theories, destroy books they judge worthless, or quarrel over whether meaningless marks conceal messages in unknown languages. The institution offers abundance so complete that scarcity returns in another form: the scarcity of trustworthy orientation, time, attention, and company.

The narrator has his own convictions. He regards the library as an ordered system; he wants to believe its forms are sufficient, even if the order is not available to him. Yet the structure of his account invites us to question the certainty of his conclusions. He has not inspected every room. No one could. His faith in a total design is inseparable from the tiny sample that any mortal explorer can see.

A reader with modern computational habits might think of exhaustive search, information theory, or vast databases. Those analogies are useful if they remain analogies. The Library of Babel does not provide a practical search engine, nor does Borges claim to have anticipated every later technology. It asks a more basic question: does adding every possible answer solve a problem when we cannot identify which answer corresponds to our actual situation?

There is an ethical dimension as well. The library's seekers are tired, frightened, and often cruel. The search for an explanation of one's life can generate persecution of people whose ways of reading differ. The narrator's tone is elegiac, not merely triumphant at a clever mathematical construction. An infinite environment can produce intense provincialism; a universal library can become the scene of destructive sectarian disputes.

Imagine finding a book that begins with an exact account of your childhood. Does that prove the rest is true? In a complete library, it does not. For any chosen continuation, other books will preserve the same beginning and then diverge. A person must still acquire reasons, evaluate witnesses, compare claims, and live with uncertainty. The great philosophical joke is a human tragedy: the library contains everything its residents say they want, but may not contain any accessible method for knowing they have found it.

## Ficciones: The Garden of Forking Paths — A Murder Hidden in a Theory of Time

A spy is running out of time in wartime England. His name is Yu Tsun. He is Chinese, has taught English, and is working for German intelligence during the First World War. He has discovered the location of a British military concentration, but his contact has been discovered. Captain Richard Madden is closing in. Yu Tsun must send the secret to his German superior without access to an ordinary communication channel.

We first encounter this account through an editorial frame that relates it to a passage in a military history. The frame suggests that a puzzling interruption in a British offensive might have a hidden explanation. We then enter Yu Tsun's statement. Already the history of a battle and the memory of one individual seem to be competing forms of evidence.

Yu Tsun is not a simple patriot in reverse. He does not admire the German regime he serves. He resents the contempt of the intelligence chief who regards him through a racist hierarchy. He wants to prove that he can accomplish a task the chief believes beyond him. This motive does not excuse the acts that follow. It makes the acts harder to flatten into a story of national loyalty.

With Madden approaching, Yu Tsun takes a train to reach a man named Stephen Albert. We do not yet know why Albert matters. The journey becomes increasingly tense: timetables, platforms, trains, the uncertainty of pursuit. At Albert's house, something unexpected happens. The host is a scholar of Chinese culture who recognizes Yu Tsun's family name and begins speaking about an ancestor of his, Ts'ui Pên.

Years earlier this ancestor had abandoned official duties to write a vast novel and construct an enormous labyrinth. The surviving novel seemed chaotic, with mutually contradictory episodes and characters who died in one passage but lived in another. No physical labyrinth was found. Albert has come to believe the two tasks were one. The novel is the labyrinth. Its structure does not unfold a single history in which an event excludes every incompatible possibility. It presents time as branching into differing futures, some of which may later converge.

Albert's reconstruction is more than a conceptual lecture to Yu Tsun. It is a generous recognition of his ancestor's achievement. A project the family had regarded as failure acquires an intelligible design. For a moment the desperate fugitive finds himself listening to someone who has taken his inherited culture seriously. The two men imagine what it could mean for alternative outcomes to coexist. Different versions of their meeting might contain friendship, hostility, or no meeting at all.

Then the trap in the story's title turns. Madden approaches. Yu Tsun asks Albert to turn toward a document, draws his pistol, and shoots him. He is arrested. The reader finally learns why the innocent scholar was chosen. Albert is not merely a man's surname; it is the name of the French town that identifies the British military target. Yu Tsun counts on English newspapers to report the murder of Stephen Albert. His intelligence chief will see the name and understand. The plan works. He sends the message by causing a death that journalists cannot ignore.

The conclusion does not celebrate the strategy. Yu Tsun acknowledges his horror and exhaustion. This is not a story in which a philosophical picture of multiple futures dissolves the moral responsibility of a choice. Albert might have lived in another imagined history; in this one Yu Tsun killed him. The relation between all possible paths and the path actually taken is the story's deepest wound.

It is tempting to describe the tale as a modern scientific theory of branching universes. That is a comparison a later reader may make, but it is not an account of Borges deriving fiction from subsequent physics. The invention belongs first to a constructed novel inside an espionage narrative. Form and plot are inseparable: we are denied one piece of information, offered another kind of explanation, and finally made to see that the mystery of the murder and the mystery of the labyrinth have been wound around each other.

The garden has no single image we can safely hold as the definitive world. What remains certain is that a human being who has imagined many futures still has to answer for what he did in one.
