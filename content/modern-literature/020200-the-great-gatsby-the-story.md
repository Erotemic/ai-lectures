# The Great Gatsby — The Story

An original, complete, spoiler-containing narrative orientation to F. Scott Fitzgerald's 1925 novel. This follows the novel as Nick Carraway tells it, including the revelation of Gatsby's past, the road accident, and the funeral. Critical discussion belongs to `021000`.

## The house across the water

In the spring of 1922, a young man named Nick Carraway moves east to learn the bond business. He comes from a comfortable family in the American Midwest. He has been to university, fought in the Great War, and returned to a life that now feels too small. New York promises movement, ambition, and a profession. He rents a modest house on Long Island, in a place called West Egg.

The name sounds quaint, but West Egg is very rich. Its enormous houses have mostly been built or bought by people whose money is comparatively new. Across a narrow stretch of water lies East Egg, where the older fortunes are gathered. Nick's house sits beside a mansion so extravagant that it makes his own place look temporary. The mansion belongs to a man Nick has not yet met: Jay Gatsby.

Nick's cousin Daisy lives across the bay in East Egg, with her husband, Tom Buchanan. Tom comes from an established wealthy family and was once a celebrated college athlete. He is strong, restless, contemptuous, and accustomed to treating other people's wishes as obstacles. Daisy is charming, quick to laugh, and harder to read than her gaiety suggests. Their young daughter is cared for mostly out of sight. At dinner Nick meets Daisy's friend Jordan Baker, a professional golfer who carries herself with an air of cool independence.

The evening is not tranquil. Tom announces his belief in racist theories about the future of white civilization, as if his fear deserves the authority of scholarship. A phone rings, drawing him away. Jordan quietly makes Nick understand what is happening. Tom has a mistress in New York, and Daisy knows it. Tom scarcely tries to conceal the affair. Daisy flirts and jokes, yet there are moments when she seems trapped inside the pleasant performance of her life.

That night, home again, Nick sees a figure on the shore of the neighboring estate. Gatsby stands alone and reaches his arms toward something across the dark water. Nick looks in the same direction and notices a small green light at the end of a dock. It belongs to the Buchanan property. He does not yet know why his neighbor is watching it.

## The other household

Tom soon draws Nick into another part of his life. On the railway route between the wealthy Eggs and Manhattan there is a bleak industrial district where ash from the city has accumulated. A garage stands beside the road. Its owner, George Wilson, struggles to make a living repairing and selling cars. George is tired and anxious. His wife, Myrtle, has ambitions and appetites that their marriage cannot satisfy.

Myrtle is Tom's lover. Tom stops at the garage and later takes Myrtle and Nick to an apartment he keeps in New York. Myrtle acts as though stepping through the apartment door has made her part of another social class. She buys things, changes clothes, invites company, and talks loudly about people she considers beneath her. Tom pays for this second household but treats Myrtle no more gently than he treats Daisy. When Myrtle repeatedly speaks Daisy's name against his wishes, Tom strikes her and breaks her nose. Nick leaves the party drunk and disturbed. The people around Tom can be hurt with very little consequence for him.

Back on Long Island, Nick begins to understand how little he knows about the owner of the mansion next door. Every weekend, music and dancing spill through Gatsby's gardens. Lights blaze. Cars carry strangers to the gates. Drinks circulate and dinner is prepared for crowds, many of whom have never met the host. People invent explanations for his fortune. Some say he was a spy; some say a murderer. They agree mostly that he must be hiding something.

Nick receives an invitation, which sets him apart from many guests who simply arrive. At the party he wanders among the rooms with Jordan, encountering gossip and spectacle. In a library, a drunken guest is astonished to discover that the grand shelves contain real books. He has evidently expected a theatrical façade. Later Nick talks to a courteous young man who remembers serving in the war. Only after several minutes does Nick realize he is speaking with Gatsby himself.

Gatsby has an attentive smile and a manner that makes people feel briefly important. He is not the swaggering figure Nick expected. He disappears into business and private conversations as his guests continue celebrating. Jordan spends some time alone with him and returns with news she is not ready to share. For Nick, the mystery of Gatsby grows more interesting than the parties.

Nick also grows closer to Jordan. She is attractive, successful, and, he suspects, willing to bend the truth when it suits her. Their connection is never as consuming as the relationships that will soon surround them. At first, the summer still seems full of opportunities to choose a different life.

## A man who has arranged his own history

Gatsby begins cultivating Nick's friendship. One day he drives him into Manhattan in a luxurious car and offers a story about his past. He says that he came from an affluent family, was educated at Oxford, traveled widely, and earned military honors. His tale seems polished enough to be memorized. When Nick doubts it, Gatsby produces a photograph and a decoration from the war, apparently to show that some parts are real.

They lunch with Meyer Wolfshiem, a businessman connected to gambling and organized crime. Gatsby speaks of him with admiration, and Nick discovers that Wolfshiem is reputed to have helped fix the 1919 World Series. Gatsby's fortune is beginning to look less like an ordinary success story. But he has not invited Nick along merely to explain his finances.

Jordan eventually reveals the missing connection. Years earlier, when Daisy was a young woman living in Louisville, she met an army officer named Jay Gatsby. They fell intensely in love before he left for the war. Gatsby had little money, though Daisy did not fully understand how little. Their relationship promised a future that could not yet be claimed. While Gatsby was away, Daisy accepted the courtship of Tom Buchanan, whose wealth and social position offered something immediate and secure. Before the wedding she received a letter that upset her badly. Nevertheless, she married Tom. They began their wealthy, unsettled married life, and Gatsby was left outside it.

Gatsby's mansion was not purchased by chance. He moved to West Egg because Daisy lived across the bay. The huge parties had a purpose too: he hoped she would appear at one, or at least hear about him. He knows that Nick is her cousin. Through Jordan he makes a request. Would Nick invite Daisy to tea, without telling her Gatsby will be there?

Nick agrees. Gatsby responds as though the future depends on the arrangement of a single afternoon. He has Nick's lawn attended to and fills the little house with flowers. On the chosen day it rains. Gatsby arrives in a state of misery, convinced that the plan is foolish and that Daisy will not want to see him. When Daisy enters, the meeting is painfully awkward. Gatsby almost knocks over a clock while attempting to appear at ease. Nick leaves them alone for a while.

When he returns, everything has changed. Gatsby is radiant; Daisy is emotional and deeply attentive. The embarrassment has given way to a reunion in which five lost years seem suddenly present. The three walk to Gatsby's house, where he shows Daisy the rooms, the grounds, the expensive objects, the clothes he has accumulated. At one point Daisy begins to cry over his magnificent shirts. Her reaction seems to include more than admiration for fabric: all the choices, time, and distances that have gathered between them are difficult to separate.

Gatsby has not simply found Daisy again. He believes he has found the life from which he was excluded. Nick understands that the achievement may be less satisfying than the image Gatsby has spent years building. No real woman can precisely match a dream rehearsed for that long. For the moment, however, Gatsby appears happier than Nick has ever seen him.

## James Gatz

As the summer continues, Nick learns more about his neighbor's real history. Jay Gatsby was born James Gatz, the son of poor farmers in North Dakota. As a teenager he refused to imagine that his parents' life had to be his own. He gave himself a different name before anyone else had given him permission to change his social position.

A wealthy man named Dan Cody once anchored a yacht near the shore where young James was working. James warned him about a coming storm and entered his service. Traveling with Cody exposed him to the habits, luxuries, and disasters of the rich. Cody meant to leave him money, but after Cody's death Gatsby did not receive the inheritance as he had expected. What he retained was an education in how wealth looked and behaved, and a determination to acquire it himself.

Gatsby's story of becoming a gentleman is partly invention and partly an effort to make invention true. He has participated in dubious business with Wolfshiem, including schemes involving illegal liquor during Prohibition. Yet the money is not simply an end in itself. He has treated it as a bridge to Daisy, who belongs to a world where people may recognize a fortune while continuing to reject the person who made it.

Tom and Daisy attend one of Gatsby's parties. Daisy does not enjoy it. The crowds strike her as vulgar, and the atmosphere exposes the difference between Gatsby's recent wealth and the social ease of her own circle. Tom becomes suspicious of Gatsby's origins. Gatsby is upset that Daisy has not admired the scene he built to attract her. He tells Nick, in effect, that the point is not merely to have Daisy leave Tom now. He wants her to declare that she never loved Tom at all. He wants the intervening years erased.

Nick objects that the past cannot simply be restored. Gatsby cannot accept this. To him, the past is not gone; it is a task that money and willpower may yet accomplish.

## The hottest day

The crisis comes on an oppressive summer day. Nick visits the Buchanans, where Gatsby is also present, and Daisy's manner toward him makes their intimacy difficult to disguise. Tom begins to understand the affair. The group decides to travel into New York, as though leaving their houses might ease the pressure. Instead, the heat gathers around them in the cars and follows them indoors.

They exchange vehicles for the journey. Tom drives Gatsby's conspicuous yellow car toward Manhattan, with Nick and Jordan; Gatsby rides with Daisy in Tom's car. They stop at George Wilson's garage, where Tom learns that George has discovered his wife has been unfaithful, though George does not know her lover's identity. George plans to take Myrtle away. Myrtle sees Tom through an upstairs window and misreads the scene, believing Jordan is the woman for whom Tom might abandon her. Her jealousy and desperation are now directed toward people who barely know she exists.

In a suite at the Plaza Hotel, the group tries to endure the heat and an increasingly unbearable conversation. Tom confronts Gatsby about his relationship with Daisy. Gatsby refuses to remain a secret lover. He demands that Daisy tell Tom she never loved him, and that she is leaving him for the man she loved first.

Daisy cannot say it. She loved Gatsby, but she has loved Tom at some point too. Her marriage has a history, a child, a social world, and habits of dependence that Gatsby's demand cannot cancel. Tom produces what he has discovered about Gatsby's criminal money. He does not need to establish himself as a better husband; he needs only to remind Daisy that Gatsby does not belong to their class and that his security is fragile. Gatsby continues insisting that Daisy choose the version of their past he has prepared for her. Daisy retreats emotionally, and Tom sees that he has regained control.

The outing collapses. Tom sends Daisy home with Gatsby in the yellow car, showing that he no longer regards Gatsby as a serious threat. Tom returns later with Nick and Jordan in the other vehicle.

On the road back, tragedy has already occurred. Myrtle Wilson is dead. A fast-moving yellow car struck her near the garage and did not stop. George is shattered, and the roadside is crowded with people trying to establish what happened. Tom knows the yellow car belongs to Gatsby. He had driven it past the garage earlier, where Myrtle could have seen him. She appears to have rushed into the road believing Tom was approaching. The car that killed her was being driven by someone else.

Nick later finds Gatsby waiting outside the Buchanans' house. Gatsby tells him what happened: Daisy was driving. Myrtle ran toward the car, and Daisy did not stop in time. Gatsby says he will take responsibility. He has positioned himself near the house to protect Daisy in case Tom turns violent. Nick looks through a window and sees Tom and Daisy seated together in the kitchen. They are not visibly reconciled by tenderness, but they share the wary intimacy of two people making decisions about what to do next. Gatsby waits outside, believing he is guarding Daisy. Inside, she and Tom are drawing together.

## The last morning

Early the next day, Gatsby tells Nick more about the beginning of his love for Daisy. He had been an officer with charm and borrowed prestige when he first entered her house. He had understood the gap between her family's wealth and his own prospects. He hoped that the chance to love her might somehow make him worthy of the world she inhabited. When he had to leave, the relationship became a promise sustained by letters, memory, and finally a fortune.

Nick is supposed to go to work. Before leaving he offers Gatsby a rare and heartfelt expression of loyalty. Gatsby's guests have not earned his admiration, and Nick increasingly understands that Gatsby has been used by people who do not intend to stand beside him. Gatsby remains at home and waits for a phone call from Daisy. The gardener suggests draining the swimming pool before autumn. Gatsby decides to use it once first. The invitation to enjoy the pool is not accompanied by any sense that the party has ended; he is still suspended in expectation.

At the garage, George Wilson has been trying to comprehend his wife's death and her affair. He is convinced the man in the yellow car both killed her and had been her lover. Tom tells George who owned the car. George follows that information to Gatsby's estate.

Gatsby is in his swimming pool when George arrives. George shoots him and then takes his own life. The murderer has killed the wrong man for the affair he imagined, and Daisy, who was actually driving the car, has escaped his reach entirely.

Nick comes to the house and finds that Gatsby's life of noise and guests has ended in near silence. The police and reporters arrive. People who once consumed Gatsby's hospitality now produce stories about his death, many as unreliable as the rumors they spread while he lived. Nick tries to contact Daisy, but the Buchanans have left without giving him a forwarding address. Wolfshiem does not want involvement. Guests who crowded the garden are suddenly unavailable.

Gatsby's father, Henry Gatz, travels from the Midwest for the funeral. His pride in his son survives the scandal and death. He brings a childhood record of James's efforts to improve himself: plans to study, work, exercise, and become a more capable person. Nick sees that Gatsby's reinvention began long before the mansion and the parties. The father has lost his son, but also carries the tenderness of a parent who once believed a boy's future would be extraordinary.

The funeral is scarcely attended. Nick is there; Gatsby's father is there; a few servants and an earlier party guest known for his enormous spectacles appear. Daisy does not come. The contrast between the multitude who enjoyed Gatsby's wealth and the handful who acknowledge his death is complete.

## Returning west

Nick's summer has become impossible to remember as an adventure. He ends his uneasy relationship with Jordan. He meets Tom again and learns that Tom told George Wilson the yellow car belonged to Gatsby. Tom regards his action as justified because he believes Gatsby deserved punishment. He does not acknowledge that Daisy was the driver or that his own affair with Myrtle had helped create the disaster. Nick sees how easily Tom and Daisy can retreat into their money, leaving others to face the wreckage of their choices.

Nick decides to leave New York and return to the Midwest. Before going, he visits the deserted shore near Gatsby's house. Across the bay, the place where the green light had shone is still there. Gatsby's dream was directed toward a real woman but also toward an imagined future: a moment in which nothing he had lost would remain lost. The dream depended on believing that enough desire could close the distance between what had happened and what should have happened.

Nick tells the story after the fact, from the distance of the place he came from. His account has given us the bright party rooms, the quiet cottage where Daisy and Gatsby met, the valley of ashes, the hotel in the heat, and the nearly empty funeral. It leaves Gatsby dead, Daisy and Tom gone, George and Myrtle Wilson dead, and Nick at last unwilling to make the same journey again. He has learned the names and fortunes of everyone involved. What remains hardest to settle is why he still feels affection for the man whose immense hope helped destroy him.

That is the end of The Great Gatsby's story. It is also the beginning of the question the novel leaves with its reader: whose version of this American summer have we actually heard?
