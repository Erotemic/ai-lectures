# Dream of the Red Chamber — Story I: The Stone and the Garden

A whole-novel overview, first half. This narrative uses the received 120-chapter *Honglou meng* as its continuity, with the pre-80 textual history discussed explicitly in the companion and in the analysis. Scene selection emphasizes the first fifty-four chapters, not a chapter-by-chapter retelling. The novel has also circulated as *The Story of the Stone*.

The boy comes into the world carrying something nobody can explain. When the attendants examine the newborn child of a great family, they find a piece of jade in his mouth. He has not acquired it by inheritance, and no one has put it there. It has arrived with him. His name will be Baoyu, Precious Jade, and for the rest of his youth the stone will be treated as a sign that his life is destined for something extraordinary. In his own mind it will be a burden as often as a treasure.

But the book begins earlier, before there is a boy or a family. In a remote age the goddess Nüwa repairs the broken sky, using stones of tremendous power. One stone is left unused. It sits on a mountain and learns to regret the purpose for which it was never chosen. A Buddhist monk and a Daoist take it down into the world of human attachments so that it may discover what it means to live among them. When it returns, its adventures are inscribed upon it. That account is what we are about to hear.

There is another beginning beside this one. A magical flower has flourished through the care of a kindly attendant, who gives it the water it needs. The flower owes him a debt it cannot pay with more water, so it will repay him in tears when the two are born as human beings. In the world of the Jia family, the attendant will be Baoyu, and the flower will be Lin Daiyu. The story places the beginning of their love in an obligation that the lovers do not remember but cannot escape. To be moved by someone, this book suggests, is not necessarily to be made happy by them. Affection may be an account opened before either person knew what it would cost.

Now imagine two great households standing side by side in the capital: the Rongguo and Ningguo branches of the Jia clan. Their ancestors won rank through military service; the family now lives upon inherited offices, court connections, a vast staff of servants, income from lands, and the belief that wealth on such a scale can renew itself indefinitely. Behind the gates are courtyards within courtyards. Senior women command domestic life, while men possess the formal titles. Household managers must answer for expenses, tempers, ceremonial obligations, visiting relatives, weddings, funerals, and the small daily disasters that accompany the care of hundreds of people. Whatever the rulers of the empire do, here inside the walls a thousand lesser orders must be issued every day.

The family's center of warmth is Grandmother Jia, an old woman whose preference can silence almost anyone. She adores her grandson Baoyu. His father, Jia Zheng, would prefer a son who applies himself to the Confucian classics and prepares for official examination success. Baoyu is clever, but the official career expected of him seems to him like an elaborate way of ceasing to be alive. He prefers the company of the young women of the household, their jokes, their poems, and their way of knowing what has happened to one another before any man bothers to ask. He can be vain and careless, and he benefits from the work of servants he does not always understand; nevertheless, his resistance to the respectable path is sincere.

When the young Lin Daiyu comes to stay with the Jias, she has recently lost her mother. Later she will lose her father as well. She has talent, pride, delicate health, and a manner sharpened by the knowledge that she is a guest dependent upon relatives. Her arrival is a scene of looking: Daiyu notes who sits where, who speaks first, which person must be obeyed, and how much emotion a girl can reveal without becoming an inconvenience. Grandmother Jia welcomes her. Baoyu, meeting this cousin, feels that she is somehow already known to him. In the logic of their unremembered earlier existence, he is right.

Their first conversation is also a small disaster. When Baoyu learns that Daiyu possesses no miraculous jade, he is furious at his own. Why, he demands in effect, should a silly object mark him as special when this person who seems so extraordinary has none? He throws it away. The elders are horrified; Daiyu has scarcely arrived and already she appears to be the occasion for a family crisis. Baoyu will lose and recover the jade again during the novel. The object that supposedly guarantees his worth repeatedly fails to guarantee his peace.

Another cousin arrives in the household with her family: Xue Baochai. She is composed where Daiyu is volatile, gracious where Daiyu is defensive, and good at understanding the demands a household places upon a marriageable girl. The Xues are wealthy, and Baochai wears a gold ornament whose inscription seems to answer the inscription on Baoyu's jade. Some in the household begin to speak of an auspicious pairing between gold and jade. For Baochai, this is not a simple fairy-tale promise. Her self-command is labor; it involves the steady suppression of impulses that would make life more difficult for herself and those around her. Baoyu may be attracted to her steadiness, but the intimacy he has with Daiyu feels harder to classify. The very qualities that make Baochai a respectable prospect for marriage make the two cousins seem to belong to different kinds of future.

We must also enter the larger city. The Xue family did not come only to visit. Its affairs include an appalling act of violence: Baochai's brother Xue Pan is implicated in the killing of a man during a dispute over a young woman who had been trafficked and sold. That young woman was once the lost daughter of Zhen Shiyin, the man whose encounter with the wandering monk and Daoist helped open the novel. She is now known as Xiangling. An official, Jia Yucun, knows enough of the case to understand it, but takes the course that serves powerful connections. The affair is quietly redirected. The book wants us to understand this while the household still seems prosperous: the Jia circle can evade consequences long before it can evade ruin. The costs of its privilege are paid first by people whose names the great family can afford to forget.

Baoyu, meanwhile, is taken into a dream of another kind. Sleeping in the chamber of his alluring older relation Qin Keqing, he enters the Land of Illusion, a place where beautiful young women and a strange presiding spirit offer him a vision of fate. He sees an album of portraits and judgments concerning the women of Jinling, the city associated with the family's world. Their futures appear in images and verses: mourning, frustrated love, exile, death, a life damaged by an ill-chosen marriage. Then songs give the same warning in another form. Baoyu does not know how to read it. He is an adolescent, not a prophet. If told that the people he loves will someday be scattered, what could he do with the information? The reader, however, has been warned. This is not a story whose disaster has to wait until the last page to be announced.

At the heart of the household sits an extraordinary administrator: Wang Xifeng, Baoyu's older cousin by marriage. She is sparkling, quick, theatrical, able to deal with a dozen petitioners while noticing which one has concealed a demand. Her husband Jia Lian is less disciplined and less careful about his appetites. Xifeng must make the household machine work, and she relishes her command of it. She can also use its power cruelly. She engineers humiliation, controls access to money, and pursues a private interest even when another person may be ruined by it. Later, when everyone says the family is collapsing, we must remember how much of its apparent order depended upon a woman expected to keep an impossible system running without ever admitting exhaustion.

The death of Qin Keqing brings a grand funeral, filled with ceremony and money. Xifeng is entrusted with managing affairs for the Ningguo house. The immediate challenge is magnificent and absurd: there are so many servants, payments, tasks, visiting dignitaries, and ceremonial timings that merely being wealthy does not make the operation function. Xifeng imposes order and punishes failure. We may admire her competence and recoil at her methods in the space of a minute. The funeral also shows how the two sides of the Jia clan are linked. A crisis that begins in one courtyard draws labor and attention from the other. These people inhabit an interdependent social world, whether or not they acknowledge the dependence.

Then comes the news that seems to make any future concern look small. Baoyu's elder sister Yuanchun has achieved high favor at court. She is permitted an exceptional visit to her parents, and the family prepares to receive an imperial consort within its own gates. They construct the Grand View Garden: bridges, pavilions, rocks, planted groves, waterways, chambers for poetry and entertainment. Baoyu is asked to suggest inscriptions for its places and does so with a sensitivity that both pleases and irritates the adults. Buildings acquire names, and the names acquire the power to make the garden seem as if it had always existed. The family dresses what is basically an enormous expense as an honor beyond price.

Yuanchun comes home, but she cannot come home like an ordinary daughter. Every moment of the visit follows court ceremony. Her relatives must show reverence even when they long to embrace her. She is moved by the sight of her family and by the costs spent on her behalf. They have created a magnificent temporary kingdom where a daughter's presence is both a triumph and a sign of how little freedom she possesses. The visit ends. She returns to the palace. The garden remains behind, a monument large enough to absorb the family's future.

The old lady and the others decide that the garden ought not remain empty. It becomes a place where Baoyu and several young women can live, study, and enjoy their youth. Each has a residence with its own character. Daiyu lives among bamboo, where the air and sound seem to answer her sensibility. Baochai's rooms are comparatively restrained. Other girls bring their own styles, ambitions, and resources. The girls are not interchangeable beauties. Tanchun is intelligent and decisive, with an administrator's eye; Shi Xiangyun is lively and expressive; Li Wan, widowed young, inhabits a quiet respectability with painful limits; Yingchun is gentle and vulnerable; Xichun increasingly looks toward renunciation. Some are cousins, some servants, some relatives by marriage, and the distinctions matter even where conversation temporarily dissolves them.

For a time, this is one of literature's great places in which young people discover the pleasure of language. They form a poetry club. They read plays and lyrics that respectable guardians do not always want them to read. They compose poems about crab feasts, flowers, chrysanthemums, and the pleasure of recognizing a thought in someone else's words. Baoyu borrows a copy of *The Western Chamber* and reads it with Daiyu. They quote from it and from *The Peony Pavilion*, allowing the words of earlier love stories to carry feelings they cannot safely name. There is wit and flirtation in these exchanges; there is also apprehension. These cousins know that a household can decide whom a girl must marry without asking what poem she has written about it.

The book does not allow the garden to become merely ornamental. Consider Daiyu in the spring, watching fallen flowers. She gathers petals rather than leave them to be trampled and gives them a small burial, singing of beauty that will not stay. Baoyu finds her and listens. He has known that people die, yet here the loss of a flower seems continuous with the possible loss of a person. Daiyu's grief is not just a decorative sign of delicate femininity. She is a sick young orphan who depends upon others for shelter. Impermanence, for her, is a reasonable description of the conditions under which she lives. Her poem lets her speak of it with more authority than ordinary conversation permits.

Outside this garden, a different kind of visitor appears: Granny Liu, an elderly countrywoman in need of help. She approaches the wealthy family through a distant connection and is received awkwardly, sometimes kindly, sometimes as entertainment. On a later visit, Grandmother Jia enjoys taking her through the luxurious garden, and the household uses her unfamiliarity with its comforts as the occasion for elaborate jokes. Granny Liu is not simply foolish. She is alert to the practical value of gifts, knows how to adapt to richer people's amusement, and carries a memory of need into a house that prefers to think money appears when summoned. What she receives matters to her relatives. What she sees will matter later to the Jias, when the direction of dependence is reversed.

There are darker consequences of living inside a structure of authority. Baoyu speaks thoughtlessly to the maid Jinchuan in his mother's presence. Lady Wang, angered by what she takes to be impropriety, strikes the girl and dismisses her. Jinchuan later drowns herself. Baoyu's father hears complaints about his son, including his friendships with an actor and suspicions connected with the maid. Jia Zheng beats Baoyu brutally. Women and servants intervene to stop it. The event reveals that affection has not abolished hierarchy: Baoyu may be the grandmother's darling, but he remains a son whose father can punish him; Jinchuan is a servant whose loss of position can destroy her.

Daiyu visits the injured Baoyu. They speak with the awkwardness of people who know a great deal about each other and cannot find a safe sentence in which to put it. He later sends her two old handkerchiefs. She understands their private meaning and writes poems upon them. Baochai also shows concern and brings an honest judgment about his behavior. This is not a contest between a good woman and a bad one. The novel gives the young man different ways of being cared for, and shows how poorly the institution of marriage will cope with his inability to make those ways equivalent.

At a celebration, the poetry club is at its most lively. Some of the garden's inhabitants boast, tease, and compete; Daiyu can be sharp because her intellect is inseparable from her feelings. Xiangyun, relieved of formal control for an evening, becomes part of an image of joyous disorder. The household still has servants to cook, clean, and carry everything away after the game. A little comedy may turn upon the inequality of the girls' positions; a moment of friendliness may cover what everyone knows cannot be said aloud. There are excellent days here, real days. Their eventual loss would have little force if the novel had not first made them desirable.

An older generation begins to make the future felt even within the garden's apparent freedom. Baoyu repeatedly disappoints those who wish him to study for office. Daiyu worries about being without a protector. Baochai understands that propriety is a kind of protection but also a trap. Wang Xifeng spends and collects and regulates, sometimes using one debt to conceal another. A family that could pay for an imperial visit now finds itself negotiating smaller and smaller expenses. Still, for much of the household, the warning remains distant. They are occupied with ceremonies, quarrels, visits, sickness, poems, and the next meal. That is precisely how a decline of this size becomes possible: there is never a morning when the entire household agrees it is time to stop and ask whether the future can support the present.

The garden is at its fullest when the novel is already showing us its price. A brilliant, sensitive child has grown among women who have taught him tenderness, and who in turn must negotiate a society that evaluates them through marriage, obedience, and use. The garden promises that their company can be a lasting world. The stone that brought Baoyu into this life knows otherwise, though the boy cannot yet admit it. Soon the household will begin searching its own rooms for danger. The young people who composed poems together will discover that their private world has always belonged to other people.

## Text and evidence companion (not spoken)

- Narrative basis: the received 120-chapter *Honglou meng*, chapters 1–54 for this selection, as supplied in the [Chinese Wikisource composite text](https://zh.wikisource.org/wiki/%E7%B4%85%E6%A8%93%E5%A4%A2). That composite uses a Gengchen-family base for the first eighty chapters and the 1791 Chengjia text for the last forty; it must not be mistaken for a single eighteenth-century autograph.
- Educational edition: Ann Waltner, [*Dream of the Red Chamber: Afterlives*](https://open.lib.umn.edu/redchamber/), especially [Introduction to the Text](https://open.lib.umn.edu/redchamber/chapter/introduction-to-the-text/) and [Cousins in the Garden](https://open.lib.umn.edu/redchamber/chapter/cousins-in-the-garden/).
- Scene audit: opening stone and flower (ch. 1); Daiyu's arrival and jade crisis (ch. 3); Xue Pan and the sold girl (ch. 4); Land of Illusion (ch. 5); Qin Keqing and Xifeng's funeral management (ch. 13–14); Yuanchun's home visit (ch. 17–18); Baoyu and Daiyu reading dramas (ch. 23); flower burial (ch. 27); Jinchuan and Baoyu's beating (ch. 30–34); poetry club (ch. 37–38); Granny Liu's second visit (ch. 39–42).
- [Wikipedia: Dream of the Red Chamber](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dream_of_the_Red_Chamber) for orientation, not sole plot or authorship authority. Names follow pinyin (Jia Baoyu, Lin Daiyu, Xue Baochai, Wang Xifeng); descriptive English phrases are original narration, not translated quotations.
