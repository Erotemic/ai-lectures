# Silappatikaram II: Madurai, the Anklet, and the Burning City

Book II of the Tamil epic, the thirteen episodes of Maturaikkāṇṭam, from the journey and arrival to the irreversible miscarriage of justice and Kannaki's transformation.

Kovalan and Kannaki are on the road to Madurai. They carry the promise of a new beginning in the form of an anklet. Their earlier wealth is gone, the marriage has been damaged, and the city of Puhar is behind them. The road winds through landscapes peopled by hunters, pastoral communities, devotees, and travelers who do not all inhabit the same social world. Their companion Kavunti, a Jain ascetic, gives the journey a language of restraint and moral consequence. The poem pauses for songs that belong to the places they pass. Those interludes matter because a story sometimes summarized as the wrath of one woman is also an epic attentive to the many kinds of lives and performances inside the Tamil country.

Madurai appears as a city of power and splendor. Kovalan has come to sell an ornament and make a fresh start; he is not approaching the royal court to challenge its authority. The couple receives hospitality, and Kannaki finds temporary shelter among people who take responsibility for travelers in need. The ordinary kindness of those who help them makes what happens next more terrible. For all the grandeur of a kingdom, a vulnerable stranger can still depend on individual households more reliably than on the judgment of its officers. The traveler who enters a market ought to be able to assume that offering a possession for sale will not become a capital offense.

A royal goldsmith has stolen an anklet belonging to the Pandya queen. The theft has created a risk for the culprit, and an opportunity for him to redirect suspicion when Kovalan arrives with Kannaki's anklet. The objects are of the same general kind, and their external resemblance is sufficient for the goldsmith's deception. He reports that the queen's stolen property has been discovered in the hands of the newcomer. The king accepts the accusation without carrying out the necessary inquiry. Orders are given, and Kovalan is killed. The injustice does not result from an unusually clever method of trial. It results from the absence of a trial that might have distinguished two similar objects. A court has taken the word of a self-interested accuser and put a stranger to death.

The narrative then turns to the woman who has been waiting for her husband. News reaches Kannaki, and the possibility of a renewed life is destroyed before it has begun. Her grief is not restrained into the silence appropriate to a private household. She becomes a public accuser, determined to confront the authority responsible. The poem's dramatic scale changes sharply. Earlier Kovalan moved between a home and a dancer's company; now Kannaki moves toward the royal court where the king's decision must be named as a wrong. The anklet that carried their hopes becomes the one surviving witness that can speak when her husband no longer can.

Kannaki enters the court and demands an accounting. The king believes the executed man was a thief. She asks what the stolen royal anklet contained. The queen's ornament, the king answers, held pearls. Kannaki's pair contains rubies or red gemstones. She breaks open the anklet she has brought, and its contents fall before the court. The difference between pearls and gems should have been established before a death sentence; the disaster is that it appears only afterward. A question of possession has become a demonstration of judicial failure so stark that the king cannot save himself by pleading lack of information. He exercised the power to kill without first obtaining it.

In the poem the king realizes that the thief has been himself, not through stealing jewelry but through taking an innocent life. The phrase is an ethical reversal, not a new criminal accusation against the ruler. He dies under the weight of his error, and the queen dies as well. But the story does not stop at the death of an unjust king. If it did, Kannaki's accusation could be contained as the successful correction of a wrong. The dead husband would remain dead, and the institution that killed him might claim to have paid for its mistake with a royal life. Kannaki's grief has gone beyond what such a settlement can answer.

She turns upon the city itself. In an act the epic presents as charged with supernatural power, Kannaki tears off one of her breasts and casts it down in wrath. Fire spreads through Madurai. The narrative associates the destruction with a moral force that has been awakened by injustice, yet it also pauses over the problem of whom the fire should harm. In its own ethical idiom it names people and beings to be spared, including the righteous, the vulnerable, and cattle. This passage should not be turned into a modern rule for justified violence. It is a disturbing scene in which the moral force of a wronged woman is imagined on a cosmic scale, and the text itself must deal with the fact that a city's inhabitants are not all responsible for the king's judgment.

The burning of Madurai is one of the most consequential reversals in classical Tamil literature. The king had the instruments of force but could not distinguish two anklets. The woman treated as a private dependent now speaks with an authority that makes the city answer for its ruler. The poem's praise of wifely fidelity is inseparable from this revelation of power, but it also creates tensions. Does Kannaki become audible only because she is the faithful wife of a slain man? Does the epic imagine her rage as correcting a collective injustice, or as surpassing what any human legal process can contain? Its scenes compel the listener to ask rather than force a single comfortable answer.

Kannaki leaves the ruined city. The poem sends her toward the hills of the Chera country, away from the court that has witnessed her proof. Her loss does not disappear through vindication. A deity or guardian figure explains how earlier deeds and destiny belong to the story's larger moral world, and the narrative moves toward Kannaki's reunion with Kovalan beyond ordinary human life. The sequence is framed in the poem's cosmology, not offered as an independently established supernatural event. The human story of a woman's grief is being carried into a religious story about the power that grief and fidelity can acquire.

The end of the Madurai book might have served as the ending of the entire epic. A poor traveler is executed unjustly; his wife proves innocence; the kingdom collapses in guilt and fire; the woman who could not regain her husband leaves the world that failed him. But the received *Silappatikaram* has a third book, and that book will change the terms of remembrance. The problem will no longer be only how a king made an irreversible error. It will be how another king makes the wronged woman into the center of a new public cult and a new account of righteous rule.

One might be tempted to call the anklet a mere symbol, but its role is more exact. It is property, potential capital, an object capable of being mistaken for another, an item whose inner contents can provide evidence, and finally the hinge on which the entire difference between life and death turns. A story can persuade us that law matters without offering a treatise on law. It lets the reader watch a man killed for lack of inspection and a woman forced to perform that inspection in the presence of those who should have done it. By the time the fire reaches the city, the small, hard sounds of gemstones falling from a broken anklet have already delivered the epic's judgment.

## Text and evidence companion (not spoken)

- Controlling witness: *Cilappatikāram*, **Maturaikkāṇṭam**, episodes 11–23, especially Kovalan's death (16), Kannaki's lament (18–19), court judgment (20), curse and fire (21–22), and the divine explanation (23). [Central Institute of Classical Tamil editions](https://library.cict.in/ebooks/cilappatikaram) and the [1939 Dikshitar translation record](https://rmrl.in/en/dl/books/34206/the-silappadikaram).
- Claim boundaries: the anklets' pearls versus red gems are what the *poem* presents as proof; the supernatural city fire is a scene within its literary cosmology. The presentation of people to be spared uses the poem's own social and moral categories, not modern universal categories.
- [Wikipedia: Kannagi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kannagi) and [Cilappatikaram](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cilappatikaram) for orientation, not primary evidence.
