# Buddhacarita, Part One: The Prince Who Saw Suffering

Aśvaghoṣa's *Buddhacarita*, Sanskrit cantos I–VII: the court, the four encounters, departure, and the first forest. This is a literary telling of the Buddha's life, not a claim that every described scene is independently established biography.

A prince is born in a flowering grove, and a kingdom imagines it can know his future. The boy is Siddhārtha, son of King Śuddhodana of the Śākya people. In Aśvaghoṣa's Sanskrit poem *Buddhacarita*, the birth comes with signs of extraordinary destiny. Queen Māyā travels to the Lumbinī grove. A child appears amid blessings, and wise men read in his body the promise of a life unlike the lives of other kings' sons. An old seer, Asita, arrives to see him. The seer rejoices at what this child might become and grieves because he will not live long enough to hear the teaching the grown prince will offer.

The poem begins by surrounding Siddhārtha with everything people ordinarily think a fortunate child should possess. His father has authority, counselors, horses, parks, gardens and a magnificent city. The inhabitants are introduced as prosperous and well ordered. This is the world Aśvaghoṣa's narrator means the listener to understand before asking what could compel a prince to leave it. The boy's future has a double meaning. His father hopes he will inherit public power; the seer's words point toward something a king cannot confer. Even in the palace, the claims of family and world have begun to pull in different directions.

Siddhārtha grows into the skills expected of a prince. There are tutors, athletic accomplishment, and marriage. The court responds to his evident excellence by trying to make his life comfortable. We might imagine a young man without curiosity, content to walk among carefully arranged pleasures. But Aśvaghoṣa's prince is attentive. The luxury built around him cannot answer a question he has not yet learned to ask. His father is not cruel for wanting him to stay. He is frightened of losing the son he loves. He tries to keep unpleasant sights away because the predicted religious life might take the prince beyond the household forever.

Then Siddhārtha asks to go outside. The city is prepared to welcome him; the roads and buildings display the prosperity of the kingdom. On one excursion he encounters an old man. The prince asks his charioteer what has happened to this person's body. The answer is not that the man has received a particular punishment or suffered a private misfortune. He has aged. Age comes to those who have been young. Nothing about the privilege of a palace exempts the prince from that fact. The sight cannot be returned to invisibility by turning the chariot around.

A later excursion brings a sick person. Again Siddhārtha asks what he is seeing, and again he learns that the event is not confined to the individual before him. Bodies become ill. They can cease to obey their owners, and no promise of royal care can remove the possibility. The prince is not merely saddened by a stranger's discomfort. He recognizes a general condition of embodied life. A royal garden is not a defense against suffering simply because those who walk in it have servants and gates.

The next encounter is death. A corpse is being carried away, surrounded by people who mourn. Siddhārtha asks the charioteer to explain. Death will come to the young and the old, to those whose families are wealthy and to those who own nothing. A body once loved becomes a body that others carry. The prince has been looking at the city as a place full of things to enjoy. Now he understands that every enjoyment is set inside time, and that a person's most devoted companions cannot accompany them beyond the limit of life.

The fourth encounter shows a different response. He sees a renunciant, someone who has left the ordinary household path in search of release. The scene is not a proof that this person's path will work. It is the first visible sign that someone can make life's universal suffering the subject of disciplined inquiry. Siddhārtha sees a possible task. He might spend his strength on understanding the condition he has discovered, rather than merely on delaying the moment when it reaches him. The repeated journeys make the prince more than a beneficiary of revelation: he observes, asks questions, receives answers, and cannot unlearn what he has heard.

His father and the court respond to his changing mood by intensifying the invitations to pleasure. A beautiful park is prepared, and women skilled in music and performance try to draw him back into the life that seems proper to him. Aśvaghoṣa gives the women personalities and strategies; they are not simply a painted wall. They must appeal to someone who is listening to an argument other than theirs. Siddhārtha sees youth and beauty in the company gathered around him, but age has already become part of how he understands youth, and loss part of how he understands possession. What would have once seemed a reward now feels temporary.

One of the court's spokesmen argues that desire belongs naturally to human life. Why reject what most people seek? Why treat the world's beauty as a trap? Siddhārtha does not deny that beauty exists. He questions whether it can answer the question that brought him back from the road. If everyone must age and die, then delight cannot be sufficient merely because it is intense. His discomfort is not contempt for the people who surround him. It is a conflict about what a good life is for. The court imagines his role in relation to kingdom and lineage; he increasingly imagines it in relation to all beings subject to change.

At night the palace changes. The performers who worked so hard to enchant him fall asleep. The prince passes among bodies in exhausted, unarranged postures. What seemed artfully beautiful in the bright hours looks different when everyone is unconscious. The scene is severe, and it carries Aśvaghoṣa's ascetic argument in an image. The point is not that ordinary people are contemptible because they sleep. It is that any theatrical presentation of permanence can be broken by the body's simplest needs. Siddhārtha takes this as a sign that he must go.

He calls for his horse Kaṇṭhaka and his charioteer Chandaka. The departure is not an uncomplicated heroic escape. A father will wake to find his son gone. A wife and child will live with the consequences. The horse and servant belong to the very system of royal care from which Siddhārtha is about to withdraw. The poem makes his leaving an event in other people's lives, not merely a private moment of illumination. He rides out toward a world in which neither attendants nor guards can resolve the problem he has chosen to face.

Beyond the city he parts from the signs of princely status. He removes ornaments and rich clothing and sends Chandaka back with the horse. The charioteer pleads with him to remember those who love him. The prince answers that all unions must eventually separate, that the departure is not an act of hatred, and that his search concerns the very suffering from which even his family cannot protect him. Chandaka does not find this reasoning painless. The horse, in the tradition Aśvaghoṣa draws upon, suffers the separation. Those who return must bring the terrible news to the palace: no enemy has abducted the prince. He has chosen to go.

Siddhārtha enters a forest where ascetics live among trees, shelters, and ritual fires. Their disciplines attract his attention. Here are people who have reorganized their whole existence around what they believe matters. Yet he also looks carefully at their practices. Some hope that hardship will bring reward in future heavens. Others follow elaborate observances of food, exposure, and physical restraint. Siddhārtha asks whether such efforts truly end the deeper cycle of suffering, or whether they exchange one form of desire for another. He is searching, but he has not promised to accept the first answer offered by people who look holy.

The canto ends without enlightenment. There is no need to hurry him toward the familiar image of a motionless figure beneath a tree. The young man who left the palace is beginning to distinguish withdrawal from understanding. He has discovered that the world is vulnerable, and that sincerely rejecting comfort does not automatically reveal how to live. The achievement of the opening seven cantos is the formation of a question large enough to demand the rest of his life. A prince trained for rule has chosen, with all the cost that choice brings, to become a student of suffering.

This first part belongs to the surviving Sanskrit *Buddhacarita*, an elaborate literary biography traditionally associated with the poet Aśvaghoṣa, who lived around the first or second century of the Common Era. The scenes are composed as poetry; miraculous imagery and courtly rhetoric are part of their literary and devotional world. They should not be converted into modern archival testimony. The poem invites a listener to feel the force of a question, while also permitting us to ask what the narrator adds to the older traditions he inherits. The palace was arranged to keep the prince from seeing the world's condition. After four journeys outside it, no wall can contain what he knows.
