# The Conference of the Birds — The Story: The Road to Simurgh

A complete orientation to the frame journey and its decisive parables in Farid al-Din Attar's Persian Sufi poem, including the excuses of individual birds, the story of Shaykh San'an, the seven valleys, and the ending. It is not a fictional chapter-by-chapter trek with invented adventures.

A great assembly of birds has gathered, each brilliant in its own fashion and each convinced of the importance of its own life. Yet something is missing. They have no king. They argue that other beings have rulers and that a multitude without a center cannot hold together. Who could preside over so many kinds of birds, each with different habits, loves, and fears? One among them knows the answer, or believes that he does. It is the hoopoe, the crested bird remembered as the messenger of Solomon. He declares that there is a sovereign they have never met: the Simurgh, whose home lies beyond the mountain called Qaf. They must go and find him.

The proposal sounds simple only until someone asks how far they must travel. The hoopoe speaks of a terrible journey, a way on which most travelers will not endure. The Simurgh is not a bird that can be summoned with a polite request. To approach him, the seekers must cross a region in which their ordinary measures of success are stripped away. Their possessions will not take them there. Neither will their good opinion of themselves. A king who was merely one more splendid creature among other creatures would not deserve such a pilgrimage. Yet the idea of a king who exceeds every familiar category is precisely what alarms them.

The nightingale is the first sort of protest. It knows the rose. It has sung for her and found all the wonder a lover needs in the perfume and color that return each season. Why abandon the beloved flower for a sovereign no one has seen? The hoopoe replies that such beauty is real but fleeting; the rose's bloom will fall. To tie a whole life to what must wither is to make an arrangement with loss and call it completion. Love must learn what it has loved and whether the lover can bear the beloved's change.

The parrot wants what other seekers might mistake for a noble ambition: everlasting life. A beautiful bird in a cage may dream of the water of immortality, hoping that the years will cease to threaten it. The hoopoe presses the question the parrot has avoided. What is the value of an endless life if the life remains enclosed in the same narrow self? The peacock has another regret. It remembers paradise and longs to return to the garden from which it was expelled. But the hoopoe asks why it is satisfied with the garden when the owner of the garden is the real object of the quest. The paradisal place, like every beautiful possession, may become a substitute for what it signifies.

Other birds offer excuses that sound more practical. The duck knows water and purity and cannot imagine leaving the stream in which it bathes. The partridge loves jewels in the mountains. The homa is pleased with its association with royal fortune. The hawk cherishes the king's hand, a position of proximity and favor. The heron remains at the edge of the sea, desiring what it cannot possess and fearing what it would require to enter. The owl has made ruins its home and dreams of buried treasure. A sparrow is simply frightened: it is small, weak, and has no expectation of surviving a journey imagined for stronger wings. The hoopoe meets each bird in the language of its cherished attachment. He does not say that all the desires are identical. He shows how each becomes an argument for never beginning.

That is the poem's first long movement, and it matters that the travelers are not yet traveling. They have constructed a meeting in which everyone can celebrate the destination while finding a reason to stay. The hoopoe must be more than a guide with a map. He is a teacher who makes people hear the excuses they are accustomed to treating as the essence of their personalities. When one bird speaks proudly of power and another wistfully of love, the hoopoe finds the shared problem beneath their different manners: each has made something limited into the measure of everything.

The leader tells stories. Some are comic, some severe, some heartbreaking. They interrupt the birds' argument not as decorative fables but as other lives in which the same desires have had consequences. One particularly long tale concerns a revered spiritual master named Shaykh San'an. He has spent many years in devotion. Disciples revere him, and his religious learning is extensive. Then a dream draws him toward the lands of Rum, the eastern Roman or Byzantine world of the tale. There he sees a Christian young woman and falls desperately in love.

His disciples are bewildered. Their teacher, once so secure in his discipline, has become willing to abandon the signs by which they know him. The young woman places demands before him. In the poem's narrative he crosses boundaries that his previous life treated as inviolable, including drinking wine and tending swine. He suffers humiliation in pursuit of her, and his followers can hardly recognize the man they came to obey. Some reproach him and turn away. His longing has become a public ruin. It is easy to say that the famous master has failed, but the hoopoe's tale is not so easily closed. The disciples have also discovered how much of their loyalty depended upon the appearance of unshakable sanctity.

A loyal follower urges the others toward prayer and compassion rather than merely condemning the fallen shaykh. The disciples seek his restoration. In the narrative's spiritual resolution, San'an returns to his faith and sets out for home. The young woman, in turn, experiences a transformation that drives her to follow him. They meet again, but the story does not grant them an ordinary romance in which every trial becomes a step toward marriage and domestic peace. She embraces the faith to which he has returned and dies. Love has exposed the master to loss, pride, and bewilderment, and the lesson he offers the birds cannot be reduced to either 'follow every passion' or 'avoid attachment at all costs.' It is a story about the terrible distance between knowing what devotion should be and discovering what one actually worships.

The birds are moved. They begin to accept that their own objections will not be settled merely by being cleverer than the hoopoe. Yet before leaving they ask questions. How can creatures so small approach an incomparable king? What if they lose themselves? How can a guide explain a road that will make familiar explanations fail? The hoopoe continues to answer through parables, sometimes bringing rulers and beggars, saints and sinners, fools and learned men into a single argument. A powerful sovereign may reveal poverty of spirit; a person with nothing may be freer than a man with palaces. A lover may discover that the thing most cherished has become a screen between the self and the beloved.

The journey finally begins. There is not a neat geographical itinerary full of invented campsites and named opponents, because the poem is concerned with spiritual stations rather than an adventure map. The hoopoe names seven valleys the seekers must cross. Their names vary a little in English translation, but the movement of the poem is clear. The first is the Valley of the Quest. To enter it is to stop treating the desire for truth as a pleasant opinion. The seeker must move, relinquish habits, and accept that familiar answers will no longer suffice.

The second is the Valley of Love. Here love is no quiet preference that sits safely beside all the other commitments of a well-managed life. It can burn away calculation. The poem's love is not merely courtship or a mood that may be described from outside. It is a force that makes the seeker willing to be transformed. The example of San'an hangs over the valley, warning that transformation may be painful and that a proud claim to mastery is particularly fragile.

The third is the Valley of Knowledge or Gnosis, a knowing that is not identical with possessing many statements one can recite. Travelers do not necessarily experience this station in the same way. Each reaches according to capacity and perception, and the hoopoe's examples put certainty under pressure. A person may have learned the words of a lesson without having encountered what the words require. The seeker must become capable of recognizing the difference.

Next comes the Valley of Detachment, sometimes called Contentment or Independence in English accounts of the stations. Ordinary comparisons lose their grip. Treasure and poverty, status and its absence, may no longer carry the meanings they did at the assembly. In the Valley of Unity, distinctions that once seemed absolute cease to secure separate centers of value. It is not simply that all birds agree on a political leader. Their idea of themselves as independent possessors of truth begins to falter before the relation of all things to the one reality they seek.

The Valley of Bewilderment follows. The traveler who hoped to finish the journey with a formula finds that the most important insight does not stay obedient to a formula. Awe, uncertainty, and a kind of bewildered recognition displace the belief that one can stand outside the truth and examine it like an object. Finally comes the Valley of Poverty and Annihilation. These words do not instruct the birds to seek physical starvation or to inflict injury. They describe the surrender of the self-sufficiency that made each bird imagine that the Simurgh would be an ornament added to its already completed identity.

Many birds never reach the end. Some surrender to exhaustion or fear; others are lost along the way. The poem does not linger on a military-style roster of casualties, because the disappearance of the crowd is itself the answer to the claim that everyone who admires a distant ideal is prepared to follow it. The great assembly has dwindled. The journey that was once a collective enthusiasm has become an ordeal shared by very few.

At last thirty birds arrive before the place of the Simurgh. They expect to find a royal presence that will reward perseverance by standing apart from them in splendor. Instead they experience refusal, astonishment, and the need to surrender their final claim of entitlement. When admission comes, they discover a recognition the poem has prepared in its own language. In Persian, the phrase si murgh means thirty birds. The seekers are thirty birds, and the Simurgh toward whom they traveled is disclosed through this startling likeness. What they sought outside themselves cannot be found by setting a sovereign object on a throne and admiring it from a distance.

The revelation does not mean that each bird was secretly a powerful god, or that the moral of this long Persian Sufi poem is simply to have confidence in one's personal talents. That would return them to the very self-regard they have struggled to surrender. They encounter the paradox that selfhood must be effaced for the reality they seek to be known. They see themselves in the Simurgh and the Simurgh in themselves, without securing a separate possession called the king. The ending speaks of annihilation and the disappearance of a stable opposition between seeker and sought. It is not an ordinary coronation.

The hoopoe led an assembly that believed it needed an authority outside itself. He did not produce a new bird to command it. He led it through the collapse of the attachments that had prevented the birds from understanding what their search meant. The poem's story closes where a merely heroic journey might be expected to reach its grand public reward. Thirty birds have survived to find that the act of finding changes what can be found, and who can be the one that finds it.

## Primary, scholarly, and orientation references (not spoken)

- **Controlling frame and structure:** Farid al-Din Attar, *Manṭiq al-ṭayr* (also *Mantiq al-tair*, *Conference of the Birds*). The leading modern English verse translation is Afkham Darbandi and Dick Davis, *The Conference of the Birds* (Penguin, 1984); it is consulted as a bibliographic orientation, not quoted or reproduced. Translated valley labels differ, and no single English rendering is claimed as the original word.
- **Detailed scholarly account:** Benedikt Reinert, [“ʿAṭṭār, Farīd-al-Dīn,” *Encyclopaedia Iranica*](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/attar-farid-al-din-poet/), especially the description of the poem's individual birds, Shaykh San'an (maqāla 14), departure (maqāla 15), and the thirty-birds ending (maqāla 45). See the entry for the attribution and manuscript problem as well.
- **Material evidence and literary overview:** Yumiko Kamada, [“The *Mantiq al-tair* (Language of the Birds) of 1487,” Metropolitan Museum of Art](https://www.metmuseum.org/toah/hd/mant/hd_mant.htm); [Met, “Shaykh San'an and the Christian Maiden”](https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/451728). The later painting illustrates reception; it is not evidence of an actual meeting between the characters.
- [Wikipedia: The Conference of the Birds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Conference_of_the_Birds); [Wikipedia: Attar of Nishapur](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Attar_of_Nishapur). The poem is a Sufi allegory in the Persian literary and Islamic religious world; this is a retelling of the received work, not a reconstruction of Attar's private life.
