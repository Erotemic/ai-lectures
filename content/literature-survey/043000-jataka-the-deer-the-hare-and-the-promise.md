# Jātaka: The Deer, the Hare, and the Promise

A selection of three individually identified Pāli Jātaka commentary narratives: Nigrodhamiga (no. 12), Rurumigarāja (no. 482), and Sasa (no. 316). The collection's prose framing and later commentary history are distinct from the older canonical verses to which many tales are attached.

There are two herds of deer in a royal hunting ground. The king wants meat for his table. The animals want to live. For a time they try what may be the most terrible kind of compromise: rather than wait for the hunters, they will send one deer to the slaughter each day. When a pregnant doe is chosen, the arrangement reveals what the word compromise has been hiding. Every day that the hunt is spared, a particular life is still taken.

The tale is called the Nigrodhamiga Jātaka, the story of the Banyan Deer. It belongs to a vast body of Buddhist birth stories, narratives about previous lives of the being who would become the Buddha. Many have short verses in a canonical Pāli collection and fuller framing prose in a later commentary. We will not pretend that the hundreds of Jātaka stories were written together at one moment. They are a transmitted repertoire, in which a monk may tell a story from the past to illuminate an occasion in the Buddha's present.

In the old story two leaders govern the herds. The first, Nigrodha, is the banyan deer, distinguished by a magnificent appearance. The second, Sākha, governs another herd. The king admires the golden or splendid deer and has given the two leaders special immunity. But for the ordinary animals the hunting has become unbearable. When the herds make their arrangement, a deer from one herd and then the other is to go willingly to the block. It reduces panic and injury while leaving the killing intact.

Then the lot falls to a doe carrying a fawn. She goes to her own leader, Sākha, and asks for a delay. Let her give birth, she requests, and then she can fulfill the appointed turn. Sākha refuses. The rules have been established, and he will not change them for a single animal. He appears fair by keeping to the agreement. She turns to Nigrodha, whose herd does not owe the next victim.

Nigrodha does not order another animal to replace her. He lays his own head upon the chopping block. The king's servants are astonished: the most carefully protected deer of all has offered himself. When the king comes to ask why, Nigrodha explains that he could not send another to die for the pregnant doe. The king offers safety to the leader and the mother. Nigrodha asks for more. What of his herd? What of the other herd? What of the four-footed creatures, the birds, the fish? The circle of protection widens each time the king thinks the bargain complete.

In the received story, the king yields. The wider promise creates an ordinary problem as well. Deer soon begin eating people's crops. Nigrodha instructs them not to ruin the farmers' work, and the people establish a way of signaling fields that the deer should avoid. Compassion has to become a practice that other beings can live with. The conclusion is not merely that a ruler should feel generous at the sight of a magnificent animal. It is that an act of mercy should alter the rules under which the weakest live.

The commentary supplies a frame in the Buddha's own lifetime: a woman ordained without knowing she was pregnant is wrongly treated as if she has violated her vows. Her innocence is established, and the story of the doe speaks to the danger of inflexible judgment. A pregnancy that seemed, to the careless official, an embarrassment is the very reason fairness requires attention. The narrative joins a concrete injustice in the frame to a desperate appeal in the animal tale.

Another deer appears in a different Jātaka, number 482, called Rurumigarāja. This one lives by a river, its coat so beautiful that humans might kill it simply to possess it. A man who has squandered his wealth is swept into the water after trying to flee his creditors. His cry reaches the deer, which enters the river and carries him to safety. The rescue is arduous. The deer makes a simple request when the man has recovered: do not reveal where I live.

The man promises. Then the queen of the region dreams of a golden deer teaching wisdom, and the king announces a reward for anyone who can lead him to such a creature. The rescued man knows exactly where one lives. He can collect a reward by undoing the promise that saved his life. He leads the royal party to the riverbank.

The deer meets the king and asks how he came to this place. The king indicates the man. Instead of fleeing, the deer tells the story of the rescue and the promise. The king is enraged by the betrayal and is ready to punish the man. The deer intercedes for the very person who has brought hunters to its home. That is a different kind of courage from plunging into the river. The deer has already saved the man's body; now it prevents a violent punishment against a man whose conduct it condemns.

The king offers protection and invites the deer to speak to the court. In versions of the Pāli narrative, the promise extends to other creatures, and again the lives of animals and the agricultural needs of people must be reconciled. These are not identical stories simply because a speaking deer persuades a ruler in both. In the Banyan Deer story, the central act is taking another's place at the slaughter. In the Ruru story, it is refusing to make vengeance the price of mercy.

For the third tale, leave the hunting ground and enter a forest where a hare lives among three friends: an otter, a jackal, and a monkey. The hare is an earnest observer of a day set aside for generosity. He encourages his companions to give food to anyone in need. Each can find something a traveler might eat. The otter discovers fish; the jackal has food of his own; the monkey can offer fruit. The hare eats grass. He thinks he has no food fit to give to a hungry human guest.

Sakka, lord of the gods, comes disguised as a brahmin to test them. When he approaches the hare, the hare offers what no other animal has proposed: if the visitor will make a fire, the hare will jump into it and give his own body for food. The test carries the story from ordinary charity toward an extreme idea of self-giving. The hare does not ask someone else to pay for his gift. It is his own life he offers.

Sakka prepares the fire. The hare enters it, but the flames do not burn him. The god reveals his identity and announces that such generosity will be remembered. He marks the image of the hare upon the moon. The story's final image takes a creature almost too small to count among kings or heroes and places its outline where the whole world can see it.

We do not have to pretend that literal animal speech or lunar markings are scientific claims. The birth stories operate through imaginative reversals. The ruler learns ethics from the animal he expects to eat. The man saved from the water becomes the one whose moral life needs rescuing. The hare with no material possessions proposes the greatest possible gift. Each tale uses impossibility to make a moral tension hard to overlook.

The stories also ask to be read with judgment. An animal replacing a pregnant doe illustrates compassion, but it does not supply a complete account of modern conservation. A deer sparing a traitor does not mean betrayal has no consequences. The hare's offering of his body belongs to a religious literature of perfection, and is not a literal instruction for a listener to injure themselves. These tales are most revealing when their difficult questions remain visible rather than becoming slogans.

In the fuller Jātaka tradition, each narrative closes with identifications between people in the present frame and beings in the ancient story. Those endings connect past actions with present relationships; they are part of the way the Buddhist community remembered the stories and their teacher. The Jātakas are not one continuous biography that could be placed on a calendar with a year for every animal birth. They are stories about how a virtue might be learned, tested, or carried from one kind of life to another.

At the end of the three tales, we can see three arrangements of risk. Nigrodha puts himself on the block so a mother can live. Ruru risks being shot to prevent the man who betrayed him from being killed. The hare goes toward the fire because it refuses to imagine hospitality as a gift that someone else must pay for. Their worlds are full of kings, hunters, rivers, and hunger. What makes them memorable is that the most powerful figure in each scene turns out not to be the one carrying the weapon.

The no. 12 present-life frame concerns the mother of Kumāra Kassapa and Devadatta's misjudgment.
- **Transmission:** The Jātaka canonical verse collection, expanded commentarial prose narrative, printed nineteenth-century translations and many regional variants differ in date and literary status. This lecture specifies three tales and uses their prose narrative witnesses; it does not claim the collection has one ancient author or a single composition date.
