# Kebra Nagast - Story II: When Zion Left Jerusalem

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

A young priest stands before the sacred place he has been taught to protect. Beyond it lies Jerusalem, Solomon's city: a king who has received the blessing of wisdom, families whose sons are preparing to depart for Ethiopia, and a people for whom the Ark of the Covenant is a sign of their bond with God. The young priest is Azariah. He believes an angel has ordered him to do the very thing his office would ordinarily forbid: take Zion away. The instructions are secret, and the companions who follow them know how easily an act of obedience to one command can look like the worst betrayal of another.

We are continuing the Ethiopian Kebra Nagast, the Glory of Kings. In the first part Queen Makeda traveled to Jerusalem, met Solomon, and returned home with a son. That son came to Jerusalem and prepared to go back to Ethiopia with noble companions and priests. Now the story's center shifts. The young king, later known as Menelik, matters enormously, but the most consequential traveler will not be human. It will be Zion, the Ark or tabernacle of divine law, which the text treats as an earthly dwelling of a heavenly reality.

Azariah arranges the sacrifices his dream required. With several companions, he enters the sanctuary at night. The story tells of an angel whose presence makes obstacles useless: doors are opened, the sacred object can be lifted, and the young men can carry what would otherwise have been beyond their power. They leave pieces of wood in the place where Zion had been and cover the substitute so the removal will not be discovered at once. For seven days the Ark remains hidden and honored by the conspirators. The narrative carefully stages its great reversal as a ritual action with a hidden interval, not a sudden public miracle. The priests have been told how to do what no one believes they can do.

The young Ethiopian king has asked his father for permission to make a final offering. Solomon grants it, and preparations for the departure continue. The reader now knows something the king does not. Solomon can give horses, chariots, rich goods, and advice, and he can bless his son as a loving father. He does not know what is concealed among the travelers' possessions. The secret creates a quiet suspense. In an ordinary adventure, the question would be whether guards discover a stolen treasure. Here the question is whether a king acclaimed for his wisdom can recognize that his most sacred inheritance is no longer under his control.

The procession sets out. The Kebra Nagast makes the Ark's passage a sequence of wonders: wagons move with remarkable speed, the travelers seem lifted or aided by heavenly power, and the landscapes they pass become stages in a victory celebration. The priestly companions begin to understand that their success is not merely their own ingenuity. The young king learns more fully what they have brought. Instead of returning to Jerusalem, the company accepts the Ark as divine favor and praises the God whose will, they believe, has changed the direction of history. The journey that began in family obligation has become a migration of sacred authority.

When the absence is discovered, Solomon's world breaks into disorder. The priests cannot find Zion where she should be. Reports move through the court. Solomon's grief turns toward anger, and the story allows him the thought of pursuing or even destroying those who have taken the Ark. But there are signs and explanations that turn his wrath aside. The movement of Zion is not treated as a mistake the king can repair by retrieving property. It is narrated as a judgment upon the faithlessness of Israel and, eventually, upon Solomon's own failures. His dream of the sun abandoning Jerusalem comes back with a new and terrible clarity.

This is a strikingly different portrait of Solomon from the triumphant figure whose wisdom captivated Makeda. The man who arranged the water bargain and planned his son's future discovers that he cannot arrange the Ark's future. He asks questions, laments, argues, and searches for a way to understand his loss. In one sense the story has humbled the wisest monarch in the world. In another, it has made him participate in the blessing of Ethiopia even against his own desire, since his son and the company Solomon himself organized have become the bearers of Zion. He is both ancestor and dispossessed father.

The book does not let the Ark's departure remain a puzzle of travel logistics. It soon introduces the daughter of Pharaoh and the wider question of worship. Solomon resists, then yields to a circumstance the narrative frames as a compromise with idols. The episode connects his personal conduct to a sacred history in which his house is no longer able to retain its glory. The rhetoric is intensely partisan. Other peoples and practices are denounced, and the book's Christian polemic extends far beyond the events of the tenth century before the common era. We should hear those judgments as elements of this medieval text's argument, not as a neutral description of entire communities.

Meanwhile, the travelers approach Ethiopia. Messengers carry news of their coming. Makeda hears that her son has been recognized and crowned, that he is returning with an extraordinary company, and, most astonishingly, that he brings Zion. The queen prepares a welcome and summons the people to rejoice. Here the earlier story comes full circle. Once she traveled toward the wisdom of a king outside her country. Now the sacred object associated with that wisdom is moving toward her. She has not become irrelevant simply because the book emphasizes a son's descent from Solomon. It is Makeda's court, kingdom, and decision to receive the returning travelers that makes the new order visible on the ground.

The return is a public scene of sound and movement: greetings, singing, processions, adornments, and celebration. The young king stands before his mother not merely as a son returned from a difficult visit, but as a consecrated ruler accompanied by priests and by the Ark. Makeda transfers royal authority to him, and the nobles take oaths. The story does not end with a private embrace. It insists on a legal and ceremonial transformation. The kingdom must be reorganized around what the travelers brought. A sacred possession becomes a constitution of sorts, though the text speaks in the language of covenant rather than modern political institutions.

Azariah and the priests offer instruction to the queen and people. They praise Ethiopia and repeat commandments about worship, justice, property, neighbors, the poor, and the proper treatment of fellow human beings. Some details closely resemble biblical legal materials. An animal lost by its owner should be returned; a neighbor should be helped when a load becomes too heavy; gleanings should be left for those who need food. This is one of the book's most concrete political passages. The Ark is not only an emblem that permits a dynasty to boast of its ancestry. In the story it also carries a demand that power be organized through religious obligation and public conduct.

Yet the vision is not merely gentle. The Kebra Nagast gives its new ruler a war and tells of the expansion or defense of his power. It describes Ethiopia as favored and uses the vocabulary of victory to support that claim. The same book that speaks vividly about helping one's neighbor can celebrate a kingdom's superiority over its rivals. That is not a contradiction we need to solve by declaring one passage the real book and the other an embarrassment. Many political epics link moral order to military strength. The question is how a reader responds when the text's confidence in its own chosen community begins to exclude other people.

The later chapters also widen beyond Menelik's life. They turn to prophecies, biblical figures, the rise and fall of kingdoms, the incarnation and crucifixion of Christ, Mary, and arguments about Zion's meaning in Christian sacred history. The work is a long theological construction with a central narrative, not a modern novel that ends as soon as its hero gets home. Its claim is that the Ark's journey and Ethiopia's kingship can be understood only in light of a history of salvation. The book repeatedly moves between an object carried through desert roads and a heavenly reality that no wagon could contain.

Zion shifts between several registers in the narrative. Sometimes the word directs our attention to the material Ark hidden in Jerusalem and brought to Ethiopia. At other moments it evokes the heavenly Zion or is interpreted through the Virgin Mary and the coming of Christ. A literal reader looking for a single object can feel that the argument has slipped away. For the compilers, the movement between object, sacred place, and Christian symbol is part of the point. The Ark is both something the story says traveled and something through which the book speaks about the way divine favor passes from one age to another.

A final political vision joins Ethiopia to the empire called Rome in the work, a realm often understood in these chapters through the Christian eastern Roman world. The book distributes great responsibilities among Christian kings and asks how their earthly thrones relate to divine authority. Ethiopia is not positioned as a remote afterthought to other people's sacred history. Its monarchy is given a central place in a world whose ultimate ruler is God. The language of succession becomes world history. That final expansion explains why the text spends so many chapters on prophecy and Christian interpretation after the Ark has already arrived: its argument is not finished until the reader sees what sort of world the Ark's arrival is meant to authorize.

What, then, has happened? A queen hears of another kingdom's wisdom and goes to learn. A king uses wisdom to gain power over her and becomes the father of a child he cannot keep. A son returns to his mother with noble companions. Young priests remove a sacred object at an angel's command. A father loses what he thought his kingdom would always possess. A mother receives a son who now carries an argument for kingship, law, and religious belonging. These are the events of the tale. The historical truth of the claimed ancient lineage and transport of the Ark is a different question, one the narrative cannot settle merely by telling itself with conviction.

The book's last effect is to alter the map on which its audience imagines sacred history. Jerusalem is not erased. It remains the place from which the queen sought knowledge, the son received recognition, and Zion departed. But Jerusalem no longer has the final word. Ethiopia is made a destination of divine choice, not a place beyond the edge of the story. We can appreciate the construction without mistaking dynastic claims for archaeological proof, and we can attend to the devotion behind the book without endorsing every hostile contrast it draws. Its deepest narrative act is a change of center: the sacred treasure goes elsewhere, and the reader must learn to look there.

## Text and evidence companion (not spoken)

### Scope and source boundary

Original English overview of *Kebra Nagast* chapters 45-117, not a chapter-by-chapter retelling. The first half follows the Ark's departure and the Ethiopian founding narrative; the latter portion identifies the theological, typological and political material rather than pretending a modern adventure narrative continues uninterrupted. Source boundary: Budge (1922) English translation. A material Ark crossing the Red Sea and ancient Solomonic descent are claims *inside the work*, not independently authenticated events. The book's anti-Jewish and exclusionary polemic is identified as polemic, not reproduced as an account of the people it targets.

### Episode locators

- Azariah's vision, the sanctuary and substitutes: chapters 45-48.
- Solomon's blessing, departure, Ark's rapid journey: chapters 49-55.
- Discovery and Solomon's lament and desire for pursuit: chapters 56-63.
- Pharaoh's daughter and the reading of Solomon's loss: chapters 64-71.
- Return, Makeda's welcome, succession and law: chapters 84-92.
- Warfare and kingship: chapters 93-95.
- Christian prophecy, Mary, Christ and heavenly Zion: chapters 66-71, 96-115.
- Ethiopia, Rome and the final cosmological-political vision: chapters 116-117.
