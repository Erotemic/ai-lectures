# The Tale of the Heike — Story I: The House Above the Capital

A spoiler-complete introduction to the rise and first collapse of the Taira in the received *Heike monogatari*, including Giō, Shunkan, Kiyomori, the wars of 1180–83, and the flight from the capital. This is narrative synthesis, not a chapter-by-chapter substitute for the long and variant-rich work.

Before the house rises, we hear how it will fall. A temple bell sounds in the imagination of the storyteller. Flowers bloom and fade. Everything living and every institution built by human pride will change. Great warriors, ministers, and sovereigns may appear to stand above the world, yet no one has discovered a way to remain above time. In the story called The Tale of the Heike, this conviction is not a moral attached to the ending. It is the atmosphere through which we will watch a family take possession of a country, and lose it.

The setting is Japan in the late twelfth century, when an imperial court still governs by titles, ceremonies, family alliances, petitions, and retired emperors who may exercise astonishing authority. Yet armed households are no longer merely hired support. The Taira and Minamoto families have both fought for powerful people at court. They have also fought one another, and the consequences have made the Taira paramount. A great warrior named Taira no Kiyomori has learned how to turn military success into the language of aristocratic office. His relatives occupy places once difficult for warriors to reach. His sons and daughters become instruments of alliance. At the family residences in the capital, ambition has become a style of daily life.

Kiyomori is not without intelligence or achievement. The story can show him moving decisively through a dangerous political world. Its indictment grows out of the ways he comes to exercise power. When a young relative is offended by a high-ranking noble's procession, Kiyomori does not accept the restraints demanded by rank and diplomacy. He prepares a punitive answer. His more cautious son Shigemori urges another course. In this family, even its most powerful members disagree about the difference between strength and good government. Shigemori knows that an insult answered with terror does not disappear; it changes the minds of everyone who witnesses it.

A young dancer called Giō knows another form of power. She has enjoyed Kiyomori's favor, together with the support that his wealth could give to her household. Then another performer, Hotoke, catches his attention. Favor can be withdrawn with hardly an explanation. Giō is displaced, and the humiliation becomes worse when she must return and perform before the woman who has replaced her. Her position has been made vulnerable not because her art has failed, but because someone's desire has changed. Giō and her family withdraw into religious life. Later Hotoke herself, seeing what the same capricious attention may do to her, comes to them. The episode is not a pause in the tale of powerful men. It tells us what powerful men can do while barely noticing that they are doing it.

The story follows contests at court that grow more dangerous. The retired emperor Go-Shirakawa is a formidable presence. Men who resent Taira advancement gather, and at Shishi-no-Tani a scheme develops against Kiyomori's dominance. Its secrecy fails. The conspirators are arrested and punished. Among them is the monk Shunkan, sent into exile with two others to an island far from the capital. The isolation is already a sentence. For exiles who have known the crowded disputes, visits, letters, and ceremonies of the city, the absence of news is a cruel second punishment.

At last a boat comes bearing a pardon. The men rush toward it and learn that only two names have been released. Shunkan's is missing. In a moment that has become one of the tale's most enduring scenes, he runs after the departing vessel and tries to hold it by force of his desperation. The sea takes the boat away. The others return toward friends and temples; Shunkan remains where there is no practical road home. A compassionate visitor later reaches the island and hears what has happened to him, but compassion cannot put the pardon back into the letter. The monk's story ends in abandonment and death. The political plot in the capital has become an intimate image of exclusion: one name left off a list, one person facing open water.

Kiyomori's hold over court politics tightens. He is the grandfather of a child who will become Emperor Antoku. The young emperor's mother is Kiyomori's daughter Tokuko, later known as Kenreimon-in. Through her, the Taira house is joined to the imperial family itself. Kiyomori can act as though the legitimacy of a dynasty and the prosperity of his clan must be the same thing. Yet there are still rival branches, religious institutions that do not obey him, and a population living with the material costs of political struggle. In the tale's moral world, success begins to seem like evidence of approaching disaster precisely when it becomes absolute.

An uprising is proposed. Prince Mochihito and the veteran Minamoto warrior Yorimasa call for resistance to the Taira. Their force is pursued toward Uji. The bridge across the river becomes a narrow place of dreadful fighting. The defenders tear up parts of the bridge, creating gaps that make the crossing nearly impossible. Warriors battle beside the river, horses fail, arrows fly, and a retreat turns into a last stand. The uprising is defeated. Yorimasa dies. But to defeat one rising is not to eliminate the grievance that produced it. The call has already traveled, and Minamoto commanders far from the capital can hear it.

The Taira government moves the imperial household to Fukuhara, near the sea, an attempt by Kiyomori to reshape the political center around his own projects. The disruption angers and disorients the court. This is not a modern administrative transfer of offices. Authority is embodied in residences, temples, sacred sites, ceremonial arrangements, and memories; to uproot them is to make power look less secure, not more. The court eventually returns to the capital, but the gesture has shown how far Kiyomori will go to make the country's institutions follow his house.

War spreads. Temples have joined or resisted the conflict, and the fighting carries a terrible price. At Nara, an attack associated in the tale with the Taira commander Shigehira becomes a conflagration. Great religious buildings burn, including the vast image hall of Tōdai-ji in the received account. The narrator treats the destruction of sacred places as a calamity that cannot be reduced to a line in an army's record. The fire has people in its path. It also consumes the work of generations. Even if responsibility for the flames and the details of the military episode are matters for historians to distinguish, in the tale their moral significance is unmistakable. The family at the height of worldly command has been drawn into an act that makes the world itself seem injured.

Then illness enters Kiyomori's house. He is seized by a raging fever, described with images of heat so extreme that water offered to cool him seems to boil away. The powerful man who could rearrange court appointments cannot command his own body to cool. Priests and relatives attend him. The family has no procedure for bargaining with a mortality that does not recognize rank. He dies in 1181. The tale's presentation is fierce, but it is not simple satisfaction: the man has left children and grandchildren who must now sustain what he built, and the enemies who were once held at bay are still gathering.

Among those enemies are Minamoto no Yoritomo in the east and Minamoto no Yoshinaka advancing from the interior mountains. They do not form one harmonious family of righteous restorers. They have distinct armies, political aims, and reasons to distrust one another. Yoritomo's organization grows stronger. Yoshinaka proves particularly dangerous on the battlefield. The contest begins to leave the Taira's preferred ground of courtly negotiation and ceremonial display. In the countryside, food, horses, terrain, and the loyalties of local fighting men can decide what no imperial title can settle.

At Kurikara the Taira army suffers a crushing defeat at Yoshinaka's hands. Different versions and later tellings delight in terrifying details of the rout, but its decisive fact is clear: the military force meant to defend Taira supremacy has been broken. Yoshinaka continues toward the capital. At court, the relatives who had inhabited the best houses and enjoyed the most secure offices now count the things they can carry. There are young children in the household, women who do not command armies, priests, attendants, and men who have never imagined abandoning the city. Their options narrow with every report from the roads.

One Taira warrior, Sanemori, receives the sort of personal attention that makes the war more than two family names. He is an aging fighter who darkens his gray hair before battle because he does not want enemies to think him too old to stand among the young. When he is killed, the discovery of his age turns the body into a lesson about the difficulty of being remembered as one wishes. War will write the story of a person in ways that even a carefully chosen appearance cannot control. The tale returns to such scenes because its warriors fear not only defeat but the possibility of being misunderstood after death.

The Taira decide to flee west. They take the young emperor Antoku and the imperial regalia available to them, intending that legitimate sovereignty will travel with the household. The capital does not become empty, but the world of the Taira inside it comes apart. Nobles who wore costly robes become refugees; buildings that once proved status become abandoned property. A poet-warrior named Tadanori goes to the famous poet Fujiwara no Shunzei with his verses. He hopes that poetry, at least, might survive the political erasure of his house. To take a collection of poems on the edge of military disaster is a gesture that may seem impractical until we understand that his life's accomplishments are not all accomplished with a sword.

The family departure is full of divided loyalties. What does it mean to follow the emperor when there are other claims to imperial authority? What does loyalty require of a servant whose children remain behind? How can a warrior promise death in battle while a household needs someone to navigate boats and obtain provisions? These choices do not resolve into a single heroic posture. The Taira move toward the sea, bringing with them their banners, treasures, sacred objects, memories of luxury, and a child on whose person they have placed the continuity of government. As they sail away, the capital becomes the place where they once had everything.

But their enemies cannot simply inherit the world they have taken. Yoshinaka enters the capital and struggles to govern it. His troops become associated with disorder; the court's hopes of relief are disappointed. Yoritomo sends his brothers against him, including Yoshitsune, a commander whose quick tactical imagination will soon become central to the tale. The Minamoto now turn weapons against one another. A house can rise by defeating its enemy, and then begin to divide over the victory. Yoshinaka loses the capital, flees, and dies. Power is already passing from one unstable arrangement to the next.

The Taira, meanwhile, have not ceased to be a family or an army. They gather in the west and prepare to hold the shores and routes that remain. Their experienced commanders know the advantages of ships, narrow approaches, and coastal positions. They still hope that a turn of fortune, an alliance, or a single decisive battle could restore them. Some may even imagine the capital waiting to welcome them back. The next encounter will not be fought in a council chamber. It will be fought on beaches, at mountain passes, from boats, and finally on water where none of their titles can keep them afloat.

The bell has not stopped sounding. It has changed its distance. When we first heard it, it seemed to announce the ruin of a proud man. Now it follows the people who depended upon him, including those who never chose the policies that brought them here. The first movement of The Tale of the Heike ends not with the defeat of an evil family, but with the removal of a whole way of life from the place it believed it owned. The sea ahead will decide what remains.

## Sources, textual boundary, and further reading (not spoken)

- Primary narrative boundary: the received *Heike monogatari*, principally books 1–9 as represented by the Kakuichi tradition. This is original paraphrase, not quotation from a modern English translation. The book numbering and episode titles follow Burton Watson, *The Tales of the Heike*, edited by Haruo Shirane ([Columbia University Press](https://cup.columbia.edu/book/the-tales-of-the-heike/9780231510837/)).
- Episode-level orientation: [*The Tale of the Heike*, episode summaries](https://sites.google.com/view/tale-of-the-heike/books/Book-One/episode-summaries) is a secondary teaching resource, not an edition of a medieval manuscript.
- Scholarship and text history: Helen Craig McCullough, *The Tale of the Heike* (Stanford University Press, 1988); Haruo Shirane, ed., *Traditional Japanese Literature: An Anthology, Beginnings to 1600*. Exact medieval versions vary; events in the narrative cannot be used without corroboration as factual transcripts of the Genpei War.
- [Wikipedia: The Tale of the Heike](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Tale_of_the_Heike); [Wikipedia: Genpei War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Genpei_War) for orientation. Neither is the controlling narrative witness.

**Loci:** Book 1 (opening, Tadamori, Kiyomori, Giō); books 2–3 (Shishi-no-Tani, Shunkan); books 4–5 (Uji, Fukuhara, Nara); book 6 (Kiyomori's death); book 7 (Kurikara, Tadanori, withdrawal); books 8–9 (Yoshinaka and the opening western campaign). The narrative stops before the decisive Taira coastal defeats; those continue in Story II.
