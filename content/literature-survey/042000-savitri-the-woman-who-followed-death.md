# Sāvitrī: The Woman Who Followed Death

An original narration of the complete embedded Sāvitrī–Satyavān episode in the *Mahābhārata*, Vana Parva, with Markandeya's frame intact. Ganguli's English translation calls the constituent sections CCLXLI–CCLXLVII; the commonly used vulgate chapters are 293–299. Numbering varies by edition.

Sāvitrī knows when her husband will die. She does not know how to prevent it. She has been told that the day is fixed, that no strength or skill of his can change it, and that even the people who love him most will be helpless. The day comes nearer while her husband works, eats, sleeps, and speaks as though there were countless days ahead. She counts each one.

Her story is told within the *Mahābhārata*, during the long exile of the Pāṇḍava brothers. Yudhiṣṭhira is grieving over Draupadī, who has endured humiliation and danger. He asks the sage Mārkaṇḍeya whether any woman has shown comparable strength in adversity. The sage answers not with an argument, but with the tale of Sāvitrī. Its listener is asked to consider what steadfastness looks like when fate is not merely a fear but a date on the calendar.

Before Sāvitrī is born, her father Aśvapati rules the Madra kingdom without a child. He undertakes a long observance, offering prayers and disciplined worship to the goddess Sāvitrī. A daughter is promised, and when she is born she receives the goddess's name. She grows into a woman of formidable presence. Suitors are intimidated by her qualities. Rather than wait indefinitely, her father sends her out to choose a husband for herself.

She travels through forests and hermitages, meeting people who have left royal courts for lives governed by labor and spiritual discipline. Her choice falls upon Satyavān, a young man who lives in the woods with his parents. He is born to royalty, but his father Dyumatsena has lost both his sight and his kingdom. The family has neither the security of a court nor the glamour of powerful relatives able to intervene on command. Satyavān helps his parents in their forest home.

Sāvitrī returns and declares her choice in the presence of her father and the sage Nārada. Nārada praises Satyavān almost without reservation: his character, courage, understanding, and kindness make him worthy of admiration. Then the sage tells Aśvapati the one fact that changes everything. Satyavān has only a year left to live.

Her father tries to persuade her to choose again. The matter is too serious for romantic stubbornness, he argues. Sāvitrī does not pretend the prediction is false. She says that the mind chooses before speech confirms the choice, and that hers has already chosen. A decision of this gravity cannot be made anew whenever its consequences become frightening. Nārada observes that she will not be turned aside; the marriage is permitted.

We should not mistake her resolve for ignorance. She knows what she has accepted. The tale allows her a kind of clarity denied to everyone who hopes that the warning will disappear if it is not spoken. She marries Satyavān, leaves the comforts of her father's household, and goes into the forest to live with his blind father and mother. For the better part of a year, she serves them and shares Satyavān's life.

Then she begins to count the days in earnest. Four days remain. She undertakes a demanding three-night observance of fasting and prayer. Dyumatsena worries over her strength, but she insists upon completing her vow. The day arrives. The old people tell her to eat. She says she will wait until evening. Satyavān takes his axe and sets out into the forest to collect wood and fruit.

Sāvitrī asks to accompany him. Satyavān has never seen her do this work. The forest paths are rough and she has fasted; why should she subject herself to the journey? She insists. Her parents-in-law consent. The two move through the trees as though on an ordinary outing, and Satyavān points out the beauty of the landscape. He is discovering how much she loves the woods; she is watching the moment when the world is supposed to take him from her.

He climbs, gathers fruit, cuts branches. Then pain strikes. His head aches terribly, his limbs fail, and he can no longer stand. Sāvitrī sits on the ground and takes his head into her lap. In the story's most intimate scene, a man who knows nothing of the deadline asks to rest, while the woman who knows everything holds him until his breath leaves.

A figure appears, dark and radiant, carrying a noose. It is Yama, lord of death and of moral order. Sāvitrī asks who he is. Yama says that Satyavān's time has ended. In the narrative, the young man is so virtuous that Yama has come personally rather than sending his messengers. He draws the life from Satyavān's body and begins to travel southward, toward the realm of the dead.

Sāvitrī follows. Yama tells her to return and perform the rites that the living owe to the dead. She has gone as far as duty requires. She answers that she will accompany her husband wherever he is carried. She reminds Yama that walking even a few steps with another establishes a bond of companionship. Her language is courteous, but its courtesy does not make it submissive. Every time Yama tries to end the conversation, she offers him another reason it should continue.

Yama is pleased by the discipline and intelligence of her speech. He grants her a boon, but excludes her husband's life. Sāvitrī asks for the restoration of Dyumatsena's sight. Granted. She continues walking. Yama grants another boon, still excluding Satyavān. She asks that her father-in-law recover his lost kingdom. Granted. There is no sudden attack on Death, no theft from an unattended gate. What she wins is won by speaking well and by refusing to stop accompanying the one she loves.

The exchange continues. Sāvitrī asks that her own father, Aśvapati, have a hundred sons, so that the kingdom he has ruled without an heir may flourish. Granted. Again she walks. Each boon addresses a real wound in the people around her. Her husband's death is the center of her grief, but she has not forgotten the blind father, the dispossessed household, or the parents who gave her life.

Finally she asks for sons of her own, born of Satyavān. Yama grants the wish. Then Sāvitrī points out what his own promise entails. How can she bear children by Satyavān if Satyavān has permanently been taken from the world? The lord who grants the boons is also the guardian of just speech. His promises cannot be meaningless. He restores Satyavān's life and allows the couple to return. In Ganguli's account Yama describes a long future for them, not merely another night before the same sudden end.

It is tempting to treat this as a trick that embarrasses a foolish god. That would miss the tone. Yama has repeatedly praised her truthful reasoning; he is not shown as a petty official outwitted by a clever loophole. The story makes the force of Sāvitrī's speech depend on fidelity to moral order. The conversation brings the obligations of justice into view one by one until the restoration of life becomes the fitting conclusion.

Back in the forest, Satyavān wakes. He remembers a strange weight of sleep, a terrible weakness, a being whose appearance he cannot fully explain. Sāvitrī tells him that they must return to his parents. It is dark, and the path is difficult. Their reunion is not a sudden transition to palace celebration. They must walk home through night and woodland, with a father and mother somewhere waiting in fear.

Dyumatsena has regained his sight, but his new vision has not freed him from anxiety. He and his wife are searching, asking where their son and daughter-in-law have gone. Sages try to console them. The old king has recovered the ability to see, yet he fears that he may never see the person who matters most. When the couple returns, their presence answers the question more powerfully than any explanation.

The hermitage gathers to hear what has happened. Sāvitrī tells them about the prediction she kept to herself, the day in the forest, her meeting with Yama, and the boons. The family learns that their rescue reaches beyond Satyavān's survival: Dyumatsena will recover his kingdom, Aśvapati will have descendants, and the marriage once shadowed by a deadline will continue. The tale gives its protagonists a future, not only a reprieve.

Mārkaṇḍeya concludes the story for Yudhiṣṭhira. Draupadī's suffering has not vanished, and the exiled brothers' own trials are not resolved by hearing about Sāvitrī. What the story supplies is an example of steadfast courage that does not require a battlefield. A woman has gone to the boundary of death and returned by listening, arguing, and keeping faith with her choice.

The scene that remains with us may be the one just before the marvel, when Satyavān's head lies in Sāvitrī's lap. She cannot stop him from falling, and she cannot make herself less afraid by pretending the predicted hour will never come. She can stay. And when Death turns and begins to walk away, she stays in motion beside him, turning a private promise into a conversation about justice that even Yama must hear.
