# Mu'allaqat I: Imru' al-Qays, the Ruins, the Horse, and the Storm

A close, audio-first encounter with one of the seven famous pre-Islamic Arabic odes, keeping the poem's distinct movements instead of turning its speaker into the hero of a reconstructed novel.

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

Two companions are asked to stop. The speaker has recognized a place that once held a camp and someone he loved. The people have gone; the signs left behind are faint, and wind has already begun to rearrange them. He wants to cry there, before what remains of the place disappears. This is the beginning of the *Mu'allaqa* attributed to Imru' al-Qays, one of the most celebrated poems of the Arabic language. It begins not with a king in battle but with a person demanding a little time to stand among abandoned dwellings. The poem will range through remembered love affairs, a long night, a magnificent horse, hunting, and rain. If we treat those movements as disconnected scenes, we miss the emotional force of their sequence. The poet stands before absence and makes language move through everything that can answer it.

The ruins of the campsite are not monumental stones. They are the remnants of portable settlement, places where people rested and then continued traveling. The lyric speaker names the site precisely through its local features and speaks as though recognition might itself recover the vanished life. Companions urge him to bear the sorrow. His answer is not that grief will solve the problem. He can only weep, and the poem makes the act of stopping to weep the first deliberate action. Many classical Arabic odes begin with a memory of deserted dwellings; the opening is often called the *nasib*. This one is so famous that it has become a model for how a poem can begin with the desire to hold onto a place while acknowledging that people must move on.

The voice turns to the lover's remembered encounters. Its mood changes drastically. Now the speaker is exuberant, proud of his daring, and sometimes startlingly boastful. He recalls an outing when his camel is sacrificed and a group of women share the meat. In a scene involving Unayza's howdah, he intrudes where he is not invited and takes delight in the trouble he causes. Elsewhere he presses affection on women whose resistance is part of the poem's self-display. It is important not to polish all of this into harmless romance. The speaker's boasting presents relationships according to the desires of a man claiming erotic success. A modern listener can admire the technical and imaginative brilliance of the verse while noticing the imbalance of power and consent in some of its scenes.

The poem is especially alive to physical detail. It can move from perfume and garments to the shock of seeing a face, from playful audacity to regret. It does not preserve one stable tone and then merely ornament it. Even within the erotic recollections there are reversals, with the speaker pleading and being refused, or imagining that a woman's choices determine the fate of his own heart. The remembered place from the opening keeps returning by another route. To remember an intimacy is to discover how thoroughly the present lacks it. These scenes are literary poses in a transmitted poem. We should not assume that every boast supplies a reliably datable event in the poet's biography.

The long night provides a central turn. The speaker finds that the hours do not pass. Darkness seems to stretch in portions that are heavy and reluctant to depart. This is not simply a weather report. The lengthening night expresses a mind unable to leave grief behind. In the opening he asked his companions to stop because a place detained him; now time itself seems to stop. The poem can make night feel like a physical burden because it has already taught the listener that remembered absence has weight. The sequence moves from the exposed ground of the campsite into an interior experience that no journey can easily shorten.

Then the poem puts motion under the speaker with an image of his horse. The animal is not a generic noble steed. It is described through its build, speed, responsiveness, and the startling energy with which it answers the rider. Descriptions of hunting and the chase turn the ode outward again. The poet displays the horse as an embodiment of readiness, able to move when everything in the previous scene seemed immobilized. The animal's force is rendered in comparisons that demand an audience familiar with bodies, weather, stones, and the pace of travel. The result is not a cavalry report. It is an exhibition of how language can make velocity perceptible.

The final storm brings another immense change of scale. Lightning flashes, clouds mass, and rain drives through a landscape that the speaker sees in vivid and specific locations. The storm floods, uproots, and alters the ground. It is tempting to say that the rain symbolically washes away his grief. The poem is less orderly than that. Water appears in many of the odes as real life, real danger, and an extravagant force beyond human control. The poet may have escaped the long night through remembered physical vigor, but he now confronts an element whose power exceeds even the splendid horse. The individual who began by lamenting the traces of a vanished campsite ends by making an entire landscape change before the ear.

This is a pre-Islamic Arabic *qasida*, a long ode built in a rigorous metrical and rhyming form. In received anthologies it occupies a place of exemplary status. The name *Mu'allaqat* is often explained through a legend that these magnificent poems were written in gold and hung on the Kaaba in Mecca. That tale became influential, but it is not secure evidence for the collection's early history. The transmitted set of poems and their textual readings were shaped by later collectors, commentators, and copyists. Even the verse count varies with the edition. Our reading follows an identifiable Arabic verse sequence rather than pretending that the language reached us unchanged from a performance by the poet himself.

The poem does not have a conventional plot ending in marriage, victory, or death. Its achievement is to order emotional and sensory experiences without subordinating them to such a conclusion. An absent lover is remembered; desire speaks with pride and vulnerability; the night becomes unbearable; the horse carries extraordinary kinetic energy; a storm sweeps across the world. The voice changes its subject but not its power to transform experience into image. That is why an ancient poem that asks us first to stop can lead us so far. The opening holds one vanished camp in view. By the end, the land itself is no longer standing still.
