# Vessantara: The Children and the Return

Second original narration of the received Pāli *Vessantara-Jātaka* no. 547, as represented in Cowell/Rouse, volume VI. This episode preserves the children's suffering and Maddī's grief rather than reducing them to evidence of Vessantara's merit.

The old brahmin reaches the hermitage while Maddī is away gathering food. Jāli and Kaṇhājinā are at home with their father. Their mother will return in the evening, as she has returned on so many evenings in the forest, with roots and fruit and whatever else she can carry. The children have no reason to imagine that the day will not end in the ordinary way. Then the visitor asks their father to give them to him.

His name is Jūjaka. He has come to Vessantara because he wants servants for his house. The request is not an appeal on behalf of starving villagers or the desperate petition of someone without a place to sleep. It is an attempt to obtain the labor of two children. The tale presents Jūjaka as selfish and unpleasant, though it also explains the domestic pressure that sent him on the journey. Vessantara's response makes the encounter terrifying: he agrees.

He tries at first to delay the departure until Maddī returns. Let the children see their mother once more, he proposes. Jūjaka refuses. He fears she will stop him or that the gift will be withdrawn. The children hear that they are to be carried off and made to work for a stranger. They hide among vegetation and in water beneath leaves, desperate to evade him. Jūjaka accuses the prince of encouraging their flight. Vessantara is moved, but he still insists that the gift stand.

Jāli and Kaṇhājinā are found. Their panic is given sustained attention in the received narration. They cling to familiar things; they speak of their mother and the life they will lose. Their father does not simply pass them across a threshold and watch an exchange of wealth. He has to confront their fear and their resistance. As they are led away, Jūjaka binds them and treats them harshly. The story records that they suffer at his hands, and it does not make that suffering disappear by explaining that a future Buddha is seeking perfection.

The earth shakes in the tale when Vessantara gives them. A reader within the Buddhist narrative understands the gift as a tremendous step toward the perfection of generosity. An ordinary listener can understand something else as well: children have been handed over without their consent. The two responses are not the same, and the second cannot responsibly be erased from a modern retelling. The enormity of the gift is part of the story's intended challenge.

Meanwhile Maddī has been delayed in the woods. The narrative surrounds her with omens and frightening obstacles. Animals prevent her from passing readily; her heart is troubled by signs she cannot explain. By the time she comes home the light is failing. The place where the children usually play is empty. She looks first in one place, then another, still expecting to hear their voices.

Her search grows desperate. She imagines where they might have wandered, how they might be hurt, whether some animal could have seized them. She calls for them as darkness gathers. Vessantara remains silent. The silence intensifies the misery. Maddī returns to the same places, seeking what is no longer there, until exhaustion overwhelms her. She faints. The prince tends her and eventually tells her that he has given the children to a brahmin.

She asks why he let her search and weep rather than speak. His explanation is that he feared the pain the truth would cause. The poem then represents Maddī as accepting the gift within the religious framework of her husband's vow. It gives her words of affirmation. Those words belong to the transmitted tradition. But the preceding search and collapse also belong to it. We should not keep only the affirming speech and discard the sorrow that made it necessary.

The gods are watching. Sakka, the king of the gods, fears that after the loss of his children the prince might be left alone if another petitioner demands Maddī. To secure her against an ordinary human claimant, Sakka comes in the disguise of a brahmin and asks Vessantara to give him his wife. Vessantara agrees again. Maddī accepts the event within the story's moral universe, and Sakka reveals himself, praises the couple, and promptly gives her back. The two remain together in the hermitage.

This second petition is a strange mixture of protection and extreme testing. The god prevents Maddī from being taken by someone who might truly separate her from her husband, but the way he achieves this is by staging the very claim he wants to forestall. He asks whether Vessantara has anything left that he cannot give, then removes the immediate cost by returning her. The children receive no such instant release from their torment.

Jūjaka travels with Jāli and Kaṇhājinā. They are frightened and exhausted. He binds them for the night while keeping himself above danger. But the tale also allows protecting powers to watch over the pair. Instead of taking them directly to his wife's house, Jūjaka is guided, in ways he does not comprehend, toward the city where their grandfather King Sañjaya lives.

The children enter a royal setting from which their family had been banished. Sañjaya sees them and recognizes who they are. The discussion that follows concerns their freedom and the price that must be paid for it. Jāli explains the terms and makes clear that the children were given to Jūjaka. The royal family must now purchase back the grandchildren it once watched depart in exile. The king pays the ransom. The children are released from the brahmin's control.

The scene is not simply a magical reversal. Their return requires adults to recognize the children as people whose suffering has consequences. The grandfather's grief, the children's words, and the formal ransom stand between the gift and reunion. Jūjaka receives wealth and rich food. In the received account he indulges himself so excessively at the feast that he dies. His end is the narrative's grim and comic answer to a man who traveled so far to turn another household's children into his own possessions.

The royal family determines to bring Vessantara and Maddī home. They journey to the forest with the children. Along the way they carry all the signs of a ruler's return: attendants, ceremonial equipment, and the resources of the kingdom. But what makes the encounter powerful is not the pageantry. It is the moment when parents and children see each other again after a separation neither side could control.

Maddī catches sight of the children. The poem compares her movement to that of an animal mother rushing toward its young. The family embraces. Relief arrives with a force equal to the earlier loss. Vessantara's parents, Maddī, the children, and the assembled company come together in a reunion the story has prepared across many scenes of walking, waiting, and grief. The narrative insists that they are restored to one another, not that the earlier ordeal was painless.

The citizens of Sivi welcome Vessantara's return, and he resumes royal life. The auspicious elephant also belongs to the restored prosperity of the realm in the story's later account. There is divine rain, a shower of wealth, and the promise that the generous ruler can continue to give. These closing marvels present abundance as the answering image to exile. Where the prince once gave away the vehicle that carried his family, a kingdom now gathers resources around their return.

We have reached the work's narrative ending, but we have not dissolved its ethical difficulty. In its religious tradition, Vessantara's uncompromising giving is a paradigmatic perfection associated with the Buddha's long path to awakening. In the scenes themselves, the children tremble, hide, are beaten, and hope to see their mother again. Maddī spends a night searching. The grandfather has to ransom the family. The poem's reverence for the giver shares a page with the wounds of those who are given.

That tension is part of why the story has remained powerful across Buddhist cultures. It has been performed, painted, and narrated in more than one regional form, and it has prompted both devotion and ethical debate. Our lecture follows an identified Pāli witness; it does not pretend to speak for every Thai, Lao, Burmese, Cambodian, or Sri Lankan rendering. The names, emphases, staging, and local meanings can differ.

There is an old temptation when telling stories of saintly generosity: make the recipients simple symbols, and the household members merely possessions that prove a hero's virtue. The Vessantara tale resists that flattening more than a short synopsis admits. It gives Jāli and Kaṇhājinā voices. It gives Maddī a long night of fear. It gives Sañjaya the work of recovering people whom his kingdom could not protect from a father's vow.

The final image should therefore include all of them. A family has returned from the mountain, but not by one person's moral effort alone. It returns through a mother's endurance, children's survival, a grandfather's ransom, divine interventions, and public change of heart. Vessantara may stand at the center of the title. The cost of his generosity is visible in everyone who stands beside him at the end.
