# Sophocles, Oedipus Tyrannus: The King Who Investigated Himself

A full scene-following original narration of Sophocles' *Oedipus Tyrannus* (*Oedipus the King*), from the plague-stricken opening through the investigation, recognition, Jocasta's death and Oedipus's self-blinding. The plot and Greek line locators follow Richard C. Jebb's 1887 public-domain prose translation. The play's performed events are distinguished from backstory supplied by its characters and from later Theban plays.

The city of Thebes is dying. A plague has entered the houses, the fields, and the bodies of the people. Mothers cannot bring children safely into the world. Cattle perish. Families carry the dead away. Before the palace, citizens kneel with branches and prayers, hoping that their king can save them.

They have good reason to ask him. Oedipus once saved Thebes from the Sphinx, a creature whose riddle no one else could answer. He did not inherit the people's trust through a quiet succession. He won it by solving a problem that seemed impossible. Now another danger has come, and the city turns to the man whose intelligence brought it relief before.

Sophocles' *Oedipus Tyrannus*, often called *Oedipus the King*, begins at that moment. It is a tragedy about a ruler searching for the cause of a disaster and discovering that the disaster cannot be separated from his own life. Its power depends on much more than a famous riddle about fathers and sons. It depends on the conduct of the search: who knows what, which testimony can be trusted, why a person resists the truth, and why Oedipus cannot bring himself to stop asking questions even when the answers are becoming unbearable.

The first figure we meet is a priest speaking for the suffering city. He does not ask Oedipus to perform magic. He asks the king to act with the capacity that once saved them. Oedipus speaks as a ruler who feels their pain. He has already sent his brother-in-law Creon to the shrine of Apollo at Delphi to learn what the god requires. The king has not been idle, and he does not need the crowd to tell him that action is necessary.

Creon returns. The oracle has answered: a pollution lies upon the city. Someone in Thebes killed the former king Laius, and the murderer remains unpunished. The plague will not be lifted until this stain is dealt with. Oedipus asks why no one brought the murderer to justice years earlier. The Sphinx, Creon explains, occupied everyone's attention. Thebes survived one crisis without resolving the crime that preceded it.

A king's murder is not a matter of family grief alone. It concerns the right ordering of the city. Oedipus vows to investigate and announces that anyone who knows the truth must speak. He promises protection to an informant, even if the guilty person is a stranger. He also pronounces a curse on the killer and on those who conceal him. Unknowingly, he places himself under the same curse. That is the first grim turn in the play: what sounds like righteous public authority is already being directed against the speaker.

The chorus of Theban elders enters with prayers to Apollo, Artemis, and other gods. The tragedy's songs are not decorative pauses in a detective plot. They give the city a voice larger than any one character, frightened by the plague and uncertain how to read divine judgment. The chorus wants the city's suffering to end. It does not yet know what form the answer will take.

Oedipus summons Teiresias, an old blind prophet. If the god knows the murderer's identity, perhaps the seer will tell it. But Teiresias hesitates and tries to leave. He says that knowledge can be a fearful burden. To Oedipus, the refusal appears intolerable. People are dying. The one man who may be able to save them has come and now will not help. Oedipus grows angry and accuses him of protecting the killer, perhaps even of taking part in the crime.

At last Teiresias speaks: Oedipus himself is the man he seeks. The king is astonished, then furious. He takes the accusation not as a revelation but as proof of a plot. Perhaps Creon wants the throne. Perhaps the prophet has lent his sacred reputation to a political conspiracy. Oedipus points out that he solved the Sphinx's riddle through his own intelligence, not through Teiresias's prophecy. Why should a blind man claim to see more clearly than the ruler who rescued the city?

Teiresias answers with a reversal. Oedipus has eyes, but he does not see where he lives or whose company he keeps. Before the day is out he will know, and that knowledge will bring him darkness. The seer describes a man who will be discovered as both father and brother to the children in his house, son and husband to the woman he has married. Oedipus rejects the words. But the audience has heard the shape of the truth long before the evidence can be assembled.

Creon arrives to defend himself against the charge of treachery. He makes a reasonable political argument: as the king's trusted adviser, related to the royal family and able to influence policy, he already possesses much of the benefit of power without the danger of a crown. Why would he gamble this position for the burdens of ruling? Oedipus is not moved. His gift for detecting an enemy has become an inclination to see enemies among his friends.

Jocasta, Oedipus's wife, comes between the men before the quarrel becomes fatal. She asks what has brought them to such anger while their city remains in danger. The chorus, too, urges restraint. Creon is allowed to go, but suspicion survives. The scene matters because Oedipus is beginning to damage the very relationships through which good information might reach him. His determination to know is entangled with the need to preserve his own reputation.

Jocasta tries to comfort him. Prophets, she says, cannot always be trusted. She gives an example from her own life. An oracle once warned that Laius would be killed by a son born to them. But the child was exposed as an infant, his ankles fastened together, and Laius, according to the story she has heard, was killed later by strangers at a place where three roads meet. Why should Oedipus fear the seer's words when that old prophecy seems to have failed?

She means to quiet him. Instead, one detail strikes him like a blow: three roads. Oedipus asks questions. Where did Laius die? How did he look? How many men traveled with him? At first the answers are small pieces of ordinary memory. A place outside the city. An older man with a certain bearing. A company of attendants, with one survivor. Then Oedipus begins to tell Jocasta a story he has not told before.

He grew up in Corinth, believing himself the son of Polybus and Merope, the ruling couple there. At a feast someone insulted him by saying he was not his father's true child. He questioned his parents, who denied the claim, but the uncertainty would not release him. He went to Delphi, hoping for an answer about his birth. The oracle did not answer the question as he had asked it. Instead it foretold that he would kill his father and marry his mother.

Oedipus fled Corinth to prevent such a future. He would not return to the parents he loved if that meant risking the oracle's fulfillment. On the road he met a carriage and its party at a junction. A servant tried to force him aside. The older man in the carriage struck him. Oedipus fought back and killed the men he encountered. He has carried that violent episode as something that happened between strangers. Now Jocasta's report turns the road into a scene of possible royal murder.

There is one point of uncertainty. The survivor who told the story of Laius's death said that a band of robbers attacked. Oedipus remembers fighting a small party himself. He orders that this old survivor be brought to him. The man's testimony may settle whether the king is guilty of the murder he promised to punish. Notice the narrowing of the investigation. The city began by asking for the guilty person. Oedipus has moved toward examining the truth of his own past.

Jocasta still hopes to protect her husband from fear. She says again that prophecies may be unreliable. The chorus sings of the claims of divine law and the danger of arrogant disbelief. Different characters are now trying to defend different kinds of certainty: faith in oracles, trust in personal memory, confidence in political judgment, or the hope that a terrifying likeness might prove to be coincidence.

Then a messenger arrives from Corinth. Polybus, the king whom Oedipus believed to be his father, has died. The Corinthians want Oedipus to take his place. Jocasta greets the news with relief. If Polybus died of old age, Oedipus did not kill his father. One part of the prophecy seems impossible now. The messenger believes he brings comfort, perhaps even a reward-worthy message. But Oedipus still fears returning to Corinth because Merope is alive. The danger of marrying his mother remains.

The messenger laughs at that fear. There is no blood relation, he explains, between Oedipus and the royal couple of Corinth. He himself delivered the child to them. The baby had been given to him by a shepherd on the slopes of Cithaeron, with the ankles injured or bound. Polybus and Merope raised the child as their own.

For the messenger, this is excellent news. He can remove the young king's fear of incest and perhaps win his favor. He does not understand that the information has reopened a much older danger. The orphan's origin is unknown; the wounded feet match a history Oedipus has never understood. The name Oedipus itself is associated in the tradition with the swelling or injury of the feet. A messenger who expects gratitude has instead brought the last piece needed to turn a suspicion into a certainty.

Jocasta understands first. She hears the Corinthian account and sees how it joins with the child she once sent away, the crossroads, and the prophecy. She begs Oedipus not to continue. She asks him, in effect, to let the investigation end while he can still live without the final answer. He thinks she may fear discovering that he came from a humble family, a thought he is willing to accept. He wants to know his true birth, whatever its social rank. His blindness is not a failure of courage. It is the inability to imagine the particular truth another person already sees.

Jocasta leaves. The chorus briefly imagines a happier origin. Perhaps Oedipus is a child of the mountain, born of nymphs or a god. Such a birth would turn the riddle of his parentage into a cause for celebration. The moment is painful because it comes immediately before the opposite revelation. Hope is allowed to speak at the very edge of catastrophe.

The old shepherd arrives. He is reluctant. He tries to evade the questions, then to deny knowledge, then to keep silent. Oedipus threatens him until he answers. The shepherd was once a servant of Laius. The infant he handed to the Corinthian messenger had come from the royal house of Thebes. Jocasta had given him the child to expose on the mountain, because of the prophecy that her son would kill his father. The shepherd could not bring himself to kill the baby. He passed him on to someone else in the hope of saving his life.

No god appears to announce the truth. Oedipus has built it through testimony. A frightened servant, a traveling messenger, an old king's death at a crossroads, the memory of a wounded infant, and a warning received at Delphi now fit into a single terrible history. The man he killed was Laius, his father. The woman he married was Jocasta, his mother. The children in his household are also his half-siblings. In trying to flee the prophecy, he had traveled toward its fulfillment.

Oedipus cries out and rushes inside. The stage does not present every horror directly. A messenger reports what has happened in the palace. Jocasta has gone to her marriage chamber and hanged herself. Oedipus broke in and found her dead. Then he took the brooches from her clothing and drove them into his own eyes. He has made himself blind with his own hands. The audience knows these acts through a witness's words, not through the spectacle of the injuries being inflicted before them.

When Oedipus emerges, the chorus sees a changed man. The ruler who could not bear the words of a blind prophet now walks without sight. But that reversal must not be reduced to a clever punishment. He is grieving for Jocasta, for the parents who raised him, for the life he thought he lived, and for the children who will now carry the consequences of the discovery. His eyes were not the only thing that failed him. His world of relationships has collapsed, and every familiar name has taken on another meaning.

Oedipus begs to be driven away from Thebes. Creon returns, no longer the conspirator Oedipus imagined, but the man who must manage the city's survival. He will not decide the king's future merely on a furious impulse. He wants the divine instructions properly observed. Sophocles does not simply show an exile journey beginning. The play closes with Oedipus seeking removal while the terms of what follows remain under Creon's and the gods' authority.

Oedipus asks especially to see his daughters, Antigone and Ismene. He fears what the revelation will do to them: the scorn, the difficulty of marriage, the contamination that others may imagine follows them from the circumstances of their birth. He cannot undo what happened before they were born, and he cannot promise them a safe future. Their vulnerability becomes the measure of what his public fall will mean in private lives.

The chorus gives the last word to a hard lesson about human prosperity. A life may appear glorious until its end; no one should declare a mortal perfectly happy before seeing how that life is completed. This is not an invitation to enjoy the fall of the great. It is a warning that intelligence, power, and good fortune cannot secure any life against every reversal.

There is something morally difficult in Oedipus's character. He has acted violently. He insults and threatens people who resist his questions. He is capable of tyranny in the very inquiry he pursues for the city's benefit. Yet he also refuses to settle for a convenient false explanation. He would have spared himself enormous suffering by allowing the matter of his birth to remain hidden. The play never presents that refusal as merely foolish. The traits that make him an effective ruler—energy, confidence, relentless curiosity—also drive him past the last point from which he could turn back.

The tragedy is often discussed as a story about fate. In the play, the oracle's prediction is fulfilled, but the audience also watches choices: the parents expose a child, the young man flees Corinth, violence erupts at a crossroads, servants conceal and reveal what they know. The writing makes the link between those choices and the foretold outcome feel terrifying without explaining away responsibility. Sophocles does not hand us a formula that removes every ethical question once fate is named.

And the play is often reduced to the revelation that Oedipus killed his father and married his mother. But that revelation by itself is the plot's premise, not its achievement. The achievement is the unfolding of recognition. Each person arrives with information that seems to solve a small problem. The priest seeks an end to plague. Creon seeks purification. Teiresias wants to avoid speaking. Jocasta wants to soothe. A messenger wants to bring good news. A shepherd wants to escape an old responsibility. Oedipus wants a true answer. Together their intentions produce a truth none can control.

Thebes began with people praying before the palace gates. It ends with a man who saved the city, then learned that he had become the thing it needed to cast out. The plague is not cured onstage before our eyes. We are left with the king standing in the ruins of what he thought he knew, asking how he can live among the people who depended on him. The search has succeeded. The cost of success is the tragedy.

## Textual basis and source notes (not spoken)

- Sophocles, *Oedipus Tyrannus*, Greek lines 1–1530, received play; Richard C. Jebb's public-domain English prose translation (1887), as digitized by the Perseus Digital Library, is the principal English witness. Not a translation of all the Theban plays, and no events from the later *Oedipus at Colonus* are inserted.
- Lines 1–215: plague, priest, Creon/oracle, chorus. 216–462: proclamation and Teiresias. 513–697: Creon's defense and Jocasta's intervention. 698–910: Laius account and Oedipus's Corinth/crossroads story. 911–1185: Corinthian messenger, birth origins, Jocasta's recognition, old shepherd. 1186–1530: palace messenger, self-blinding, Creon, daughters and closing chorus.
- The death of Jocasta and the self-blinding of Oedipus are reported by an onstage messenger; they are not graphically acted in the extant drama. Oedipus requests exile; the play does not show a completed journey to Colonus.
- The Oedipus myth had other ancient versions. The theatrical audience could know the general backstory, but the events of this play must not be silently replaced with later summaries or modern psychoanalytic interpretations.
