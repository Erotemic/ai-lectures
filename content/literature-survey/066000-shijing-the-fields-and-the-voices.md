# Shijing: The Fields and the Voices

A narrated encounter with five identifiable poems from the received *Classic of Poetry*: 1, 6, 113, 154 and 155, without forcing the anthology into a single plot or a single authorial voice.

There are songs a court might wish everyone to sing, and songs that sound as though someone has had enough of the court. A lover sees water birds along a river. A bride approaches marriage beneath branches heavy with blossom. Farmers call their exploiters rats. A household counts its labors through the turning year. A bird, having lost its young, struggles to keep the rest of its nest from being destroyed. These voices do not come from one character moving through a single adventure. They live together in one of China's oldest and most influential anthologies, the *Shijing*, or *Classic of Poetry*.

The received collection contains 305 poems, organized into *Airs of the States*, *Minor Odes*, *Major Odes*, and *Hymns*. It was transmitted and interpreted over many centuries; some songs preserve early Zhou-era materials, but the anthology as received has a long textual and editorial history. Tradition associates its selection or arrangement with Confucius. That tradition is part of its reception, not a reason to state as historical fact that he personally chose every poem. Ancient commentators often assigned political lessons and episodes to apparently intimate songs. Modern readers sometimes prefer a direct emotional voice. The poems can sustain different readings, and an honest treatment should let the words and images come first.

Poem one is *Guan ju*, named for the cry of water birds. It opens beside a river, with birds calling in a manner that establishes a setting for human longing. A young man desires a woman described as good and fitting. He turns her image over by day and in restless sleep, longing for a meeting that has not yet come. Water plants appear, gathered this way and that, while music—strings, bells, drums—evokes a hoped-for union. The song's structure matters more than any invented exchange between named lovers. The poem does not give the woman a biography or record a wedding day. It lets the listener experience desire as a pattern of searching, imagining, and returning.

In centuries of commentary, *Guan ju* became an exemplary poem about regulated affection and honorable partnership. That is a powerful historical use of the poem, but it is not the same thing as evidence that its first singers intended every later moral explanation. Nor should we flatten the song into a modern courtship script in which we know exactly what each person privately wanted. We hear a voice naming an admired woman and imagining the musical joy of being joined to her. What began with the sound of birds becomes a relationship between the restless mind and the order it hopes to find.

Poem six, *Tao yao*, often translated by reference to the peach tree, has a more celebratory rhythm. The branches are full of blossoms, then ripening fruit, then abundant leaves. Each flourishing image accompanies a wish for a woman as she marries and enters a new household. The poem does not need a plot twist. A marriage is already a passage between families, and the tree's change from flower to fruit to leaf gives the short song a sense of growth and continuation. It is easy to see why such a poem could become familiar in ceremonies. Yet it also makes the young woman's future household, rather than a private declaration of romantic passion, its principal measure of happiness.

Now listen to number 113, *Shuo shu*, the Great Rats. The speaking voice is not preparing a wedding. It addresses enormous rats that eat grain yet return no care to the people who produce it. The accusation repeats in successive stanzas, changing millet to wheat and seedlings, and each time the singers threaten to leave for a better country, a place where their labor will no longer be swallowed. Read as an attack upon exploitative rulers, the rats are powerful political figures. Read as a rustic curse, they are creatures made outrageous by the directness of complaint. In either case, a repeated image turns hunger and extraction into a collective voice: we feed you; you do not care for us; we may go.

The poem does not identify its exact tax collector, landlord, or date. It would be irresponsible to assign it without qualification to one demonstrable peasant rebellion. What it does preserve is a form of social criticism spoken through an animal metaphor. Its promise of a happy land may be a real destination or a place the singers need to imagine in order to say they deserve better. That uncertainty is not an obstacle to hearing it. We know the force of its complaint because the poem repeats the conditions of an unfair relationship until departure becomes its only imaginable answer.

The longest of our selections is number 154, *Seventh Month*, from the *Airs of Bin*. Here time is the principal character. The poem follows months through cold, thaw, sowing, silkworm work, weaving, harvest, hunting, storing food, repairing houses, and serving the ruling household. Its calendar can be confusing in translation because it refers to an older seasonal reckoning, and the order of tasks sometimes circles backward to establish a whole year's pattern. Rather than a notebook of precisely measured farm dates, it is a poem that makes a life audible by naming work as it returns.

At the first sign of cold, people worry about clothing. Children and adults bend toward the fields. Women gather mulberry leaves to feed silkworms. Later the cloth is dyed in different colors, some of it destined for those of higher standing. Hunting produces furs for a lord; the workers keep a different share. Crickets draw closer to human dwellings as the weather changes, moving from fields to walls and finally beneath a bed. The poem is attentive not only to food and cloth, but to the way a seasonal change alters the sound and space of a household. Cold is not a metaphor when a door needs sealing and a family needs to survive the year.

The calendar also reveals hierarchy. Men hunt and labor; women gather and spin; the ruler's clothes and stores take the finished results. Some stanzas celebrate ritual feasting and the wine that accompanies gathering. Others sound exhausted by obligations that do not end with the harvest: once crops are put away, workers must turn to building and maintenance. The poem's recurrent movement toward service and seasonal abundance can be heard as praise of a well-ordered agrarian society, but we should not pretend its workers live without pressure. Their labor is what allows the final celebratory hall to fill.

Our fifth selection, number 155, *Chi xiao*, the Owl, takes us to a nest. The speaker, a bird, protests against an owl that has taken its young and asks that the remaining home be spared. It gathers materials, strengthens the doors, and struggles in the wind and rain until its voice seems exhausted. It is tempting to add elaborate conversations and name the guilty human figures. But the words themselves give us a vulnerable creature engaged in urgent repair. In later Zhou political interpretation, the poem became entangled with the Duke of Zhou and an appeal concerning his rule. That is a distinct interpretive tradition. Within the speaking image we are first asked to hear the labor of making a fragile shelter safe after a loss.

These five poems move from desire to marriage, complaint, labor, and protective grief. Their forms are different, and their speakers are not members of one extended fictional family. The water birds do not grow up to become the peach bride; the farmers are not secretly the inhabitants of the owl's nest. Bringing the poems into one lecture is a way of listening across a collection, not a way of merging them into a false epic. Their recurring images do something an ordinary list of moral teachings cannot do: they make desire move with the river, exploitation squeak through the figure of a rat, and fear take the shape of a damaged nest.

The *Classic of Poetry* would go on to become central to education, ritual culture, and literary conversation in China. Its poems were cited as political evidence, memorized as models of expression, and reinterpreted through successive commentaries. But before any commentary, their sounds place us near bodies working and wishing in particular landscapes. A listener who hears only royal instruction misses the complaint of the hungry. A listener who hears only anonymous folk emotion misses the uses to which an anthology of such songs could be put. The collection's importance lies partly in that tension: centuries of people heard these short voices and found reasons to argue about what they meant.

## Source companion (not narrated)

- Primary received anthology: *Shijing* / *Classic of Poetry*, 305 poems. [Chinese originals with James Legge's public-domain English translation](https://xinxingren.gen.nz/classics/%E8%A9%A9%E7%B6%93_Book_of_Odes.html). This lecture independently paraphrases selected scenes, not a complete translation of every stanza.
- Individual numbered texts: #1 關雎 (*Guan ju*, *Airs of Zhou and South*), #6 桃夭 (*Tao yao*), #113 碩鼠 (*Shuo shu*), #154 七月 (*Qi yue* / *Seventh Month*), #155 鴟鴞 (*Chi xiao* / *Owl*). Numbers follow the standard received 305-poem sequence.
- Text-critical and editorial context: [Columbia University Press, *The Classic of Poetry*](https://cup.columbia.edu/book/the-classic-of-poetry/9789882374263/) for editions and study; note that later traditional political assignments and the Confucius editorial legend cannot be assumed to describe original performance contexts.
- Orientation: [Wikipedia, Classic of Poetry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Classic_of_Poetry). Do not assume all songs were written at one date or treat allegorical identifications as proven historical event descriptions.
