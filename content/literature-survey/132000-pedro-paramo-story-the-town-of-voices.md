# Pedro Páramo — Story: The Town That Remembered Its Dead

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

Juan Preciado comes to Comala because his mother has died. Dolores had spoken of the town as a place she once knew intimately: a valley of houses, gardens, rain, and the ordinary promise that life might continue. She has also spoken of Pedro Páramo, the father who abandoned them and who owes them something more than a greeting. Near the end of her life she asks her son to go there and seek what was denied them. Juan agrees, perhaps more because she is dying than because he believes he will find anything.

The road to Comala descends through heat. Juan meets Abundio Martínez, a man driving burros, and asks about the way. Abundio knows the town and knows Pedro Páramo. He tells Juan that Pedro fathered him, too. Then, with a matter-of-factness that makes the news even more disturbing, he indicates that Pedro has been dead for years. Juan is looking for a father he cannot meet. He keeps going anyway, toward the houses his mother once described.

Comala does not match her memory. The streets feel abandoned. Sounds carry from places where nobody should be. A woman appears and vanishes. Juan is guided to Eduviges Dyada, who says she was his mother's friend. She receives him as though his arrival were expected, yet the rooms and conversations seem to belong to an earlier time. Eduviges tells him about Dolores, her marriage, and the way hopes can turn into a life spent waiting for someone who will never act decently. In the night there are noises and voices that no ordinary guest can identify. One sound belongs, or seems to belong, to a horse moving restlessly after its rider has died.

The novel begins to open other times while Juan is still trying to understand the town before him. A boy named Pedro Páramo remembers Susana San Juan, the girl he loved when he was young. The rain, the school days, the beauty of her presence—these memories come from a Comala still alive. It is strange to hear such tenderness associated with the man whom everyone else seems to remember as an owner, a threat, a power that crushed the place. But people do not become tyrants by ceasing to have desires. Sometimes they turn every other human being into the means of satisfying one desire.

Juan meets or hears of Damiana Cisneros, who once worked for Pedro at the great estate called the Media Luna. Her presence is no more secure than Eduviges's. The town appears to offer one explanation and withdraw it, as if every witness is speaking from a room that time has already emptied. Juan wanders, overhears voices from walls and alleys, and tries to make those voices into an account. He finds shelter among a strange isolated couple, Donis and a woman who is both his sister and his partner. Their house is close, airless, and filled with shame. The woman speaks as though guilt marks her body. Juan can no longer trust the boundary between a person in front of him, an old memory, and a figure shaped by fear.

The murmuring becomes unbearable. He cannot sleep or breathe freely. He loses the sense that there is a road by which he could climb back out of Comala. Then the story makes clear that the voice with which we entered the novel has itself been speaking from death. Juan has died, overwhelmed by the town and its whispers. He has been buried beside Dorotea, a woman whose own life was marked by deprivation and longing. From their shared grave, their conversation continues. They listen to voices from other graves, to remembered speech, to stories still waiting to be told. The town's history will not arrive as a document. It will arrive through the people who cannot stop remembering it.

Far back in the story Pedro inherits the Media Luna from his father Lucas, along with debts and the need to consolidate an estate. He has learned that an owner need not possess every field by straightforward purchase. Fulgor Sedano, the estate manager, helps him turn obligation into leverage. Land can be acquired through pressure; old claims can be made to disappear; a person can be induced to sell or to surrender when refusal becomes too dangerous. There is a family named Preciado to whom the estate owes money. Pedro decides that marriage to Dolores will solve the debt and improve his position. What seems to Dolores a marriage between people becomes to Pedro a transaction between property interests.

Dolores gives birth to Juan. But love and security do not follow from the wedding. Pedro grows impatient with her, and she eventually leaves the Media Luna. The son grows up with his mother and with the memory of a town she tells him about as a lost home. Many years later he will arrive in Comala seeking the man who made their life what it was. At the time of Pedro's rise, however, the future is not visible. What is visible is a landlord accumulating land, power, and control over the livelihoods of other families.

Pedro has other children. One of them, Miguel, is recognized and indulged. He rides recklessly, abuses people, commits violence against women, and benefits from the power of his father's name. The parish priest, Father Rentería, knows some of his crimes personally. Miguel has destroyed lives close to the priest, including that of his brother and the safety of his niece Ana. Yet when Miguel is killed in a riding accident, the priest is pressured by the rich father's gifts and position. He struggles between a demand for justice and the church's sacramental duties, and his decisions reveal how far the church in Comala has been drawn into the estate's power.

Dorotea, the woman who will one day share Juan's grave, has lived on the margins of this same world. Her desire for a child is exploited by her own hopes, while her poverty leaves her vulnerable to humiliating arrangements. She has served Miguel's appetites by procuring women for him. The novel does not present the wrongs committed in Comala as the work of one hand alone. Pedro can command, but others cooperate, look aside, compromise, and sometimes persuade themselves that the compromises are unavoidable. Some people benefit from proximity to his power. Many cannot escape its consequences.

All this time Pedro remembers Susana San Juan. As a boy he loved her before he owned the valley. Now she has been away for years and returns with a life that cannot be folded into his memories of her. Her father, Bartolomé San Juan, is a miner, and Susana has endured terrible experiences and loss. She remembers another man, Florencio, with an intimacy that Pedro cannot reach. Her recollections move between tenderness, fear, the sea, and the dead. A childhood bond does not give Pedro the right to her mind or her past, but he has become a man who confuses having someone within his walls with possessing them.

Pedro maneuvers to have Susana at the Media Luna. Her father disappears in circumstances shadowed by Pedro's control. Pedro marries Susana and watches over her, hoping for a return of a girl who now exists only in his memory. Susana lives in another emotional world. Her speech and dreams turn repeatedly toward Florencio and the experiences that Pedro never shared. His vast property can keep servants at the house and buy the help of the priest; it cannot make Susana love him. When she dies, the loss is not something Pedro can settle, purchase, or avenge against one rival.

Outside the estate, Mexico is changing. Armed movements associated with the Revolution cross the countryside. Pedro deals with these fighters as he deals with almost everyone: he calculates how much they need, what he can give them, and how they might be turned toward his interest. Money, armed men, and the ability to outlast a particular faction help the Media Luna survive. The arrival of revolution does not instantly end the local arrangements of domination. Men die; others replace them. The land still has an owner who believes he can decide the fate of its people.

At Susana's death, church bells ring for a long time. People from the region hear them and gather, interpreting the noise as a reason for celebration. Music and festivities spread while Pedro grieves. He sees a town celebrating when he believes it should be silent. He answers with a punishment on the scale of his power. He will no longer sustain Comala's life. He withdraws his labor and resources and lets hunger and neglect finish the destruction that years of coercion have begun. The lively place Dolores remembered becomes the empty, scorched place where her son will hear only voices.

Years pass. Pedro grows old and increasingly isolated. The younger men around him die or move away. In one of the novel's final movements Abundio returns, grieving the loss of his wife and seeking help with the costs of death. This is the same Abundio who will guide Juan toward Comala, and another son whom Pedro has never truly acknowledged. Abundio is desperate and drunk. His encounter with Pedro turns violent; Pedro is stabbed and collapses, his life ending without the grandeur that his power once commanded. The man who owned so much is reduced to a failing body.

This death occurs before Juan's descent, though the novel saves it for the end. The beginning and the ending have been speaking to one another all along. A son arrives searching for a father. Another son, without recognition or resources, has already killed him. Between them is an entire town turned into memory, with no single voice authorized to tell its story. Juan and Dorotea listen from below ground; Susana speaks from a chamber of memories Pedro never entered; the former servants, lovers, victims, and accomplices continue to sound through the emptied streets.

We cannot leave Comala with a neat account in which every ghost is explained. The novel has shown enough to tell us why the town died, but it has not allowed its people to become merely evidence in a case against Pedro. They retain their competing memories and unfinished longings. Pedro tried to make a place and everyone in it answer to his will. After his death the voices remain beyond his reach. A listener who went down the road to find one father has heard instead the lives of a community.

**Original work:** Juan Rulfo, *Pedro Páramo*, first published by Fondo de Cultura Económica in Mexico City in 1955; recommended authorized editions include the [Fondo de Cultura Económica / Fundación Juan Rulfo publication history documented in the *Enciclopedia de la Literatura en México*](https://www.elem.mx/obra/datos/2838). A copyrighted modern novel, not a public-domain text. This original English survey is a **selective interpretive plot account**, not a translation, performance of the novel, or chapter-by-chapter substitute. The complete primary novel was not reproduced or exhaustively collated during this content pass. Weatherford's English translation](https://www.harvardreview.org/book-review/pedro-paramo/); [Jason Wilson, “Pedro Páramo by Juan Rulfo,” *The Cambridge Companion to the Latin American Novel*](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/abs/cambridge-companion-to-the-latin-american-novel/pedro-paramo-by-juan-rulfo/5346A1C16BF51E58906DF8BF0AFA133A); [Wikipedia: Pedro Páramo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pedro_P%C3%A1ramo).

**Plot checkpoints for later source collation:** Juan's deathbed promise to Dolores; guide Abundio and the revelation of Pedro's death; Eduviges, Damiana, Donis and the unsteady town; Juan's death and Dorotea as listener from the grave; young Pedro and Susana; Fulgor's estate calculations and the Dolores marriage; Miguel's violence and Father Rentería; Susana and Florencio; revolution at the Media Luna; bells, abandonment of Comala, and Abundio's final encounter with Pedro. **Do not assign a universal numbered-chapter structure**: the original novel is a sequence of unnumbered fragments, and editors disagree in how fragments are counted.

**Interpretive boundaries:** Susana's speech is not evidence for a single clear medical diagnosis. Her father's disappearance and some other events are mediated by partial accounts; avoid claiming that the narrative supplies a documentary criminal trial. Historical rural Mexico and the Mexican Revolution are settings and critical contexts, not a license to date every fictive episode precisely.
