# The Golden Ass, Part Two: The Lamp and the Trials of Psyche

A continuous retelling of the inset Cupid-and-Psyche story in Apuleius's *Metamorphoses*, beginning Book IV and continuing through Book VI. The story is told by an old woman inside the robbers' cave to the captive bride Charite; it is not presented as an independent ancient Greek epic or a self-sufficient book by Apuleius.

Inside the robbers' cave, the abducted young woman Charite is terrified. The old woman tending the thieves tries to distract her with a tale of another young woman whose marriage seems to have gone terribly wrong. Lucius, the educated traveler trapped in a donkey's body, hears it too. No one knows that a speaking human consciousness is listening from behind an animal face.

There was once a king with three daughters. Two were beautiful enough to attract marriage proposals. The youngest, Psyche, seemed to people more beautiful than any mortal could rightfully be. Travelers came to see her. Instead of paying honor to Venus, they offered admiration to Psyche, as though a living girl's face had replaced the goddess's shrine. Yet the worship did not bring the girl a happy life. No one dared approach her as an ordinary prospective wife. Her sisters married; Psyche remained alone, stared at from a distance by people who feared to come near.

Venus heard what people were saying and grew furious. She summoned her son Cupid, who could make humans and gods love against their better judgment, and ordered him to punish the girl. Psyche's father sought divine advice about the extraordinary unhappiness of his beautiful child. An oracle gave a devastating instruction: dress her as a bride, lead her to the edge of a mountain, and leave her there to marry a fearsome being. Her parents obeyed, grieving as they carried out the ritual. Psyche went up the mountain in bridal dress, and the procession turned back without her.

The west wind lifted her gently and set her down in a valley. She came to an impossible palace with treasures everywhere, rich rooms, and tables that filled with food. Voices without bodies offered to serve her. The building was beautiful, but it also made her loneliness stranger: she had comfort, privacy, and no clear account of who controlled the place. At night someone came to her bed. He was tender to her and made her his wife, but insisted that she must never look upon him. She could hear his words and feel his presence; she could not see his face.

For a while their life was happy within that restriction. Then Psyche asked to see her sisters. Her mysterious husband warned that the visit would bring danger, but agreed. The sisters entered the glittering palace and saw wealth beyond anything they possessed. They embraced Psyche and then went home consumed with envy. When they returned, they began to poison her certainty. Who was this husband who refused to be seen? How could she know he was not the monstrous creature described in the oracle? Might he intend to devour her when she was too helpless to escape?

Psyche resisted and then believed them. Her sisters supplied a plan: hide a lamp and a sharp blade, wait until the husband sleeps, see what he really is, and kill him if the thing in the bed is dangerous. The young wife faced an intolerable choice. If the warnings were true, she was in mortal danger; if false, she would betray the person she loved. She prepared the lamp.

In the night she uncovered the flame and held it above the bed. The supposed monster was Cupid himself, beautiful and sleeping. The danger she feared was not the creature foretold by the sisters but the act she was now committing. In her amazement, she leaned close. Hot oil spilled from the lamp onto Cupid's shoulder. He woke wounded, saw that she had looked, and flew away. A rule that had held their world together was broken. Whether that rule was fair is a question the story does not have to settle for us. It shows both Psyche's fear and the injury of distrust.

Psyche tried to follow him and fell. The palace disappeared. She wandered in search of her husband, but the world was now full of gods with competing loyalties. The sisters' envy, meanwhile, had made them rash: each imagined she might take the place Psyche had lost. Their confidence in the west wind as a willing servant led them to leap from heights to their deaths. The narrative is cruel to them, and we need not smooth that cruelty into a mild children's warning about jealousy.

Venus learned what had happened and punished Cupid for his disobedience. She also pursued Psyche, furious that a mortal had become her rival and then entangled her son in trouble. When Psyche finally came under the goddess's power, Venus assigned tasks that appeared impossible for a mortal girl to perform.

First Psyche was handed a vast heap of mixed seeds and grains. She had to sort everything before evening. It was a servant's work expanded until it became absurdly difficult. Psyche sat before the pile in despair. Ants saw her predicament and came in an organized army; each small creature carried a fragment into its proper place, until the task was complete. Venus returned and refused to accept that Psyche could have finished unaided. That suspicion was correct; yet the source of assistance lies outside the goddess's ability to command all things.

Next she was told to obtain golden wool from rams so violent that approaching them would mean death. Psyche moved toward the water in despair, but a voice from the reeds told her to wait until the animals had passed into shade and quiet. She could then gather tufts caught among the branches where the rams had rubbed themselves. The work required patience rather than a contest of strength. Psyche succeeded by understanding when a task was possible and when it was not.

The third task was more dreadful. Venus demanded water from a high and dangerous spring, associated with the waters of the underworld. The rock was steep, and monstrous guardians blocked the approach. Psyche could not climb safely or reach the stream. An eagle intervened, took the vessel, and fetched what she needed. Yet another creature accomplished what a solitary human could not.

For the final task, Psyche had to go into the realm of the dead. She was to ask Proserpina, queen of the underworld, to place some of her beauty in a little box and bring it back unopened. Psyche thought of death as the only way down. Instead, a tower spoke and told her a way to enter and return alive. She must prepare two coins and two cakes, pay the ferryman, placate the guardian dog, and refuse distractions that would consume the means of her return. In the house of the dead she would receive the box, but she must not open it.

She managed the journey. In a different tale, that would be triumph enough. But on the way back, almost free of danger, Psyche's old curiosity returned. How could she appear before Cupid after so much suffering without borrowing a little of the beauty sealed within? Surely she could open the container, take a trace, and close it. She lifted the lid. Inside there was not beauty she could wear, but a sleep like death. It overcame her and she fell where she stood.

Cupid had not ceased to love her. When his injury healed and he escaped the confinement imposed by Venus, he found Psyche unconscious. He put the deadly sleep back where it belonged and revived her. This time he did not offer a hidden palace and a prohibition. He flew to Jupiter to seek a settlement in which Psyche could become his wife openly. Jupiter assembled the gods, raised Psyche to divine status, and authorized the marriage. The girl who had once been treated like an idol of beauty became a goddess not because the crowd had been right to worship her, but because divine favor finally settled her precarious place among gods and mortals. Their child is named Pleasure.

What kind of story have we heard? The arrangement resembles a fairy tale: an unknown bridegroom, jealous sisters, repeated impossible tasks, animal helpers, a descent to death, a final marriage. It is also embedded in a novel where magic has already turned an eager young man into an abused donkey. Psyche's longing to see her husband parallels Lucius's determination to see Pamphile transform. Each thinks that knowledge just beyond the limit of ordinary vision will grant safety or power. Each obtains sight and loses a world.

Yet the parallel has limits. Lucius was looking for magic to entertain himself; Psyche was pressured by fear, loneliness, and the counsel of people she believed she should trust. Lucius's transformation leaves him at the mercy of strangers, while Psyche's adventures require gods and creatures to help her again and again. There is no single moral that will do justice to both.

The story is especially moving when we remember its first audience. Charite is waiting in a robber's cave after being seized from her wedding. The old storyteller asks her to imagine a bride carried into the unknown, separated from her beloved, punished, endangered, and finally reunited. The comfort is not that all marriages are safe, or that every captor will prove to be Cupid. It is a fictional future in which separation is not final. Lucius hears the same assurance while still wearing the wrong body. At the end of the tale he remains a donkey, and the robbers still own him. The animal listener has learned that divine rescue is possible within stories; his own rescuing has not yet come.
