# Kebra Nagast - Analysis: Who Gets to Carry Zion?

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

Imagine being told that your kingdom has been placed at the edge of a story told by someone else. The wise king lives elsewhere; the sacred law comes from elsewhere; the great ancestral events happened elsewhere. Now imagine a book that does not deny the old story, but makes it turn in your direction. A queen travels to a famous king and learns from him. Her son returns with priests and a sacred treasure. What was once the center becomes the point of departure, and the land to which the treasure travels becomes the new setting of divine favor. This is the principal literary achievement of the Kebra Nagast. It does not merely attach Ethiopia to biblical history. It reorganizes that history around an Ethiopian claim.

The title is usually rendered Glory of Kings. Its language is Ge'ez, the classical Ethiopian literary and liturgical language. The text survives as a medieval Christian compilation that brings together narrative, law, royal genealogy, biblical interpretation, prophecy, and polemic. A fourteenth-century context for the received work is widely defended in modern scholarship, although discussions of older traditions and the relation of manuscript layers are complicated. None of that makes the Solomon and Sheba episode a contemporary record of the ancient events it depicts. The book belongs to the history of medieval Ethiopian imagination and kingship even when it tells stories set many centuries earlier.

Why did that setting matter? In the late thirteenth century a ruling line identified as Solomonic established itself in the Ethiopian highlands. Yekuno Amlak's accession is conventionally dated to around 1270. Dynastic succession is never only a matter of blood. Someone must also explain why one family should be obeyed, what its authority means, and how it relates to law, land, and religion. A story about descent from Solomon could make that claim ancient; a story about the Ark crossing into Ethiopia could make it sacred. Pierluigi Piovanelli has argued that the Kebra Nagast is best understood as an early fourteenth-century intervention in the religious and political legitimation of such elites, not as evidence that those elites really descended from an ancient king of Jerusalem.

The work borrows material from a passage many listeners already know, but with striking freedom. First Kings describes a queen from Sheba who hears of Solomon's fame, tests him with questions, sees his wisdom and riches, and departs after an exchange of gifts. It does not recount a sexual encounter or a son. The Kebra Nagast gives the queen the name Makeda, extends her journey into a conversion, describes Solomon's manipulation of her, and produces a child who becomes an ancestor of kings. Where the biblical account is almost a diplomatic visit, this book creates a genealogy, a ceremony of succession, and the migration of the Ark. The addition is not a forgotten paragraph we can quietly insert into the Bible. It is a new literary construction with its own purposes.

There are other ways the Solomon-and-Sheba material has traveled. The Qur'an tells of a queen who receives news from Solomon, tests the situation around his court, and comes to acknowledge divine authority; in that scriptural account she is not the mother of a Solomonic heir. Jewish traditions and later Islamic stories also elaborate the queen in different ways. We should not arrange these into a simple ladder of increasingly correct versions. They are related textual traditions, produced in different communities with different interests. The Kebra Nagast belongs among them, but what distinguishes it is the claim that the queen's descendants and the Ark bring an inheritance specifically into Ethiopia.

The name Makeda is important for more than character identification. She is a ruler who acts before any son appears. She listens to Tamrin's report, commands a journey, talks with Solomon, and returns to govern. Without her curiosity there would be no meeting. Without her continued rule there would be no Ethiopian throne to which the son could return. Yet the text also makes her body the channel through which the father's ancestry is transmitted, and it gives her less control over the encounter that produces the heir than its celebration of the dynasty might suggest. The book needs a powerful queen and simultaneously builds a story that permits authority to pass from her toward male succession. That unresolved tension should remain in any serious overview.

The water episode makes the tension unmistakable. Makeda asks not to be taken by force. Solomon swears, then makes her swear not to take his possessions. He has arranged for her to be thirsty and knows she will want water. He waits until she reaches for it and uses her need to extract release from his promise. The narrator admires Solomon's intelligence, but readers need not confuse ingenious manipulation with freely given consent. Some retellings leave out the thirst and transform the episode into a tender affair between two rulers. That makes the work easier to celebrate, but it also destroys one of the clearest places where its assumptions about power become visible.

Its most consequential character is not, however, a man or woman. It is the object called Zion. To modern ears the name may suggest a city, a hill, a community, or a political claim. Here it often denotes the Ark of the Covenant, the tabernacle of the law, and a sacred presence associated with Israel's relationship to God. The book also speaks of a heavenly Zion, and its Christian interpretation can connect Zion with Mary and with salvation in Christ. These meanings should not be flattened into a single wooden chest. The movement between material object and theological symbol is how the book makes a traveling possession into a cosmic argument.

Consider how the Ark leaves Jerusalem. If the tale had merely said that an Ethiopian prince stole it, the conclusion would be morally and legally unstable. Why should theft confer rightful spiritual authority? The narrative solves that problem by placing the decisive action in a vision granted to Azariah. An angel announces that God has willed the removal; the priests make the substitution; the object travels under miraculous assistance. The human actions still look like a secret removal, but the book places divine command behind them. It narrates the difference between what worldly guardians think they own and where God chooses to dwell. Whether a modern reader accepts that divine command as real is separate from understanding why the text needs it.

The Ark's departure also reorganizes the portrait of Solomon. At the beginning his wisdom seems almost inexhaustible. He knows how to rule, how to teach, and how to bend a situation to his purpose. By the midpoint he has lost his son to Ethiopia; by the next movement he has lost the Ark. The young companions he selected are the very people who carry Zion away. His wisdom becomes limited by a destiny he cannot anticipate. This reversal can be read as theology: God's will exceeds that of the greatest human king. It can also be read as political literature: a monarch who already commands universal admiration gives up, however unwillingly, the thing that makes the Ethiopian monarchy glorious.

The narrator insists that loss has reasons. Solomon is judged through biblical patterns in which royal temptation, religious compromise, and the behavior of his household have consequences for the kingdom. Much of the Kebra Nagast is made of rereadings of scriptural episodes, not new dramatic incidents. It connects Solomon's later life to stories from Kings, and it recasts the fate of sacred favor as something that can pass from one people to another. These passages include harsh rhetoric about Jews and other religious communities. In the work those claims support a medieval Christian version of supersession: one community is said to inherit a role another has forfeited. They are evidence about the authorial world of the text, not a factual assessment of the communities disparaged.

What might it mean to read a text of profound religious importance that also contains language capable of injuring other people? We can begin by refusing the choice between total endorsement and complete dismissal. We can describe its theology correctly, including how powerfully it makes an Ethiopian Christian audience the agent of sacred history. We can also name the hostile devices it uses, their targets, and their historical conditions. A critical reading does not need to strip away the work's faith. It needs to keep faith, textual art, royal interest, and prejudice distinguishable even when the text itself binds them together.

The section on the return to Ethiopia shows why the book is more than a story of bloodlines. Makeda welcomes her son, noble companions take oaths, and Azariah instructs the people about conduct. The passage is full of small obligations: help a neighbor with a fallen load, return a wandering animal, protect the vulnerable from fraud, treat the poor fairly. Much of this comes from biblical law. It is easy to miss because the bigger images are so theatrical: sun, Ark, angel, king, procession. Yet the narrative uses those images to introduce a particular moral and institutional order. A dynasty is legitimate, it argues, not only because of its ancestor but because its community receives and maintains the law.

There is also a problem of historical time. The narrative takes place in the age of Solomon, but its narrating consciousness knows about Christ, the Virgin Mary, councils of bishops, and divisions of the Christian world. It moves between an ancient setting and later theological claims without trying to disguise the difference. Modern historical fiction often works hard to keep characters from knowing the future. The Kebra Nagast uses prophecy and typology precisely to make the future present within the past. The Ark's earlier journey prepares for Christian sacred history; events in Israel are reread as signs that their meaning will be disclosed later. The writer is not trying to preserve the psychological limits of an ancient court. The writer is showing how many ages belong to a single divine pattern.

The final chapters make this especially clear. Having brought the Ark home, the work does not stop. It speaks about Christ, the heavenly city, and the relationship of Ethiopia to the realm it calls Rome. This is a vision of Christian world order, not an appendix accidentally attached to a successful adventure. The legendary prince and his mother's throne are now joined to an argument about who should govern and worship in the world that follows the biblical past. The Kebra Nagast operates on at least three scales at once: the family, the kingdom, and the universe. One of its great compositional skills is to make the same object, Zion, travel across all three.

We should be careful with translations. E. A. Wallis Budge's 1922 English version is a useful complete public-domain textual witness, and its numbered chapters make it possible to check where a scene occurs. It is still an early twentieth-century translation with its own editorial terminology and decisions. It does not eliminate the need for modern Ge'ez scholarship, manuscript comparison, or attention to Ethiopian interpretation. This overview is based on that named English edition and separately identified scholarship; it is not a collation of surviving Ge'ez manuscripts. That limitation is especially important when a technical theological term or politically charged phrase is doing much of the interpretive work.

Nor should we talk as though the book represented the whole of Ethiopia. It is situated in particular religious and royal projects. Ethiopian peoples, regions, and religious communities have never been reducible to a single medieval document, and the histories of Jewish, Muslim, and Christian communities in the Horn of Africa cannot be derived from its polemics. Likewise, the existence of important Ethiopian traditions concerning the Ark does not establish that archaeology has verified the object described in the book. A living cultural and religious claim deserves accurate description; its historical demonstration requires a different kind of evidence.

Finally, consider its relation to other epics in this library. In the Aeneid, a displaced people carry household gods toward a future city whose history seems destined. In the Shahnameh, a line of kings bears an enormous weight of memory and moral failure. The Kebra Nagast creates a related drama of inheritance, but its decisive movement goes against what a reader of the Bible may expect: a sacred center is not guarded by staying put. It moves. And the people who receive it have to explain what that movement permits and demands. This comparison is a way to see the work's literary shape, not proof of direct borrowing between those poems.

So the book's question is not simply whether the Queen of Sheba had a son. Its harder question is how a community can claim a place inside a sacred past that at first seems to belong elsewhere. It answers by sending a queen outward, bringing a son back, and allowing Zion to leave a king whose wisdom had seemed unsurpassable. The result has served as a source of pride, devotion, and political legitimacy. It has also invited historical criticism and ethical scrutiny. A careful reader can understand all those responses without mistaking legend for archive, power for proof, or eloquence for innocence.

## Text and evidence companion (not spoken)

### Source and interpretive boundaries

The named English witness is E. A. Wallis Budge (1922); no critical collation of Ge'ez manuscript variants was performed. The fourteenth-century and post-1270 dynastic setting is a modern scholarly argument (especially Piovanelli 2013), not the chronology claimed by the story. Biblical 1 Kings 10 and Quran 27 are comparison texts, not substitute plot sources. Ark transfer and ancient descent are narrated traditions rather than archaeological conclusions. The narrative's supersessionism, coercive water scene, and exclusions are discussed as features of the text and not endorsed.

### Text and scholarship locators

- The name Makeda and developed Ethiopian parentage: *Kebra Nagast* chapters 21-34 versus 1 Kings 10:1-13.
- Water bargain and coercion: *Kebra Nagast* chapters 29-31.
- Solomon's dream and limits of wisdom: chapters 30-31, 58-70.
- Zion's theological meaning: chapters 10-18, 45-55, 68-71, 96-117.
- The alleged transfer of Zion and authorized action: chapters 45-60.
- Makeda's political continuity, vows and law: chapters 84-92.
- The Solomonic dynasty and ideological context: Piovanelli, *Aethiopica* 16 (2013), and modern Ethiopian history.
- Supersession, prophecy, Christian kingdoms: chapters 64-71, 93-117.
- Distinct Quranic Sheba narrative: Quran 27:20-44.
