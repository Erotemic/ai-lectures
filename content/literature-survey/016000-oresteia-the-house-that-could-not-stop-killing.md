# Oresteia: The House That Could Not Stop Killing

A continuous, original dramatic narration of Aeschylus's three surviving tragedies *Agamemnon*, *Libation Bearers*, and *Eumenides*, first performed at Athens in 458 BCE. The three plays are followed in their order, using H. W. Smyth's 1926 translation for scene/line cross-checks. The fourth festival play, the satyr drama *Proteus*, is lost; its contents are not reconstructed here.

A man lies awake on the roof of a palace. He has done this night after night for nearly a year, searching the distant hills for a signal fire. If the fire appears, it will mean that Troy has fallen. The man is a watchman, and he serves the house of Agamemnon, leader of the Greek expedition. His king has been gone ten years. In the house below rules Agamemnon's wife, Clytemnestra. The watchman knows enough about that house to fear speaking freely about it. At last a beacon shines. He rejoices that the war is over, but his relief is tangled with dread.

That is the opening of Aeschylus's Agamemnon, the first play in a trilogy called the Oresteia. Its characters live in the aftermath of a war whose heroes we have met in the Iliad and the Odyssey. But the action here is not about Achilles, or even about the sack of Troy. It asks what happens when violence committed for a family, or a city, returns home and demands another payment. A sacrifice creates a grievance. A grievance justifies a killing. A killing demands another. The house cannot discover how to stop.

The chorus of Argive elders tells how the Greek fleet was stranded at Aulis, unable to sail for Troy. The winds would not carry the ships. The seer Calchas announced that Artemis required a terrible price: Agamemnon must sacrifice his daughter Iphigenia. Agamemnon faced a choice that could hardly be called a clean choice. Break the expedition and the commitments of the kings, or kill his own child. He chose to kill Iphigenia. The chorus remembers the girl pleading, her robes falling, the force required to silence her. The war's great victory is already inseparable from an act within the victors' family.

Clytemnestra has spent those years with her own answer to the death of her daughter. When word arrives that Troy has fallen, she describes an elaborate chain of signal fires bringing the news across the sea. She speaks like a ruler who can organize a kingdom. The elders wonder whether the message is reliable, but a herald confirms the Greek victory and its suffering. Men have died; others have been lost at sea. For a long time the chorus has waited for Agamemnon to return, and now the king is at the gates. His arrival ought to settle the household. Instead it completes a trap.

Agamemnon enters with a captive woman beside him. She is Cassandra, daughter of Troy's defeated royal family, a prophet whose warnings are heard yet not believed. To Clytemnestra she is an added humiliation: the conqueror returns not merely after his daughter's sacrifice but accompanied by the captive whose presence displays the conqueror's entitlement. Clytemnestra receives him with extraordinary ceremony. She orders rich purple fabrics spread under his feet, asking him to walk over costly cloth as he comes home. He hesitates. To accept honor of this kind seems to him dangerously close to a divine privilege. She persists; he yields and enters.

The purple path has become a small drama of its own. Is it the welcome a victorious king deserves? A wife's show of submission? An act of temptation that will make him step into a hidden death? The play allows these meanings to coexist. While he disappears inside the house, Cassandra remains outside, silent at first as Clytemnestra orders her in. When she finally speaks, her vision fills the stage with a history of murder.

Cassandra sees children slaughtered long before this day. She sees their flesh served at a feast. That is the inherited crime of the house of Atreus. Agamemnon's father, Atreus, took vengeance on his brother Thyestes by feeding him the flesh of his own sons. The present household stands on the memory of an atrocity that no descendant can simply choose to ignore. Cassandra also foresees the very next act: a king caught in a murderous snare, blood spilling in the bath, and her own death beside his. She knows the truth, but knowledge does not save her. She goes inside because the danger is already at the door.

Cries come from within. Agamemnon is struck down. The chorus argues over what to do, and Clytemnestra appears beside the bodies of the king and Cassandra. She does not hide the killing. She takes responsibility and describes the woven entanglement with which she caught him, the repeated blows, and the satisfaction of revenge. She is not asking to be pitied as someone who acted without choice. She declares that she has paid back the death of Iphigenia.

The chorus does not accept this as an end. It sees another death added to the house's debt and asks what justice could now mean. Clytemnestra is joined by Aegisthus, her lover, who inherits a grievance of his own from the feast at which his father Thyestes was dishonored and his brothers were killed. He boasts that Agamemnon's death settles the old account. Yet the more he explains, the more obvious it becomes that this account can never be finally settled. A new generation still lives. Orestes, son of Agamemnon and Clytemnestra, is far from home. The chorus speaks of his possible return. The first play ends not in restored order but in a palace held by victors whose own victory has announced its successor.

The second play, Libation Bearers, begins at a grave. Orestes has returned secretly and offers a lock of his hair to his dead father. He sees a procession approaching: his sister Electra and a chorus of women carrying offerings to the tomb. Clytemnestra has sent them because she has dreamed an alarming dream. In it she gave birth to a snake, held it at her breast, and nursed it; the snake bit her and drew blood with the milk. The palace has begun to fear the son it drove away.

Brother and sister recognize one another and plan revenge. Electra has lived with the humiliation of a mother who killed her father, and Orestes carries a divine instruction: Apollo has commanded him to avenge the murder or suffer punishment. Their grief is not a private feeling detached from action; it is the language by which they prepare to kill. At the tomb they call upon Agamemnon's spirit, seeking a force that will act through the living. Their prayers grow fierce. The women of the chorus remember other dreadful acts by women in myth. The performance makes the audience feel the pressure of revenge before the avengers have committed their own crime.

Orestes and his friend Pylades approach the palace disguised as travelers. Orestes claims he has heard that Orestes himself is dead. Clytemnestra appears and receives the news. Her response is difficult to read: grief and relief seem to struggle within it. An old nurse, who cared for Orestes as a child, is genuinely devastated. She is sent to bring Aegisthus, and the chorus instructs her to change the message so he comes without guards. The plot depends upon the smallest of domestic movements: a nurse's message, a visitor's disguise, a household still organized by the duties of welcoming strangers.

Aegisthus enters and is killed. Then Clytemnestra faces the son whom she was told had died. She bares the breast that fed him and begs him to consider whom he will kill. Orestes hesitates. For a moment the drama reaches a point at which its old commands no longer fit together. Apollo told him to avenge his father. His mother is pleading as the person who gave him life. Pylades, who has been almost silent, reminds him of the god's command. Orestes chooses to obey it and kills her.

He shows the bloody cloth that covered his father's murder and declares himself justified. But the justification does not last as peace. He sees figures whom the chorus cannot see: the Erinyes, avenging powers of blood-kinship, dark and terrible, rising to pursue the murderer of his mother. His mind and his body can no longer remain inside the house. Orestes runs toward Delphi to seek purification and protection. The second play ends with the avenger himself becoming the hunted man.

The third play, Eumenides, opens not in Argos but at Apollo's shrine in Delphi. A priestess enters, then emerges terrified. Orestes is at the altar, and around him sleep the Furies, hideous beings who follow the scent of blood. Apollo instructs Orestes to go to Athens and seek Athena's judgment. Before the pursuers wake fully, the ghost of Clytemnestra rises and rebukes them. She has become the murdered parent crying for vengeance, much as Agamemnon was the murdered parent in the previous play. Her very death gives her the standing she once denied the dead king. The symmetry is merciless.

The Furies accuse Apollo of protecting the man who has killed his mother. Apollo replies that the murder of Agamemnon was intolerable and that he has cleansed Orestes. But no purification persuades the old powers that the debt has been canceled. The argument is not simply between kindness and cruelty. It is between rival claims about the bonds that create obligations, and about who has authority to determine when a crime has been answered.

Orestes reaches Athens and grasps the image of Athena. His pursuers catch up with him. Athena appears and hears both sides. She refuses to settle the quarrel by simply asserting that one party is right. Instead she establishes a court of citizens to hear evidence and vote. The Furies prosecute: Orestes killed his mother, and blood relations cannot be waved away. Orestes replies that his mother murdered his father and that Apollo ordered the vengeance. Apollo then advances an argument the modern listener should hear without being asked to accept: he treats the father as the true generator of a child and the mother as the one who carries another's seed. Athena, born from Zeus without a mother, openly favors the father in this contest.

The verdict is not the uncomplicated triumph of a reasonable new system over an irrational old one. The votes are equal, and Athena's announced rule makes a tie sufficient to acquit Orestes. The court saves him, but it does so under an argument that privileges a father's claim and diminishes a mother's. Aeschylus allows the result to be celebrated without removing the disturbance. The old powers have not lost because they had no case; they have lost despite a case strong enough to divide the jurors.

The Furies rage. They threaten to turn the land barren and poison its people. Athena does not bind them in a pit, drive them into exile, or declare that a civilized city has no further need of them. She speaks to them patiently and offers a place of honor beneath the city. They can protect fertility, family, and civic well-being. Their power to frighten is not erased, but it is brought into an ordered relationship with the community. Over the course of the closing movement, the terrifying Erinyes are welcomed as revered powers, later known as the Eumenides, the Kindly Ones. A procession leads them to their new dwelling.

It can be tempting to summarize the trilogy as the replacement of revenge by law. Certainly it traces the founding of a civic court and ends with adjudication rather than another killing. But that summary leaves out half the achievement. The wronged old powers are not told that their grievances were foolish and should be forgotten. They are offered honor and a home. The city can survive only if it persuades those who have power to harm it to become its guardians. Law must do more than pronounce a result; it must make continuing life possible for those who lost.

This is why the three plays need to be heard together. The watchman waits for the signal that war has ended. Agamemnon returns and is murdered. Orestes comes home to avenge him and becomes polluted by his own act. Only when the quarrel travels out of the family and into a public forum can a different ending be imagined. Yet the price of that different ending is visible. The judgment is contested; the mother's claim is discounted; persuasion must do work that a ballot alone cannot accomplish.

Aeschylus staged these plays for Athenians in the fifth century before the common era, when public argument, civic institutions, and remembered acts of violence were very much alive. He did not compose a single modern novel called the Oresteia. He presented a series of dramatic confrontations, with choruses who sing, question, accuse, and sometimes fail to intervene. Their voices matter because there is no neutral narrator standing above the murders to explain that everything happened for the best.

The ending is a procession, not a wedding or the restoration of Agamemnon's house. Orestes has been released from the accusation, and the city must now keep faith with the powers it has invited to dwell within it. A signal fire first told the watchman that one long catastrophe was over. The trilogy's final torches light a passage toward a civic settlement whose success has to be maintained. There is no guarantee that violence is gone forever. There is at least a way not to answer every death with another.
