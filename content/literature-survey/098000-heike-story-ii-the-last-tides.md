# The Tale of the Heike — Story II: The Last Tides

The concluding movement of the Taira story: Ichi-no-tani, Yashima, Dan-no-ura, the loss of the child emperor, and the nun's final account at Ōhara. The tale's literary episodes and later theatrical adaptations are not merged as though they were the same source.

The Taira have been driven from the capital, but they have not disappeared. Along Japan's western shores and islands they still have ships, warriors, supporters, and an emperor who is also a child of their house. Behind them lies a city filled with the memory of their prosperity. Before them are the straits and harbors where they hope military experience will offset their enemies' growing strength. It is tempting to call what follows a last stand. Yet there will be more than one last stand, and each defeat will leave the survivors with fewer places to imagine a future.

At Ichi-no-tani the Taira build a position supported by sea and difficult land. An attacking army ought to find few easy approaches. Minamoto no Yoshitsune, one of the brothers of the increasingly dominant Yoritomo, chooses a route his enemies regard as nearly impassable. In the tale's celebrated account, a force descends a steep mountain approach and strikes from an unexpected side. Other Minamoto units close in. The Taira camp, poised between hill and shore, begins to break. Soldiers rush toward boats. Men who assumed that the sea guaranteed escape discover that boats can leave without them.

Here the storyteller does something remarkable. The scale of the battle shrinks into the few yards between two riders on the beach. The Minamoto warrior Kumagai Naozane pursues a Taira horseman into the shallow water and forces him to turn. Kumagai expects an enemy whom he can kill and report as a prize of war. He pulls away the helmet and sees a young face. The youth is Atsumori, scarcely older than Kumagai's own son. He belongs to the defeated house, and he has been raised in a world that values music as well as combat. Kumagai hesitates. To release the boy might save one life. But other attackers are approaching, and to hesitate longer will expose both men. He kills Atsumori.

The discovery of a flute among the young man's belongings gives the killing its lasting shape. The Taira camp has been a place where a warrior might rise in the morning after music. Kumagai has not killed an anonymous member of a defeated army. He has ended a life organized around pleasures, relationships, and training as intricate as his own. The tale connects the event with Kumagai's eventual turn toward religious life. Later religious drama gives the two men's encounter additional forms, but the essential wound already belongs to the war tale: a victory that its victor cannot keep uncomplicated.

Other Taira nobles die in scenes of their own. Tadanori, the warrior who carried his poems to Shunzei, is recognized and killed. The courtly pursuit of good verse and the military pursuit of an enemy head have met on the same field. Shigehira, associated in the tale with the burning at Nara, is captured alive. The ordinary distribution of victors and vanquished has given way to individual fates. A man responsible for an atrocity in one episode may be a dignified sufferer in another. The narrative does not fully reconcile these perspectives, and it is stronger for preserving them.

The Taira retreat farther. Some relatives become monks or seek escape from the cycle of fighting, but leaving warfare does not always mean finding safety. The young and old continue to move through a landscape in which the Minamoto can choose when to force an encounter. A coastal campaign becomes a question of whether the Taira can remain gathered long enough to defend their imperial charge. Yoshitsune's reputation grows as a commander whose most effective weapon may be his willingness to attack where his enemies think the ground or weather will protect them.

The next great confrontation comes at Yashima. The Taira have taken shelter near the coast, with a maritime position they trust. Yoshitsune drives his men toward the enemy with speed and deception. The Taira again move out to their boats. Combat crosses between land and water. A warrior's horse must enter the surf; a bow may be lost to the current; the line separating military glory from humiliating mishap is thin. Yoshitsune, the tale says, risks himself to recover a bow that has fallen into the water. His companions think it foolish. He does not want enemies to seize a weapon that would reveal how slight his own bow is. Reputation can turn even an object drifting in the waves into something worth a life.

Then, in a scene almost impossible to imagine at the center of a modern battle report, a boat moves out bearing a fan mounted high on a pole. The fan is a challenge. Can anyone on shore shoot it? The Minamoto choose the archer Nasu no Yoichi. He rides his horse into the water and faces the swaying target. There is wind. The boat moves. Everyone watches. Yoichi prays, draws, and releases. The arrow takes the fan, which falls into the sea. For a moment, the opponents seem to share an understanding that an extraordinary skill has been displayed. The fan is beautiful in flight, but it also reminds us that spectators of war may praise precision without stopping the killing that surrounds it.

A man dances in celebration on the Taira boat. When he becomes a target, the mood changes. The scene refuses to remain the festive interlude it almost became. The people who cheered the shot must confront another shot's human consequence. Each army wants to be magnificent in the eyes of the other, and the magnificence lasts only as long as someone does not answer with an arrow meant for the body rather than the fan.

The final contest approaches at Dan-no-ura, in the narrow waters between the western islands. The battle is carried by ships and by the tides. The Taira have known the sea longer as an ally. Their formations and experience promise at least the chance of victory. The Minamoto bring their own vessels and men. As the fight develops, the currents matter, and so do decisions made aboard individual ships. The poem's various versions tell the military sequence with differing emphases, but they agree on the catastrophe. The battle turns against the Taira and the remaining household is caught among defeated boats.

Kiyomori's son Tomomori understands that the end has come. In the received tale he makes himself heavy for the water and disappears beneath it, refusing capture. Later performances will transform his death into images of an anchor that have become famous on their own terms; that image should not be mistaken for the exact episode of every Heike text. Across the sea men leap, fall, or are dragged. A whole generation of court-connected warriors is being destroyed within sight of relatives who cannot help them.

On one vessel stands the grandmother of the child emperor Antoku, often called the Nun of the Second Rank. She asks where the emperor wishes to go, and the young boy cannot understand the end before him in the terms the adults use. She tells him that there is another capital beneath the waves. Then she enters the sea carrying him. The child has borne a royal title through a war he could not direct. Now that title cannot save him, and the image of the underwater capital exposes the cruelty of calling dynastic interest a child's destiny.

Antoku's mother, Tokuko, known as Kenreimon-in, also enters the water but is rescued. That survival can seem like mercy to those who watch the battle. To the survivor it opens a longer ordeal. She has lost a child, parents, relatives, a political world, and the house in which her life had meaning. Other survivors are captured; sacred objects are sought and recovered according to the traditions through which the defeat will be remembered. Yet the imperial regalia do not become uncomplicated tokens of victory. Their fate belongs to the story of rival claims to legitimate rule, and the child who held one of those claims is dead.

When the battle ends, a listener might expect the tale to end also. The ruling house has fallen. Its fiercest opponents are victorious. The final conflict has delivered the answer announced by the opening bell. But the work extends its attention beyond the triumph. The fate of the surviving Taira relatives continues into punishment and execution. Even a child, Rokudai, descended from Kiyomori, becomes entangled in the victors' fear that an old lineage may again become a claim to power. The political world is not healed by having a new dominant house.

Nor is the new house free from conflict. The Minamoto are not a single person and their triumph has not made all their ambitions compatible. Yoshitsune's military brilliance and Yoritomo's political project pull in different directions; the wider historical aftermath is one of struggle, not of happy reconciliation. The story has no reason to pretend that a military victory abolishes envy or the tendency of the powerful to confuse private safety with public justice.

The work's final and most searching movement returns to Kenreimon-in. She has taken religious vows and withdrawn to a small dwelling at Ōhara, beyond the capital. A great world has compressed to a narrow life among mountains and devotional routines. She remembers courtly elegance, the living faces of the lost, and the sea. Yet the tale does not have her spend every moment in undifferentiated grief. She still lives in days, and the days require acts: tending a simple place, worship, speaking with visitors, remembering without ceasing to function.

Go-Shirakawa, the retired emperor who had stood near so many earlier struggles, comes to see her. This encounter has a different tempo from the scenes in which warriors rush over a bridge or down a cliff. Two people who once occupied a world of elaborate rank now meet around the plain evidence of its disappearance. She recounts her suffering through the Buddhist image of the six realms of existence. She has experienced something of heavenly splendor and of torment, desire, violence, and hunger within the span of a single life. Her account is devotional language, not a claim that the text has documented six literal rebirths. It is a way to measure the distance between a woman once joined to the throne and a woman left to contemplate the losses of that life.

What can the visitor offer? Not the restoration of the dead, nor a promise that the old family will rise again, nor a story in which the child's drowning was justified by the needs of history. The scene makes room instead for witness. Someone has come who remembers the court, and someone survives who can describe its fall. The story's many battles have created the conditions for this quiet conversation, which in turn changes how we remember those battles. What seemed a contest over offices and armed households now appears as an immense production of absence.

The final devotional movement follows Kenreimon-in to death. The bell from the beginning can be heard differently now. At first it sounded like a warning to rulers who thought themselves permanent. At the end it sounds over the people who endured those rulers' decisions and over the victors who may repeat them. The impermanence of worldly glory is no longer only a neat lesson about pride. It is grief, pity, and the difficult work of remembering people who could not carry their world with them.

So The Tale of the Heike does not end by asking us to admire the better fighters. It asks what fighting does to the idea of victory. A boy carries a flute to his death. Another boy is carried into the sea. A woman remains alive to tell how a splendid house vanished. Between the first bell and the last, there are battles enough to fill an epic. The reason this one is still heard is that it makes us listen after the battle has stopped.

## Sources, textual boundary, and further reading (not spoken)

- **Received work:** *Heike monogatari*, notably books 9–12 and the separately designated *Kanjō no maki* or Initiates' Book in the Kakuichi tradition. Book numbering and the Initiates' Book structure follow [Burton Watson / Haruo Shirane, *The Tales of the Heike*](https://cup.columbia.edu/book/the-tales-of-the-heike/9780231510837/); this lecture uses independently composed English prose.
- **Other full versions and scholarship:** Helen Craig McCullough, *The Tale of the Heike* (Stanford, 1988); Hiroshi Kitagawa and Bruce Tsuchida, *The Tale of the Heike* (University of Tokyo Press, 1975–77). The recorded history and recited literary tradition are related but not identical sources.
- **Atsumori / Nasu no Yoichi / Dan-no-ura:** Book 9 (Ichi-no-tani, Atsumori, Tadanori), book 11 (Yashima, fan, bow, sea battle), book 12 and Initiates' Book (aftermath and Ōhara). A battle detail familiar from a later play may not belong to the Kakuichi work.
