# The Nibelungenlied — The Story: The River and the Burning Hall

A single continuous, spoiler-complete telling of the received Middle High German epic, from Kriemhild's dream to the destruction of the Burgundian guests at Etzel's court. It follows the traditional 39-aventiure sequence without reproducing a chapter-by-chapter outline.

A young woman dreams that a falcon she has raised is torn apart by two eagles. Her mother says the bird is a man Kriemhild will one day love, and that she must expect sorrow. Kriemhild answers that she would rather avoid love altogether than risk such pain. The story tells us the dream will come true, and then brings a young warrior to her family's court. From its first moments the poem has placed love and death in the same room.

Kriemhild is the sister of three Burgundian kings, Gunther, Gernot, and Giselher, who rule at Worms on the Rhine. Their household is wealthy, admired, and anxious about rank. Visitors are received according to elaborate distinctions, and a person's name can carry the memory of battles that few of the listeners have seen. Into this court comes Siegfried, son of the king of Xanten. He has won a reputation far beyond his years. Tales tell of a dragon slain, of the enormous Nibelung treasure, and of a cloak of invisibility that can increase its wearer's strength. In the poem's telling he has a nearly invulnerable body, with one small place left exposed. That weakness is not yet known to his enemies.

Siegfried speaks boldly to the Burgundian court, almost provoking conflict, but he becomes its ally. When enemies threaten Gunther's lands, Siegfried leads the defense with formidable success. His deeds make him too valuable to ignore, and Gunther comes to rely on his strength. Siegfried's private hope is to marry Kriemhild. He catches glimpses of her at court, and the promise of a marriage becomes entangled with the service he performs for her brothers. To win the woman he loves, he is drawn into another courtship, one in which the language of marriage hardly conceals the force exerted upon the bride.

Gunther wants to marry Brunhild, a queen who lives across the sea at Isenland. She will accept only a man who can defeat her in three contests of strength. Men who fail may be killed. Gunther is brave and high-born, but he cannot equal the feats demanded of him. Siegfried has a solution. Wearing his invisibility cloak, he can secretly do the work while Gunther appears to triumph. So the Burgundian king travels to Brunhild's court with an extraordinary advantage that she has not agreed to consider. The games are performed in public. A spear is thrown, a stone hurled, a leap made. Brunhild thinks she is testing Gunther's strength. She is really contending with Siegfried, unseen.

She loses the contests and comes to Worms as Gunther's promised wife. Siegfried marries Kriemhild. The court celebrates two alliances at once, but one was produced by fraud. Brunhild is deeply troubled by what seems to her an inequality in the household: why should a man she has been led to regard as Gunther's subordinate marry the king's sister? At the wedding feast the question of who is above whom is not a harmless concern for manners. Status governs obligations, respect, and the marriage bonds that have just been announced. The greater the celebration, the more dangerous it becomes to ask what has been concealed.

At night Brunhild resists Gunther. The poem turns their marriage chamber into another contest of power. Gunther cannot subdue her. Siegfried again intervenes in disguise and overcomes her physically so that Gunther can claim the marriage. The scene involves sexual domination, coercion, and deception; no retelling should convert it into a charming trick. In the struggle Siegfried obtains Brunhild's ring and girdle and later gives them to Kriemhild. They become the objects through which a secret act will return in public. Nothing truly disappeared when the celebration ended. The court has put its future inside a lie.

For a time both marriages seem to yield prosperity. Siegfried and Kriemhild live at Xanten; they have a child. But Gunther's household has not forgotten how Siegfried's position was represented. When the couples meet again at Worms, the two queens quarrel over precedence. The conflict culminates at the cathedral doors. Brunhild insists that as Gunther's wife she holds the higher rank; Kriemhild refuses the insult and answers with the knowledge she believes the stolen objects give her. She produces the ring and girdle and says that Siegfried possessed what should have belonged only to Brunhild and her husband. The public words strike at a queen's dignity, but their meaning is tangled because the deception itself was already dishonorable. Neither woman has been permitted to know the whole arrangement that the men created.

Hagen, Gunther's grim and capable vassal, decides that Siegfried must die. He represents the danger to the royal household as a matter of loyalty. Gunther wavers. Loyalty has been defined so narrowly that to preserve the honor of one family it becomes permissible to destroy the ally who helped that family gain its triumphs. Hagen needs a means of killing a man almost invulnerable in battle. He finds one through Kriemhild's love. Pretending that Siegfried must be protected from enemies, he persuades her to mark the small spot on his back where a leaf had prevented the dragon's blood from touching him. She believes she is helping to keep her husband alive. She has shown his murderer the place to strike.

On a hunting expedition Hagen and the others contrive a pause beside a spring. Siegfried is thirsty and bends to drink. Hagen takes the opportunity. His spear strikes the unprotected place. The hero who seemed impossible to defeat falls through a trick that has moved from court ritual to the forest, from a queen's confidence to an ambush. The murderers bring his body home and arrange a false explanation of what happened. Kriemhild knows that the story is a lie. The poem's grief is not abstract: she has to look at the dead man and recognize that those closest to him at court have had a hand in bringing him there.

As the corpse lies before her, the wounds are said to bleed afresh in Hagen's presence. This is a literary form of accusation, a visible demand that guilt not hide behind titles or evasions. The bleeding does not establish forensic evidence for modern readers; it tells us what kind of moral certainty the narrative wants the scene to carry. Kriemhild's mourning becomes a permanent fact of the court. Siegfried's father begs her to return with him, but she remains near the place where the killers live. Her brothers offer support, imperfectly. Gunther's complicity makes reconciliation impossible, while Giselher's affection makes it painful to leave.

There is also the treasure. The Nibelung hoard once belonged to Siegfried. It could give Kriemhild independent influence, allowing her to reward allies and act beyond the control of the men who have injured her. Hagen fears what such wealth might enable. He takes the treasure and sinks it in the Rhine. That secrecy is more than a theft. It hides a material possibility of justice or revenge, and it allows the man responsible for her husband's murder to control another part of the dead man's legacy. The river will keep its secret into the poem's final scene.

Years pass. King Etzel of the Huns, a mighty widower, seeks Kriemhild as his queen. His envoy, the margrave Rüdiger, offers assurances of status and protection. Kriemhild is reluctant; her grief remains fierce, and the new marriage would take her far from her homeland. Eventually she agrees. She travels down the Danube to Etzel's lands, is received with splendor, and becomes the mother of another child. The new court appears rich enough to replace what she has lost. But love cannot replace the first murder, and wealth cannot erase the knowledge that Hagen still lives.

Kriemhild now possesses something she lacked at Worms: the influence of a queen at a different and powerful court. She persuades Etzel to invite her brothers and their followers to a grand visit. The invitation is formally a gesture of family hospitality. Its purpose is much darker. The Burgundians are suspicious, and Hagen in particular knows he has reason to fear her. Yet the logic of courtly honor draws them toward the journey. To refuse a ruler's invitation and a sister's summons would carry its own shame. Some voices counsel caution. The expedition nevertheless proceeds, taking a large retinue of warriors toward the Danube and beyond.

Warnings gather along the road. Supernatural figures predict disaster, and a river crossing becomes a symbolic boundary between a country the men can return to and a country from which they may never come back. Hagen encounters the ferryman and forces a passage. He destroys the boat after the crossing, ensuring that the company must continue. The men who once fought over prestige at a wedding now travel into a world where the available choices are narrowing with every mile. The story has changed its scale but not its subject: a code of honor persuades people to continue when the danger is plain.

At Bechelaren, Rüdiger receives them with extraordinary generosity. He and his family offer food, lodging, gifts, and bonds of affection. The young Giselher is linked in promise to Rüdiger's daughter. It is one of the poem's last scenes in which hospitality seems capable of bringing strangers into a shared future. Later, when the violence becomes unstoppable, this meeting will make one command particularly cruel. The man who has taken the guests under his protection will be forced to decide between loyalty to his lord and loyalty to those he has welcomed.

The Burgundians arrive at Etzel's court. Their sister receives them with hostility she scarcely conceals. Hagen will not apologize and will not give back the treasure he hid. He carries Siegfried's sword, which for Kriemhild becomes both a reminder of her husband and an emblem of the killer's insolence. Dietrich of Bern, a respected warrior living under Etzel's protection, understands the danger and tries to warn them. Watchful guards stand through the night. The guests still have their weapons. Formal hospitality has become a room full of armed people pretending that court ceremony can still govern what happens next.

The fighting begins when forces stirred up against the Burgundians attack their followers. Hagen responds with devastating violence. In the feast hall, the killing reaches even Etzel and Kriemhild's little son Ortlieb. It is not possible to preserve a boundary between guilty adults and children once retaliation has been invited into the room. The Burgundians hold out against larger numbers, killing those sent to destroy them. Etzel's household is transformed from a place of celebration into a battlefield. The events build through massacres, failed negotiations, insults, and offers that no longer seem capable of being honored. The guests themselves become increasingly terrible as they attempt to survive.

Kriemhild demands the surrender of Hagen. The remaining Burgundians refuse to abandon a companion and vassal, however much they may understand that his earlier crime helped bring them here. Their loyalty is both impressive and destructive. It preserves their solidarity in the moment and condemns those who might have been rescued by giving up one man. The queen orders the hall set on fire. Men face heat, smoke, and the refusal of their enemies to allow escape. They hold on, but each hour consumes more lives.

Rüdiger is asked to fight the men whose welcome at Bechelaren he made sacred. He cannot reconcile his obligations. The poem gives him the agony of a man who has been honorable according to more than one code and discovers that the codes can become mutually exclusive. His choice leads to his death. Giselher, once promised to his daughter, is among those drawn into the fatal clash. Later Dietrich's men also become victims of the struggle. Nearly everyone whom the poem has taught us to respect is now caught in the machine of retaliation. Individual generosity and courage have not changed its direction.

At last Dietrich himself captures the surviving leaders, Gunther and Hagen, and gives them into Kriemhild's power in the hope that she will preserve their lives. She wants the lost treasure and wants to hear Hagen submit. He refuses to reveal its hiding place while one of his lords remains alive. She orders Gunther killed and has his severed head brought before Hagen. Now the condition has been met, but Hagen still refuses. The Rhine has kept the hoard, and he alone will take its secret into death. Kriemhild takes Siegfried's sword and kills him herself.

The victory she has demanded for years is achieved at the cost of almost every person whose life the poem has followed. There is no moment when she can return to her first marriage, restore a child, or bring back a brother. Hildebrand, appalled that Hagen has been killed in this way, strikes Kriemhild down. The last violence falls upon the person who set out to avenge the first. The courts of Burgundy and Etzel are left in mourning. A narrative that began with a dream of a falcon destroyed by other birds ends with almost no one able to tell the story without grief.

The Nibelungenlied does not offer a simple rule for choosing between revenge and forgiveness, or between loyalty and self-preservation. It shows people bound to each other by promises that were supposed to give life structure. The promises become reasons for killing, and the killings become fresh obligations of revenge. Siegfried's great strength could not save him from betrayal. Kriemhild's new power could not restore what betrayal took. At the end, the hidden treasure is still in the river. The poem's living wealth has been spent on death.
