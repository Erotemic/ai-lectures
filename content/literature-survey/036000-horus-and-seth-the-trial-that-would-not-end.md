# The Contendings of Horus and Seth: The Trial That Would Not End

Original narration following *The Contendings of Horus and Seth* in Papyrus Chester Beatty I, recto, a Ramesside Egyptian literary composition. The principal textual basis is the Bibliotheca Alexandrina translation, cross-checked against the online line-structured transcription/translation. The story is not flattened into a universal Egyptian myth: this is a particular surviving version, with comic, violent, and sexually explicit material.

The court has been hearing the same case for so long that the gods are weary of it. One claimant is young Horus. His father Osiris is dead, and Horus insists that a son should inherit his father's office. The other is Seth, the powerful brother of Osiris. Seth does not intend to give up the crown simply because a son has appeared. He is older, strong, and useful in the dangerous work of defending the sun god's boat.

This is the problem at the center of the ancient Egyptian tale called *The Contendings of Horus and Seth*. It is not told as a solemn genealogy in which every god knows the answer before the story begins. It is a dispute argued before a council that can be persuaded, deceived, offended, and sometimes made ridiculous. The gods quarrel like a divided household. They sit, speak, lose patience, depart, write letters, and start all over again.

A papyrus from the New Kingdom preserves this long, unruly story. Its characters belong to Egypt's larger religious world, but the narrator who gave us this version was interested in what happens when rightful succession is repeatedly obstructed, and in the ingenious things a mother will do to secure her son's inheritance.

At the outset Horus makes his claim before the assembly. Thoth, associated with writing and judgment, supports him, as do other gods. They reason that Osiris has a son. Why give a father's position to a brother while that son is alive? Isis, the mother of Horus and widow of Osiris, rejoices whenever the argument turns her son's way. It appears that the court is close to deciding.

Then the presiding sun god, Re-Harakhty, becomes angry. He is not ready to accept the council's recommendation. Seth boasts that he is the powerful fighter who wards off threats to Re's boat. His strength, he argues, is a claim to rule. The gods do not resolve whether the throne should belong to a blood heir or to the figure they believe can best protect divine order. The discussion, instead, grows more contentious.

They decide to consult Neith, an older goddess, by letter. Thoth writes, as though the gods were a group of officials requiring an authoritative opinion from another jurisdiction. Neith's answer favors Horus. She urges the assembly to give him his father's office and suggests compensation for Seth so that the rejected claimant will not simply be humiliated. It is a practical settlement: recognize the heir, provide something for the powerful rival, and end the quarrel.

The reply does not end anything. Re-Harakhty is offended by the implications, and the council quarrels once more. At one point an insulting remark leaves the sun god withdrawn and miserable. Hathor approaches him and uses a shocking sexual gesture to make him laugh and return to public business. The scene can sound startling beside the solemn ritual images of Egyptian temples, yet it belongs to this surviving story. Laughter is one of the means by which an exhausted divine court starts functioning again.

Seth insists he can defeat Horus directly and deserves the throne by force. Thoth and others ask why a living son should be displaced in favor of his uncle. Isis intervenes insistently, and Seth refuses to let the case proceed while she is present. He knows she is clever, persistent, and deeply invested in the outcome. The court therefore moves to an island and orders the ferryman not to bring any woman across who resembles Isis.

Isis arrives disguised as an old woman. She asks the ferryman for passage, telling him she needs to feed a hungry boy. He has orders not to take women. She offers food. He refuses. Then she offers a gold ring. The ferryman accepts the payment and carries her to the island. A prohibition thought to be absolute has yielded to an ordinary act of bargaining. The trick is funny until one remembers that the fate of Egypt's kingship is supposedly being protected by the rule.

Once across, Isis takes another form. She becomes an attractive young woman and catches Seth's attention. She tells him a story: a herdsman had died, leaving his cattle and property to his son; a stronger stranger had come and claimed what belonged to the boy. What, she asks, should be done? Seth is outraged by the stranger's conduct. Of course a fatherless son must not be robbed of his inheritance. Isis then reveals herself. Seth has supplied the principle that condemns his own claim. The seduction was a trap of reasoning, not merely a disguise.

For a moment the court seems to have been given its answer by Seth's own mouth. Yet the argument goes on. Divine pride does not vanish simply because a speaker has been caught contradicting himself. The story shifts from legal devices to contests that would hardly be admissible as evidence in a human court.

Seth proposes that he and Horus change into hippopotamuses and remain beneath the water. Whoever surfaces too soon will lose the contest. They plunge into the river or sea. Isis waits in fear for her son. She fears that Seth may have killed him. She makes a harpoon and throws it into the water. But her weapon strikes Horus first. He cries out that she has pierced her own child. She commands it to release him and throws it again.

This time the harpoon finds Seth. He calls to Isis as her brother and pleads for mercy. She cannot bear his appeal and releases him too. Horus is furious. His mother has spared the enemy he believes must be defeated. In a sudden act of violence he cuts off her head and carries it away. The story does not treat this as a final, permanent killing. The headless Isis appears as a transformed figure, and the divine court reacts with alarm. Horus's desire for justice has broken against the tangled obligations of kinship: Seth is his enemy, but he is also Isis's brother.

Horus is found lying in an isolated place. Seth takes revenge by tearing out his eyes and hiding them in the earth. In the strange logic of this tale, the buried eyes become flowers. Hathor finds the injured Horus, takes milk from a gazelle, and restores his sight. The episode moves with the speed of a dream. A body is wounded, transformed, and healed, but the wound remains an image of what the dispute has cost.

The council again urges Horus and Seth to stop fighting. Seth invites Horus to his house and beds are made for them. During the night Seth commits a sexual assault designed to disgrace his rival and establish domination over him. Horus preserves evidence of what occurred and goes to Isis. Rather than surrender to humiliation, she arranges a counter-trick. She secretly places Horus's semen on lettuce in Seth's garden, knowing that Seth will eat it.

The next hearing begins with Seth's assertion that he has sexually mastered Horus and should therefore prevail. The judges react with disgust toward the younger claimant. Horus refuses the accusation. Thoth calls for the semen to answer. What Seth deposited does not answer from Horus; Horus's own substance answers from within Seth. Seth's supposed proof has been reversed against him. The story turns a violation and the claim of sexual superiority into a contest over whose testimony and bodily signs are actually believed.

Still the court delays. Another contest concerns boats. Seth and Horus are ordered to race in vessels of stone. Horus understands that a genuine stone boat will not float. He builds a craft of wood and makes it look like stone. Seth attempts the more literal feat and ends up with a vessel that sinks. The mock-heroic competition exposes the absurdity of a process in which ingenuity, deceit, and brute force have replaced clear judgment. The side that is right is not always the side that behaves transparently, and the court has created a setting that rewards manipulation.

The dead Osiris finally intervenes from the world below. Letters are exchanged between his realm and the council of living gods. Osiris reminds them that he is not a powerless absence. He presides over the destiny awaiting gods and humans after death. His son should not be cheated of an inheritance while divine judges continually postpone their responsibility. The exchange becomes sharp. Those above answer back; Osiris invokes the consequences of the justice administered below. No one can escape the place over which he rules.

At last the gods decide. Horus is installed in his father's office. The White Crown is placed on his head. Isis rejoices in public, the divine assembly celebrates, and the question of succession receives the answer for which the story has argued so long. Seth is not simply erased. Re-Harakhty takes him into his company, and his frightening power is given a place in the sky, associated with thunder. Once again the settlement must make a place for a defeated but potent adversary.

This ending invites a tempting simplification: good Horus wins; bad Seth loses. The text is less tidy. Horus has injured his mother. Isis has deceived almost everyone, including those who tried to keep her out of court. Seth has threatened, manipulated, and attacked, yet his position as defender of Re's boat gives him a function the gods cannot simply deny. Re-Harakhty has governed the hearing badly; the council has needed repeated reminders of its own principles. The court is a place where justice is named constantly but achieved only with difficulty.

Nor is this a generic transcript of everything Egyptians ever believed about Horus, Seth, and Osiris. Egyptians had many stories, rituals, and images of these deities. The papyrus preserves a particular narrative voice, capable of bawdy humor, bureaucracy, elaborate trickery, and moments of cruelty. The story need not agree with every temple inscription in every detail. What matters is the way one ancient storyteller could use the divine struggle to imagine how disputed authority is decided.

The tale's length is part of its meaning. The case should be simple. An heir asks for his father's office. A powerful uncle refuses. The gods could settle it. Yet years and contests pass because every person who has authority to resolve the dispute has reasons not to do so. As the proceedings continue, harm spreads beyond the question on which the council was first asked to vote.

Eventually a crown is placed on Horus's head. This is the story's public resolution. Its deeper comedy and violence remain in memory: a god who cannot bear criticism, a mother smuggled onto an island with a gold ring, a rival tricked into defending the rights of a stranger's son, eyes hidden in a mountain and restored with milk, and letters arriving from the ruler of the dead to a court that will not make up its mind. The gods have reached a judgment. The story has made us see how hard it was for them to reach one.

## Textual basis and source notes (not spoken)

- **Primary witness:** Papyrus Chester Beatty I, recto, *Contendings of Horus and Seth*, 20th Dynasty Late Egyptian text. Bibliotheca Alexandrina offers text and a full English rendering https://inscriptionslibrary.bibalex.org/presentation/monument.aspx?ins_id=13&lang=en&mon_id=2873 . For passage divisions, line-by-line modern rendering (machine-assisted and therefore secondary for philology): https://en.mouseia.org/texts/horus-and-seth .
- **Scene anchors:** court, Neith's letter, laughter of Re-Harakhty (papyrus opening); Isis's ferry passage and parable (middle); hippopotamus contest, Harpoon and injured Horus; Seth's sexual assault and Isis's reversal using lettuce, Thoth's adjudication; stone-boat competition; letter exchange with Osiris; installation of Horus, Seth's heavenly role (papyrus concluding pages). Bibliotheca Alexandrina text and Mouseia parts 1–16 provide locators.
- Not interchangeable with other Egyptian versions of the Horus/Seth conflict. Sexual assault, Isis's beheading and the semen contest are in the received papyrus narrative; do not omit them in order to produce an artificially sanitized moral tale.
- Secondary references: Lichtheim, *Ancient Egyptian Literature*, volume II, pp. 214–223, and Simpson, *The Literature of Ancient Egypt* (cited by Bibliotheca Alexandrina).
