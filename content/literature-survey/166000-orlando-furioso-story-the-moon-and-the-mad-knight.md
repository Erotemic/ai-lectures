# Orlando Furioso — Complete Story: The Moon and the Mad Knight

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

A knight walks into a quiet place and sees two names carved into a tree. They have been written together. Nearby, the same names appear on a rock, then in a cave. One name is Angelica, whom the knight has loved for a long time. The other is Medoro, whom he does not know. At first he tells himself there must be an explanation. Perhaps Angelica has written about him in a disguise. Perhaps the words mean something other than what they plainly say. Then he hears what happened here, and his world breaks apart. He takes off his armor. He tears trees out of the ground. The most celebrated paladin in Charlemagne's service becomes a naked, destructive wanderer who scarcely knows his own name.

That knight is Orlando, the furioso of Ludovico Ariosto's *Orlando Furioso*. Yet his madness is only one current in a book that can carry us from a Paris under siege to an island of enchantment, from an abandoned bride on a shore to the palace of a magician, from a battlefield in North Africa to the Moon itself. The received poem has forty-six cantos. It reached its final form in 1532 after earlier editions in 1516 and 1521. What follows is a connected telling of its major plots, especially the stories of Orlando, Angelica, Bradamante, Ruggiero and Astolfo. We will move between them as the poem does, though we cannot reproduce all its intersecting adventures in one recording.

The scene is not the France of ordinary history. It is the imagined age of Charlemagne and his warriors, already reshaped by centuries of romances. Across this world, Agramante, a ruler from Africa, leads a great war against the Christian emperor. Saracen and Christian knights fight under banners that sometimes matter less than friendship, personal honor, desire or a private oath. The poem follows an earlier romance by Matteo Maria Boiardo, *Orlando Innamorato*, in which Orlando had already fallen helplessly in love with Angelica. We need only one fact from that unfinished predecessor: the magnificent fighter, whom legend had made a model of heroic service, begins this story unable to govern his own heart.

Angelica is a princess from Cathay. Men treat her as a prize, a reason to fight one another, a thing the fortunate victor might acquire. At the start she flees the Christian camp. Several knights rush to follow her, including Orlando's cousin Rinaldo and rivals from different sides of the war. Each hunter has his own conviction that he deserves to win. Angelica's immediate aim is simpler than all their codes: to get away. She will spend a large part of the poem navigating the peril produced by other people's devotion to her.

Watch what happens when the narrative seems to promise a fight. One knight reaches another; they recognize a common object of pursuit; a confrontation appears inevitable. The next turn may instead bring a temporary alliance, a new rival, a magic object, or the sudden appearance of someone else. Pursuit becomes a form of postponement. No one travels through this world without colliding with a story already in motion.

Elsewhere a woman in armor follows a knight she loves. Her name is Bradamante, and she can fight the men around her on equal terms. She loves Ruggiero, a warrior serving Agramante's side. The difference in their allegiances and faiths is only one obstacle. Ruggiero's guardian is Atlante, a magician who knows something about the young man's future and is desperate to postpone it. Ruggiero is destined for a great marriage and a distinguished posterity, but also for a death that his protector cannot bear to see. Atlante therefore keeps trying to save him by imprisoning him in safety.

For Ruggiero, the result is a strange kind of captivity. If Atlante prevents every dangerous choice, he also prevents the knight from living the life that would make those choices meaningful. Bradamante has to find him. She is misled, threatened, and at one point left to fall into what looks like a trap. She finds assistance from the magician Melissa, who is committed to a different future: the union that Atlante fears. Their contest is conducted not just with swords but through the power to interpret destiny. One magician sees impending death and tries to stop time. Another sees a line of descendants and attempts to help two people reach each other.

One of the poem's most memorable inventions is the hippogriff, an impossible flying creature that carries a knight above the usual world of roads and horses. Ruggiero rides it far from familiar battlefields and lands on Alcina's island. The island first appears as a realm of seductive pleasure. Alcina seems beautiful, hospitable and entirely devoted to her guest. Yet the beauty is enchantment. Her former lovers have been transformed into trees, rocks and other things, discarded when their usefulness to her ends. Astolfo, an English paladin who has had his own extraordinary adventures, has suffered this fate too.

There is comedy in a proud warrior's inability to distinguish love from captivity, but cruelty sits beneath it. Melissa helps Ruggiero see Alcina without her deceptive glamour and escape. A great many future adventures will depend on this liberation. The flying horse returns him to the air, and for a time the scenery opens into seas and distant islands. Ruggiero's flights show the joy of a story that will not remain inside a single kingdom, but they also expose the difference between being able to go anywhere and knowing where one ought to go.

On a rocky shore he sees a woman chained up for a sea monster. It is Angelica, whom her pursuers have finally left exposed to a danger more literal than marriage. Ruggiero fights to rescue her, using the hippogriff and his weapons against the monstrous creature. He does not become a perfectly disinterested rescuer, however. Desire follows the rescue. Angelica uses the magical ring that allows her to escape sight, and vanishes from him as she has vanished from so many knights. We can admire the adventure while refusing to pretend that saving someone gives the rescuer ownership of the saved.

The same kind of creature threatens another woman, Olimpia, in a different episode. Olimpia has loved Bireno and suffered on his behalf. She awakens abandoned on an island after he sails away, and the scene pauses on the practical cruelty of being left with no safe route home. Orlando eventually rescues her from the sea monster. Ariosto repeatedly joins an extravagant fantasy danger to a recognizable human one: broken promises, the exposure of a person with less power, a lover who discovers that devotion can be used against her.

Bradamante's search for Ruggiero continues through gardens, forests and supernatural prisons. At Atlante's enchanted palace, a visitor seems always to hear or glimpse the very person most urgently sought. A man rushes around a corner, certain that his beloved or his stolen possession is just out of reach. Another does the same. They search the same building and fail to see how it works. The palace is not a simple lock on the door. It is a structure built from their desires. What traps them is the conviction that one more turning will finally complete the pursuit.

This makes the enchanted palace a close relative of the greater poem. Ariosto invites us to follow a character, then leaves that character at the edge of a decision and moves to another. The reader, too, wants the missing person to appear. The story itself becomes a set of rooms joined by the doors of frustrated expectation.

Meanwhile the war does not wait. Agramante's armies threaten Paris. Rival commanders dispute who deserves glory; strong knights ignore orders for private quarrels. Rodomonte, an immensely powerful warrior on the attacking side, tears through opposition with terrifying fury. He is capable of extraordinary courage and extraordinary contempt. The siege is not simply a contest between good Christians and bad invaders in which every individual fits the label. The poem's combatants can recognize valor across the lines of faith, and the supposedly exemplary heroes often fail to keep their own obligations. But Ariosto's romance still inherits crusading categories that are part of its historical fiction, not categories a modern history of actual peoples should adopt without question.

Other women enter the action as actors rather than merely as objects of pursuit. Marfisa is an independent warrior who fights fiercely and seems answerable to no one. Bradamante combines courage with anger, uncertainty, jealousy and devotion. Neither becomes less interesting when the plot refuses to give her complete control. Their skill makes the most obvious assumptions about male heroism impossible to maintain, even while the marriage plans around them remain governed by the dynastic expectations of their world.

We have now seen many people fall in love without becoming much wiser. Angelica's story is about to change the balance. She encounters Medoro, a young warrior whose devotion is not to her but to a fallen lord. He has risked his life trying to recover the dead man's body and is wounded. Angelica cares for him. She tends the injury and discovers that love is possible without a tournament of champions outside her door. Medoro is not the strongest man in the poem, nor the one with the grandest name. He is a young soldier who has suffered, and she chooses him.

They live together for a time in a place where trees and stone can receive inscriptions. They marry and depart. The names they carve survive their presence. The marks are a record of happiness for the couple and a delayed catastrophe for the knight who has spent so much of his life seeking her. There is an irony in that ordinary act of writing: two lovers leave evidence for themselves, without intending to address Orlando at all. He will read it as though it had been placed in the world to destroy him.

When Orlando arrives, the poem has allowed the audience to know more than he does. The words on the tree make us uneasy before he has understood them. He attempts to deny what he sees. He invents innocent explanations for the repeated names. Then he learns from the people who sheltered the couple that the two were indeed married. His resistance to the truth gives way. We have reached the scene with which we began, and Ariosto makes it a disaster of perception as well as desire. The knight cannot bear to see the world accurately because the accurate world does not contain the future he had imagined.

Orlando breaks the trees bearing the inscriptions. He wrecks what he can touch, then passes beyond deliberate revenge. He throws off the signs of knighthood. Armor, horse, weapons and social identity fall away. He roams like a savage force through woods and settlements, destroying animals and frightening people. The name of the poem is not an affectionate description of a brave man with strong feelings. This is a hero stripped of self-command, a dangerous man whose greatness gives him greater power to do harm when he loses it.

The army has lost its champion at the very moment it needs him. Ariosto allows that practical consequence to stand beside the private one. Orlando's passion has consumed attention that should have gone to the defense of his people. The story has made readers enjoy a thousand deflections from duty; now one of those deflections takes on the force of punishment.

How can such a man be restored? Not by a better sword, and not by finding Angelica and making her love him. The answer comes from Astolfo, whose adventures take the poem beyond the ordinary measure of enchantment. Riding the hippogriff, he travels far into Africa, reaches the realm of the Ethiopian king, and uses extraordinary means to deliver people from monstrous threats. He journeys toward an earthly paradise, where Saint John guides him. The route then leads upward to the Moon.

The Moon is the strangest archive in the book. Things that humans lose on Earth are stored there. Promises, hopes, wasted time, reputations, favors, tears and ambitions take on forms that can be collected and recognized. Among the lost things are the wits of men. They are kept in vessels. Astolfo finds the portion that belongs to Orlando. A knight's sanity, which no army can recover by force of arms, can be found as an object in a warehouse of human folly. The image is comic and philosophical at once. A person loses reason not only through disease or magic, but through the ordinary extravagance with which human beings pursue honors and desires.

Saint John's presence also introduces a bracing reflection on poetry and reputation. Great rulers are praised; their achievements are remembered; the makers of verses may transform what the world believes about them. Is fame a transparent record of merit or partly the work of those who tell the story? Ariosto, a poet writing for a court, permits a joke about the power of poets to interrupt a story that itself promises immortal fame to heroes. The Moon holds not just Orlando's wits but a satirical account of the world that called him great.

Astolfo returns. With companions he overpowers the mad Orlando, restrains him, and makes him inhale the contents of the vessel. The man recovers his reason. His body is restored to the service of his mind; his love for Angelica no longer rules him. The rescue is physical, almost absurd, and yet moving. Someone who could not save himself has friends willing to hold him down long enough for him to become himself again. The poem does not reward his love by giving him the woman. It cures him by allowing him to live without her.

War moves toward an end. Astolfo's campaigns in Africa undermine Agramante's power. Orlando rejoins the struggle. At the island of Lampedusa, a decisive combat brings together three champions on each side. Orlando, Brandimarte and Oliviero fight Agramante, Gradasso and Sobrino. The encounter is violent and costly. Brandimarte is killed. Oliviero is badly hurt. Orlando kills Agramante and Gradasso, and the war that has crossed so many landscapes approaches its conclusion. Victory does not restore the friend lying dead. Ariosto lets a poem capable of casual marvels slow down for mourning.

While all this happens, Bradamante and Ruggiero still have not achieved the life that seemed promised to them. Their loves have repeatedly been interrupted by war, enchantment, religious allegiance and dynastic caution. Ruggiero eventually survives a shipwreck and undergoes Christian baptism, but the change does not clear every obstacle. Bradamante's family prefers a marriage to Leo, the son of a powerful emperor. The woman who could defeat knights in combat confronts the possibility that even her extraordinary skill will not secure a choice of husband.

She arranges a condition: only a man capable of defeating her in combat should be permitted to marry her. She can rely on her ability, but courtly politics are more intricate than a tournament. Ruggiero has by then saved or been saved by people whose gratitude and honor bind him in unexpected ways. Leo, impressed by the unknown warrior who has displayed such great prowess, rescues him from imprisonment. In return, he asks that same warrior to fight Bradamante on his behalf. Ruggiero is trapped in a contradiction. The obligation to repay a generous rescue requires him to fight the woman he loves while apparently securing her marriage to another man.

The battle between Bradamante and the disguised Ruggiero is one of Ariosto's finest inversions of chivalric expectation. Their skill does not make the situation clearer. It makes the deception harder to resolve. The strongest fighter can be the least free person in the arena. Eventually the truth comes out. Leo recognizes who has served him and what the service has cost. He withdraws his claim. Bradamante and Ruggiero can marry. The poem's promise of a future line of Este rulers is now ready to be fulfilled. A romance that so often suspended the happy ending finally permits one.

But there is a last interruption. At the wedding feast Rodomonte arrives, angry and ready to challenge Ruggiero. The celebration becomes a deadly contest. Ruggiero defeats and kills the challenger. The last movement of the enormous poem is not a peaceful curtain lowered upon a stable household. It is a sudden return of the violence through which the whole world has been ordered. Even when a marriage has been won and an epic war is ending, the code of single combat can reappear to demand a body.

Imagine, now, the two most important endings beside one another. Orlando survives by recovering his reason and letting go of the woman who does not love him. Ruggiero reaches the woman who does love him, and his marriage becomes the visible promise of a ruling family. The first story is about the surrender of possession. The second is about the long negotiation of union. Both require a knight to confront the difference between what he wants and what a world of obligations will allow. Neither exists outside the wars, spells and competing loyalties surrounding them.

The poem has filled the route between these endings with gardens, rivers, monsters, false appearances, joyful and grievous surprises. Yet its abundant movement is not just decoration. The knight chasing an image through Atlante's palace, Orlando tearing apart a landscape because it records a marriage, and Astolfo discovering lost reason in a vessel on the Moon are variations on the same puzzle. Human beings chase what they imagine will complete them. They may be fooled by magicians, by other people, or simply by themselves.

Ariosto can laugh at this and grieve for it. He can make a mighty fighter ridiculous, then allow him a real loss. He can fill a magical island with comic temptation and let the discarded lovers become trees. He can praise Bradamante's courage while exposing the family arrangements that constrain her. He can make a marriage splendid and then interrupt it with a death. The plot does not have one emotional key; it works by changing keys without stopping the music.

When Orlando walked into the grove, the names Angelica and Medoro meant that another person's happiness had become the end of his dream. When he finally left his madness behind, the world had not given him a better answer from Angelica. It had given him his ability to live in the world she had chosen. That is a surprisingly difficult lesson for an epic of enchanted weapons and heroic names. Perhaps it is also why this vast story of impossible flights, fierce warriors and warring kings has remained so alive: it knows that the most dangerous enchantment can be the future a person insists on seeing, even after the world has written something else.

## Text and evidence companion (not spoken)

### Edition and narrated scope

This is an original **whole-arc literary retelling of selected major threads** in Ariosto's 1532 forty-six-canto version, primarily checked against William Stewart Rose's public-domain English verse translation and a scholarly digital-text project. It is **not** a canto-by-canto synopsis. Earlier 1516 and 1521 editions are not conflated with the final version. Boiardo's *Orlando Innamorato* supplies the inherited situation; events from the predecessor are not passed off as part of Ariosto's poem. Rose's translation omits or softens some sexual passages, noted by Bowdoin's digital project. The retelling does not reproduce copyrighted recent translations.

### Primary narrative locators

- Cantos I–IV: Angelica's escape; Bradamante's search, Atlante's magical interventions, Melissa's help.
- Cantos VI–XI: Alcina's island; Ruggiero's liberation; Angelica and the sea monster; abandoned Olimpia.
- Canto XII: the palace of projections, a model of desire and delay.
- Cantos XIV–XVIII: siege and defense of Paris; Rodomonte's violence.
- Canto XIX and XXIII–XXIV: Medoro and Angelica; inscriptions; Orlando's discovery and loss of reason.
- Cantos XXXIII–XXXIX: Astolfo's journey, the Moon, the vessel of wits and Orlando's recovery.
- Cantos XL–XLIII: Agramante's defeat; Lampedusa; death of Brandimarte.
- Cantos XLIV–XLVI: Leo, Bradamante's challenge, Ruggiero's marriage and the final duel with Rodomonte.
