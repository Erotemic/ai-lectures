# Li Sao: The Road Out of Chu

A source-conscious listening journey through *Li Sao* (*Encountering Sorrow*), the long poem in the *Chu Ci* traditionally attributed to Qu Yuan. The poetic speaker is not automatically the historical poet, and the imagined journeys are not literal travel records.

A man is surrounded by fragrant plants. He gathers orchids, tends carefully chosen herbs, and fastens blossoms to his clothing. Around him other people compete for advancement, bend their principles toward whoever has power, and seem ready to call a straight line crooked if that will please the court. He believes he has prepared himself for service. The more carefully he tends his own character, the farther he seems to stand from those who decide whom a ruler should trust. What should a person do when the virtues cultivated for public life become the very reason that public life rejects him?

This is the situation at the heart of *Li Sao*, often rendered as *Encountering Sorrow* or *The Lament*, preserved in the Chinese anthology *Chu Ci*, the Songs of Chu. It is traditionally associated with Qu Yuan, a political figure of the southern state of Chu during the Warring States era. Later readers made the poem the record of a loyal minister who suffered slander and banishment. That reading has a long history, but the poem itself is stranger than an autobiographical complaint. It moves from family names and political examples into celestial journeys, encounters with divine figures, divination, and a final inability to leave home. It should be heard as a poem of vision, not as a journal whose fantastic places must be mapped onto the earth.

At the beginning, the speaker names his descent and the beautiful name he was given. He presents virtue as something cultivated. He gathers flowers in morning and evening, changes his adornments, and tends plants as if the garden could show the condition of his mind. The names of herbs matter in Chinese verse in ways that are hard to carry into English: fragrance, purity, and the discipline of care become a vocabulary of moral distinction. A garden can fail, however. Flowers wither. People whose promise once seemed excellent may turn to expediency. The speaker fears growing old before he can accomplish any work worth leaving behind.

He remembers exemplary rulers of the past and corrupt ones who lost their way. The references are part of an argument: good government is not a lucky product of possession or birth, but a practice that can be betrayed. The ruler whom the speaker hopes to serve listens to slander and misunderstands him. Other courtiers wear the appearance of virtue like clothing that can be changed for advantage. The speaker's frustration is not that he has been denied a comfortable post; it is that he believes the polity is being steered toward danger while the people who could correct the course refuse to look.

Then the poem makes a turn that has puzzled commentators for centuries. A female figure, often called Nüxu or the Sister, reproaches the speaker for his inflexibility. Why continue to distinguish himself so sharply when others adapt to their circumstances? Why choose the lonely road? The scene is not a public hearing. It brings the counterargument into the poem's own voice. If the speaker's standards make him incapable of living with other people, have those standards become another kind of pride? The poem does not erase the question by giving the Sister a grotesque villain's face. It proceeds through the speaker's refusal to let an entire world's behavior set the measure of his own conduct.

He turns toward exemplary antiquity again, addressing the legendary sage ruler Shun. The argument grows too large for the palace where it began. The speaker prepares to travel beyond the ordinary world, and the poem enters a scale of mountains, clouds, bright powers, and mythic routes. Dragons and flying horses draw a chariot; celestial bodies mark a journey that ordinary roads could never contain. The transformation is startling. A political complaint is being expanded into a search for a world where worthy qualities might finally be recognized.

He travels through a heaven that has its own gates. He tries to approach divine figures, but even there his way is obstructed. A gatekeeper delays or refuses him. In one famous sequence, the speaker's journey reaches the precincts of the celestial palace without gaining the audience he seeks. The feeling is familiar despite the impossibility of the geography: to have prepared for a conversation all one's life, arrived at the door, and found that the door is controlled by someone who will not listen. The celestial journey repeats the pattern of exclusion from the earthly court.

The speaker pursues images of beautiful women and ideal partners. In the long Chinese commentary tradition these searches often stand for the quest for a ruler who will recognize a loyal minister. Yet the poem's imagery is not a simple code in which every woman is a particular king and every journey a move between historical states. It has the motion of ritual, erotic quest, dream, and political longing together. The speaker thinks of the goddess associated with the Luo River, of other remote figures, and of messages that may not reach their intended recipient. His attempts fail or are frustrated by doubt, delay, and the difficulty of knowing what another being intends.

The repetition carries the argument. Each new destination seems, briefly, to offer an escape from a corrupted present. Each produces another version of the old disappointment. The problem cannot be solved simply by increasing the size of the landscape. A journey through the clouds may still encounter jealousy and misrecognition. The speaker has come far enough to see that his frustration may accompany him wherever he goes, even if his own convictions remain sound. The poem does not reduce the failure to one villain at one court. It becomes a problem about relation: where can a self that insists on certain values find someone who can answer them?

He seeks advice from figures associated with divination. Ling Fen offers one assessment; the shaman Wu Xian is consulted as another authority. Both belong to the poem's world of ritual and mythic counsel. Their answers press the speaker to consider whether other places, other rulers, and other companions might exist. The movement between advisers is important. Someone who accuses the court of failing to listen still wants his own judgment confirmed. He has not ceased to ask whether his course is correct. The consultation scenes turn the poem from a solitary declaration into a struggle to decide whether fidelity requires staying or leaving.

The next ascent is immense. He gathers a retinue and prepares an imagined departure from the world that has refused him. A thousand chariots move in the poetic imagination. The number conveys more than the status of a dismissed official; it is the scale of a sovereign journey. Scholars have noticed that moments like this resist an interpretation of the speaker as merely an identifiable court minister. He can assume an authority in the poem that historical explanation alone does not settle. The poetic voice changes its scale without apologizing for the change.

The chariot rises and travels. For a moment the departure seems possible. Then the travelers look back toward Chu, the place from which the poem began. The movement falters. The horses resist leaving the homeland, or the speaker sees it and cannot carry out the final separation. The effect is more painful than a gatekeeper's rejection. No one is now ordering him to remain. The world he wanted to escape has become the place he cannot abandon. He has discovered that exile is not merely being forced away from home; it can mean carrying home so deeply that even an imagined road out of the sky cannot remove it.

In the short concluding coda the speaker says that no one remains with whom good government can be made. He resolves to follow Peng Xian, a figure whose identity and significance have been debated. Later biographies of Qu Yuan connect his story to death in the Miluo River, and the poem has often been read as anticipating such an act. But the line is not a certificate of how the poet died. The speaker's last movement belongs to a poem that has repeatedly crossed the border between symbolic and impossible places. It deserves to remain in that difficult poetic register rather than being forced into a simple historical scene.

Early imperial readers, including the influential commentator Wang Yi, interpreted *Li Sao* through a biography of Qu Yuan: loyal service, unjust rejection, exile, and moral refusal. That interpretation shaped two millennia of Chinese literary memory. It is a real part of the work's history, not an obvious error to discard. At the same time, modern scholars have shown how much the poem's imagined transformations can exceed the political allegory that later readers made familiar. Both facts can be true. The poem was read as the voice of a betrayed official, and it contains more than a transparent account of one person's career.

The fragrance at the beginning is the key to the ending. The speaker spends the poem preparing a self worthy of relation: to a ruler, to a beloved, to an inherited tradition of good governance, and to a homeland that has rejected him. Gardens and chariots seem to have nothing in common, but both are ways to imagine cultivation. Plants take work and time. Journeys require direction. If the world rewards slander and false adornment, how does a person avoid becoming false in the effort to be seen? *Li Sao* refuses to let the question be answered by a successful promotion, a new marriage, or an easy escape. The speaker takes an impossible journey and comes back to the burden of loving a place that will not love him correctly. In that unresolved return lies much of the poem's power.

For a short translated excerpt with attribution to David Hawkes, see [Columbia University, *Encountering Sorrow*](https://afe.easia.columbia.edu/main_pop/ps/ps_china-quyuan-encounteringsorrow.htm). **Do not reproduce Hawkes's copyrighted translation.**
- Scholarly interpretation: Stephen Owen, [*Reading the Li sao*](https://scholar.harvard.edu/sites/scholar.harvard.edu/files/sowen/files/reading_the_li_sao_tnr.pdf), especially the sequence of Nüxu, Shun, celestial travel, divination by Ling Fen, and Wu Xian, with discussion of the Han historicizing commentary. The PDF was referenced for source research, not reproduced.
- Historical interpretive witness: [Chinese Text Project, Ban Gu's preface on *Li Sao*](https://ctext.org/wiki.pl?chapter=169363&if=en&remap=gb), a later interpretive account, not the original poem itself.
- Orientation: [Wikipedia, *Li Sao*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Li_Sao) and [*Chu Ci*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chu_Ci). The attributed political biography is not silently converted into a verified first-person diary; mythic travel is described as poetry. The paraphrase is newly authored.
