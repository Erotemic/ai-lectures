# Beowulf, Part I: The Hall and the Dark Water

The opening through the defeat of Grendel's mother in the Old English heroic poem, approximately lines 1–1887. A complete first movement with the speeches, feuds, gift-giving, and dangers that make the hall more than a monster's target.

An old king has built the greatest hall his people have ever seen. Hrothgar calls it Heorot, a place where his warriors can eat, drink, hear songs, receive gifts, and remember that their king has made a common world out of men who might otherwise be enemies. The roof beams rise high. The benches fill. Inside the hall a poet sings of creation, of a world brought into brightness and given to living things. Outside, someone hears the singing and hates it. His name is Grendel. The poem calls him a descendant of Cain and places him among beings estranged from human fellowship. Before we learn much about his body, we learn what provokes him: the sound of joy made together.

Grendel attacks at night. He finds the retainers sleeping, kills men, carries off bodies, and returns to the desolate places where he lives. The king's hall becomes a place of dread. Year after year, for twelve winters, the Danish people cannot drive him away. Hrothgar remains king, but his greatest public achievement has become the visible sign of his failure to protect those gathered under his roof. The poem begins far earlier, with the rise of the Danish royal house and the ship burial of Scyld, so its audience already knows that kings and kingdoms pass. The monster gives that historical knowledge a sudden bodily form. A hall made to preserve communal life is invaded by something that refuses it.

Across the sea in the land of the Geats, a young warrior hears the news. Beowulf is a follower of King Hygelac and a man famous for extraordinary strength. He does not wait for Hrothgar to ask. He chooses companions and crosses the water to offer help. The coast guard who meets the strangers is not foolishly hostile. He sees armored men coming from an unknown ship and asks who they are and why they have arrived. Beowulf gives his lineage, names his king, and states his purpose. The guard judges the answer worthy of trust and sends them onward. In this story, reputation and speech matter before strength can be tested. A hero must make himself intelligible to a community that has reasons to fear strangers.

Hrothgar recognizes the name. Beowulf's father, Ecgtheow, once killed a man and became entangled in a feud; Hrothgar settled the blood-payment and helped bring peace. A debt between generations now gives the young Geat's arrival a deeper meaning. Beowulf is not simply a wandering monster hunter. He belongs to a network of obligations that began before his birth. He promises to face Grendel without a sword because the creature uses none. The challenge sounds audacious, but it also exposes the risk on which heroic honor depends: courage must be demonstrated where failure can destroy the man who makes the promise.

At the feast another warrior, Unferth, challenges him. Was Beowulf not once defeated in a dangerous swimming contest against Breca? What good is this visitor's boast if an earlier test exposed his limits? Beowulf replies with a different account of the ordeal: he and Breca were separated by storm, and he fought sea creatures through the night. The exchange is sharp, proud, and public. It is easy to hear it merely as a contest of egos. But Hrothgar's court needs to judge whether the newcomer can truly do what he promises. Queen Wealhtheow passes the cup through the hall, greeting the king and the retainers in a ceremonial order that makes visible their places within the household. The hall is already beginning to recover its function as a place where people speak, answer, and bind themselves to one another.

Night falls. Hrothgar and his Danes withdraw, leaving the Geats in Heorot. Beowulf removes armor and weapon and lies ready among his companions. Grendel comes from the moors, forces the door, and sees a company of warriors asleep. He seizes and devours one of them. Then he reaches for Beowulf. A hand closes on his own with a force he has never encountered. The creature's instinct is to flee, but he cannot. Beowulf holds him. The hall itself seems about to break under their struggle. Benches shatter, timbers shake, and the noise of combat carries through the night. The monster who has terrorized a kingdom is suddenly desperate to escape one human grip.

Grendel cannot break free. His arm and shoulder tear apart. He struggles toward the fens, mortally wounded, and disappears into the dark. Beowulf hangs the severed limb in the hall as proof of the victory. Men enter Heorot in daylight and look at the damage, stunned that the monster who once drove them from the room has been defeated there. They track his bloody path toward the water but do not yet see the body. The triumph is real. It is also incomplete. A dead creature can have kin, and in this poem kinship is never only a private matter. It is the machinery through which gifts, remembrance, revenge, and peace move from one generation to another.

The Danes celebrate with stories. A court poet compares Beowulf's feat with older heroic deeds, including the dragon-slayer Sigemund and the doomed Heremod, whose strength was corrupted by his treatment of his own people. These comparisons matter because the poem does not assume that violence alone makes a good ruler. A warrior may win a fight and still fail those who depend on him. Hrothgar praises Beowulf and rewards him with precious objects and horses. The gold passes through hands in front of witnesses; giving publicly is part of how rulers create loyalty. Hrothgar also compensates the family of the Geat whom Grendel killed. Victory does not make that man's death vanish.

At another feast the hall hears a darker song: the story of Finn and the fragile peace between feuding peoples, reinforced by a marriage that cannot stop bloodshed from returning. Wealhtheow again moves through the ceremonial space and speaks of her sons' future. Gold, marriage, memory, and succession all lie beneath the heroic celebration. Hrothgar's hall feels secure for the first time in years. Men settle down to sleep. The poem has taught us enough about revenge, however, to understand what follows even before another creature arrives. Grendel had a mother. Someone has lost a son.

She comes to Heorot at night. She is frightening, but her purpose is intelligible in the violent moral world the poem describes: she intends to avenge her child. She kills Aeschere, one of Hrothgar's closest advisers, and carries away Grendel's displayed arm. When Hrothgar calls Beowulf back, grief has replaced celebration. The king describes a haunted mere, an uncanny lake among cliffs and trees, where fire seems to flicker on the water and even a hunted stag would rather die on the bank than leap into the pool. The account is not a reliable field guide to a Scandinavian landscape. It is a way of making the entrance to a hostile world felt through fear itself.

Beowulf follows the tracks to the mere. Along the way he sees Aeschere's severed head on the shore. He arms himself for a different fight. Unferth, whose earlier challenge accused him of boasting, now lends him a great sword called Hrunting. The loan is more than a practical transaction. The courtier has admitted, by his action, that the stranger may be the one able to carry the danger farther than any Dane. Beowulf asks Hrothgar to care for his companions and to send his gifts home if he dies. These are the terms on which a warrior enters the place from which he might not return: not certainty, but a public reckoning with the cost.

He plunges into the lake. It takes a long time to reach the bottom. Grendel's mother seizes him, drags him into an underwater dwelling, and attacks. The blade of Hrunting fails to wound her. She throws him down and draws a knife; his armor saves his life. Beowulf sees an enormous sword, forged for giants, hanging nearby. He grips it, strikes, and kills her. He finds Grendel's corpse and cuts off its head. The giant sword's blade melts in the poisonous blood, leaving only its decorated hilt. That remnant is what he can carry back, a fragment from a world whose strength has dissolved. Above, his companions have watched bloody water and feared he was dead. Some Danes have already departed, but the Geats remain.

Beowulf rises with Grendel's head and the sword hilt. The lake is no longer the place of endless terror it appeared to be. Back in Heorot, Hrothgar studies the hilt and speaks of how easily prosperity can mislead a strong man. He remembers the danger of a heart that becomes proud, forgets mortality, and thinks its fortune will last. His counsel matters because Beowulf himself may someday rule. The old king is giving him something different from gold: a warning about the kind of man that victories can make. Hrothgar is grateful that his hall has been saved, and he knows that no hall stays safe forever.

There is a final round of gifts and farewell speeches. Beowulf prepares to return to his own king across the sea. His triumph does not end the story of the Geats or of the Danes; the poem has already scattered reminders of wars that will come to both. Beowulf has crossed into another people's danger and restored a shared place to them, yet he has also seen how quickly public joy turns into a new occasion for grief. To understand the second half of his life, remember not only his strength but Hrothgar's words. The young warrior who overcame creatures in the hall and under the water will one day be an old king facing a danger he can no longer defeat without paying a different price.

## Text and evidence companion (not spoken)

- Textual witness: Old English *Beowulf*, lines approximately 1–1887, surviving uniquely in British Library MS Cotton Vitellius A. xv (Nowell Codex). Modern title and the manuscript's date are not proof of the date of original composition.
- [British Library, manuscript description](https://www.bl.uk/stories/blogs/posts/beowulf-online); [Electronic Beowulf, fifth edition](https://ebeowulf.uky.edu/); [Gummere's public-domain translation](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/981); [Wikipedia: Beowulf](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beowulf) for orientation.
- Line checkpoints: Scyld and Danes (1–85); Heorot, Grendel and twelve winters (86–193); Beowulf arrives and speaks (194–490); Unferth and Wealhtheow (499–641); encounter with Grendel (702–836); victory/gifts, heroic songs and Finnsburg (837–1250); mother's revenge (1251–1344); mere and dive (1345–1517); fight and giant sword (1518–1650); the hilt and Hrothgar's sermon (1651–1816); parting (1817–1887).
- The poem's Christian narrative voice and Scandinavian legendary setting are not documentary evidence for events in sixth-century Scandinavia; characters' speeches are literary acts, not stenographic records.
