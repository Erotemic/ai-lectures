# Njáls Saga — Story II: The Burning, the Courts, and the Return

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

Gunnar is dead, but his friend Njáll remains, and the saga has not yet reached its darkest event. Njáll lives at Bergþórshvoll with his wife Bergþóra and his sons. He is famous for counsel and for knowing what a dispute will become if someone takes one more revenge. His eldest son Skarphéðinn is brave, cutting in his speech, and far readier than his father to make a threat real. The family's future will depend on whether a wise man can restrain those he loves when they are convinced that violence is a duty.

The sons travel abroad, fight, make allies, and gain experience. They return with Kári Sölmundarson, who joins the family through marriage. Their old disagreements with Þráinn Sigfússon, Gunnar's kinsman, become dangerous. Þráinn has sheltered an outlaw who brought trouble on Njáll's sons, and attempts at reconciliation have not cleared away their suspicions. One winter the adversaries meet on ice. Skarphéðinn leaps forward and kills Þráinn with a blow so swift that the tale makes the manner of the killing part of his fame.

Here the narrative takes a turn we might not expect. Þráinn has a young son, Höskuldr. Njáll does not treat the boy as a future enemy whom his household must defeat before he grows. He takes him into his own care. Höskuldr becomes his foster-son, and Njáll loves him. He helps him acquire status and a chieftaincy. In a book of inherited obligations, the taking in of a dead enemy's child is an act of deliberate repair. It tries to make kinship where there might have been revenge.

The surrounding world is changing, too. The saga places Iceland's conversion to Christianity amid arguments about law and collective survival. Missionaries travel and meet opposition. Chiefs and ordinary people weigh allegiance, violence, and what it would mean for neighbors to follow different religious rules. At the national assembly, the Althing, division threatens to become open conflict. The lawspeaker Þorgeirr, himself committed to the old religion, takes time to consider the decision. He lies beneath his cloak before announcing a way forward: the people should maintain a common law and accept Christianity, with transitional arrangements concerning older practices. The account presents conversion not as a quiet private revelation shared by all, but as a settlement needed to keep a society together.

Njáll welcomes the new faith. In the saga, his welcome of the new faith poses an ethical question that every later act must confront. What should men do when duty to avenge a kinsman collides with a teaching of forgiveness? If Iceland can change religion by agreement, can Njáll's household also change its manner of settling wrongs? The next episode gives a devastating answer.

Höskuldr, the foster-son, has married Hildigunnr and leads a respected life. A schemer named Mörðr Valgarðsson is jealous of Höskuldr's standing. Mörðr understands where the old tensions lie and feeds suspicion between Höskuldr and Njáll's sons. He tells one side what will make it fear the other and makes his false reports sound like proof. Skarphéðinn and his brothers, with Kári, accept the provocation. They go to Höskuldr while he is working in his field and kill him.

The victim is not ambushing them or preparing to raid the house. He is doing farm work. The saga gives his death a terrible gentleness: he falls without returning their violence, and the words attributed to him are a prayer for the men who have killed him. The foster-son whom Njáll raised to end a feud has been murdered by the sons Njáll raised himself. No legal ingenuity can make this irony vanish. When Njáll learns what has happened, he is stricken. The blow is not only a death in the wider community; it is a blow from inside his own house against something he most valued.

Hildigunnr must decide what a widow is expected to do. Her uncle Flosi is a powerful man with followers and obligations of his own. When he comes, she takes the clothing in which Höskuldr was killed, stained with his blood, and throws it over Flosi. The gesture asks him to take the dead man's injury upon himself. He is reluctant to be driven toward a catastrophe, but his standing as a kinsman and leader is now at stake. The widow is not merely a spectator; she knows how a visible sign can turn an old claim into an immediate public demand.

Men try to settle the killing at the Althing. Friends on both sides gather, compensation is weighed, gifts and pledges are arranged. For a moment a remarkably generous settlement seems possible. It requires rival men to bear the embarrassment of backing down and to let silver and formal agreement replace an expected death. Then Skarphéðinn cannot keep his contempt to himself. He insults Flosi in language meant to strike at his manhood and standing. All the work of careful negotiation breaks apart. Pride breaks what law has nearly repaired.

Flosi and his allies prepare a raid on Bergþórshvoll. Njáll sees what is coming. People have spoken of ominous dreams and signs, but the material danger is plain enough. The household has killed a respected man, humiliated the leader who demanded compensation, and allowed a gathering of armed enemies to form. On the night of the attack, Flosi's company surrounds the farm. Fighting outside gives way to the attackers' decision to set the house on fire.

The walls and roof that once held meals, marriages, debates, and the ordinary labor of many lives now become a trap. Flosi calls for women and others whom he will spare to come out. Njáll is offered a chance to leave, for his enemies acknowledge his standing and do not want to be known as the men who burned him. But Njáll, old and unable to defend himself with the others, refuses to abandon the people inside. Bergþóra refuses to leave her husband. Their grandson stays with them. They make themselves ready for death and lie down together beneath a covering while the fire moves inward.

Skarphéðinn continues to resist. His wit has lost none of its edge, but the stubborn courage that made him frightening in battle cannot defeat smoke, falling beams, and flame. The sons die in the burning. Kári alone among the principal male defenders escapes, reaching the outside through a desperate opening. He lives because he can get out; the people he loves are dying behind him. At first escape looks like rescue. Soon it becomes the beginning of a new obligation.

After the fire, the household is examined, and the dead are found. The saga gives Njáll and Bergþóra a solemn dignity in death. To their supporters this is not simply a raid that happened to succeed; it is an act that stains the men who ordered it. Kári cannot accept silence. The law must have its say, and vengeance is also gathering.

At the Althing the burning is pursued through complicated lawsuits. The pleadings are not decorative interruptions in the adventure. The exact constitution of a court, the proper statement of an accusation, the order of witnesses, and who may challenge a legal form all matter. Clever opponents exploit technical defects. Allies shout, threaten, and argue. One participant, Þórhallr, enters the struggle despite an agonizing injury to his leg. The courtroom controversy finally becomes a battle at the assembly itself. Men who came to seek judgment take up weapons within sight of the institution that was supposed to hold them together. The scene is one of the saga's most severe jokes: the law is sophisticated enough to describe nearly every offense, but insufficient to keep men from committing new ones as they argue about the old.

A settlement is attempted. Flosi and other burners face terms, and some depart Iceland. Kári continues to track those he holds responsible. The pursuit moves across the seas to the Orkneys and into the wider northern world. In the saga's later episodes we encounter overseas lords, the battle near Dublin remembered as Clontarf, stories of signs and fatal omens, and travelers whose fortunes have been caught up in struggles much larger than an Icelandic farm. The poem of women weaving battle into the fabric of fate belongs to this late, violent atmosphere. Kári still strikes at enemies, yet each journey increases the distance between the original injury and the men now paying for it.

Flosi's own road changes. He goes on pilgrimage to Rome, seeking absolution for the burning. Kári too travels to seek spiritual release. We should not mistake either journey for proof that the past can be repaired. Dead people do not come back when living people repent. But the saga slowly puts forward a new possibility: the living men might stop passing the same debt along.

In the final movement Kári comes to Flosi's home. There has been enough reason for each man to expect the other's hatred. Instead Flosi receives him, and the two are reconciled. Kári marries Hildigunnr, the widow whose bloody cloak once summoned Flosi to vengeance. This marriage is not a simple erasure of Höskuldr's murder or the burning of Njáll. It is an acknowledgment that the people who inherit an injury can also choose not to make the injury their entire future.

The saga that began with a failed marriage settlement ends with the possibility of a different kind of household. Gunnar could not leave his home, and he died there. Njáll would not leave his family, and he died with them. Kári lived by escaping a fire and spent years learning that survival did not oblige him to keep killing forever. The final peace is hard won and incomplete. Its makers know exactly what has been lost, and their decision not to answer each loss with another death brings the saga to its difficult close.

This is an original audio-first synopsis, not a quotation or translation of the saga.

**Locators:** Njáll's sons, Kári and Þráinn (ch. 82–95); Icelandic conversion and common law (96–105); Höskuldr's murder, Hildigunnr and failed atonement (106–123); the burning and its discovery (124–131); legal challenge, battle at the Althing and overseas aftermath (132–158). Legal and geographical detail is summarized selectively; the episode is not a reconstruction of every pleading or every journey.

**Historical and critical context:** [Svanhildur Óskarsdóttir and Emily Lethbridge, eds., *New Studies in the Manuscript Tradition of Njáls saga*](https://scholarworks.wmich.edu/mip_nmw/1/) (2018); [Theodore Andersson, *The Growth of the Medieval Icelandic Sagas*](https://www.jstor.org/stable/10.7591/j.ctt7zghg) (2006). The saga's account of Iceland's conversion and of Clontarf is literature written retrospectively, not an independent transcript of the events. Conversion may be compared with other sources separately. [Wikipedia: Conversion of Iceland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christianization_of_Iceland); [Wikipedia: Battle of Clontarf](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Clontarf).

**Interpretive boundary:** Describing Hildigunnr's action as a public call for vengeance, and the reconciliation as a deliberate ethical choice, interprets the saga's plotting; the narration does not pretend to report unverifiable private thoughts of historical figures.
