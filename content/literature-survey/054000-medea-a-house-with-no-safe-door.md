# Euripides, Medea: A House with No Safe Door

A full story-first encounter with Euripides' *Medea* (431 BCE), following the received play's prologue, episodes, choral songs, and ending. The Argonautica offers a different literary Medea; this is not its sequel by the same author.

A woman stands inside a house in Corinth, and everyone outside it is afraid to hear what she might say. The old nurse knows her. The children's tutor knows what the boys' father has done. The children themselves have no idea that their ordinary walk through the city has become dangerous. Before the woman appears on stage, the audience has already heard her grief and the nurse's terrible wish: if the ship Argo had never been built, if Jason had never sailed after the Golden Fleece, perhaps the world would not have come to this doorway.

The woman is Medea. She came from Colchis, far to the east of Greece, and helped Jason accomplish tasks he could not accomplish alone. In leaving with him she betrayed her father, abandoned her homeland, and became implicated in deaths the Argonaut stories remembered in different ways. Now the couple and their two sons live in Corinth. Jason has decided to marry the daughter of Creon, the city's king. He talks of this marriage as an advantageous arrangement. Medea knows it as desertion. In a Greek city where she is a foreign woman without her father's protection, the difference between a husband's promise and a husband's refusal is not merely private heartbreak. It is a question of where she and the children can live.

The nurse speaks because Medea has stopped trusting ordinary company. She will not eat properly; she lies in misery, thinking of what has been lost. But the nurse's fear is not simply that her mistress will hurt herself. She has seen Medea's expression turn toward her children, and she fears what that glance may become. When the tutor arrives with the boys, he has news overheard in the city: Creon may banish Medea and her sons. The household's already fragile future is being closed down by another man's decision. The children go inside, and Medea's cries answer the nurse's apprehension.

Women of Corinth gather as the chorus. They are not a court, and they have no power to order Jason to return. But they can listen. Medea comes outside to speak to them. She describes the vulnerability of women in marriage: the bargain a woman enters, the household in which she must learn to live, and the difficulty of escaping a husband who has turned against her. Her own exile doubles those injuries. She has no mother or brother nearby to visit. Her speech draws sympathy without making her harmless. She asks the women to keep silent if she discovers a way to repay the people who have wronged her. The chorus agrees.

Creon arrives with an unmistakable order. Medea and the children must leave Corinth. He admits he is afraid of her: not of a foreign stranger with no resources, but of a clever person whose grief might become dangerous to his daughter. Medea changes her tone. She appeals to his role as a father. She asks not to remain forever, only for one more day, enough to arrange provisions and a refuge for the children. Creon knows he is yielding to a woman he distrusts. Nevertheless he grants the day. One day: in tragedy that small extension can be the whole space in which catastrophe grows.

As soon as he leaves, Medea tells the chorus she used his tenderness against him. She is not preparing to become resigned. She wants the king, the princess, and Jason to pay. At first the revenge is directed toward the adults who planned the marriage, and she considers how a woman with few public allies can strike them. Poison and deception become possibilities. Yet she has another difficulty. Once the powerful are dead, where can a foreign woman implicated in their deaths go? A plan for vengeance needs a plan for escape. Without shelter, she would exchange one danger for another.

Jason enters expecting to explain himself. Their argument is among the great confrontations of Greek drama because neither speaker permits the other to choose the terms. Jason says he has acted sensibly. Royal marriage can secure wealth and status, including benefits for the sons. He suggests Medea should be grateful that she now lives among Greeks, where her abilities can be recognized. Medea answers by naming what she risked for his expedition and what he promised her afterward. To describe betrayal as a clever household investment does not erase the oath that made a household possible in the first place. He offers money and introductions; she refuses both. The quarrel has not reconciled them. It has taught each exactly how the other will interpret the coming disaster.

Aegeus, king of Athens, arrives from Delphi. He is troubled by childlessness and has consulted Apollo's oracle, whose answer he does not yet understand. On his way home he stops in Corinth. Medea sees the opening she needs. She promises that her knowledge of remedies may help him obtain a child. In return she asks for shelter in Athens if she can reach it herself. Aegeus refuses to carry her out of Corinth, because he will not knowingly provoke his hosts. But he swears that, once she reaches his house, he will not surrender her to enemies. Medea binds the promise with a solemn oath. It is a cruel turn in the play: a king seeking children secures a refuge for a woman who will soon choose what no parent should choose.

Now Medea can set her entire design in motion. She sends for Jason and performs a reversal. She has thought better of her anger, she says. His new marriage may indeed be for the children's advantage. She asks that their sons be allowed to remain in Corinth, rather than share her exile. She presents her own change of mind as hard-won reasonableness. Jason, relieved to hear his language coming from her mouth, accepts the performance. The children are sent to his new bride carrying fine gifts: a beautiful robe and a golden crown. Medea knows what the gifts will do. They are a mechanism of death wrapped in the form of reconciliation.

The chorus sings uneasily of Athens, which Medea has been promised as a refuge, and of the children walking toward their father's new home. A city praised for wisdom is about to receive a fugitive whose deed will make every moral category difficult. When the boys return, the tutor brings cheerful news: the princess accepted the presents, and the children have apparently been spared exile. Medea hears good news as the signal that her revenge has advanced. She looks at her sons and wavers. Their faces and their approaching absence tear against the plan she has made. She imagines leaving with them, abandoning the intention to kill them. Then she imagines enemies using them to hurt her and returns to her resolve. The scene does not allow us to regard her as a machine that has never loved. The horror comes precisely because love is present and cannot stop her.

A messenger arrives from the royal palace with an account of the gifts. The princess first recoiled from the sight of Jason's children. Then she admired the robe and crown. She dressed herself, looked at her reflection, and gave herself the attention of a bride. Soon the ornaments became instruments of suffering. The crown burned at her head; the robe clung to her body. Creon rushed to save his daughter and died with her when he became entangled in the deadly garment. The messenger describes bodies and a household so ruined that the listener cannot mistake this for a tidy victory over an arrogant king. The first part of Medea's revenge has destroyed the bride and her father, and no ordinary physician can undo it.

The boys are still inside Medea's house. She tells the chorus she must act quickly before her enemies come. The women beg her not to turn against the children. She enters. The murder takes place offstage, while cries from within tell the audience the sons understand what is happening. The chorus can hear and cannot stop her. The playwright does not place the children in view to make the act bearable or comprehensible. He makes the audience listen to its limits. In the same performance where Medea won sympathy for an abandoned woman, she has crossed into an act that sympathy cannot excuse.

Jason returns to save his sons from the vengeance he expects Corinth to take. He believes Medea may have killed the royal family and that the boys are therefore in danger. He discovers that he is too late. Medea appears above him in a vehicle associated with Helios, the Sun, whom she claims as her ancestor. She has the children's bodies with her and will not give them to Jason. The elevation matters: the man who thought he could manage her future cannot reach even the dead children. Jason begs to touch or bury them. She refuses. He calls her a destroyer of her own house. She calls him the breaker of oaths whose betrayal made this ruin possible. Neither statement cancels the other.

Medea announces that she will bury the boys herself at Hera's sanctuary and establish commemorative rites in Corinth. She will go to Athens, where Aegeus has sworn to receive her. She also foretells a bitter death for Jason associated with the remains of his famous ship. The chorus ends with a thought about the gods bringing outcomes no one anticipated. It is not an absolution. The god-given escape has not settled the moral question. Aeschylus and Sophocles often put a recognizably human conflict beneath divine pressure; here a human crime is carried beyond the reach of the man who demands judgment.

There are ways to retell Medea as the evil sorceress, or as the wronged outsider, or as an argument against the marriage arrangements of a Greek city. Euripides makes each of those readings incomplete if it is allowed to become the whole story. Jason really has broken his commitments. Medea really is endangered by exile. The women who first heard her pain have reason to understand it. And she really kills children who bear no responsibility for her husband's act. A good hearing of the play has to hold those facts at once. The ending does not reward us with a reliable judge who explains how they fit together.

This is not the same Medea as the one who travels with Jason in Apollonius's much later *Argonautica*. The Greek stories changed as poets and performers returned to them. Euripides gives us a particular version, performed at Athens in 431 BCE, beginning at the moment when the heroic expedition's famous prize has become irrelevant to an actual household. The Golden Fleece once promised a kingdom and a future. In Corinth, that future lies in two small bodies that neither parent can bring home. The door the nurse feared has finally opened, and there is no safe place on either side.
