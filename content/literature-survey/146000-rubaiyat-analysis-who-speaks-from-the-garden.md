# The Rubáiyát — Analysis: Who Speaks from the Garden?

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

The last gesture of Edward FitzGerald's Rubáiyát is the overturning of a glass. A companion is asked to remember someone who will no longer be present at a future gathering. There is a peculiar literary problem concealed in this intimate request: who is the person asking? The cover of the 1859 book says Omar Khayyam, an astronomer-poet from Persia. The words, their sequence, and much of their shape belong to FitzGerald, a nineteenth-century English writer. The poems behind the English version were gathered across centuries, and many quatrains attributed to Khayyam cannot securely be assigned to him. The voice we hear is, in an important sense, made of several histories. A responsible reading must hold those histories apart without pretending that the finished English poem therefore has no artistic life of its own.

Start with the form. A rubāʿī is a Persian quatrain, a compact four-line unit often rhyming in the first, second, and fourth lines, with the third line opening a space for a turn in thought. It can sound like a proverb, a confession, a joke, a skeptical objection, or a miniature philosophical drama. Unlike the opening of a novel, a quatrain need not promise that a character or event will return in the next stanza. Each poem can stand alone. This helps explain why collections attributed to Khayyam vary and why the order of quatrains can change dramatically between books. If a translator presents the material as one continuous autobiographical confession, we should ask whether the Persian witnesses authorize that continuity.

The historical Omar Khayyam lived in the eleventh and early twelfth centuries. He was a major mathematical thinker and was involved in astronomy and calendrical scholarship. His treatises, discussed in the history of Islamic science, matter independently of any poetic collection. He investigated classifications of cubic equations, geometrical methods of solution, and questions about mathematical proof. It is tempting to imagine that his quatrains are simply the personal diary of a mathematician who decided all calculation was futile. That story is pleasingly dramatic but unsupported. The philosophical texts associated securely with him, the scientific work, the early testimonies about verses, and the much later manuscript collections are different kinds of evidence.

The attribution problem is substantial. Quatrains circulated in manuscript culture, acquired names, lost names, and were copied into expanding collections. A memorable skeptical verse might be attached to a famous skeptic; a striking lament over death might be associated with a man whose name already evoked questions about fate. Scholars have proposed quite different groups of likely authentic poems. The centuries separating Khayyam's lifetime from prominent surviving collections mean that no edition can simply announce all its quatrains as verbatim products of one author. This does not mean that none are his. It means that a lecture cannot cite an English stanza and confidently announce that it reveals exactly what Khayyam believed in a particular year.

FitzGerald encountered this tradition through his friend Edward Cowell and transcripts of Persian manuscripts, including the Ouseley manuscript now at the Bodleian Library. His edition was published anonymously in 1859. The title placed an astronomer-poet on the cover, but the book was an English artistic reconstruction. FitzGerald chose and rearranged quatrains and sometimes went further than close translation, creating linked stanzas out of the material available to him. The result is especially coherent as a movement through a garden: morning invitation, recollection of kings, argument with scholars, the potter's shop, rising moon, and the empty place where the speaker once sat. That coherent arc is a literary achievement, but it belongs first to the English book.

The importance of 1859 is not only that it gives us a date. The first edition has seventy-five stanzas. FitzGerald revised the poem repeatedly, publishing further versions in 1868, 1872, and 1879. Familiar lines can differ, and a passage prominent in a later version may not occupy the same place in the first. Readers frequently mix quotations from the editions without noticing. For this overview we chose the first, not because it is the one true Rubáiyát, but because it has a definite and inspectable shape. A good literary study names its text rather than allowing several versions to dissolve into an imaginary standard original.

What kind of person does that version create? Certainly a speaker who likes to drink, or at least likes to talk about wine. Yet a reader who decides that every cup in the poem simply means literal alcohol will miss much of the resonance; a reader who declares that all wine must be a coded reference to mystical union will miss another kind of force. Persian poetry has long used taverns, wine, gardens, beloved companions, and intoxication in varied contexts, literal, erotic, philosophical, skeptical, and mystical. FitzGerald emphasizes sensuous and skeptical possibilities, and he writes for nineteenth-century English readers who bring their own religious arguments to the words. The most defensible interpretation does not require us to assign one unchanging meaning to every grape.

At first the cup can represent enoughness. A modest meal and the company of a loved person seem preferable to royal power. Then the same cup becomes a defense against fear of dying. Later it serves as a way of mocking religious or philosophical systems that promise certainty beyond experience. In the potter's shop it becomes an object whose material nature is crucial, clay that once may have been a living body. Near the end a cup is a tool of memory. The poem changes the meaning of a repeated image as the emotional argument deepens. If we collapse all the wine into a simple slogan about pleasure, we lose the craft that makes those transformations possible.

FitzGerald also constructs a conflict between time and permanence. Empires vanish, their palaces turn into animal habitats, and their names survive chiefly as literary memories. The garden renews itself, but this renewal does not grant immortality to the person watching it. A rose may bloom again next year, yet the companion who sees this year's flower may be dead. That difference between recurring natural cycles and finite individual life creates the poem's aching pleasure. The speaker is not foolishly ignorant of mortality when he asks for another glass. He asks because he knows. The poem presses delight and grief against one another until they become hard to separate.

The attacks on theological certainty are sometimes startling. If God makes a person with particular weaknesses, how can the maker punish that person for being made in such a way? If the future is determined, what becomes of responsibility? If sages claim to know the fate of the soul, what evidence can they give that their words are more than words? These are real philosophical problems, not merely an excuse for drinking. They are also not identical to a systematic argument by the historical Khayyam. FitzGerald's voice moves between doubt, defiance, complaint, resignation, and renewed enjoyment. It is the movement that we can study directly in the first English edition; the theology of the original Persian corpus requires a different textual inquiry.

This is where the historical Khayyam's mathematical reputation can mislead a modern audience. One might expect a famous mathematician to approach human fate as a theorem awaiting proof, then to abandon the project in bitterness. But the historical mathematical treatises do not demonstrate that their author wrote each skeptical stanza later attributed to him. Nor does mathematical skill imply one particular religious conclusion. A sophisticated reader can value the echo between geometry and the book's recurring images of patterns, vessels, and cycles while admitting that the echo is a reading of FitzGerald's sequence, not evidence of a hidden mathematical code placed there in eleventh-century Persia.

A related temptation is to say that the poem predicts modern secular thought and therefore speaks to us because it is already like us. There are genuine affinities: doubt about predestination, interest in the present life, the difficulty of grounding cosmic justice. But that shorthand risks removing the poem from Persianate intellectual histories in which skepticism, Islamic theology, philosophical rationalism, asceticism, mysticism, court culture, and poetic play already had rich and complicated relationships. A verse that asks why the potter made a crooked pot can enter a conversation about divine justice, not simply a victory parade for modern unbelief. It is better to allow the question to remain a question.

The Victorian reception adds another layer. FitzGerald's little book barely attracted attention at first, then moved through networks of readers, artists, and writers. Figures associated with the Pre-Raphaelites admired it; illustrated editions later helped turn it into a visual and decorative phenomenon. The book gave many English-speaking readers an imagined Persian garden populated by roses, nightingales, and philosophical drinkers. It also helped generate enormous interest in the poetry of Khayyam and encouraged later translators to work directly with Persian manuscripts. We should be able to say both things: the version opened a door, and it framed what came through the door in distinctly English and Victorian terms.

That framing is sometimes called Orientalism. The word is useful when it directs attention to who possessed the power to select, translate, publish, and define a foreign literary tradition for an English market. It becomes less useful if it makes all cross-cultural artistic work equivalent or if it assumes that Persian readers had no agency. FitzGerald did not invent the Persian quatrain, and his book should not stand in for the entirety of Persian literature. But it is also not merely a clumsy error to be thrown out. Its rhythms, dramatic sequence, and images profoundly affected how the collection circulated in English. The task is to admire the poetry without mistaking the English garden for a transparent window into all of Persia.

Consider how an English literary reputation can alter the apparent meaning of an author's name. In Iran and in specialist scholarship, Khayyam's legacy includes mathematical, astronomical, and philosophical achievements as well as disputed quatrains. In much of the English-speaking world, the name evokes a garden and a wine cup above all. This is not a harmless difference in emphasis when later readers use the English book as documentary evidence about the life and mind of a medieval thinker. Names travel more easily than edition histories. The lecture's first responsibility is to put that history back in view.

A small contrast may help. Suppose we encountered a single anonymous Persian quatrain saying that the flowers will bloom after we are gone. Its brevity might strike us as a concentrated insight and then let us go. Put it between FitzGerald's quatrains about fallen kings and his later quarrel with the potter, and the same thought becomes a stage in a longer emotional argument. It turns the rose into evidence that splendor dies, then prepares the clay imagery in which dead bodies return as ordinary matter. Sequence changes interpretation. FitzGerald's greatest intervention is not necessarily one striking new metaphor; it is the deliberate construction of a speaker who changes his mind and returns to his own fears.

The end makes that design visible. The early morning is full of summons and doors; the late poem is full of absence. The companion's hand will still be able to raise or invert a glass after the speaker's hand cannot. The object that meant the pleasure of drinking now means the refusal to forget the dead. The gesture contains no argument proving an afterlife or proving its absence. It admits the limits of knowledge and proposes a human response within those limits. Whatever one believes about mortality, the request can be understood: when you return to a place where we were happy together, remember that I was there.

We can now see why the work deserves a place in a literature overview catalog even though it has no plot in the ordinary sense. It offers an education in compact poetic reasoning, recurring imagery, and the way the arrangement of independent fragments can create a sustained emotional journey. It also asks a demanding question about translation. Can a poem be a masterpiece and still be a poor guide to the exact intentions of the poet whose name it bears? The answer, here, is yes. That is not a reason to love it less. It is a reason to read with two kinds of care: attention to the extraordinary English work before us, and respect for the Persian texts and intellectual history that cannot be compressed into it.

At the end of this analysis, there are therefore two kinds of empty space. One is the place left by a dead guest, honored by the inverted glass. The other is the gap between the historical Khayyam and the speaker FitzGerald made. Neither can simply be filled by wishful thinking. We can treasure the English poem, study the Persian witnesses, and allow both the pleasure and the uncertainty to remain. The garden is not smaller because we know how it was assembled. It becomes a richer place to visit.

## Text and evidence companion (not spoken)

### Critical boundaries

The historical Omar Khayyam (c. 1048–early twelfth century), his authenticated mathematical and philosophical corpus, the contested corpus of attributed Persian quatrains, and FitzGerald's *1859 English poetic sequence* are separate objects. This analysis avoids presenting an English stanza as autobiography or treating a Victorian editorial arc as original Persian authorial intention. Its treatment of Orientalism, wine imagery and the linking function of the empty cup is an explicitly argued literary interpretation, not a claim that every reader or philologist must agree.

The first edition is 75 quatrains; later editions were revised and have different selections. Scholarly discussion of disputed attribution, translations, and the science is grounded in Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy and Encyclopaedia Iranica. The reception of the booklet through Victorian literary circles is recorded in Iranica, not reconstructed from anecdotes without sources.
