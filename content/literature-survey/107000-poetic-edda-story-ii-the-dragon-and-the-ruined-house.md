# The Poetic Edda — Stories II: The Dragon and the Ruined House

The Sigurðr, Brynhildr, Guðrún, and Atli heroic materials, told through explicitly named Eddic lays and their manuscript prose links. This is a route through selected poems, not a claim that they form one continuous, originally unified epic. Some episodes have conflicting variants, and the surviving central manuscript has a physical gap.

A man is trying to teach a young hero how to kill a dragon. Regin, who knows the value of metal and the perilous history of treasure, has a reason to want the creature dead. The dragon guards gold that was once claimed as ransom; Regin believes that the gold should return to him. His student is Sigurðr, son of Sigmund, a warrior whose name will travel far beyond the northern poems that preserve parts of his story. But even before a sword is forged, the treasure has already stained one family with murder.

The tale told in the *Reginsmál* begins farther back than Sigurðr. Three gods, Odin, Hœnir, and Loki, came upon an otter and killed it. What appeared to be an animal was Ótr, a being of another kind, one of the sons of Hreiðmarr. The father demanded compensation, and the gods obtained gold from a dwarf called Andvari. The bargain carried a curse. The gold was enough to fill the skin and cover it, but it could not buy an end to desire. One of Hreiðmarr's sons, Fáfnir, murdered his father to seize the treasure and withdrew into a monstrous, dragon-like existence as its guardian. Regin, another son, remained and carried the old grievance forward. He now wants Sigurðr's sword to complete a quarrel that began before Sigurðr had anything to do with it.

Regin has learned enough of the world to make his purpose sound like instruction. He tells the youth of the treasure and presses him toward the task. Sigurðr has his own obligations, among them avenging his father's death. His weapon, Gram, is fashioned from the broken sword associated with Sigmund. The making of it is an act of inheritance: a power interrupted in one generation is recovered in the next. But the sword has barely returned to service before Sigurðr is entangled in the greed of a family that has destroyed itself. A dragon is easier to identify as an enemy than a man who speaks as one's teacher.

Sigurðr digs a place from which he can strike the serpent as it passes. Fáfnir comes from his lair, terrible and dangerous, and the sword finds him from below. In the poem called *Fáfnismál*, the dying dragon and the victorious youth speak. Their encounter is not a simple cheer for force. Fáfnir asks questions and gives warnings. The treasure has caused deaths already. Whoever takes it will inherit peril with the gold. Sigurðr knows enough to hear the warning. Yet he has risked his life for this victory, and warning is not the same thing as a way back to innocence.

Regin arrives to claim his part. He asks that the dragon's heart be prepared. Sigurðr sets it to roast, and when he touches the cooking flesh he burns a finger. He puts his finger to his mouth and tastes the blood. Suddenly the cries of birds become intelligible. Their song tells him that Regin means to kill him and keep the treasure. The revelation has the cruel symmetry of the whole episode. Fáfnir murdered a father for gold; Regin has used a young warrior to recover it; and Sigurðr has learned, too late to avoid bloodshed, what kind of pupil a greedy teacher hopes to produce. Sigurðr kills Regin and takes the hoard, along with a burden that no one can see by looking at the gold alone.

The birds also guide his attention beyond treasure. A woman lies sleeping within a ring of defenses, associated with the powers of battle. Sigurðr rides to the mountain and finds a figure in armor. He removes the helmet and cuts away a mail coat that has enclosed the sleeper. The figure is a woman who awakens and greets the world. In the received material called *Sigrdrífumál*, she is a battle-maiden and a dispenser of knowledge. She gives Sigurðr counsel: how to use and recognize runes, how to behave, how to deal with danger. For a moment the heroic tale pauses at the place where the warrior could learn a different kind of strength, the attention needed to choose among perils rather than merely to survive them.

Then the manuscript itself breaks. In the principal surviving codex, pages are missing from the middle of the heroic material. What we possess has not merely suffered the ordinary confusion of oral stories retold over centuries. The physical book has a loss where a modern reader might most want certainty about promises, encounters, and betrayals. Other poems, their prose links, and later writings let us follow related stories, but they do not create an original missing page. We can tell the shape of the disaster without pretending that the source is whole.

The name Brynhildr enters the tragic sequence. In some parts of the northern tradition the awakened battle-maiden is identified with Brynhildr; in other poems the relationships and identities cannot be fitted together without difficulty. We should not hide that difficulty, because it changes how we hear the characters. The later heroic story brings Sigurðr to the household of Gjúki, with its sons Gunnar and Högni and their sister Guðrún. Sigurðr marries Guðrún and becomes a valued ally. Meanwhile Gunnar seeks Brynhildr, a woman of extraordinary pride and power whose marriage cannot be won by ordinary courtly assurances.

The means by which she is won involve deception. In versions connected with the heroic poems, Sigurðr assumes Gunnar's appearance or position to pass the ordeal that Gunnar cannot accomplish. The woman who believes she has been approached by one man has actually been approached by another. The deception makes an arrangement between powerful houses possible, but it places a falsehood at the center of two marriages. What one party calls a triumph of loyalty to a friend another can experience as a violation of trust. Brynhildr learns enough to understand that the honor she believed she possessed has been compromised.

A quarrel between the women brings concealed events into speech. Guðrún, Sigurðr's wife, knows things that Brynhildr has not been allowed to know. The contest over husbands' status becomes a contest over who possesses the truth. In the heroic lays the accusation is more than an embarrassment at court. If Brynhildr was deceived in the conditions of marriage, then the people around her have rewritten her life without her consent. Her fury is a demand that the deception be answered. The demand turns toward Sigurðr, and the circles of alliance begin to collapse.

Gunnar and Högni are bound to Sigurðr by ties that make open killing dishonorable. The solution is no less terrible for being indirect. A younger brother, Gutthormr, is urged to carry out the murder. The poems do not give one completely consistent account of the moment of Sigurðr's death. Some locate it in the bed, some in other circumstances. What matters across the accounts is the betrayal of the man who had placed his strength at the service of those who now destroy him. The dragon-slayer cannot protect himself from a household's decision. His sword once restored a father's broken power. It cannot restore trust after it has been deliberately cut away.

And now we hear another poem, a different kind of event. Guðrún is beside Sigurðr's body. In *Guðrúnarkviða I*, women gather around her and try to help her weep. They tell of their own losses. Their grief is sincere, but it does not immediately reach her. She cannot yet release what has happened into tears. The lament is not an ornament hung on the side of the adventure. It is the place where the consequences of the adventure finally become the poem's center. When Guðrún sees the extent of Sigurðr's wound, grief breaks through. The hero who killed the dragon has become the object that another person must learn to live without.

Brynhildr's fate is also bound to the death. In *Sigurðarkviða in skamma*, her anguish and fury lead toward self-destruction, and her demands for funeral arrangements continue her terrible insistence on being recognized. The later poem *Helreið Brynhildar* imagines her speaking as she journeys toward the world of the dead. There she defends her own life against accusation. The reader who heard only the court's account of her behavior is forced to hear her version. Even death does not reduce the heroine to an explanation supplied by someone else.

The story does not stop where a modern romance might stop, with the death of the hero and the lament of the women. The later heroic poems turn increasingly toward Guðrún. She is given in marriage to Atli, a ruler whose name recalls Attila in the wider Germanic heroic tradition. Her brothers, Gunnar and Högni, survive Sigurðr, but their earlier betrayal has not made them safe. Atli desires their treasure and invites them to his hall under assurances that conceal danger. Guðrún attempts to warn them. The warning itself becomes part of the story's fatal irony: people who recognize a trap may still enter because honor and pride forbid a retreat that would look like fear.

In *Atlakviða*, Gunnar and Högni come to Atli and find the hospitality false. They resist, and they fall into their enemy's power. Atli wants the hoard. Gunnar demands proof that his brother Högni is dead before he will reveal its hiding place. A heart is brought to him; one account distinguishes the heart of a frightened servant from that of the courageous Högni. When Gunnar is assured that Högni has been killed, he announces that the secret of the gold will die with him. Atli can take a man's life but cannot force a dead brother to speak. Gunnar is put into a pit of serpents. The treasure that first set Regin against Fáfnir has arrived at another room full of men who believe it more valuable than the lives around it.

Guðrún does not remain the silent mourner we met beside Sigurðr. She becomes the avenger of her brothers. Her acts are shocking. She kills her children by Atli, serves their flesh to their father in a feast, and reveals what he has eaten. She then kills Atli and burns his hall. The poem's violence is not clean, and the singer does not offer a comfortable separation between heroic courage and horror. Guðrún's grief for one set of kin drives her to destroy another. The feasting hall, where hospitality should secure a bond, becomes the site at which betrayal is repaid in a form that makes all future hospitality seem uncertain.

A second poem, *Atlamál*, gives a related but different account of Atli's treachery and Guðrún's revenge. It expands domestic detail and alters some events. We need not force those tellings into an invented master narrative. The shared catastrophe is sufficient: gold, false invitation, broken kinship, murder, and a woman who refuses to let one more betrayal pass unanswered. If the dragon's treasure once promised possession, here possession has narrowed to a secret that no living person can enjoy.

Even then the heroic cycle continues. The poems associated with Guðrún's later life tell of her daughter Svanhildr, sent into another dangerous marriage. A ruler named Jörmunrekkr orders Svanhildr killed when he believes an accusation against her. In *Guðrúnarhvöt* and *Hamðismál*, Guðrún calls on her sons Hamðir and Sörli to avenge their sister. They go toward a powerful enemy, but their own anger destroys their judgment along the way. Their expedition ends not in the healing of the family but in a final act of violence and their deaths. The woman who once could not weep has become a voice urging the last surviving sons toward a revenge that consumes them.

The sequence we have followed is often remembered as the legend of Sigurðr, the dragon-slayer. Yet as the poems proceed, his death leaves the stage to women who cannot be contained in the role of a hero's reward. Brynhildr demands to tell her own cause. Guðrún grieves, survives, and becomes capable of acts the early bereaved woman might have found unimaginable. The gold passes through men, but the moral weight of the poems accumulates in the people made to bear the results of its pursuit.

It is important to end by saying what kind of listening this has been. We have moved among the *Reginsmál*, *Fáfnismál*, and *Sigrdrífumál*, the fragments and lays of Sigurðr, the lament of Guðrún, the story of Atli, and the late revenge poems. We have not recited one complete original epic preserved intact from a single ancient singer. We have crossed poems that disagree, passages linked by prose, and a gap where the manuscript cannot answer us. That is not a defect to conceal in order to manufacture a smoother story. The very survival of competing voices lets us hear what different tellers thought the tragedy meant: how greed breeds itself, how honor licenses treachery, and how the dead continue to govern the acts of those who remain.

- **Editorial boundaries:** *Sigrdrífumál* is damaged at the Codex Regius lacuna; the awakening episode and the later Brynhildr material should not be seamlessly joined as if all sources agreed. The betrayal and killing of Sigurðr vary across Eddic poems and later *Völsunga saga*, which has not been silently imported as this lecture's primary source. Likewise the kinship revenge stories differ between *Atlakviða* and *Atlamál*, and the final Guðrún and Jörmunrekkr material is selected rather than exhaustively retold. Atli's identification with Attila belongs to legend and reception, not to an eyewitness history of the fifth century. The Norse and German versions of the Burgundian destruction are substantively different: Guðrún is not simply the Kriemhild of the *Nibelungenlied* with names translated.
