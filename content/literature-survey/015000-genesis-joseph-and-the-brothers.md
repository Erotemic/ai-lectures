# Genesis, Part Three: Joseph and the Brothers

An original narration of Genesis 37–50, including the chapter 38 Judah-and-Tamar interlude, Benjamin's ordeal, and the deaths at the end. The manuscript follows the received Hebrew book, not a composite historical biography of Joseph or a harmonized film plot. The source notes preserve the ambiguities of the merchants in Genesis 37 rather than pretending every transmission question is resolved.

A young man is looking for his brothers. He has been sent from home to find them tending the flocks, and has walked beyond the place where he expected them to be. A stranger sees him wandering and points him toward another pasture. The young man's name is Joseph. He is seventeen. His father loves him especially, and has given him a conspicuous robe. He has also had dreams in which his brothers bow before him. Joseph has told the dreams to the very people they would humiliate. The brothers see him coming from a distance, and before he reaches them they begin to plan his death.

This is the opening of the long final story in Genesis. Its strength is in the distance between what the characters think they know and what the listener gradually learns. The brothers see a favorite son who expects to rule them. Jacob, their father, sees the boy whose mother Rachel he loved. Joseph sees himself as the dreamer whose strange visions are worth repeating. None sees the famine, the Egyptian court, or the reunion that will make the dreams almost unbearable to remember.

Reuben objects to murder and urges the others to throw Joseph into a pit instead, intending to rescue him later. The brothers strip Joseph of his robe and leave him in an empty cistern. Then they sit down to eat. While their brother is trapped nearby, a caravan bound for Egypt approaches. Judah proposes selling him rather than shedding his blood. Genesis's wording also brings Midianite traders into the scene, a feature of the received narrative that has received much textual discussion. The outcome is clear even if the sequence of traders is not always made perfectly smooth: Joseph is carried away to Egypt. Reuben returns to the empty pit and tears his clothing in despair.

The brothers stain Joseph's robe with a slaughtered goat's blood and send it home for Jacob to identify. They do not need to speak the lie in full. Jacob supplies the explanation himself: a wild beast has torn his son apart. He refuses to be comforted. This is the terrible cleverness of the deception. A father's grief becomes its own evidence, and the brothers who caused it now must live inside it.

At this point the book turns aside, apparently leaving Joseph behind, and tells the story of Judah and Tamar. Judah has sons, one of whom marries Tamar and dies. Under a family obligation, another son is to provide offspring for the dead brother; he refuses and dies as well. Judah sends Tamar back to her father's house while withholding his youngest son. Years pass, and Tamar recognizes that the promised arrangement will not be honored. She disguises herself and meets Judah on a road, taking his personal seal, cord, and staff as security. When her pregnancy is discovered, Judah condemns her until she produces those objects. He recognizes them and admits that she has been more in the right than he has. This chapter is not an accidental moral aside. It places the question of recognition, withheld obligation, and the capacity to admit wrong beside Joseph's coming story. Judah, who helped sell a brother, must learn to recognize a wrong he has done himself.

Joseph arrives in Egypt as a slave. He is sold into the house of Potiphar, an officer of Pharaoh. He works well and becomes trusted, but a new danger comes from his master's wife. She repeatedly demands that Joseph lie with her; he refuses. One day she seizes his garment, and he flees, leaving it behind. She uses the garment to accuse him of assault. Like the robe sent to Jacob, an article of clothing becomes the instrument of a false story. Joseph is imprisoned. The book does not romanticize his situation: skill and virtue have not protected him from slavery, accusation, and punishment.

In prison, Joseph wins responsibility for other prisoners. Two men who once served Pharaoh, the chief cupbearer and chief baker, are confined there. Both have dreams and are troubled by them. Joseph interprets the dreams, giving the cupbearer hope of restoration and the baker a warning of death. Events follow his words. Yet the cupbearer forgets Joseph when he is restored, and more years pass. Dreams are now moving the story toward power, but not quickly enough to rescue the interpreter without further suffering.

Then Pharaoh dreams of seven fat cows emerging from the Nile, followed by seven gaunt cows that swallow them. He dreams of seven healthy ears of grain consumed by seven blighted ones. The court cannot satisfy him with an interpretation. The cupbearer remembers the prisoner. Joseph is brought out, cleaned and dressed for the royal presence. He says interpretation belongs to God, and explains that both dreams announce the same coming sequence: seven years of plenty followed by seven years of famine. He proposes that grain be stored during abundance for the coming scarcity. Pharaoh is impressed not only by the interpretation but by the capacity to act upon it. Joseph is placed in charge.

The boy once thrown into a pit becomes a governor in Egypt. He oversees food stores, marries, and has two sons, Manasseh and Ephraim. When famine comes, people come to buy grain. A government built around preparation can now feed those who arrive, though the later chapters also show how Egyptians' resources and land pass under Pharaoh's control as scarcity deepens. Genesis permits admiration for Joseph's foresight while still describing the transformation of a population's dependence on the throne.

Far away, Jacob's household needs grain too. He sends his sons to Egypt, keeping Benjamin, Rachel's younger son, at home. The brothers arrive before Joseph and bow to the official who controls the grain. They do not recognize him. He recognizes them at once. The dreams of his youth have reached their strange fulfillment, but the reunion is postponed. Joseph accuses the brothers of being spies and interrogates them about their family. He keeps Simeon as a hostage and demands that they return with the younger brother they claim to have left at home.

The brothers are frightened and speak among themselves as though the Egyptian official cannot understand. They remember Joseph's anguish when he begged them not to harm him. Now they think their own suffering may be a reckoning for that old cruelty. Joseph understands their language and withdraws to weep. It is one of the narrative's most painful devices. The reader knows that the authority before them is the abandoned brother, while the brothers confess the guilt without knowing who hears.

Joseph sends grain back with them and secretly restores the money they paid. The brothers discover it and are terrified. Back home, Jacob resists sending Benjamin. He has already lost Joseph; the disappearance of another son of Rachel is more than he can bear. Reuben offers a desperate guarantee, but it does not persuade him. Eventually the famine forces a choice. Judah pledges himself for Benjamin's safety and convinces their father to let the boy go.

At the second visit, Joseph welcomes the brothers to a meal and seats them in age order, to their amazement. He sees Benjamin and must again withdraw to weep. He gives Benjamin a disproportionately generous share. The scene is not simple forgiveness disguised as a feast. Joseph is still testing men who once resented the favorite child and sold him. Will they do to Benjamin what they did to Joseph when the opportunity comes?

The test is prepared by hiding Joseph's silver cup in Benjamin's sack. After the brothers depart, Joseph sends a steward to search them. The cup is found. The brothers return, and Joseph declares that Benjamin alone must remain as his slave. The others may go free. The conditions reproduce the old temptation with terrible precision: sacrifice one favored brother, rescue yourselves, and tell the father what you please.

Judah now speaks. He recounts their father's love for Benjamin and the grief over the lost son. He asks how they could return without the boy. He offers to remain in Benjamin's place as a slave. The brother who once proposed selling Joseph has come to a point where he is prepared to lose his own freedom so that another brother can go home. Joseph can no longer restrain himself. He orders the Egyptian attendants away and reveals who he is. His weeping is so loud it can be heard outside.

The brothers are stunned. Joseph tells them not to be consumed by self-reproach, because God has sent him ahead to preserve life through the famine. This is Joseph's interpretation of his suffering, offered from within the story. It does not make the sale innocent or the years of captivity unreal. He names the injury precisely: they sold him. But he now sees a future made possible in spite of it, and he asks them to bring their father into Egypt while the famine continues.

When Jacob hears that Joseph is alive, he can scarcely believe it. He travels south with the family, stopping to offer sacrifices at Beersheba. In a night vision God tells him not to fear going down to Egypt and promises that his descendants will become a great people there. Joseph goes to meet him. Father and son embrace, and Jacob says he can now die in peace because he has seen Joseph alive. The reunion fulfills a wish that had been kept buried for years beneath the bloody robe.

The family settles in Goshen. Jacob meets Pharaoh. The old wanderer blesses the ruler who has given his son such power, and describes the years of his life as few and difficult compared with those of his fathers. He is not exaggerating within the world of the book. His life has contained exile, bereavement, deceit, dangerous journeys, and finally the restoration of a child he thought dead.

As famine continues, Joseph's arrangements enable the royal grain stores to feed the people, but Egyptians exchange first money, then livestock, land, and service for food and seed. The land largely passes into Pharaoh's possession, with a standing share due to the crown; the priests' land is treated differently. The same administrative system that saves lives also increases the state's reach. The story records that cost, even if it does not stop to deliver a modern economic verdict.

Jacob nears death. He adopts Joseph's sons Ephraim and Manasseh in a blessing, crossing his hands so that the younger receives precedence. Joseph objects, but Jacob knows what he is doing. Once more Genesis turns the order of elder and younger into an unsettled question. Jacob blesses his sons, speaking of their characters, their acts, and their futures. Some words are tender; others are stern recollections of violence. He asks to be buried with his ancestors in the cave purchased by Abraham. When he dies, Joseph and his brothers carry him back to Canaan and bury him there.

With their father gone, the brothers fear Joseph's revenge. They send a plea for forgiveness. Joseph weeps again and answers that he is not in God's place. They meant harm, he says; God intended good to preserve many lives. He promises to provide for them and their children. They do not become people who have never committed the wrong. They become a family trying to live after recognizing it.

Joseph grows old in Egypt. Before he dies, he tells his relatives that God will surely attend to them and bring them from Egypt to the land promised to their ancestors. He asks that his bones be carried with them when that day comes. The book ends not with a completed return but with Joseph embalmed and placed in a coffin in Egypt. The family is safe, for now, yet away from the promised land. A coffin waits for a journey no one living at the end of Genesis has made.

That ending explains why Genesis is not a collection of reassuring endings even when it tells of rescue. Eden is closed. The flood survivors must rebuild. Abraham never owns the land promised to his descendants beyond a burial plot. Jacob survives but limps. Joseph lives and forgives, yet dies in a foreign country. The thread joining these stories is not an easy triumph over uncertainty. It is the possibility of carrying obligation, memory, and hope forward when one generation cannot finish what it began.
