# Kojiki, Part II: The Hare and the Heavenly Descent

Selected later narratives from *Kojiki* book I: the White Hare of Inaba, Ōkuninushi's trials, his establishment and relinquishment of earthly rule, and the heavenly descent of Ninigi.

In the land of Inaba a small white hare sits without its skin. Travelers approach, and the hare asks for help. The first men who pass give cruel advice, telling it to bathe in salt water and lie in the wind. The wounded animal obeys and suffers more. Behind them comes a younger brother who asks what happened. The hare confesses that it tricked a company of sea creatures into forming a bridge so it could cross the water, then boasted of the deception before reaching safety. One of the creatures tore off its skin. The younger traveler does not praise the trick. He also refuses to make the trick a reason to abandon the injured creature. He tells the hare to wash in fresh water and rest among the soft flowers by the bank. The hare is healed.

The helpful young man is Ōnamuji, later known as Ōkuninushi, a central figure in the *Kojiki*'s account of rule over the land. His elder brothers are on a courtship journey, hoping to marry a princess. The hare predicts that she will choose the younger brother instead. The prediction angers the others. Their envy becomes lethal. They devise traps that kill their brother, and divine helpers restore him to life. The pattern repeats: an apparent domestic rivalry is revealed as a struggle over who can endure danger and emerge with a claim to authority. The hero does not succeed because the older brothers obey rules of fair competition. They use violence whenever their prestige is threatened.

Ōnamuji eventually travels into the realm associated with Susanoo, the unpredictable deity who had once been expelled from heaven. There he encounters Susanoo's daughter Suseri. She favors him, but her father imposes deadly tests. The young man is sent to sleep in a room filled with serpents, then among other dangerous creatures. Suseri provides protective objects or advice that allows him to survive. When Susanoo sends him onto a plain and sets the grasses alight, he escapes through the help of a small creature that directs him toward a hiding place. The tests are vivid, but they also establish what the story thinks the making of a ruler requires: endurance, assistance, and an ability to survive powers stronger than himself.

At last he escapes with Suseri and carries away treasures associated with kingship. Susanoo pursues them to the boundary of his domain, then shouts instructions and names that amount, paradoxically, to recognition. He tells the young man to establish a great dwelling and become a strong ruler. The old violent force has been displaced by someone who has lived through its trials. The story then turns to songs, marriages, and the complicated relationships of Ōkuninushi's household. There is jealousy, bargaining, and persuasion, not one simple ascent from suffering to an untroubled throne. The work refuses to separate divine sovereignty cleanly from human emotions.

Ōkuninushi's establishment of the land also depends on other beings. In one episode a tiny visitor, Sukunabikona, arrives from across the water. He can help make and order the country. Different powers cooperate in the work of organizing the world, and not all of them can be reduced to the genealogy of a single court. The *Kojiki* records divine names and local associations whose meanings may resist a modern listener's demand for one streamlined plot. Those complications matter. The land is not empty until a future imperial ancestor appears; it already holds claims, cults, and agents of its own.

From the high plain of heaven, Amaterasu and associated deities seek to extend authority over this earthly realm. Their emissaries do not immediately succeed. Some become absorbed into earthly connections; others are compromised or fail to return. Negotiation and force enter the story together. At last representatives reach Ōkuninushi and press for the transfer of rule. His son Kotoshironushi accepts the demand. Another son, Takeminakata, resists and is defeated in a contest of strength. Ōkuninushi agrees to yield the visible land, while requesting a grand dwelling and acknowledging a distinct realm of divine responsibility. The narrative is not a neutral record of a treaty between historical states. It is a mythic account of a transfer that can make later political authority appear both ordained and negotiated.

The moment is particularly revealing because the work allows an earthly ruler a history of his own before he yields. He was the man who helped a wounded hare, survived murder by his brothers, escaped Susanoo's ordeals, and established relationships with powers of the land. He is not a nameless obstacle removed from a vacant territory. The story of the transfer gains meaning precisely because something substantial is being transferred. It also reveals how an imperial narrative can preserve memories of other centers of sacred power while placing them within a hierarchy oriented toward the court.

The heavenly descent follows. Amaterasu's grandson Ninigi is sent down with sacred objects that later readers associate with imperial regalia. His arrival marks a transition from the conflicts of gods on high to the genealogical narratives of rulers on earth. The text describes the route, entourage, and setting with the density of a ritual record. Not every divine name needs to become a dramatic speaking character. The accumulation is part of what the composition is doing: attaching offices, lineages, powers, and places to the new order. The descending ancestor is meant to arrive in a land already made intelligible by the preceding stories.

Ninigi's earthly life includes a marriage story that explains human mortality. He is offered two daughters, one beautiful and associated with blossoms, the other associated with the lasting endurance of stone. He chooses the blossom-like woman and rejects her sister. A divine parent declares that the descendants of this union will be as transient as flowers rather than as enduring as rock. The explanation is not a philosophical proof of why humans die. It is a remembered choice rendered as an origin of fragility: the coming royal line will be illustrious, but it will not be immortal.

Another narrative follows a descendant, Hoori, who loses his brother's fishhook and travels beneath the sea in search of it. He meets a sea deity's daughter, marries, returns with gifts of tides, and eventually reasserts his place among his kin. The story has the movement of a folktale: a lost object, a journey into a different world, marriage, hidden power, and a difficult homecoming. Within the *Kojiki*, however, it also extends the genealogy that will connect heaven, sea, and earthly rulers. The same text that enumerates names and ceremonies can suddenly give the listener an adventure about a missing hook.

The *Kojiki*'s first book therefore does not stop with a cosmic creation and then mechanically list ancestors. It follows the formation of land through violence and birth, the emergence of rule among powers already present, the yielding of one order to another, and the introduction of mortality into the very family that claims heavenly descent. This is a narrative about legitimacy, but its figures are not featureless emblems of authority. A vulnerable hare remembers mercy. A powerful father tests and then recognizes a son-in-law. A ruler gives up a land he has helped establish. A descendant loses a fishhook and finds a world under the sea. These stories make political claims memorable by giving them the movement of lives.

What happens when the divine genealogy becomes a genealogy of people called emperors? The next book follows the legendary eastern expedition of a ruler later known as Jimmu, and then episodes of rulers, warriors, sisters, lovers, and disputed successions. The historical status of those early reigns is not established by the story's confidence in naming them. But the literary sequence matters: the descent from heaven is being turned into a story of how authority comes to occupy particular roads, mountains, and settlements. A myth of the cosmos is becoming a memory of a polity.

- Episode checkpoints: White Hare of Inaba; elder brothers' attacks; Susanoo's tests; escape and marriage; land-making; embassies and Takeminakata's resistance; transfer of rule; descent of Ninigi; blossoms and rocks; Hoori's missing fishhook.
