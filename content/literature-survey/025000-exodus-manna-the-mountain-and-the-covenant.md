# Exodus, Part Two: Manna, the Mountain, and the Covenant

Original narration of the received Book of Exodus 15:22–24, following the transition from escape to community formation. The chapter summaries and ethical tensions are anchored to the KJV passage, with Yale's Exodus course used for scholarly framing. Biblical law is presented as law within an ancient text, not as modern legal instruction.

The water has closed behind the escaping Israelites. The songs have been sung. Then they walk for days without finding water they can drink.

The Book of Exodus, which began with oppression in Egypt and followed a spectacular confrontation between Moses and Pharaoh, now tells of a different kind of danger. No king is holding the people in bondage. Instead they are thirsty. They are hungry. They have children and animals. The wilderness can make the language of deliverance seem distant, because a person's next drink is a more immediate question than the triumph just celebrated at the sea.

At Marah the water is bitter. The people complain to Moses. He cries out to God, is shown a piece of wood, and throws it into the water so that it becomes drinkable. The journey resumes, passing through an oasis, and then hunger begins to press. The people remember Egypt in a strangely selective way. They speak of the pots of meat they once knew, and say it would have been better to die there than to starve in the open country. The bitter labor and the decree against infants have not vanished from the story. Fear has made the old prison look like a kitchen full of food.

God promises provisions. In the evening birds cover the camp; in the morning a fine substance lies on the ground. The people ask one another what it is, a question associated in the story with the name manna. Moses tells them to gather enough for each person. They discover that they cannot store an unlimited reserve for themselves. Portions kept against the day's instruction spoil, while on the day before the Sabbath they gather twice as much and the surplus keeps. When some go out on the Sabbath to collect more, they find none. The gift becomes a lesson in rhythm, limit, trust, and the stubborn impulse to secure tomorrow by hoarding today.

The people complain again at Rephidim, where there is no water. Moses fears that a crowd driven by thirst may turn its anger against him. At divine direction he strikes a rock and water comes forth. The place is named with words that remember testing and contention. Exodus does not present the people as exemplary students who absorb each lesson after one demonstration. Their demands repeat; so does the necessity of feeding and leading them.

A military threat follows. Amalek attacks, and Joshua leads fighters against the attackers. Moses stands where the battle can be seen, holding up the staff associated with the signs in Egypt. When his hands remain raised, Israel gains ground; when they fall, the enemy gains. Moses grows tired. Aaron and Hur seat him on a stone and support his arms until the fighting ends. The picture is memorable because a leader who has faced Pharaoh is now unable to hold up his own hands without assistance. The victory is told through a strange interdependence of fighting below and support above.

Then an older man arrives with Moses's wife and sons. He is Jethro, Moses's father-in-law, a priest in Midian. Moses tells him of the deliverance, and Jethro responds with praise and a shared meal. The following day he watches Moses sit in judgment from morning until evening while people queue for decisions. Jethro sees that this cannot continue. Moses is wearing himself out and exhausting the people. He should teach the principles, Jethro says, but appoint capable persons to hear ordinary disputes, reserving the hardest cases for himself. Moses listens and changes his practice. The narrative gives a crucial administrative reform not to the central prophet but to a visitor with experience and concern for his family.

By the third month after the departure, the Israelites reach the region of Sinai. The landscape becomes a place of thunder, smoke, cloud, trumpet sound, and boundaries. Moses ascends the mountain and receives a message about the relationship being offered: the people, delivered from Egypt, are to become a treasured people and a holy community, bound to the God who freed them. They answer that they will do what has been spoken. Yet the encounter is so intense that the ordinary person may not simply walk up to the peak. The mountain is both a meeting place and a boundary around dangerous holiness.

The people hear commands that have become famous as the Ten Commandments. They begin with the identity of the liberating God and require exclusive worship. They prohibit making and worshiping images, misuse of the divine name, and violation of the Sabbath. They command honor toward parents and prohibit murder, adultery, theft, false witness, and coveting a neighbor's household and possessions. This is not simply an abstract list of universal moral principles delivered without setting. The commands are spoken to a people who have just been brought out of slavery, and they frame a covenant between a particular deity and a particular community.

The people are frightened by the sound and fire, and ask Moses to stand between them and the divine speech. That request establishes a recurring tension. They want to be part of a people addressed by God, but they cannot bear unlimited direct contact. Moses moves between the people and the mountain, a mediator whose closeness to the divine both gives him authority and sets him apart.

What follows is often skipped in dramatic retellings. Chapters twenty-one through twenty-three contain regulations about servants, injury, debts, restitution, property, festivals, courts, and worship. A person expecting another chase may find the laws tedious. But the story has made them central: after liberation, what will a society actually do when two neighbors dispute an animal, someone is injured through negligence, a vulnerable person is exploited, or the powerful try to bend a judgment in their favor?

Some instructions restrain violence and insist on fairness. The text warns against mistreating a stranger because the Israelites themselves knew what it meant to live as strangers in Egypt. It warns against oppressing widows and orphans. It tells judges not to favor rich or poor merely because of status. It requires restitution for theft and sets limits on vengeance. The people are being asked to remember the experience of being powerless as they become responsible for power over one another.

But these laws do not describe a society with modern legal equality. They regulate forms of slavery rather than abolish slavery; they treat male and female servants differently; they make distinctions among households and statuses that modern audiences may reject. Some penalties are severe, and some legal categories are far from what we would consider just. Taking the text seriously means saying both things: it contains commands to protect vulnerable people, and it belongs to an ancient world in which vulnerability and rights are distributed unequally. We cannot understand its history by pretending all of its rules are modern principles in disguise.

The law also has an agricultural calendar. Rest for laborers and animals, the seventh day, the seventh year, and the great recurring festivals bind communal time to worship and production. Time itself becomes a way of describing the desired order. The people who could not control their hours under Pharaoh are being taught to organize work and rest under another authority, with obligations they are to remember and teach.

At the close of this section God promises guidance toward the land, but the promise is not portrayed as instant possession. There are dangers ahead, peoples already living there, and stern commands about separation and worship. The narrative's historical and ethical consequences cannot be removed by extracting only a benign moral from its laws.

Chapter twenty-four gives the covenant a solemn form. Moses repeats the words and judgments; the people again consent. An altar is built, and twelve pillars represent the tribes. Sacrificial blood is used in a ritual sealing the covenant. Then Moses, Aaron, his sons Nadab and Abihu, and seventy elders ascend and share a remarkable moment of vision. They see the God of Israel, and beneath the divine presence the text describes a brilliant paving, like sapphire or clear sky. They eat and drink. The same book in which touching the mountain could be deadly now shows representatives of the people at a meal in the presence of the divine.

Moses continues farther up. A cloud covers the mountain, and for six days he waits. On the seventh day he is called into it; the appearance of the glory is like consuming fire to those below. Moses remains on the mountain forty days and nights. The people have heard the law, performed the ritual, and watched their mediator disappear into cloud. They now have to live with a promise they cannot keep checking by sight.

The movement from Egypt to Sinai is often called the Exodus as if leaving were the whole event. These chapters propose something more demanding. Freedom from the overseer is not automatically justice among neighbors. A starving crowd may remember bondage as security. A powerful leader may need advice about delegation. A society that receives laws may still violate them. And a people gathered around a holy mountain must decide what to do while the leader whose word they trust is absent from view.

The book is preparing its next crisis. The tablets are being given on the mountain. Below, frightened people are already wondering whether Moses will ever return.
