# Gunditjmara: Budj Bim and the Living Country

A narrated encounter with **publicly shared** Gunditjmara creation accounts, the
Budj Bim cultural landscape, and the people who continue to care for it. This is
original historical and literary narration **about** a living oral tradition,
not an authorized performance, transcription, or newly composed Gunditjmara
songline. Story ownership remains with Gunditjmara knowledge holders. Read the
source and cultural-attribution companion after the spoken text.

There is a place in south-western Australia where a mountain, a stretch of broken stone, a lake, and the sea belong to the same story.

Stand, in imagination, beside Tae Rak, the lake also called Lake Condah. Water moves among reeds and dark volcanic rocks. Some of the stone is piled into walls; some has been arranged into narrow passages and holding ponds. These are not random remnants of a ruined wall. The openings matter. The height of a barrier matters. So does the direction of the water, and the season in which that water rises. Across this country, people learned to guide the movement of kooyang, the short-finned eel, into places where the animals could be kept alive and harvested when needed.

The landscape is known as Budj Bim. It is part of the Country of the Gunditjmara people, whose connections to it have endured into the present. Far from the picture of a wilderness waiting to be inhabited, this is a place made and maintained by people over thousands of years. It is also a place whose stones are part of the body of an Ancestral Being.

The two statements do not cancel one another. To understand this country, we need to hear something of the creation account that its Traditional Owners have chosen to share publicly, and then follow that account outward into the water, the work, the violence of colonisation, and the return of water to the lake. The result will not be the recitation of a sacred song, or a claim to know every story held in Gunditjmara Country. It will be an encounter with a story whose keepers are still here.

## When the law men came

In a publicly shared account credited to Gunditj Mirring Traditional Owners Aboriginal Corporation, the creation of this landscape begins with four law men. These are not ordinary travellers on an ordinary day. They belong to the ancestral time in which Country, living beings, and responsibilities came into relation. The law men took the form of mara, men, and brought law and powers and ceremonial responsibilities with them. They moved in different directions.

One remained.

He lay down. His immense body became part of the land, taking form in the volcanic peaks known as Tappoc, or Mount Napier, and Budj Bim, the mountain long called Mount Eccles in colonial maps. At Budj Bim, the Ancestral Being was revealed in the erupting landscape.

That is the outline made public by the Gunditj Mirring knowledge holders in the Australian Broadcasting Corporation's Deep Time project. It is important to identify whose account this is. The story has not reached us as a chapter written by an otherwise unknown ancient author. Its public presentation has a present-day source and present-day custodians. Nor does the published outline tell us everything the Gunditjmara know. It does not supply the movements of each of the other law men in detail, the words of a song, or a sequence of speeches spoken at the mountain. Those gaps are not invitations for someone else to fill them with fiction and call the result tradition.

But even the outline changes how we look at the country. The mountain is not simply a landmark against which a story happens. It is part of what has happened. The story is present in its shape.

Another account, published in material prepared for the World Heritage nomination, explains the shape in more detail. The rounded hill is understood as the forehead of the Ancestral Being. The scattered scoria cones are his teeth. In the Dhauwurd wurrung language, the name Budj Bim is glossed as high head; tung att refers to the teeth. A stone underfoot, the slope of a volcanic hill, and the course of a flow are not merely scenery for a tale about a giant. They are among the forms through which the Being is known.

This understanding of landscape is not a quaint prehistoric explanation to be replaced by a geological one. The Gunditjmara account tells of identity, ancestry, law, and relationships with Country. A geologist asks when magma reached the surface and how lava diverted a creek. Both approaches pay close attention to the same place, but they do not ask exactly the same question.

## The mountain and the flow

Consider what a volcanic eruption actually leaves behind. Molten rock passes over older ground. It cools into resistant stone. Some areas stand higher, some remain hollow, and waterways that once followed one route find themselves obstructed. Water gathers where it could once escape. A long flow can connect places that look separate on an ordinary map: a mountain inland, a wetland, another watercourse, and a coast.

Budj Bim was one of the volcanoes of south-eastern Australia's Newer Volcanic Province. Researchers using argon dating have estimated an eruption age of about thirty-seven thousand years, with several thousand years of uncertainty in that estimate. Nearby Tower Hill was dated to a similar period. A stone tool found beneath deposits associated with Tower Hill provides a separate, important clue to human presence in this region around the time of those eruptions. It is not a tool excavated from underneath Budj Bim itself.

A volcano can be dated geologically, but a surviving telling does not carry a timestamp. We cannot prove that any present wording of a creation account was repeated without alteration for thirty-seven thousand years. Scholars have considered whether elements of Gunditjmara oral tradition preserve memories of the volcanic events, and that possibility deserves serious attention. But treating the idea as a demonstrated, continuous transcript would make the evidence say more than it can.

For the Gunditjmara, this is deeper than a hypothesis about remembered geology. The community's account speaks of long presence and of a Being who revealed himself in Country. The story belongs to a continuing set of relationships. Its survival does not depend on producing a perfect match between a modern scientific date and each sentence of a modern published version.

The eruption changed the possibilities of life in the surrounding country. The Tyrendarra lava flow spread away from Budj Bim and affected drainage across the landscape, forming wetlands and helping to shape Tae Rak. A barrier of stone could hold water. A depression could become a pond. A creek could rise and fall with the seasons, but the stone it crossed would remain. Over immense stretches of time, people worked with these conditions rather than merely observing them.

At first glance, water seems to be the opposite of lava: one moving and cool, the other moving and hot. At Budj Bim, the action of the earlier flow helped establish the passages through which the later water would travel. The mountain, the lake, and the eel are parts of one place, not isolated items on a historical timeline.

## Kooyang and the problem of moving water

To see the achievement of the Gunditjmara aquaculture system, imagine a practical problem rather than a monument. Water does not arrive at the same height every day. Wetlands swell in the rains and contract as conditions dry. Fish and eels move with these changes. If all a community can do is wait at the edge of the water and hope to catch whatever passes, abundance comes and goes on the water's terms.

But suppose the water can be guided. A narrow channel brings it through a stretch of stone. A weir checks its flow. A basin holds some of the water back when levels elsewhere fall. An eel can be directed into a holding pond and remain there alive. When a household or a gathering needs food, the animal is available. This is not the same as catching an eel once and hoping it keeps. It is management of an aquatic environment over time.

The Gunditjmara built and modified channels, stone weirs, dams, and ponds along the Budj Bim lava flows. The system's underlying idea is visible in the shape of the works: control the movement of water, and you can influence the movement and storage of kooyang. Some passages are narrow enough for trapping; other structures help retain wet-season water. This is not one enormous machine built on a single day. It is a network adapted, repaired, and developed over generations.

The kooyang themselves have a life beyond these wetlands. Short-finned eels migrate between freshwater country and the ocean. A season of movement through rivers and creeks becomes a season in which people can bring the animals into managed waters. A healthy system depends on knowing how the wetlands change, how eels behave, and what the next season is likely to require.

The traps, in turn, require another material. The Gunditjmara made woven eel baskets, known as gnarraban, from plant fibres gathered on Country. A basket fitted to a stone passage could catch animals brought by the current. The reeds and grasses of the wetlands were not incidental plants around a technological installation; they were part of the installation's continuing use. Stone, vegetation, water, and animal movement had to be understood together.

Accounts of Gunditjmara life describe fish being smoked for storage and exchange, and the aquaculture networks supporting substantial communities. The point of storage is not simply that people could eat on a difficult day. It changes what a settlement can do. It permits work and trade to continue when the immediate catch is poor. It supports gatherings. It makes long-term relationships with neighbouring communities possible on a different scale.

Archaeological study places the documented aquaculture system at least six thousand six hundred years in the past. That is a minimum supported by the evidence, not a claim that everything began on a known date, nor that the whole landscape was designed at once. The fact that a stone structure survives tells us something solid. It rarely tells us the name of the person who laid its first stones, the conversation by which a new channel was agreed, or the precise sequence of improvements across thousands of seasons.

It is tempting to turn this achievement into a story of one inspired ancient engineer. That would actually make the account smaller. What the remains demonstrate is more demanding: knowledge carried forward, adjusted and used by communities whose children and grandchildren inherited both a working environment and responsibilities toward it.

## Houses in Stone Country

There were homes here as well as waterworks. Across parts of the landscape, archaeologists and Gunditjmara knowledge holders identify remnants of circular stone-walled dwellings, sometimes in groups. The dark basalt could form the lower walls of a shelter, with other materials used above it. The stone was available because of the flow. The dwellings were placed in a landscape where food and water could be secured through practiced management.

An ABC account shared with Gunditj Mirring describes huts arranged with their entrances away from cold winter winds. This is a small but revealing detail. It brings the scale of the story back from a volcano and millennia to the repeated business of keeping warm and dry. There is no reason to imagine ancient life here as permanently exposed to the weather. People built for the climate they knew.

A shelter and an eel pond are connected kinds of knowledge. One is a decision about stone, wind and weather. The other is a decision about stone, current and animal movement. Both depend on watching the same Country closely. Neither makes sense if Aboriginal history is reduced to people moving without settled places or without deliberate management of resources.

The most convincing evidence of occupation is not a picturesque ruin alone. It is the arrangement of works across a lived landscape: stone walls, water channels, holding ponds, places of dwelling, places where plant fibres could be gathered, and routes to other communities. The single remarkable structure becomes more intelligible as part of a network of ordinary needs and practiced solutions.

Gunditjmara Elders and community organisations have worked to keep and renew this knowledge, including the arts of managing kooyang and weaving baskets. When a practice continues into the present, it changes what we mean by an archaeological site. Its significance is not exhausted by how old a particular wall may be. The account turns on the relationship between people and place, including the people who remain able to speak for it.

## Toward Sea Country

Follow the line of the lava flow and the waterways south. The wider Gunditjmara Country includes more than the lands around the mountain. It includes forest, rivers and forested waterways, Stone Country, and Sea Country. The coastal land and waters connect the stories of the inland landscape with places beyond the shore.

One of those places is Deen Maar, an island now also called Lady Julia Percy Island. It has a continuing importance in Gunditjmara accounts of spiritual life. In the Gunditjmara-authored Nyamat Mirring Plan, Tyson Lovett Murray speaks about its importance as a place associated with ancestral spirits and recalls a story of a giant whose body became the island after being carried to sea. We should not assume that every account involving a giant is automatically another chapter of the same story about the law man at Budj Bim. The publicly shared accounts establish a connected cultural landscape, but they do not give an outsider authority to splice their figures and episodes into a new master plot.

What we can say is significant enough. The stories do not stop at the edge of a government-protected park. Sea Country is not an afterthought to the inland world. In the present day, Gunditjmara Traditional Owners are articulating how cultural responsibilities, fishing, coastal habitats, ancestral places, and decisions about what happens offshore belong together.

The point is not that every natural feature encodes a sentence awaiting our decipherment. It is that places can carry histories, obligations, and meanings that people continue to interpret, maintain, and teach. A story about an ancestor becoming part of Country can also be a story about where people stand and what they must care for.

## The stones as refuge

The history of this landscape did not end when Europeans arrived on the Australian coast. For the Gunditjmara, colonisation brought violence and attempts to take control of their Country. From the early nineteenth century, sealers and whalers visited the coast. In 1834, Edward Henty established a European settler presence at Portland. The movement of livestock and colonists across Gunditjmara land led to conflict over land, water, lives, and livelihoods.

During the 1840s, violent resistance in the region came to be known as the Eumeralla Wars. The stony land that colonists often found difficult to cross was familiar ground to Gunditjmara defenders. The lava that had created ponds and building stone also made possible routes of movement and concealment that outsiders did not readily understand. The ancestral landscape became part of a struggle over who would live on and govern it.

It would be a distortion to tell that struggle only as the romantic defence of a heroic people against a single villain. There were killings, dispossession, the disruption of families and livelihoods, and long campaigns of survival and political work. These are not a separate story pasted onto a prehistoric landscape. They are part of what happened to the people whose cultural knowledge had sustained life there.

The colonial mission system imposed new constraints. The Gunditjmara did not simply accept removal to wherever administrators wished to place them. After resistance to relocation away from their Country, a mission was established at Lake Condah in 1867. The location was near Tae Rak and within sight of Budj Bim. It was a place where people could remain nearer their Country, but it operated within a colonial system of surveillance and restriction. Later legislation and administrative decisions split families and disrupted life at the mission. The mission formally closed in 1918, but Gunditjmara people continued to live at the site and maintain connections to it.

Meanwhile, the water itself was threatened. European drainage schemes began altering the wetlands in the nineteenth century. After major flooding in 1946, a channel was built to drain Tae Rak. Gunditjmara Elders objected, but the drainage proceeded. By the 1950s, the lake had lost much of its former capacity to hold water. The effect was not just a different view across the lake. Channels, weirs, and holding ponds depended on a pattern of inundation. Change that pattern, and many ancient works can no longer function as they once did.

The history of this place therefore contains more than one sort of damage. There was the taking and restriction of land. There was pressure on people to abandon cultural practices. And there were physical changes to the water system that made those practices harder to carry on. Yet cultural knowledge persisted. The fact that a channel had ceased to carry its former flow did not mean everyone had forgotten what it was for.

## When water returned to Tae Rak

The return of water was not an accident of weather. It was the result of sustained Gunditjmara advocacy, land-rights work, research, and cooperation directed toward restoring the lake and its cultural landscape.

In 1987, former Lake Condah Mission lands were returned to Gunditjmara people. In 2007, the Federal Court of Australia recognised native title rights in part of Gunditjmara Country. Other important steps followed. In 2010, a cultural weir was completed to help restore water levels at Tae Rak, reconnecting the lake with parts of the wetland system that drainage had impaired.

Think again about the narrow stone passage with which we began. For the channel to do its work, water must reach it at the right level and move through it. A dry channel may display past engineering; a functioning wetland allows the knowledge embodied in that engineering to become practical again. Restoration thus changes the meaning of preservation. It is not only protecting an old wall from damage. It is restoring, as far as possible, the conditions of a living system.

In July 2019, the Budj Bim Cultural Landscape was inscribed on the UNESCO World Heritage List for its Aboriginal cultural significance. International recognition mattered. But the listing did not discover the place's importance, and it did not create Gunditjmara responsibility for Country. The people whose knowledge and works made the landscape had been explaining, defending, and caring for it long before that decision.

Today the landscape is owned or managed by Gunditjmara Traditional Owners through a network of cultural and environmental responsibilities. Their organisations, rangers and knowledge holders are not merely witnesses describing a vanished world for visitors. They are participants in decisions about water, land use, heritage, access, and the future of the place. The story continues in that practical, political sense as well as in cultural transmission.

## What kind of story is this?

In a course on epic and narrative traditions, it is easy to expect every entry to have the structure of a book. We meet a named hero; a conflict begins; the hero journeys, fights, returns, or dies. A text survives. Centuries later, we read it aloud.

Budj Bim asks us to expand that expectation. The openly shared creation account is brief beside a twelve-book epic. Its characters are ancestral law men, and its central transformation takes place in the shape of Country. Yet there is also a longer human account: generations building and modifying waterworks; families living beside the wetlands; violence endured under colonisation; the fight for rights and the effort to restore water. These histories are connected, but they are not interchangeable chapters of an ancient manuscript.

That is why we should not casually call this lecture a Gunditjmara songline. Songs and routes of ancestral knowledge belong to their custodians and may require particular authority to share. The fact that a public account names an Ancestral Being does not license an outsider to invent a song, stage a ceremony, or connect separated traditions as if they were scenes of one lost epic. What we can do is listen carefully to what Gunditjmara knowledge holders have chosen to make public, give them credit, and understand the country to which their accounts refer.

One more question is worth carrying away. What has endured here? Not a static museum display, nor a story whose truth consists solely in matching a geological clock. The Gunditjmara relationship with Country is visible in oral knowledge and in the built world, in the recovery of water and in the right to make decisions about the land. The stone channels can be measured. The date of an eruption can be estimated. A court can recognise a legal claim. Those are different forms of knowledge and different kinds of action. None alone contains the whole life of the place.

Return, finally, to the water among the dark stones. The form of the land owes much to an eruption in deep geological time. The managed channels testify to thousands of years of accumulated skill. Their disruption records a history of dispossession. Their restoration records deliberate work by descendants of the people who made them. And the mountain beyond remains Budj Bim, the Ancestral Being revealed in Country, named and cared for by Gunditjmara people.

The landscape is not the end of the story. It is where the story continues.

**What this lecture is:** An original educational essay, not an adaptation of
an identifiable complete song cycle. The distinct published fragments of
Gunditjmara creation accounts are reported with attribution, not merged into
invented dialogue, lyrics, routes, or rituals. Landscape, engineering, and
post-contact history are documented from additional sources. The explanatory
connections between those sources are this lecture's interpretations.

**Story custodians:** Gunditj Mirring Traditional Owners Aboriginal
Corporation and Gunditjmara knowledge holders. ABC *Deep Time* credits its
Budj Bim material to the corporation and identifies that narrative as
Indigenous Cultural and Intellectual Property. Its publicly accessible
presentation is **not** a blanket license to reproduce the original wording,
recordings or ceremonies, especially for commercial use. This lecture does not
reprint or recite its text, nor claim cultural authorization or a local
knowledge-holder's voice. The term *songlines* is therefore used only as a
point of discussion, not as a title to a purported newly produced songline.
