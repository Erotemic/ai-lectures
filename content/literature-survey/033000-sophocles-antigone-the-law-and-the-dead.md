# Sophocles, Antigone: The Law and the Dead

A full original scene-by-scene narration of Sophocles' *Antigone*, following the Greek play (lines 1–1353) as transmitted in Richard C. Jebb's public-domain 1891 translation. The narrative is set after the deaths of Oedipus's sons, but the play was probably composed earlier than *Oedipus Tyrannus* and is not the second act of a Sophoclean trilogy.

A city has survived a battle. Its walls still stand. The attacking army has been driven away, and the bodies of the defenders can be carried to their graves. But one corpse has been left outside the gates by order of the new king. Anyone who covers it with earth will be put to death.

The dead man is Polynices, son of Oedipus. He came against his own city with an army and fought his brother Eteocles, who was defending Thebes. The brothers killed each other. Their uncle Creon now rules a city whose victory and grief are tangled together. He orders that Eteocles be buried with honor. Polynices, whom he calls a traitor, must remain exposed to animals and weather. The punishment is intended to continue beyond death: a public declaration that the enemy of the city does not deserve the care that normally separates the dead from the living.

Two young women stand before this decision. Antigone and Ismene are sisters to both dead men. Sophocles' *Antigone* opens not in the council chamber but in their private conversation. It asks what happens when an official command forbids someone to perform a duty that appears older than official commands themselves. Yet it is not merely a story in which one character is good and everyone else knows the answer is bad. It is about a family already damaged by catastrophe, a ruler afraid that weakness will destroy a newly rescued city, and people who can see fragments of the problem while refusing to hear one another.

Antigone tells Ismene what Creon has ordered. She has decided to bury Polynices. She asks her sister to help lift and cover the body. Ismene is horrified. Their father Oedipus suffered public disgrace and ruin; their mother died; both brothers have just killed each other. Now Antigone proposes to challenge the ruler and add another death to the family. Ismene believes the sisters have no power to fight the men who govern the city. She wants to survive. She offers counsel that sounds fearful but not malicious: the law is dangerous, and they have already lost enough.

Antigone rejects the argument. The fact that a decree comes from a king does not make it right in the eyes of the gods. The dead are owed rites, and Polynices remains her brother whatever he did against Thebes. She will carry out the burial even if no one joins her. Ismene tries to dissuade her, then offers to keep her intention secret. Antigone refuses even that comfort. She would rather the deed were known. The scene makes their difference visible before any official can impose a penalty: one sister measures duty by the dead, the other by the danger to the living family that remains.

The chorus enters celebrating the city's deliverance. The attacking champions were driven back. Eteocles and Polynices fell together. Thebes has survived. The old men of the chorus have every reason to be relieved, yet their songs now belong to a community whose triumph will not remain simple. A king's attempt to secure the meaning of victory is about to become another cause of civic disaster.

Creon appears before the elders and announces his principles of rule. The safety of the city must come first. A ruler who places private friendship above the common good, he argues, cannot be trusted. An enemy of the city must not receive the same honors as its defender. There is force in the speech. Thebes has just endured war, and the possibility that loyalty to a powerful family might outweigh loyalty to the city is a real political fear. But Creon has allowed a necessary distinction between civic friend and enemy to become a claim that he can determine the obligations owed to the dead.

A guard arrives with bad news. Someone has sprinkled dust over Polynices's body and performed the minimal signs of burial. The guard is frightened less by the corpse than by the king's temper. He expects to be accused of involvement, punished for negligence, or made the substitute for a culprit no one has seen. He speaks defensively, tries to explain exactly what the watchmen discovered, and struggles to escape a conversation in which every answer may be dangerous.

Creon reacts with anger. Someone must have been bribed, he says; money corrupts the city and makes men betray their duties. He threatens the guards if they fail to identify the offender. His certainty is revealing. He can imagine self-interest, factional plotting, and the purchase of disobedience. He has trouble imagining that someone might act for reasons that neither enrich her nor advance a political conspiracy.

The chorus sings of humanity's astonishing power. People sail stormy seas, farm the earth, tame animals, devise speech and civic law, and learn to resist countless threats. Yet death remains beyond their mastery. This famous song can sound like a hymn to human progress, but in the play's setting its praise is unstable. The same inventiveness that makes city life possible can be used in ways that violate what a city most needs to honor. Human beings can make laws. They cannot make themselves immortal or abolish the obligations that death creates.

The guard returns, and this time he has a prisoner: Antigone. After the first dust covering had been removed, the watchmen hid and waited. A storm of dust swept across the plain. When it cleared, they saw the young woman at the corpse, mourning and performing the burial rites again. She did not deny what she had done. The guards seized her and brought her to the palace.

Creon demands an admission. Antigone gives it. Did she know the decree? Yes. Why disobey? Because the order of a mortal ruler cannot annul the laws that the gods have established for the dead. She does not claim ignorance, mistake, or a private exemption. She claims that the decree was wrong. Creon hears in this not only defiance but public humiliation. If a young woman can overrule his command, what is left of his authority? The dispute becomes inseparable from his fear of being made to appear weak.

Ismene is brought in. Now that Antigone has been caught, she offers to share the blame and die with her sister. Antigone refuses. Ismene would not help when the act could still be done; she cannot now take half the credit or half the responsibility. Their first disagreement returns in a more painful form. One sister has done the deed and will not surrender its ownership. The other has discovered that the survival she chose may be a life without the person she most wanted to keep.

The danger extends into Creon's household. Antigone is betrothed to his son Haemon. Creon refuses to let that connection alter his sentence. He dismisses the young woman's value as a wife and suggests that his son can marry someone else. The language reveals how quickly his defense of public principle has become an assertion of household command. A choice about the burial of a dead enemy will now determine the life of his own family.

Haemon enters. At first he speaks respectfully to his father. He does not begin with an ultimatum. He says that a son should value his father's judgment. But he also reports what people in the city say when the king cannot hear them. They pity Antigone, who is to die for honoring her brother. They do not see her as a mercenary agitator. Haemon asks Creon to listen to opinions he has not allowed into his council. A flexible tree, he suggests, survives a flood better than one that will not bend. A good sailor loosens a sail when the wind threatens to break the ship.

Creon takes the appeal as insubordination. Must a father accept lessons from a young man? Must a ruler obey the crowd? Haemon answers that a city does not belong to one person alone. The argument becomes a contest over who is entitled to speak. Haemon is not saying that popular opinion is always right. He is asking whether a leader can remain wise if he treats disagreement as evidence of treason. Creon hears a son choosing a woman over his father. Haemon sees his father refusing the chance to govern with judgment.

The quarrel ends badly. Creon has briefly threatened to kill Antigone before Haemon's eyes; the son leaves in fury. Creon decides not to execute Ismene. Instead he will shut Antigone alive in a rocky chamber beyond the city, give her a little food, and leave her to the gods below. He presents this arrangement as a way to avoid direct responsibility for her death. The distinction is empty. The tomb has become a prison, and a living woman is to be sent to the place intended for the dead.

The chorus sings of the dangerous strength of love, which can set kin against kin and overthrow reason. Then Antigone approaches her imprisonment. She is no longer only the unyielding speaker who told Creon that she was right. She grieves for the marriage she will never have, the children she will never bear, and the life through which a young woman might ordinarily become part of a household. She compares herself to Niobe, the legendary woman turned to stone in sorrow. The elders do not entirely endorse her comparisons. Their dialogue shows how a chorus can pity a character and still argue with her.

Antigone asks what principle she has violated. She has performed an ancient duty for a dead brother. Why should that make her accursed? But her speech also holds pain and contradiction. At one moment she declares herself sustained by divine justice; at another she wonders why the gods seem not to defend her. The tragedy does not give her the serenity of someone who knows the outcome. She goes toward darkness believing her action was necessary and fearing the terrible cost she must pay for it.

A new visitor appears: Teiresias, the blind prophet, led by a boy. He has tried to read sacrificial signs and found them corrupted. The birds are fighting, and the offerings will not burn properly. The city has been polluted by the exposed corpse, whose flesh is carried by birds and dogs to altars and hearths. Teiresias speaks of the cost of Creon's determination: the dead man has been denied his place below, while a living woman has been shut among the dead.

Creon answers him as he answered the guard. Perhaps the prophet has been bought. Perhaps money is behind the warning. The habit of explaining all resistance as bribery has now reached a sacred adviser whose words should disturb any ruler, even one who questions them. Teiresias responds with a terrible prediction. Creon will pay a death from his own household for the deaths and dishonors he has caused. A line between public decree and private consequence, which the king thought could be maintained by force, is about to vanish.

After the prophet departs, the chorus urges Creon to yield. Only now does he bend. He decides to bury Polynices and release Antigone. The change is real, but delay has consequences. He first goes to honor the exposed body and give it proper rites; then he hurries to the sealed chamber. The play arranges the sequence so that the king's last attempt at repair cannot outrun the damage his earlier certainty has done.

A messenger brings the news. Antigone has hanged herself inside the chamber. Haemon was there, grieving beside her. When Creon approached, his son turned upon him in rage. Haemon tried to strike his father, failed, and then killed himself beside Antigone's body. This is not a father deliberately murdering his son with a weapon. It is a son dying by his own hand after the father's command made every future he wanted impossible. Creon's responsibility is devastating precisely because the tragedy does not simplify the sequence of acts.

Creon's wife Eurydice hears the messenger. She asks for the truth and listens. Then she goes silently inside. The chorus fears what such silence may mean. Soon another messenger emerges to say that she, too, has killed herself, cursing Creon for the deaths of her sons. When Creon returns carrying Haemon's body, the new grief is waiting for him. He has not saved the city by preserving his authority. He has brought ruin into the household for whose continuity he once spoke so confidently.

At last Creon admits the error. He begs to be led away. He cannot repair the deaths by confessing, and the gods do not offer a sudden miraculous rescue. Antigone is dead. Haemon is dead. Eurydice is dead. Polynices has received the burial the king tried to prevent. Creon, who insisted that the state must not bend for one family, remains alive to carry the destruction of his own.

The chorus closes with an insistence upon wisdom and reverence. Great words spoken in pride bring great blows, and learning may come only after suffering. But the play has made this lesson more difficult than an easy opposition between a wicked tyrant and an innocent rebel. Creon's opening concern for the city's survival is understandable. Ismene's fear for her remaining family is understandable. Haemon's plea for listening is reasonable and tactful until his anger overtakes him. Antigone's claim for her brother is powerful, but she does not leave the stage untouched by grief and doubt. The tragedy is made from the ways that these partial truths fail to find a common life.

One final historical distinction is worth keeping in mind. In the legendary life of Oedipus's family, Antigone's defiance comes after her father's ruin and the brothers' deaths. But Sophocles probably wrote and staged *Antigone* before his *Oedipus Tyrannus*. These are separate plays composed for different occasions, not sequential volumes of a single novel that the author drafted in story order. Knowing the family background helps a listener understand the stakes; pretending the plays are chapters in one planned trilogy does not.

The final image is not of a battle, but of a ruler with his son's body, looking toward the house where his wife lies dead. The decree is no longer the urgent problem. The king can revoke it, and he has tried to do so. The problem is that some actions cannot be undone at the moment their author becomes willing to change. The dead do not come back because a law is corrected. Sophocles has shown us a person who discovers wisdom only after he has destroyed the people who could have lived with him under wiser rule.

## Textual basis and source notes (not spoken)

- Sophocles, *Antigone*, Greek lines 1–1353; Richard C. Jebb public-domain English prose translation (1891), accessible through Perseus and Harvard's Center for Hellenic Studies revised/Jebb-derived reading. Retelling follows the received play, not a harmonized mythological cycle.
- Lines 1–99: Antigone and Ismene; 100–331: victory song, Creon's decree, burial report; 332–581: chorus on human skill, arrest and sisterly confrontation; 582–780: Haemon's appeal; 781–943: love, Antigone's lament and sentencing; 944–1114: choral song and Teiresias's warning; 1115–1353: reversal, deaths reported, Eurydice and Creon's ruin.
- Polynices's first burial is a light dust covering; guards remove it and then catch Antigone returning. Creon first buries Polynices and then goes to release Antigone, which is too late. Haemon attempts to strike Creon but dies by his own hand; Eurydice also kills herself.
- The play's literary chronology and the legendary family's internal chronology differ. The *Oresteia* is a separate Aeschylean trilogy; Sophocles' three surviving Theban plays are not a single production.
