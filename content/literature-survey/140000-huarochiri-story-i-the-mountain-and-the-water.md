# The Huarochirí Manuscript — Story I: The Mountain, the Poor Man, and the Water

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

There is a woman in the highlands, alone beneath a lúcuma tree. She is a powerful being, one of those whom the people of Huarochirí call huacas, and she has refused every suitor. A fruit falls near her. She eats it. Later she discovers that she is pregnant, although no man has approached her. She does not know that Cuniraya, another huaca, put his seed into the fruit. A year after the child is born, she summons the powerful men and beings of the region and sets her baby before them. Surely, she thinks, the child's father must be one of these splendid visitors. The child crawls across the gathering, past the finely dressed figures, to a person sitting apart in rags. He climbs onto the poor man's lap.

This scene comes from the Huarochirí Manuscript, an extraordinary collection written in Quechua in the central Andes around the beginning of the seventeenth century. The tales were already old when they were written down, but the document itself belongs to an age of Spanish colonial rule. It is not the adventures of one hero. It is a landscape speaking through stories: stones that were once people, a stream whose route was settled by animals, a mountain born from five eggs, and communities that remember why they live where they do. We will follow several connected tales in a continuous telling. We will not pretend that the manuscript arranges them as a modern novel, or that every community tells them in the same way.

The woman under the tree is Cahuillaca. The ragged figure is Cuniraya Viracocha, a being who can create terraces and irrigation channels by his command but often travels looking like someone with nothing. When the child identifies him, Cahuillaca is horrified. She takes her son and runs toward the sea. Cuniraya throws off his disguise, puts on radiant gold, and calls after her. Now, he is certain, she will admire him. But she does not turn around. She reaches the Pacific coast near Pachacamac, and the story says that she and her child become stones in the sea. Cuniraya's power has not won her willingness. The narrative allows him immense creative force and cunning, but her refusal remains final. We should not mistake the secret impregnation for a consensual courtship merely because the perpetrator is a huaca.

As Cuniraya pursues her, he asks animals whether they have seen the fleeing woman. Their answers shape their futures. A condor tells him she is near, and he gives it power over dead animals in the highlands and a place in ceremonial regard. A skunk tells him he will never catch her; he condemns it to an odorous, nocturnal existence. The puma offers hope and is promised strength and a place in ritual. The fox says that she has gone too far and receives contempt. The falcon gives encouraging news and is honored; other birds are cursed. It is a surprisingly sharp account of how a creature's qualities might come from a single encounter long ago. But the animals are also drawn into Cuniraya's impatience. They did not make Cahuillaca run. Their destinies depend on whether they tell him what he wants to hear.

These are not necessarily the first events in the sacred past. The manuscript itself repeatedly admits that it does not know exactly how one age fits beside another. In a still earlier time, there were beings called Yana Ñamca and Tuta Ñamca. Then came a terrible power, Huallallo Carhuincho, who overcame them. The people in his age lived under strange conditions. Food grew with astonishing speed. When people died, they could return on the fifth day. But this plenty had its own horror: Huallallo allowed parents two children and demanded one to devour. A world can be fertile, the story suggests, and still be ruled by terror.

One day a llama refuses to eat. Its owner has taken it to fine pasture, but the animal is distressed. The man becomes angry, throws a stripped maize cob, and scolds it. Then the llama speaks. The sea is about to rise; in five days the waters will cover the land. There is still time to reach the high mountain of Huillcacoto with provisions. The frightened man obeys. When he reaches the summit, every kind of animal is already gathered there. The ocean rises until almost nothing remains above the surface. Water reaches the tail of a fox, blackening its tip. After five days the sea withdraws. Almost all humankind is gone, and the survivor begins human life again. The manuscript's colonial narrator offers a Christian comparison to the Flood, but the story as told is rooted in its own mountain, its own speaking llama, and its own explanation for the fox's tail.

Another remembrance says the sun once vanished for five days. Darkness upset the order of ordinary things. Grinding stones and mortars turned against the people who used them, and animals pursued their former keepers. The text that preserves this story again draws a Christian parallel, this time to the darkness at Christ's death. We should hear two layers without collapsing them: a remembered time when the world nearly unmade itself, and a colonial writer trying to place that memory within a different religious chronology.

Now we come to a man with almost nothing. His name is Huatiacuri, and people mock him for living on potatoes cooked in the earth. His poverty matters because a mighty man nearby has become very ill. Tamtañamca has a splendid house, rare animals, and people who treat him as a great source of knowledge and power. He claims to understand what animates the world. Yet no healer can discover the cause of his disease. His visitors come and go. His prestige cannot repair his body.

On a mountain path Huatiacuri falls asleep, or at least lies still enough to hear two foxes meet and exchange news. One speaks of the wealthy man's sickness. The cause, it says, lies hidden in his own household: a sexual transgression involving his wife, serpents concealed near the roof, and a two-headed toad beneath the grinding stone. The foxes are not consulting a scholar or a king. They simply know something the supposed masters of wisdom do not. Huatiacuri listens, remembers, and goes to the mansion.

At first the household laughs at a man dressed so poorly. But Tamtañamca has grown desperate. Huatiacuri says he can heal him, on the condition that the lord give him his younger daughter in marriage. The offer is accepted. Huatiacuri names what has been concealed. The costly house must be dismantled to reach the serpents; the toad beneath the grinding stone must be exposed. The story says the wife admits the act described by the fox, and after the creatures are removed the lord recovers. The cure has destroyed the illusion that wealth and authority are the same as knowledge. Huatiacuri also tells the healed man to honor a power not yet fully born: his father, Pariacaca.

There is another difficulty. Tamtañamca's elder daughter is married to a wealthy man who cannot endure the thought that his new brother-in-law is a potato-eating pauper. The rich husband proposes one contest after another to humiliate him: drinking and dancing, fine clothing, feats of building and display, the kinds of accomplishments by which a house of prestige proves that it is superior. Huatiacuri goes to Pariacaca for counsel. What the rich man buys or commands, the poor man secures through his father's supernatural assistance. In one contest, he lays himself out like a dead guanaco so that foxes approach with drink and musical instruments; he frightens them away and takes what they have dropped. Other ordeals also turn the wealthy man's certainty against him. Ultimately the brother-in-law's defeat becomes a transformation into a deer, and the story associates his humiliation with the animal's flight. This is not an uncomplicated triumph of a virtuous underdog. Huatiacuri wins help from a terrifying father, and the punishment for his rival exceeds anything a modern reader might call fair.

Pariacaca's birth has been waiting in the mountains while these human contests unfold. On Condorcoto there are five eggs. From them emerge five falcons, and from the falcons emerge humanlike powers. At some moments the manuscript speaks of the five as brothers; at others it leaves unclear whether the others are Pariacaca's brothers or children. The important fact for the tale is that this power is multiple. A mountain, a lineage, and a collection of sacred presences are bound into one name. Huatiacuri knew of the eggs before nearly anyone else, when the rich people of the old world had not recognized what was coming.

Pariacaca begins moving through the land. He too can appear as someone poor and badly clothed. At a village feast he sits neglected while the others drink. No one serves him until a woman takes pity and offers maize beer. He warns her to leave with her family in five days and not to reveal his identity. When the days pass, the gathered people keep feasting, unaware of the disaster above them. Pariacaca climbs a hill and becomes a storm. Red and yellow hail and rushing rain sweep through the settlement. Torrents gouge the valleys and carry the people away. The woman who gave him drink has escaped. Here a rule appears again: a stranger's shabby appearance is a test of hospitality. In this story the result is not merely a lost opportunity but the remaking of a valley by violence.

Then, at a place belonging to the Cupara people, the problem is not too much water but too little. The fields are drying. Chuquisuso, a woman who cultivates maize, weeps over the dwindling supply. Pariacaca sees her and promises to release more water, but demands intimacy in exchange. She does not simply accept the first offer. First, she insists, the crops must be watered. He releases water. She asks for more: a proper canal carrying it to the lower fields. Pariacaca commands animals to build the course. Pumas, serpents, foxes, and birds take their places. The fox insists on leading, then is startled by a partridge and tumbles down a slope. The other creatures put the serpent in charge. That is why, the story says, the channel runs lower than it might have. The crooked line of an irrigation canal is now a visible consequence of animal pride and fright.

Only after the work has been accomplished does the story say Chuquisuso and Pariacaca unite. She chooses to remain at the canal's intake, becoming a stone there. The Cupara people honor her, and the act of cleaning the canal is linked to her memory. This is a story about desire and a huaca's bargaining power, certainly. But it is also about the right to water. A community's irrigation works, their annual collective labor, and a stone beside the channel all belong to the same account of how the world became habitable.

The struggle with Huallallo Carhuincho, ruler of the earlier and crueler age, now reaches its full force. Pariacaca hunts the being who ate humans. The battle is described through fire, torrents, mountains, and changing landforms. Where Huallallo's fiery dwelling stood, the waters of a lake rise to extinguish it. The enemy flees toward the eastern lowlands. Pariacaca does not erase him from all existence; he defeats him, imposes a sentence, and assigns him a diminished sphere. One tradition says that the devourer of people must now eat dogs and be served by the Huanca. The victory tells a history of competing sacred powers, but it also records a claim about which communities came under whose authority.

And afterward the victorious mountain must be visited, offered to, remembered. People carry coca, drink, and other gifts to the shrine, then return to their villages, where relatives ask whether their powerful father was pleased or angry. The festival is a living relationship rather than the last scene of a completed quest. Pariacaca's story does not end when Huallallo falls. Every time people walk a path, open a canal, gather for a ceremony, or address a stone by name, they are encountering the consequences of the story.

If we were to look for one moral, we would be frustrated. Cuniraya can create fields and violate Cahuillaca's will. Pariacaca can bring water and destroy a village. Huatiacuri can expose pretended wisdom and accept a marriage negotiated as payment for healing. The powers of Huarochirí are neither distant abstractions nor exemplary human citizens. They are woven into land, food, sex, weather, danger, hierarchy, and survival. The next part of the manuscript's world is not what happens after a hero goes home. It is how different families and peoples explain their obligations to these beings, their conflicts with one another, and the arrival of powers from outside the mountains.

## Text and evidence companion (not spoken)

**Scope:** Original English narrative overview of selected connected episodes in the early portion of the *Huarochirí Manuscript*: ancient huacas and Huallallo (ch. 1); Cuniraya, Cahuillaca and the animals (ch. 2); ocean flood and sun's disappearance (chs. 3–4); Huatiacuri and the five eggs (ch. 5); Pariacaca's feast, storm and Chuquisuso (chs. 6–7); conflict with Huallallo and the cult (chs. 8–9). The text is a **cycle of stories**, not a reconstructed single precolonial narrative. Chapter-number locators refer to the 31-chapter Quechua manuscript as presented in the Salomon–Urioste scholarly edition, not a promise of a chapter-by-chapter retelling. Other episodes are reserved for the second story lecture.

**Witnesses and names:** The primary scholarly orientation and chapter order are [Frank Salomon and George L. Urioste, *The Huarochirí Manuscript* (University of Texas Press, 1991)](https://utpress.utexas.edu/9780292730533/), whose book preview and [JSTOR contents / chapter descriptions](https://www.jstor.org/stable/10.7560/730526) have been checked. Event details have also been checked against a publicly readable [Spanish rendering of the Quechua manuscript](https://kuprienko.info/francisco-de-avila-dioses-y-hombres-de-huarochiri-fragmentos/); its text is a reproduction with uneven orthography, not an authoritative original-language edition. No modern translation passages are reproduced. Names have many scholarly spellings: Pariacaca / Paria Caca / Paryaqaqa; Cuniraya / Cuni Raya; Chuquisuso / Chuqui Suso; Huatiacuri / Huatya Curi; Huallallo Carhuincho / Huallallo Caruincho.

**Cautions:** Cahuillaca's pregnancy follows Cuniraya's deception; the lecture does not present that as voluntary. The Huatiacuri episodes express the manuscript's moral judgments about the wife, poverty, and class; these are not factual claims about historical families. The narrative speaks of powerful beings through contested names and sometimes conflicting genealogies. Flood and darkened sun are narratives from an early colonial Quechua source, not asserted dates or external geological findings. No claim is made that the narrative's events document a particular precolonial battle. For general orientation see [Wikipedia: Huarochirí Manuscript](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Huarochir%C3%AD_Manuscript).
