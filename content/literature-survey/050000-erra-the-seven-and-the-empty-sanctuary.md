# Erra and Ishum: The Seven and the Empty Sanctuary

An original narrative of the first movement of the Akkadian *Erra and Ishum* poem, conventionally arranged in five tablets. This unit follows the opening dialogue and the attempt to draw Marduk from his sanctuary; the sequel is indexed separately.

The god of devastation is at rest. He is not dead, and his weapons have not disappeared, but the great harm he can do to the inhabited world has not yet been set in motion. His name is Erra. The poem begins with a danger more disturbing than a battle: the possibility that a force capable of destroying cities might choose to rise simply because it wants its strength acknowledged.

Beside him is Ishum, a divine warrior and guide, sometimes the speaker who warns, sometimes the one who will carry out orders, and finally the voice by which disaster can be checked. Their relationship is the center of the *Epic of Erra*, or *Erra and Ishum*. It is not a conventional story of one heroic person setting out from home to win a prize. Much of its action takes place in speeches. Threats, counsel, demands, concessions, and boasts determine what happens to actual streets, households, and temples.

The poem belongs to the Akkadian literary tradition of first-millennium Babylonia. It survives across a number of manuscripts written on clay tablets, assembled by scholars into five tablets of a long work. Its fifth-tablet colophon names Kabti-ilani-Marduk, son of Dabibi, as the person associated with the composition and tells a story of a nocturnal vision. That ancient claim of authorship is part of the literary witness, not independent evidence that we can watch an identifiable writer receiving a dream. The tablets speak with a voice that knows catastrophe can come from within the divine order, not only from outside it.

The opening establishes Ishum's terrible competence. He is no harmless figure who recommends kindness because he knows nothing of war. He carries formidable weapons. He is acquainted with force and its uses. It is precisely this capacity that makes his attempts to restrain Erra consequential. Someone who can fight recognizes that fighting can become a catastrophe when it is made into an end in itself.

Erra is weary and reluctant to rise. His weapons have been set aside. In the poem's opening dialogue, the forces around him begin to call him back to action. Among them are the Seven, often called the Sebetti, fierce divine companions whose energy is associated with battle. They resent idleness and speak of the fame that war can produce. A god who is supposed to be powerful should not grow accustomed to silence and comfort. A weapon that never leaves its place might as well have forgotten what it was made to do.

To human ears this sounds like a familiar kind of provocation. They are not arguing that a named city has committed a documented offense or that a treaty has been violated. They are stirring up a reputation. If Erra does not fight, others may think he is weak. If he wishes to be feared, let him demonstrate why he is feared. The destructive campaign begins as a dispute over whether violence is needed to prove one's identity.

Ishum tries to hold the matter still. He asks Erra what the proposed attack would do to gods and human beings. He understands that a war against the inhabited land will not be confined to those who speak in the divine council. Whatever is decided here will descend upon farmers, tradespeople, parents, temples, roads, and city walls. He is the poem's warning that a force may be powerful and still have no good reason to use its full power.

Erra will not be turned away so easily. He gives the counterargument of the violent ruler. The world has grown complacent. His standing has been neglected. A great disturbance will make people recognize him again. The urge to restore prestige can dress itself as a need to restore order. But there is a difference between an insult that troubles a god's pride and a wrong that justifies destroying towns. Ishum keeps bringing that difference into view; Erra keeps pressing past it.

The poem then directs us toward Babylon, and above all toward the sanctuary of Marduk, its leading god. Marduk's temple and cult image are not mere decorations. Within the story, their presence anchors the relationship between the city and the gods. The statue's dazzling appearance expresses authority, stability, and proper worship. If the king of the gods leaves his accustomed place, the world may not remain as secure as it looks.

Erra approaches Marduk with a problem framed as concern. The divine image's brilliance has diminished; its adornment or ritual condition needs repair. How can a magnificent ruler remain in a sanctuary if the visible sign of his magnificence is allowed to dull? The proposition is hard to reject because it speaks in the language of care for the temple. A man wishing to rob a house might first persuade its guardian to step outside. Erra's appeal is more dangerous: he wants the great guardian of Babylon to move away from his position.

Marduk hesitates. The poem recalls earlier disorder when he was absent from his appointed station. The warning is not a superstitious aside. It supplies the story's central principle: the integrity of the world is connected to divine presence and to the maintained order of worship. Repairing an image may be proper, but even a proper repair becomes a source of danger if it creates an opening for a force that seeks ruin.

Erra promises to handle the difficulties. Needed materials can be found, specialists can do their work, and he himself will watch over things while Marduk is away. The terms sound almost administrative. A sanctuary must be repaired. Someone will supervise its protection. The language makes the transition seem routine, though we and Ishum have heard Erra's hunger for violence. Behind this ordinary plan lies a calculation about absence.

Marduk's departure or displacement is one of the poem's most debated portions, in part because the textual witnesses become damaged at important points. We should not pretend to possess a perfectly continuous account of the god's every step or of the means by which his absence ends. But the story's causal direction is evident. The established guardian moves from his station, and Erra gains an opportunity to spread the disorder he has been seeking.

The first visible consequences are not trophies won by Erra in honorable combat. They are the loosening of relations that allow people to live. Cities depend on more than stout walls. Their residents need decisions that can be trusted, water and food that continue to arrive, rulers whose commands have some relation to right conduct, and neighbors who do not expect betrayal at every turn. Once fear becomes the chief authority, each person begins to treat another as a danger. The poem makes cosmic disturbance legible as civic catastrophe.

At every stage Ishum remains both near Erra and opposed to his purpose. He has to speak to the god who is causing harm while that god has the power to do more. This is not a duel in which the enemy's defeat will settle the argument. Ishum's challenge is to persuade a violent being to stop wanting what violence promises. He cannot simply become Erra's more effective weapon, for that would complete the very program he is trying to prevent.

The Seven are useful to Erra because their presence makes conflict feel possible and even thrilling. They give the divine war leader an audience and a company. The excitement of preparing a campaign obscures the ordinary people who will bear its costs. In the telling, the movement from restless divine speech to the threatened removal of Marduk shows how a catastrophe may be authorized before anyone on the streets knows that their safety is being traded away.

The poem is often read against the remembered devastation of Babylonian cities and the experience of warfare in Mesopotamia. It is important not to turn that setting into a claim that every destructive episode encodes one single identifiable battle. The composition uses the imagery of ruined neighborhoods and displaced people to ask a question that goes beyond one event. How can gods who are supposed to maintain the world become the agents of its collapse? And what does a person do when the ordinary channels of protection have been opened to a destroyer?

Listen to the opening once more. Erra does not begin with an urgent need to rescue an innocent victim. He begins with the shame of inaction. The Seven offer him the thrill of an audience that admires strength. Ishum, who knows strength, asks what it is for. Marduk is approached through a plan that sounds like necessary upkeep. The sanctuary is left exposed not by a battering ram but by words. The first half of this story teaches the cost of persuasion when its aim is to make disaster seem like duty.

The tablets will go on to give that choice a human scale. They will describe a land in turmoil, a god who claims the power to destroy all that lives, and Ishum's continuing effort to turn him aside. The opening already gives us what we need to understand the struggle. The danger is not that nobody has warned Erra. The danger is that he has been warned, has heard, and still wants to go.
