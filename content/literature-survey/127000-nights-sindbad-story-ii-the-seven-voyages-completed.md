# Sindbad the Sailor — Story II: Seven Voyages Completed

The fifth, sixth, and seventh voyages and concluding hospitality frame, with Laurence Housman's 1907 retelling as named narrative basis. Seventh-voyage textual alternatives are identified in the non-spoken companion and analysis.

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

Sindbad has been speaking to his poorer namesake for four days. Four voyages have brought him riches and brought him close to death. He has sailed past islands of treasure, eaten at the tables of kings, and crawled from an underground tomb. At the end of every journey he has returned to Baghdad and declared himself content to stay. At the beginning of the next, the sea has called him again. The porter named Sindbad, who began by complaining about his own burdens, has been listening to a rich man explain that some fortunes are purchased with terrors nobody would willingly repeat. The fifth voyage begins with a deliberate investment. The sailor buys a ship.

He hires a crew, loads merchandise, and goes to sea as a man who means to direct his own affairs. At an island the merchants find an enormous white egg. Those who hear its description may already know what it is. On an earlier voyage the roc had carried Sindbad from a deserted island into the diamond valley. This time the other travelers break open the shell and kill the young bird inside. Sindbad warns them that the parents will return. No ship is fast enough to escape the anger of such creatures. Two rocs appear in the sky with giant stones in their claws. One stone falls close, raising a wave that nearly overturns the vessel. The second strikes. The ship breaks apart, and Sindbad is cast upon another island, alive but once more separated from everything he had owned.

He walks through a beautiful landscape and meets an old man seated beside a stream. The stranger seems too frail to cross the water alone and gestures for help. Sindbad offers to carry him. He bends down and allows the man to climb onto his shoulders. When they reach the other side, the old man locks his legs around Sindbad's neck and refuses to descend. He grips so hard that Sindbad nearly chokes. Wherever the old man wishes to go, he kicks and squeezes until Sindbad carries him there. The sailor is made into a beast of burden, unable to sleep or travel on his own terms. He fears that the man will ride him until he dies.

At last Sindbad collects grapes, presses their juice into a vessel and lets it ferment. He drinks from the liquid and feigns cheerful intoxication. The old man demands a taste. He drinks deeply, loosens his grip, and falls helplessly to the ground. Sindbad throws him off and kills him, breaking the captivity. Other travelers who find him say he has escaped the Old Man of the Sea, a tormentor from whom almost no one comes back alive. The island of the fifth voyage has offered two kinds of terror. First there is sudden destruction from above; then there is a weight so continuous that it converts the traveler into someone else's conveyance. Escape in both cases requires what the sailor has learned from the earlier journeys: watch how power is exercised, and discover the opening it leaves.

With the help of merchants, Sindbad joins a trade in coconuts. He accompanies men who go into forests inhabited by monkeys. They throw stones upward, provoking the animals to throw coconuts down in return. The merchants gather the nuts and sell them. Sindbad takes a share, trades through other ports for pepper, aromatic wood and pearls, and eventually returns to Baghdad. He has made wealth, not from a magical cavern or a king's gift, but from observing how a landscape and its inhabitants can be drawn into commerce. His account is pleased with the cleverness and profits. A later listener may also hear the strain and compulsion in his repeated exchanges with a hostile world.

The sixth voyage begins almost before the memory of the fifth has cooled. A group of merchants tells him of places he has not seen. Sindbad assembles goods and returns to the sea, although he has made another promise to remain in Baghdad. The vessel loses its bearings. It reaches a mountainous coast where the wind and current smash the ship against rocks. A few survivors stagger onto shore. They find a dreadful abundance: timber and merchandise from broken ships, precious stones, ambergris, riches that none of the people who lost them have carried home. There is wealth everywhere and almost nothing to eat. One by one Sindbad's companions die. He buries them, counts the remaining food, and realizes that the coast is likely to become his own grave.

A river runs into a cavern beneath the mountain. The current vanishes into darkness. Sindbad decides that the black passage is his last possible route away. He makes a raft, loads on it the jewels and treasures that a drowning sea has accumulated, and allows the river to carry him underground. No lantern can make the whole way visible. He must trust the current, though he has no idea whether it will lead to the sea, a waterfall, or rock. He falls asleep from exhaustion. When he wakes, people are standing over him. He has emerged into a prosperous region identified in the story with Serendib, now called Sri Lanka. Local people receive him kindly, and he comes before the ruler.

The king is delighted by the traveler's story and honors him with gifts. Sindbad has reached a place of dazzling wealth and ceremonial splendor, a court he describes in terms meant to impress his Baghdad audience. He tells the ruler about the caliph Harun al-Rashid and the power of his city. Before Sindbad leaves, the king sends rich presents and a letter to Harun, asking that friendship pass between rulers. Sindbad brings the embassy home and is rewarded. Again he declares that no storm, no jewel and no rumor of distant lands will persuade him to risk his life. He will remain with his family. The resolve might finally have held, but now a ruler's command displaces a merchant's appetite.

Harun al-Rashid summons him and asks him to carry a diplomatic answer and gifts to the king of Serendib. Sindbad begs to be excused. He has already been shipwrecked six times; the thought of another voyage makes his knees weak. The caliph praises his courage, recognizes his sufferings, and insists. Sindbad departs with a magnificent horse, costly textiles, precious vessels, and a letter of goodwill. The voyage outward succeeds. The king receives the embassy graciously. Sindbad performs his task and sets sail for home, hoping the seventh journey will prove to be a short and honorable conclusion.

Before he can reach Baghdad, armed men in boats surround the ship. They attack the crew and take the survivors prisoner. Sindbad is sold into slavery to a merchant far from home. His owner asks what skills he possesses. The traveler tells him he knows how to shoot a bow. The man takes him into the forest and orders him to hunt elephants, whose tusks can be sold as ivory. Sindbad spends days in the trees, watching for the great animals, killing them and reporting what he has found. His labor yields profit, but its price is violence repeated over living creatures that have done him no wrong.

One day the elephants themselves approach in a mass, break down the tree from which he has been hunting, and bear him away. He expects to die. Instead they carry him to a place filled with the bones and tusks of elephants that have died there. It is as though they are telling him that his owner may gather ivory from the dead and cease killing the living. Sindbad returns and tells the merchant what he has seen. The merchant rejoices in the discovery. He can now obtain more ivory with less danger to the hunters, and Sindbad's service is rewarded. The episode gives the animals something nearly absent from earlier voyages: a collective intelligence that answers exploitation not only with force but with a sign.

Sindbad is freed and enabled to return with goods. He reaches Baghdad for the last time. He has been a prince's guest, a pirate's captive, a trader, a husband, a survivor of shipwrecks, and a man who took the lives of animals for another man's wealth. The riches he brings home are real. So are the people and creatures whose losses the story has allowed him to describe. He tells the porter that this is how he came to his present state. He invites his poorer namesake to live with him and be his companion. The two men named Sindbad sit in the same house at last, no longer entirely strangers, though their lives remain very different.

The sailor's seven days of narration have been a long reply to the porter's complaint. Do his stories justify his wealth? The tale allows him to believe they do. He has suffered terribly, risked almost everything, and survived. But the porter has also carried heavy burdens; he simply never had a roc, a caliph or a mountain of diamonds to convert his suffering into riches. Their conversation ends in hospitality, not a mathematical balancing of desert and reward. The famous traveler can tell his listeners what he saw, but his final discovery is not a new island. It is the chance, after all that movement, to return to the people who have been waiting for him at home.

## Text and evidence companion (not spoken)

- **Controlling witness:** [Laurence Housman, *Sindbad the Sailor & Other Stories from the Arabian Nights* (1907), Fifth through Seventh Voyages](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/47285/47285-h/47285-h.htm). Housman's text supplies this lecture's seventh-voyage diplomatic mission, captivity and elephant-ivory conclusion.
- **Versions:** [Richard Burton, *The Book of the Thousand Nights and a Night*, volume VI](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/58722/58722-h/58722-h.htm) provides seventh-voyage variants. The material is not described as a fixed original Arabic text with no textual alternatives. The seven-voyage Sindbad cycle also circulated with a degree of independence from the Nights collection.
- **Narrative caution:** Serendib corresponds to historical Sri Lanka as a geographical name, but the marvels, local customs, and animal actions in the story are literary inventions or transformed reports; they are not reliable ethnography. Housman is an illustrated English retelling rather than a primary medieval eyewitness record.
- [Wikipedia: Sinbad the Sailor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sinbad_the_Sailor); [Wikipedia: Sri Lanka](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sri_Lanka).
