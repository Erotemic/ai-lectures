# The Golden Ass, Part One: A Guest in the House of Magic

An original retelling of Apuleius's Latin *Metamorphoses* or *The Golden Ass*, Books I–IV (before and around the inset Cupid-and-Psyche tale). Narrative locators follow the eleven-book text, checked against the book-by-book reference at Poetry in Translation and the historic Adlington translation. Apuleius's novel is not Ovid's *Metamorphoses*. Our protagonist is the fictional Lucius; the narrator should not be simply identified with the historical author.

The traveler rides toward Thessaly with an appetite for marvels. He has heard that the country is full of witches, secret rites, and people who can change their shape. Most sensible visitors might avoid a place with such a reputation. Lucius is drawn to it. He is educated, talkative, curious, and sure that whatever danger surrounds the practice of magic, it will be far more interesting to witness than to miss.

That curiosity is the small engine that sets a very large Latin novel in motion. Apuleius wrote his *Metamorphoses*, commonly called *The Golden Ass*, in the Roman world of the second century of the Common Era. The surviving novel runs to eleven books. It follows one man who becomes an animal and cannot immediately become human again. But its roads are full of other people's stories: tales told over meals, in hiding places, beside beds, or by strangers who meet almost by accident. When somebody begins a story, the main action may wait for pages while another world opens inside it.

On the road, Lucius meets a traveler who says that a friend of his has been the victim of a witch. The friend's name is Socrates, though he is no relation to the famous philosopher whose name we know from Athens. Aristomenes, the teller, recounts finding this Socrates nearly ruined by an affair with a woman named Meroe. Her ordinary trade conceals extraordinary powers. When he tries to help his old friend escape, Meroe and another witch enter their lodging at night, pronounce fearful words, and stab Socrates in the neck. They draw out his heart and replace it with a sponge. The scene belongs to a nightmare in which a traveler is awake enough to feel terror yet unsure whether he may believe what he has seen. In the morning Socrates can walk and speak. Aristomenes almost convinces himself it was a dream. But later, when Socrates stops at a stream and bends to drink, the sponge falls out. He collapses and dies.

What could a listener do with that story? Believe it? Laugh at it? Run from Thessaly? Lucius is eager to hear more. The fearful tale acts as both warning and invitation. It shows how the novel operates: the world may contain real magic, or travelers may be taken in by astonishing accounts; in either case, curiosity itself can make a person vulnerable.

Lucius arrives at the house of Milo, a wealthy but notoriously miserly host. There he encounters the servant Photis, who is attractive and sharp, and he begins a passionate affair with her. The arrangement gives him a way into the household's secrets. Milo's wife, Pamphile, is said to practice magic. Lucius does not merely happen upon forbidden knowledge: he wants to watch it at work. His intimacy with Photis becomes a route toward an experiment he has already decided to perform on himself.

But the nights before that experiment are full of comic and ugly surprises. At a dinner party a guest tells how he was paid to guard a corpse from witches who might steal pieces of its body for their spells. He watched through the night and seems to have succeeded. Later, however, it emerges that the witches could not reach the corpse and took parts of the living guard's face instead. The story moves from a grim assignment to a humiliating joke, with magical intervention and public exposure piled upon one another.

Lucius himself stumbles into a terrible-looking fight outside the house. He sees figures gathered at the door and, believing they are robbers, attacks them with his sword. The next day he is brought before a crowd on a charge of murder. He gives an elaborate defense. The audience seems severe; the charges are monstrous. Then the bodies are uncovered. They are not men at all, but pierced leather wineskins. The town has been celebrating a festival of Laughter, and Lucius has been made its unwitting spectacle. He has felt the terror of a capital trial so that other people can enjoy his embarrassment.

The joke matters because it is not entirely harmless. The townspeople have staged a mock catastrophe around a foreign guest and watched him suffer before they disclose the trick. He cannot know which threats are real, which imaginary, and which deliberately manufactured for somebody else's pleasure. He is already learning what it means to lose control of a story about himself.

Photis then admits that Pamphile's magic caused the wineskins to move as though they were intruders. Lucius's appetite only sharpens. He persuades her to let him watch Pamphile transform herself. Hidden where he can see, he witnesses the witch cover her body with an ointment and become an owl. She spreads her wings and flies. Lucius is entranced. He wants to become an owl too, to take the air on that new body and return when he wishes.

Photis brings out a jar of ointment. He rubs it on his skin, anticipating feathers and flight. Instead hair spreads across him; his face lengthens; his fingers are joined in hooves; a tail appears. He has become a donkey. The human mind remains inside a body that can bray but cannot speak its private thoughts aloud. The ambitious experiment has produced not a powerful night bird but a beast of burden.

Photis knows what should reverse the change. He must eat roses. The remedy is astonishingly simple: somewhere there must be a few flowers, and a human life will return. But that night there are no roses available, and the animals are put away. Before they can solve the problem, robbers attack the house. They load the transformed Lucius with the goods they steal and drive him away.

The change in his social position is immediate. While he believed himself a human guest, he could choose a story to tell or a bedroom to enter. As a donkey he is assigned work. His new masters beat him, overload him, accuse him of laziness, and assess his value by what they can force his body to do. He knows their words, but they do not understand his. A human mind sits silently inside a creature the world thinks it may strike with impunity.

On the road, Lucius sees roses and lunges toward them, hoping for rescue, but the circumstances repeatedly prevent him from reaching the flowers. In one encounter he is driven away from apparent relief by fear that those watching will kill the donkey who eats their ornamental plants. What he needs for salvation is ordinary and close; what surrounds it is a structure of human control. The novel's joke has become a sequence of beatings, fatigue, hunger, and captivity.

The robbers take him to their cavern. They boast of their raids and tell each other elaborate stories of failed exploits. One robber tried to enter a house disguised in a bear skin. The disguise was initially cunning, but a real animal's treatment awaited him once the city discovered the fraud. Such episodes echo Lucius's own predicament. Men borrow animals' appearances for advantage; Lucius has an animal's appearance imposed on him and cannot take it off.

Then the robbers bring in a young woman whom they have abducted. Her name is Charite. She has been torn away from her wedding and brought into the cave for ransom. When she cries and fears that she will never see her beloved again, an old woman who cooks for the thieves tries to comfort her by telling a story. The old woman does not begin with local robbers or a kidnapped bride. She begins with a king and three daughters, one of whom is so beautiful that people forget to worship the goddess Venus.

That daughter is Psyche. The old woman's story will occupy much of the next three books, and for a time the donkey's humiliations recede behind the extraordinary sufferings of a young woman. It is not an interruption to be skipped. The tale shares the novel's central concerns: a dangerous curiosity, a forbidden act of seeing, a beloved person hidden by disguise or darkness, and a transformation that cannot be undone by cleverness alone.

At the robber's cave, the irony is sharp. A captive bride is being comforted with the story of another bride promised into a terrifying unknown. Nearby stands a donkey who understands every word but cannot tell either woman who he really is. The novel has turned its curious traveler into the perfect, helpless audience. Lucius wanted to hear magical stories. Now he can do little else, even when the story is his own.

## Narrative evidence and textual boundaries

- Primary text: Apuleius, *Metamorphoses*, Books I–IV (pre-Cupid-and-Psyche material), using [Poetry in Translation's eleven-book index](https://www.poetryintranslation.com/PITBR/Latin/Apuleiushome.php) to locate episodes, with [Wikisource Adlington translation (1566)](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/The_Golden_Ass_of_Apuleius) as a second historic witness. Prose is a new retelling, not a translation.
- Scholarly framing: [Brill, Apuleius Online introduction](https://scholarlyeditions.brill.com/apo/), distinguishing this Latin *Metamorphoses* from Ovid's poem; [Wikipedia: The Golden Ass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Golden_Ass).
- Episode anchors: I Aristomenes/Socrates/Meroe; II Photis and Thelyphron; III trial and wineskins, Pamphile the owl, Lucius the donkey; IV robber stories and Charite kidnapped, with Psyche's tale beginning near the end.
