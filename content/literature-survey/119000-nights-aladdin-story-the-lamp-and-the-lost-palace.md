# Aladdin — The Story: The Lamp and the Lost Palace

A continuous, spoiler-complete retelling of the Galland–Diyāb *Aladdin*, including the magician's brother. The received narrative is situated in a storybook China; it is not a report about historical China.

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

In a great city of China, in the geography of a storyteller rather than a mapmaker, a tailor called Mustapha has a son who will not learn his trade. The boy is Aladdin. While his father works, Aladdin finds games in the street more interesting than stitches, and by the time his father dies the family's security is scarcely thicker than the cloth his mother can spin. She works because somebody must. Her son has charm and appetite, but he has no plan. If a stranger were looking for a capable young man to trust with his fortune, Aladdin would be a strange choice. A stranger is indeed looking for him, and his reasons are worse than Aladdin could imagine.

A traveler arrives and asks the boys at play whether any of them is Mustapha's son. On hearing Aladdin's name, he embraces him with tears. He claims to be Mustapha's brother, long absent from home. He gives the boy coins, instructs him to tell his mother that his uncle has returned, and soon appears at their poor house with gifts and an outpouring of affection for the dead tailor. Aladdin's mother can hardly reconcile this splendid relative with her husband's silence about him. The traveler answers every doubt with grief and generosity. He promises to establish Aladdin in commerce, to clothe him, and to make a gentleman of him. It is exactly the promise the family would most like to hear.

The man is no uncle. He is a magician who has crossed a great distance because his calculations tell him of a wonderful lamp buried in an underground treasury. The treasure can be reached only through the agency of this particular boy. He cannot safely go down himself. He needs Aladdin to believe that he is loved. On an excursion beyond the city, with delicious food and talk of a merchant's future, the supposed uncle leads Aladdin into the hills. There he makes a fire, casts powders upon it, and speaks words that split the earth. A stone entrance appears, descending to a chamber no ordinary traveler has seen. The magician places a ring upon Aladdin's hand and sends him below.

The instructions are strict. Pass through the chambers without touching the forbidden objects. Walk through the strange garden. Find the old lamp, put out its light, empty its oil, hide it in your clothes, and come straight back. In the garden, fruit hangs from trees in colors no orchard has ever produced: red, green, yellow, blue, perfectly clear. Aladdin thinks them pretty glass and fills his pockets. They are diamonds and rubies, emeralds and other stones whose price he does not know. He finds the lamp, dull and dirty, and begins to climb. But burdened with the stones, he cannot make the final ascent without a hand from above.

The magician extends a hand only for the lamp. Aladdin, frightened and weighed down, asks to be lifted out first. Neither will yield. The magician's hunger for the object he has spent years pursuing erupts into fury. He seals the entrance with another spell and leaves Aladdin in the dark, certain that the boy will die there. It is the first lesson the lamp offers: the man who promises to make you rich may intend to keep the treasure and leave you buried with the story of how it was found.

For two days Aladdin is trapped. He begs for help. In despair he clasps his hands, rubbing the ring the magician had given him. A being appears, announcing its service to the holder of the ring. Aladdin does not ask for a palace. He asks to be outside, where he can breathe. The creature transports him out of the earth. He staggers home, carrying the lamp and the colored stones. His mother hardly recognizes him. He tells her about the false uncle and the cave, and she understands that her household has escaped an attempt at murder.

They need food. His mother looks at the lamp and thinks that, cleaned, it might fetch a little money. As she rubs it, another great spirit appears, a more formidable servant of the lamp. She faints in terror. Aladdin is quick enough to ask for a meal. Silver dishes arrive filled with food, as if a wealthy host had ordered the house transformed for a feast. Afterward he sells the dishes, one at a time. At first he is cheated of their value, because he knows nothing of silver or commerce; later he learns better prices. Gradually the boy whom his father could not make into a tailor discovers that wealth requires some understanding even when it has been delivered by magic. Yet the power at his disposal remains astonishing. He no longer needs to know where food comes from. He needs only to remember where he has hidden a lamp.

One day a proclamation closes the city streets: the sultan's daughter, Badr al-Budur, will go to the bath, and nobody may look upon her. Aladdin contrives to see her face. He is seized by a desire that immediately outruns his position. The princess is the daughter of a ruler; he is the son of a poor tailor. He persuades his mother to go before the sultan and ask for her hand. She is horrified by the distance between the two households, but Aladdin insists. At court she waits for days, too frightened to speak, until the sultan finally asks her errand. She presents a bowl of the jewels Aladdin picked up as playthings in the underground garden. The ruler knows at once that this is no ordinary gift.

The sultan is willing to consider the match, but his grand vizier wants the princess for his own son. He secures a delay, promising to produce greater wealth. Aladdin waits on the sultan's word. Then celebration floods the streets: the princess is to marry the vizier's son after all. Aladdin's response reveals the unkind side of his new power. He commands the lamp's servant to remove the bride and her husband, bed and all, from their chamber on their wedding night. The frightened groom is taken away and left shivering. Badr al-Budur is returned unharmed to her room but terrified by what neither she nor her family can explain. The ordeal happens again. The marriage, never peacefully begun, is dissolved. The episode advances Aladdin's fortune by violating the wishes and security of others. It belongs to the story even when a retelling would prefer a more uncomplicated hero.

The sultan has promised his daughter to a wealthy man. Aladdin now displays what supernatural wealth can purchase. His mother brings treasures; servants arrive in magnificent clothing; horses and attendants assemble in dazzling order. Aladdin appears not as a boy of the streets but as a figure the court can understand. The sultan demands proof that his future son-in-law can support a princess in royal state. Aladdin has the lamp's servant build a palace of marble, gold and silver, brilliant stones, gardens, stables and riches, with a great hall whose windows shine like the sky. An elaborate carpet unites this new palace with the old one. Badr al-Budur and Aladdin are married amid celebrations, and she comes to her new home.

One window in the great hall is left unfinished. When the sultan notices it, Aladdin invites him to complete it as a mark of his own generosity. The ruler summons his finest artisans and opens his treasury, but no number of gems seems enough. At last Aladdin quietly has all the jewels returned and commands the spirit to finish the window. The sultan beholds a perfection his own resources could not approach. He admires the son-in-law who has made it possible. The vizier cannot conceal his unease. How can an untrained youth produce a palace that defeats a kingdom's craftsmen? Aladdin's transformation is public, celebrated and, to anyone who guesses the truth, precarious.

The story gives him several years in which to act as something better than the idle boy of the beginning. He assists the sultan, leads soldiers with success, and distributes gifts that win the affection of ordinary people. His household and marriage appear secure. But across the sea the magician discovers that his chosen victim still lives and possesses the treasure. He returns to the city and sees the palace that should have been his. One object makes all this splendor vulnerable: an old lamp whose value scarcely anyone knows. He buys a basket of clean new lamps and walks near the palace calling for old ones in exchange. The offer is so ridiculous that servants laugh. Badr al-Budur, who has never been told the secret, agrees to exchange the dusty lamp lying in the hall. Nothing seems lost, except an ugly piece of metal.

The magician now has what he came for. He summons the lamp's servant and commands that Aladdin's entire palace, with the princess inside, be carried to Africa. The palace disappears from the sultan's view. Where it stood the ground is bare. The sultan sees not a marvelous feat but an outrage against his daughter and orders Aladdin arrested. Crowds who have benefited from Aladdin's generosity rise in his defense. The sultan, fearing disorder as well as grieving for his child, grants him a limited time to restore her. Aladdin is once again a man without a house. He still owns the ring, but the ring's spirit is weaker than the spirit of the lamp. It can carry him to the missing palace; it cannot command the palace to move back.

He is carried across the world and reaches the palace in its new place. Badr al-Budur is alive, furious and frightened. She has resisted the magician's claim that her husband is dead and that she should accept another. Aladdin tells her the whole history of the lamp at last. He cannot simply seize the magician in open combat; the man carries the vessel that gives him superior power. They devise a plan. The princess changes her manner, offers the magician hospitality, and lets him believe that his courtship might succeed. He drinks the wine she sets before him, not knowing she has prepared it with a deadly drug. When he collapses, Aladdin takes the lamp back and orders the palace restored to its city. The sultan watches it reappear where it belongs, and mother, daughter, husband and father are reconciled.

The magician has a brother, however, and this brother learns of his death. Determined on vengeance, he travels to Aladdin's city and finds a holy woman called Fatima, beloved because people believe her touch can heal. He kills her, steals her clothing, and appears before the palace veiled as the respected ascetic. Badr al-Budur welcomes the supposed holy woman, asks her to stay, and proudly shows her the palace. The disguised intruder praises the room but suggests a single improvement: if the egg of the immense bird called the roc were suspended from its dome, nothing would equal it. The suggestion seems harmless; the princess asks Aladdin to obtain it.

Aladdin summons the lamp's servant and repeats the request. For the first time the spirit answers not with obedience but rage. The roc, in this telling, stands in a special relation to these beings; to demand its egg as decoration is an affront that would bring destruction. Yet the spirit knows the suggestion did not originate with Aladdin. It warns him that the magician's brother has murdered Fatima and taken her place. Aladdin feigns a sudden headache and asks the supposed healer to come close. When the assassin attempts to strike, Aladdin kills him. He tells the princess what has happened, and the last concealed enemy of the lamp has been removed.

At the end Aladdin has not merely grown rich. He has grown up in a world where every extraordinary advantage creates new dependence and new danger. The ring saved him when he was poor; the lamp brought food; its wealth won the princess and built the palace; its loss nearly destroyed them all. Badr al-Budur is not the decorative reward of the adventure. She is essential to the recovery of the palace, acting when the lamp cannot be wrested from its owner by force. The man who began by refusing to work becomes a ruler after the sultan's death, and the tale gives his family a long line of descendants. For a moment we may picture the poor tailor's house again, and the lamp on its shelf: so ordinary an object that a stranger could offer to trade it for a brighter one. That is where the whole kingdom nearly vanished.

## Text and evidence companion (not spoken)

- Controlling transmitted story family: Antoine Galland, *Mille et une nuits*, later volumes, drawing on narratives supplied by **Hannā Diyāb in Paris in 1709**. Named public-domain English presentation: [Burton, *Supplemental Nights*, volume III, Galland English translation](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/60889/60889-h/60889-h.htm); compare [1898 *Arabian Nights Entertainments*, Aladdin](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/The_Arabian_nights_entertainments_(Longman_1898)/Aladdin_and_the_Wonderful_Lamp). The manuscript used to reconstruct what Diyāb actually narrated does **not** survive. This is original prose, not a translation of a hypothetical ancient Arabic original.
- Plot checkpoints: Mustapha and the false uncle; magic ring versus lamp; jewel garden; Badr al-Budur's earlier marriage and coerced dissolution; palace's unfinished window; lamp-for-lamp exchange; missing palace in Africa; princess's drugged reception; second magician disguised as Fatima; roc's egg and warning. **The secondary Fatima episode is part of this received story**, not a newly invented sequel.
- Historical transmission: [Bibliothèque nationale de France, *The Thousand and One Nights*](https://heritage.bnf.fr/bibliothequesorient/en/thousand-and-one-nights-0); [Paulo Lemos Horta, "Tales of Aladdin and Their Tellers"](https://wordswithoutborders.org/read/article/2020-04/tales-of-aladdin-and-their-tellers-from-aleppo-to-paris-paulo-lemos-horta/); [Ruth B. Bottigheimer, *East Meets West*](https://digitalcommons.wayne.edu/marvels/vol28/iss2/5/), with her particular theory of European influences identified as a scholarly argument rather than consensus.
- [Wikipedia: Aladdin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aladdin); [Wikipedia: Hannā Diyāb](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hanna_Diyab). Character ethics and the late tale's political setting are not historical reports about China. No dialogue here claims to reproduce a lost Diyāb performance.
