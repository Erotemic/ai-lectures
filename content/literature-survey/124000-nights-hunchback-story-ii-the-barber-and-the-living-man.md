# The Hunchback Cycle — Story II: The Barber, Six Brothers, and the Living Man

Second and concluding narration, following the tailor's explanation, the barber's six distinct brother-stories, and the return to the fishbone in John Payne's 1882 translation.

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

The tailor is standing before the sultan, trying to save himself and three other men from a death sentence. He has promised a story more surprising than the apparent death of the royal entertainer. It begins with a young man in Baghdad whose face, dress, and manners should have secured him an easy welcome wherever he went. Instead he had a pronounced limp, and he was unwilling to be in a room with a barber. The tailor had learned his story at a dinner party, and now he repeats it at court.

The young man had once seen a beautiful woman at a window. His wish to meet her became so consuming that an intermediary arranged a private visit when the woman's household would be away. He needed only to prepare himself and go at the appointed hour. For his preparations he summoned a barber. The man arrived with instruments, good advice, calculations about the weather, opinions on every profession, and a conviction that his advice ought to govern his customers' lives. He praised his own silence so lavishly that the young man wondered whether he would ever be permitted to leave his chair.

The barber would not complete the shaving without first telling the youth what he should do that day. He questioned the young man's plans and decided there must be a secret appointment. When the youth became agitated, the barber became certain his own intervention would be helpful. The young man escaped at last and hurried to the house of the woman. The barber followed, watched, and made a public alarm. The chance of a quiet visit collapsed into scandal. The young man tried to flee and was injured in the confusion, leaving him lame. There was little in the affair to support the barber's claim of wisdom. A man who considered his incessant talk a public benefit had ruined the very opportunity he was supposed to help prepare.

The tailor tells the sultan that the offended young man would rather leave the gathering than sit beside the barber. The barber, who was also a guest that day, had indignantly denied the accusation. He called himself the Silent One and offered proof of his remarkable restraint: a story of himself and his six brothers. The tailor and the other dinner guests had been compelled to hear that entire account before they could resume their meal. Now the tailor reports what the barber said, hoping that even a story told secondhand will be remarkable enough to save the four people waiting for sentence.

He claims that once he was dragged before the caliph in the company of criminals. Convinced that the group was on its way to a feast, he had joined them without understanding their danger. Even as execution threatened, he managed to make his behavior proof of his extraordinary restraint. The irony is nearly perfect: to prove he can be silent, he must deliver a lengthy account of his silence. He introduces his brothers one by one, describing misfortunes that he presents as proof that he alone of the family possesses discretion.

The first brother was a tailor. A wealthy woman looked down at him from a window, praised his skill and persuaded him to work for her. She had him make clothing without payment, then drew him into further tasks through hints of affection. At last her household's promises led to a marriage arrangement that was no marriage at all. Humiliated, exploited, and forced into a grotesque labor that injured him, the tailor found that the promise of favor from the rich had been another way to take his work for nothing. The barber tells the tale as if the brother's gullibility explains the cruelty directed against him.

The second brother was tempted by a woman who appeared to welcome him. To gain the affection he imagined was waiting, he agreed to one small humiliation after another. His facial hair was altered, his appearance made ridiculous, and the laughter around him increased as his judgment weakened. He thought each new demand must be the last obstacle before a desired reward. Each surrender merely made the next more likely. The story ends with the brother discredited and injured, while the people who led him on have learned how easy it is to make longing obey a command.

The third brother was blind. He lived among companions who had some small savings. A dishonest man took advantage of the trust and private arrangements by which the blind men protected what they had. When the intruder was discovered, he manipulated the authorities with a story that made the victims seem guilty. The men who most needed the law to protect them found that a plausible lie, delivered by someone with power to observe and accuse, could overturn everything they had carefully kept. The barber recounts their humiliation with relish. We are not obliged to share his amusement.

The fourth brother was a butcher whose trade depended on people trusting the weight and quality of his meat. A stranger repeatedly paid with coins that proved not to be coins at all. When the butcher confronted the cheat, the man's tricks made the butcher himself look monstrous and criminal to the crowd. He lost his livelihood and suffered violence. The story turns on a frightening reversal: the person cheated becomes the person publicly accused. In the barber's telling, every attempt to restore justice comes too late, after the deceiver has prepared the next appearance.

The fifth brother sold glass, and his entire fortune lay before him on a tray. As he waited for customers, he imagined the money he would earn from his wares, then the larger trade he would establish, then the wife and household he would command. In his fantasy he became so grand that he kicked out in contempt at an imagined figure. His foot struck the real tray. The glass shattered, and his whole imagined prosperity disappeared along with his actual capital. Yet that is only the first disaster. Another opportunity offered to him proves to be a cruel entrapment. A woman's apparent help leads him into a house where men attack him and leave him among the dead. He survives through desperation rather than the wealth he had so easily envisioned.

The sixth brother, poor and hungry, visited a rich man famous for his jokes. The host pretended to present a feast, gesturing toward invisible dishes and ordering invisible servants to bring invisible food. The guest was expected to behave as though he could eat, drink and enjoy it. Instead of exposing the cruelty at once, he performed the part brilliantly. He praised the meal and pretended to grow drunk on wine that had never been poured. Then he acted out the recklessness of drunkenness, striking the host himself. The rich man finally laughed with genuine appreciation and gave him real food. The brother's quick wit won him a place in the household, but the fortune did not last. After the patron's death, he too was robbed and damaged by people stronger than himself.

Six brothers, six sets of wounds. In the tale the tailor repeats, the barber describes exploitative courtship, deception in trade, unequal justice, hunger, and the arrogance of the wealthy. Yet he offers them chiefly as evidence of his superiority. He seems unable to hear the sorrow in what he tells. The sultan delights in the tailor's report and asks for the barber to be fetched. When the old man arrives, the king has all the confessions and nested accounts summarized for him. The barber listens and insists that his great wisdom has been misrepresented by people who call him talkative.

At last the barber turns to the entertainer's body. He asks to inspect it. The court has already heard four confessions and more stories within them; there is no imaginable escape from a fishbone hidden inside a throat. But the barber knows something the confessors did not. Looking closely, he declares that the entertainer is not dead. He takes out a case of instruments, applies a treatment to the neck, and uses his tweezers to pull the obstructing bone from the throat. The man coughs and sneezes and rises alive. The court erupts in astonishment.

The tailor's wife had believed she caused a death. The physician had believed he caused it. The kitchen official had believed he caused it. The broker had believed he caused it. A judicial procession had nearly killed four people for a crime that none of them had committed. The one person they all described as a corpse had been unable to speak for himself. Now, with the piece of fish removed, the performer returns to life and to the ruler who had feared losing him.

The sultan orders the chain of events recorded, not in the private pages of a doctor but among the memorable wonders of his court. He pardons the accused, rewards the guests whose stories have entertained him, gives the tailor an appointment, and takes the barber into royal service. The hunchbacked entertainer receives honors as well. The barber's instruments have saved the performer, and his stories have helped preserve the condemned. But the narrator lets us see another possibility: had anyone asked the right question earlier, or possessed the necessary knowledge, the gallows would never have been built.

Outside the story, Shahrazad is still speaking to a different ruler. Her own audience will decide, at dawn, whether to let her continue. The Hunchback cycle has placed a story inside a story, then another story inside that, until the proposed execution has been postponed by listening. Its last joke is gentle only in comparison with everything that preceded it. The man thought dead is alive, while the people who were ready to kill in his name are left with their lives and a tale to tell.
