# The Poetic Edda — Analysis: The Voices in the Manuscript

Literary and textual analysis of the anonymous Eddic poems, with attention to genre, the Codex Regius and its gaps, variants, Christian-era preservation, and the ethical complexity of the Sigurðr and Guðrún cycles.

Suppose a modern reader asks for the plot of the *Poetic Edda*. It is a reasonable request if a book has been placed in their hands, and it is also a trap. The volume contains a prophecy about the end of the gods, practical advice from a voice associated with Odin, a comic account of Thor pretending to be a bride, riddling contests, love stories, heroic laments, and desperate revenge. These works do not all belong to one narrative sequence. Nor do we know them as a book composed at one moment by one poet. To understand their importance we first have to distinguish the thing medieval people made from the thing modern publishers sell under a convenient title.

The principal witness is an Icelandic manuscript commonly called the Codex Regius of the *Poetic Edda*, written in the thirteenth century, long after Iceland had become formally Christian. It preserves a sequence of mythological poems followed by heroic poems, and it contains prose notes that help connect episodes in the latter. Some poems of Eddic character survive only in other medieval manuscripts. The modern category *Poetic Edda* is thus a way of discussing a group of related works, not the name of a single pagan holy book carried unaltered through the centuries. No exact original moment of composition can be assigned to the collection as a whole. Individual poems have their own histories, and those histories are often disputed.

The manuscript's Christian setting is a crucial fact, but it should not be made to do more work than it can. Christian scribes and readers preserved poetic material concerning gods their church did not worship. Some poems may contain much older traditional material. Others may show influence from Christian or other medieval literary ideas. In *Völuspá*, the vision of a devastated world rising again has invited comparison with Christian apocalypse and renewal. That comparison is worth making; it is not permission to label every hopeful image a Christian interpolation or every frightening image pure pre-Christian belief. The surviving poem is a literary witness. We must read the witness before guessing the world that stood behind it.

*Völuspá* is particularly instructive because its form is already a theory of knowledge. A seeress speaks not simply about what happens but about what can be known by a mortal or divine listener. Odin is powerful and restless for information. The seeress commands attention because her knowledge reaches into the past and the future, across the very death of the gods. A modern retelling often converts the prophecy into a neat chain of causes and effects: first this, then that, then the great battle, then a new world. The poem's actual experience is more discontinuous. It moves through huge remembered and foreseen images, assuming that parts of their meaning are already charged for the audience. Its omissions are part of its force.

That form also complicates our familiar question about whether the poem is optimistic. The world returns, but the return is told by the same voice that has just demonstrated the fragility of every settlement. The audience has been taught to distrust permanence. We cannot know from the surviving poem exactly what every medieval listener took the final vision to mean. We can say that the poem refuses to grant the gods a privileged escape from time. Their deaths are not a temporary inconvenience. A cosmic order can be magnificent, and still be destined to pass.

The wedding comedy *Þrymskviða* appears to belong to another universe. Its action is exceptionally compact: a stolen hammer, an impossible ransom, an angry goddess, a disguise, an extravagant feast, and a moment when the stolen weapon is put within its owner's reach. Yet the poem depends on some of the same vulnerabilities the seeress reveals. Thor's immense strength is useless while the hammer is hidden. The gods must improvise, consult, and make a show of something they are not. A powerful god risks social humiliation to secure a tool essential to divine defense. The poem's humor is made from a threat that remains serious throughout the joke.

It is easy to call the disguise a story about gender and stop there. But the machinery is more precise. Freyja refuses to be transferred as a bride because someone else has lost property. Thor fears that the costume will make him appear less manly. Loki invents explanations for the appetite and eyes that plainly do not fit Thrym's expectations. The giant's willingness to believe those explanations is part of his defeat. The poem exposes how much a social role is made of costume, ritual, and others' readiness to interpret what they see. That is a literary observation; it does not authorize us to assign a single modern theory of identity to the poem's anonymous maker.

Now set the wisdom of *Hávamál* beside the comedy. Both are interested in appearances and survival among other people. The guest who enters a hall must notice what is happening; the drinker must recognize how easily confidence becomes foolishness; the man who seeks power must guard what he says. A substantial portion of the poem is not otherworldly at all. It is advice about social life in a dangerous environment. Yet its speaker also recounts the acquisition of poetic mead and an ordeal in which knowledge of runes comes from hanging wounded on a tree. Practical wisdom and uncanny sacrifice sit beside each other without a neat modern boundary between secular manners and sacred knowledge.

The poem is not a reliable set of uniform ethical commandments. Its advice has contradictions of mood and purpose. Some stanzas praise loyalty and generous hospitality. Others offer suspicion, self-protection, or hostile generalizations about women. A careful teacher should not erase the hostile passages or attribute them casually to the whole of Norse society. Nor is every sentence a universal proverb endorsed by an identifiable historical Odin-worshipper. A composite poem gives us a range of poetic voices and social assumptions. To read it critically is to ask who is being instructed, what risks the speaker imagines, and what the advice permits its hearers to do to other people.

The heroic poems change the focus once more. At first the Sigurðr story looks like an adventure about inherited weapons and a dragon's hoard. In *Reginsmál* and *Fáfnismál*, the man who kills the monster discovers that the men who explain the task have their own debts and appetites. The curse of the gold gives the treasure a mythical danger, but the family's crimes are comprehensible without supernatural machinery. Fáfnir kills his father. Regin prepares to betray his pupil. Sigurðr survives through both physical courage and an accidental new capacity to understand birds. The most interesting part of slaying the dragon may be what follows: he learns that his teacher is more dangerous than the monster whose death was supposed to make everyone safe.

The story is then fractured by a physical loss in the Codex Regius. The missing leaf or leaves interrupt the heroic material near *Sigrdrífumál*, and the history of Brynhildr and Sigurðr must be reconstructed from poems with variant accounts and from related later writings. A responsible overview can explain the main betrayal without pretending that there was once a neatly unified version whose exact words we know. When a witness is damaged, our uncertainty is not an annoying detail to be swept into footnotes. It changes how certain we can be about what a character knew, promised, or did.

There is also a temptation to make Sigurðr the sole center of the heroic tradition because killing a dragon is easy to advertise. But in many of the poems the emotional center lies with Brynhildr and Guðrún. Consider *Guðrúnarkviða I*. Its drama is almost the opposite of the dragon killing. At first nothing happens except that a widow cannot weep. Other women tell her what they have suffered. They cannot unlock her grief through argument or by offering examples of worse loss. The speech does not restore the dead hero. It makes us hear a consciousness approaching a pain for which social rituals provide no immediate cure.

Brynhildr's position is no less complex. She is connected with a deception by which another man has crossed the boundaries of her marriage. Her fury becomes destructive, and the poems do not require us to admire every act she demands. Yet they also allow her to argue and to speak after death. To describe her merely as a jealous rival is to erase the problem that makes the rivalry possible: the actions of men who arrange status and alliance while treating a woman's knowledge of what happened to her as expendable. These poems are capable of giving opposing persons their own moral gravity. They are not thereby excusing murder.

The tragedy's continuation is especially instructive beside the *Nibelungenlied*, another medieval work already treated in our library. It is easy to recognize the names: Sigurðr and Siegfried, Guðrún and Kriemhild, Gunnar and Gunther, Atli and Etzel. But recognition is not identity. In the northern *Atlakviða*, Guðrún avenges her brothers after her husband Atli has had them murdered. In the Middle High German poem, Kriemhild engineers a catastrophe to take vengeance on her own brothers for the death of her husband Siegfried. That is not a trivial difference in spelling or scene order. It turns the direction of the story's moral obligations almost inside out.

*Atlakviða* also refuses the comfort of a cleanly righteous avenger. Guðrún kills her children by Atli and forces him to face what he has eaten. She destroys the hall. We can understand why she hates Atli without blessing all the forms her hatred takes. A society can make kinship sacred and thereby create obligations so fierce that people justify the destruction of other kin. The heroic code is neither a universal law of human behavior nor a convenient explanation that removes responsibility. It is a set of competing claims enacted under intolerable pressure. The poems let us hear the pressure as violence and lament, not only as bravery.

These differences in genre also affect the later cultural life of the poems. Modern fantasy often takes from the Edda a set of named gods, monsters, weapons, and end-times images. Opera and modern retellings take dragon-slaying, cursed treasure, and heroic love. Both are legitimate acts of reception if their sources are acknowledged. Neither should be mistaken for a faithful transcript of the medieval collection. A modern story assembled from the *Poetic Edda*, the *Prose Edda*, the *Völsunga saga*, and the *Nibelungenlied* can be artistically powerful. It is still an assembly. The consequences of borrowing become especially important when later authors erase the distinctions among women whose roles are fundamentally different in the older witnesses.

There is a more generous way to understand the collection's lack of a single plot. The *Poetic Edda* has survived because disparate poetic forms were preserved close enough to be read together. Prophecy can tell of a world's limits. Comedy can display the ridiculous ways power depends on cooperation. Counsel can ask what a person needs to live among strangers. Lament can ask what happens after action has ended and the body is still there. Heroic revenge can ask whether violence offered as duty can ever stop reproducing itself. None of these forms can do all the others' work. Their differences make the collection larger than any plot that might be imposed on it.

Our reading should therefore keep two commitments at once. We may follow connected heroes far enough to care what becomes of them, and we should also be willing to stop and say that a different surviving poem tells the event differently. We may speak of ancient myth and legendary history, but not as though a thirteenth-century Icelandic manuscript were a contemporary report of a pagan world. We may enjoy the stories without pretending that enjoyment requires belief, and we may criticize the stories without requiring medieval poets to share our assumptions. What is most valuable about the *Poetic Edda* is not that it offers a complete code for life. It preserves several ways of making human and divine limits audible, and lets the differences among them remain.

## Critical and bibliographic companion (not spoken)

- **Primary texts:** Henry Adams Bellows, trans., *The Poetic Edda* (1923), [Project Gutenberg text](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/73533). Particularly *Völuspá*, *Þrymskviða*, *Hávamál*, *Reginsmál*, *Fáfnismál*, *Sigrdrífumál*, *Brot af Sigurðarkviðu*, *Guðrúnarkviða I*, *Sigurðarkviða in skamma*, *Atlakviða*, *Atlamál*, and *Hamðismál*. Bellows's historical and compositional hypotheses are dated and are not silently treated as modern consensus.
- **Modern critical control:** Edward Pettit, [*The Poetic Edda: A Dual-Language Edition*](https://www.openbookpublishers.com/books/10.11647/obp.0308) (Open Book Publishers, 2023). Publication includes poem-specific manuscript evidence and commentary. Comparative reading of the heroic story: [*Nibelungenlied* English introduction](https://www.cyberlibrary.org/en/books/the-nibelungenlied-revised-edition-38468/chapters/1/). Orientations: [Wikipedia: *Poetic Edda*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poetic_Edda), [Wikipedia: *Völuspá*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/V%C3%B6lusp%C3%A1), [Wikipedia: *Hávamál*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/H%C3%A1vam%C3%A1l), [Wikipedia: *Þrymskviða*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%C3%9Erymskvi%C3%B0a).
- **Interpretive boundary:** The manuscript date is not a composition date for each poem; Christian mediation is not itself evidence that every mythic image was invented by Christian scribes. The conflict between Guðrún's northern revenge on Atli and Kriemhild's German revenge on her brothers is substantial and must not be harmonized. Genre, gender roles, audience, and ethics are discussed as literary interpretations, not as universal prehistoric Nordic social facts. We have narrated representative poems and a source-traced heroic arc, not every Eddic poem.
