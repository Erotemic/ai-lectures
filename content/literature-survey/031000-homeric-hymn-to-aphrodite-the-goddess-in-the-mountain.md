# The Homeric Hymn to Aphrodite: The Goddess on the Mountain

An original scene-first narration of the long *Homeric Hymn to Aphrodite* (Hymn 5, lines 1–293). Based on the 1914 H. G. Evelyn-White public-domain translation. The hymn's Anchises and newborn Aeneas belong to an early Greek narrative distinct from Virgil's later *Aeneid*.

A goddess has spent her life making other people fall in love. She can unsettle gods who believe themselves beyond desire, and mortals who cannot afford the distraction. Her power has made her proud. Then Zeus decides that she, too, will discover what it is to want someone whose life she cannot protect from time.

The goddess is Aphrodite. The mortal is Anchises, a young man tending cattle on Mount Ida, near Troy. The *Homeric Hymn to Aphrodite* follows a meeting between them, but it begins much farther away, in the ordering of divine power. The poem is not only a love story. It is a contest over who can make whom vulnerable, and a meditation on what an immortal must accept when love crosses into a mortal life.

At the beginning the singer praises Aphrodite's influence over creatures of earth, sea, and air. The animals mate under her pressure. Human beings seek one another. Most gods are susceptible to her power. She delights in describing the pairings she has contrived between immortals and mortals, the embarrassments and children that follow. Even a god accustomed to ruling others can become subject to desire.

The poem makes three important exceptions. Athena does not yield to Aphrodite's persuasion; she belongs to warcraft, skill, and another kind of authority. Artemis, devoted to hunting and the wild, likewise remains outside Aphrodite's reach. Hestia, keeper of the hearth, has refused marriage despite the approaches of Apollo and Poseidon, and Zeus has granted her a lasting position in the households of gods and humans. These exceptions prevent the hymn from claiming that one divine power abolishes all the others. Aphrodite's reach is enormous, but it has boundaries.

Zeus has allowed Aphrodite to make him desire mortal women. Such relationships produce stories that his fellow gods may mock or admire. Now he turns the device back upon its wielder. He makes Aphrodite fall in love with Anchises. The hymn does not imagine a spontaneous romance between equal strangers. It begins with Zeus exercising his own authority over the desire that Aphrodite normally governs.

Aphrodite sees Anchises among the cattle on Ida and feels longing. The location is important. This is not a meeting in a royal hall, or at the market of a busy city. The mountain offers a world of grazing animals, rough paths, and a life lived under the open sky. Anchises belongs to the Trojan landscape, though the goddess arrives from a very different home.

She goes first to her sanctuary at Paphos in Cyprus. There the Graces tend her, washing, dressing, and adorning her in garments and ornaments suited to a goddess. She then travels across the sea to the mountains. Wild animals follow in her train. The wolves and lions, bears and leopards ordinarily driven by hunger or danger, come under her transforming attraction. The hymn shows desire moving through living nature before Aphrodite meets the man who is to become its human focus.

Anchises is apart from his companions, who have gone elsewhere with the herds. He sees a woman of extraordinary beauty standing before him. She is a goddess, but she has arranged her appearance to suggest a noble mortal bride. He is astonished. Rather than immediately reaching for her, he wonders whether he is looking at Artemis, Leto, Aphrodite, Themis, Athena, or one of the nymphs who belong to the mountain. If she is immortal, he offers worship and a place for an altar. The first thing her beauty produces is not confident possession but uncertainty about her status.

Aphrodite denies that she is a goddess. She says she is a mortal woman from Phrygia, daughter of the king Otreus, and tells a story of how Hermes brought her to this place. According to her account she was taken from the company of young women celebrating Artemis. Hermes promised that she would marry Anchises and be the mother of noble children. She describes her parents and homeland in enough detail to make her story sound like the ordinary negotiation of a distinguished marriage. Even as the goddess of love she relies upon a tale about family, origin, and social legitimacy to obtain what she wants.

Anchises hears her and is overwhelmed. He knows there may be danger in an encounter so sudden and so splendid. Yet his longing wins. He declares that he wants to be with her, even if death should follow. The choice feels romantic in the moment. The poem has already taught us that the difference between divine and mortal bodies makes such declarations perilous. The mortal can promise his life because it is his to lose. The goddess's life cannot end in the same way.

They enter his dwelling. The room is not a glittering palace. There is a bed, coverings, the practical things a prosperous herdsman might possess. The poet describes Aphrodite casting aside her elaborate garments, and the two become lovers. The scene is narrated in a way that also acknowledges Zeus's strange punishment. For once Aphrodite is the one caught by overwhelming desire. She has entered the circumstances she was accustomed to arranging for others.

After their union, she puts on her divine clothing and stands before Anchises in the fullness of her power. She wakes him and demands that he look at her. The illusion of a mortal bride falls away. Anchises is terrified. He remembers warnings that a human who shares a bed with a goddess may not escape unharmed. He begs her not to leave him weakened or destroyed. His earlier willingness to die sounds very different after he learns who stood beside him.

Aphrodite reassures him that he will not be destroyed simply for having been her lover. Yet she does not promise to make him immortal. To explain why, she tells two stories from the divine family, and each turns upon the problem of a mortal's body.

The first concerns Ganymede, the beautiful young son of the Trojan house whom Zeus carried away to live among the gods and serve as their cupbearer. His family grieved when he disappeared, and the story links that loss with divine compensation and a place beyond ordinary human aging. But the success of Ganymede's translation to heaven is not a solution that can simply be repeated for Anchises. The gods' choices are not interchangeable gifts from a common store.

The second story is more frightening. Eos, goddess of the dawn, loved a mortal named Tithonus and asked Zeus to make him immortal. The request was granted, but it failed to include eternal youth. Tithonus could not die, yet he grew old. His strength and beauty disappeared; eventually he was confined as age took away the qualities that had first drawn the goddess to him. Immortality without release from aging became a cruel condition rather than a prize.

The hymn does not complete Tithonus's fate with the later familiar detail that he turned into a cicada. That belongs to other versions of the story. Here the image that matters is someone who cannot end and cannot remain young. The morning goddess's love did not give her the knowledge to ask for a whole human future.

Aphrodite turns back to the man before her. If Anchises could remain always as he is at this moment, she would gladly call him her husband. But his body will age. Mortal decline will approach him, and even the gods dread that kind of limit. She cannot bear to watch him become what he must become. The difference between them is not an obstacle removed by desire. It is the condition that shapes the ending of their meeting.

Then she reveals that she will bear their child. The boy will be named Aeneas. She connects his name with her own grief or distress at having lain with a mortal man; this is the hymn's ancient etymological explanation, not a proof about how the name arose historically. Aeneas will be raised for his earliest years by mountain nymphs. These beings occupy a state between the purely immortal and the ordinary human: they live for an immense span, and their lives are tied to the growth and eventual falling of great trees. When the child is older, the goddess will bring him back to Anchises.

The promise matters because Aphrodite cannot take the place of a mortal wife and stay through every season of a human household. The child will live between the worlds. His mother is a goddess; his father is a man of the Trojan land. Nymphs will nurse him; then he will be delivered to his father to take his place among mortals. The poem looks forward toward a line of human descendants who carry a trace of divine contact without escaping human fate.

Aphrodite gives Anchises instructions about what to say when people ask who bore the child. He should say that the mother was a mountain nymph. He must not boast that Aphrodite herself slept with him. If he names her in pride, Zeus will punish him with a thunderbolt. The last words of their encounter therefore concern speech and secrecy. The power that brought the goddess to the mountain does not allow her to make the relationship a public triumph.

There is a striking contrast between the goddess's false claim when she first arrives and the lie she now asks Anchises to tell. At first deception opens the door to intimacy. At the end concealment protects a divine boundary. Love has not made open honesty possible. Their meeting can shape a child's future, but it cannot become the ordinary story of a couple living together openly in one place.

The singer closes by bidding Aphrodite farewell and turning toward another song. That formula recalls the purpose of a hymn: the poem is an act of praise. Yet its heroine has been humbled. She retains great beauty and enormous power, but she has learned through the same vulnerability she imposes on others. Even the goddess who awakens love cannot remove the passage of time from the mortal she loves.

This story also matters for another work in the library, Virgil's *Aeneid*. In that later Roman epic, Aeneas carries his father Anchises out of burning Troy and searches for a new home. We can now see why his parentage carries so much force in the earlier Greek tradition. His mother is not simply a name in a family tree. She is the goddess who discovered that her power over desire stops short of the power to save every life it touches. His father is the mortal who could be loved by a goddess but could not share her kind of time.

On Ida, the mountain remains. The herds return. Anchises must go on living with the knowledge of what happened, with a warning about what must not be said, and with a promise that a child will one day come down from the heights. Aphrodite departs into the sky. The hymn leaves behind an image of love that has changed both participants without making them equal, and of a future that will belong to the child rather than to the impossible marriage of his parents.

## Textual basis and source notes (not spoken)

- Anonymous *Homeric Hymn to Aphrodite*, hymn 5, lines 1–293. English witness H. G. Evelyn-White (1914), accessible in the index-linked Greek/English text.
- Lines 1–52: reach of Aphrodite, exceptions Athena/Artemis/Hestia, Zeus's counterplot; 53–104: Aphrodite's adornment and arrival on Ida; 105–166: disguised claims and union with Anchises; 167–214: revelation, fear and Ganymede; 215–251: Tithonus and mortal old age; 252–293: Aeneas, nymphs, secrecy, and warning.
- The hymn offers a **poetic folk etymology** of Aeneas's name; do not report it as established comparative linguistics. The cicada ending for Tithonus belongs to later or different witnesses, not this received hymn.
- The Greek poem is not Virgil's Roman *Aeneid*, and its portrayal of Aeneas's prospective future should not be silently harmonized with the later epic.
