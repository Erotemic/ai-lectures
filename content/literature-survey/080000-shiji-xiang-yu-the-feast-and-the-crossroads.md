# Shiji II: Xiang Yu, the Fall of Qin, and the Feast at Hongmen

A narrative reading of *Shiji* chapter 7, with chapter 8 for the rise of Liu Bang. This is the first half of an extended account of the Chu–Han struggle.

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

The young Xiang Yu was being instructed in the things that were supposed to make a man of his class accomplished. He showed little patience for learning to write, and when offered swordsmanship he tired of that too. According to Sima Qian, he told his uncle that writing was good only for keeping track of a name, and swordplay could teach a man to fight one opponent. He wanted to learn how to fight ten thousand. The uncle began teaching military methods, but the young man did not finish that course either. This portrait is too sharply patterned to treat as an unrecorded childhood conversation made suddenly certain by its presence in a famous book. It is Sima Qian's way of introducing a formidable capacity tied to an equally formidable impatience. Xiang Yu will be a military leader able to break armies. Whether he can build a government after his victories is another question.

Xiang Yu grows up under Qin rule, a world created by conquest and disciplined command. The Qin emperor has claimed an authority that earlier kings could only imagine; his regime draws roads, taxes, soldiers, labor, and laws into an increasingly integrated state. It is also hated in many regions that have lost their independence. Xiang Yu belongs to a family with ties to the old state of Chu. When the emperor dies and rebellions spread, his uncle Xiang Liang becomes involved in the restorationist struggle. Liu Bang, meanwhile, rises from a very different background. He is a local official who becomes an insurgent leader, one of many men moving within a rapidly changing field of alliances. Later imperial memory will make him the founder of the Han dynasty. Sima Qian places Liu and Xiang within interlocking stories so that the reader sees more than the victory of one preordained hero.

At first the anti-Qin forces cannot settle who is in charge. The Chu cause claims a king descended from the old royal line, but war is fought by generals whose authority comes from armed men and successful campaigns. Xiang Liang's death puts greater pressure on his nephew. At Julu, where Qin forces surround a rebel army, Xiang Yu makes his decisive reputation. The account famously describes his men crossing a river, sinking the boats, and destroying the means by which they might retreat. The soldiers must win, because defeat leaves them nowhere to go. There is a stark theatrical logic to this act. It makes commitment physically irreversible. Xiang Yu's attacks help break a major Qin army, and other generals recognize that his force now commands fear and respect. In the telling, military prestige arrives in one magnificent moment; political problems do not vanish with it.

The competing advance on Qin's heartland produces a dangerous difference. Liu Bang reaches the capital region before Xiang Yu. The last Qin ruler submits, and the old dynasty's government appears for an instant as an object that a new leader might take intact. Advisers urge Liu to protect the people, avoid unnecessary plunder, and treat the conquered territory as the foundation of a durable order. He withdraws beyond the passes to await the larger anti-Qin coalition. Xiang Yu comes with a stronger army and is furious that a rival has entered first. He also learns that Liu's people may have intended to hold the passes against him. This creates the scene that has become one of the most famous banquets in Chinese historical literature: the meeting at Hongmen.

Before the banquet, an intricate web of personal ties interrupts the apparent simplicity of two opposing armies. Xiang Bo, related to Xiang Yu, has an old connection with Liu Bang's adviser Zhang Liang. He rides at night to warn Zhang Liang of the danger. Zhang Liang will not abandon his lord; instead he tells Liu. Liu meets Xiang Bo, offers courtesy and kinship, and makes assurances about his intentions. The rival camps are not impersonal blocks. Family, hospitality, and private obligations thread across the military line. Such connections can save a life, but they also make it difficult to know where loyalty lies.

Liu Bang goes to Xiang Yu's camp accompanied by a relatively small group and explains that men spreading malicious reports have set the allies against one another. Xiang Yu receives him at a feast. The seating is recorded carefully, because rank and direction tell us who is publicly honored. Fan Zeng, Xiang Yu's adviser, believes Liu must be eliminated before he grows stronger. He sends repeated signals to his commander, but Xiang Yu does not act. Another man proposes a sword dance within the hall, using the movement of entertainment to create an opening for murder. Xiang Bo takes up a sword too and protects Liu's body as they dance. The danger does not arrive as a formal declaration. It moves within the rules of a celebration, concealed by a performance all the guests must pretend to understand as harmless.

Liu Bang's warrior attendant Fan Kuai enters with terrifying boldness. He speaks against the proposed killing, presents his master's achievements as service to the common cause, and exposes how ruinous it would be to punish the first man who took the Qin capital. Xiang Yu, struck by his courage, gives him meat and drink. The audience can sense the battle being fought in speech and seating while armies remain outside. Liu Bang eventually withdraws on a pretext and escapes to his own camp. He leaves gifts behind. The attempt to trap him has failed, not because the plot was too complicated, but because Xiang Yu could not or would not settle on a choice at the decisive moment. Fan Zeng is furious. Sima Qian makes the banquet a pivot: the stronger general has allowed a political rival to leave the room alive.

It is tempting to read every scene as proof that Xiang Yu was incapable of strategic thought. That goes too far. He is operating among old promises, new claims, and allies he cannot simply execute without consequence. Sima Qian nevertheless shapes the narrative around a contrast. Liu Bang is often willing to accept advice, endure humiliation, and exchange immediate advantage for time. Xiang Yu is brilliant and forceful, but he tends to rely on personal command and emotional certainty. Those are themes of the book, not universally agreed psychological findings about two men who lived more than two thousand years ago.

The fall of Qin, it turns out, has not ended the struggle. Xiang Yu presides over a settlement that divides conquered territories among rival rulers. Liu Bang receives a western kingdom and the designation King of Han; others are sent to territories they do not want, or are denied what they believe they deserve. Xiang Yu returns east rather than making the Qin heartland his own administrative center. The partition makes discontent into a system. Every disappointed general and every displaced king carries the possibility of a new rebellion. A victorious army can assign titles, but titles alone cannot give every person a reason to accept them.

This first part of the story ends, then, without the peaceful succession that a reader might expect after the overthrow of a dynasty. Qin has gone, but the war has merely changed its language. Before, rebels could say that they were fighting an oppressive emperor. Now each must say what kind of order will replace him and why others should submit. Xiang Yu's great victories have given him a position above his competitors. At Hongmen he let one of those competitors escape. We will see, in the remainder of the account, why a general's decision at a feast could matter more to the future dynasty than a triumph on the battlefield.
