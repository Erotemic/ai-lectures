# Les Misérables — Complete Story: The Candlesticks and the Barricade

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

A traveler reaches a French town in 1815 with a yellow passport. He has walked a long way. At one inn the proprietor looks at the passport and refuses him a bed. At another he is turned out again. When he asks for food, people see the document before they see the man. Its color announces that he is a released convict, and he discovers that freedom can be another kind of sentence. Jean Valjean has spent nineteen years imprisoned after stealing bread for his sister's hungry children and making repeated attempts to escape. He is forty-six, strong, exhausted, and furious at a world that has demanded payment long after the original crime. By nightfall he is ready to believe that every human door is closed. Then someone directs him to the residence of the bishop of Digne.

Bishop Myriel opens the door. There is no guard and no interrogation. The bishop lives modestly, with his sister and a housekeeper. He seats the stranger at his table, speaks to him as a guest, and offers him a bed. Valjean has never been treated so kindly by someone who knows his history. During the night he rises, takes the bishop's silver plates, and escapes. The police catch him and drag him back. The bishop could tell them that they have found a thief. Instead he says that he gave Valjean the silver, and he adds the silver candlesticks that the fugitive had left behind. The officers depart. The bishop tells Valjean that this gift is meant for a new life. No supernatural light is required. The scene is remarkable because one person who could use the law to punish has declined to do so. But the gesture does not instantly transform Valjean into a saint. On the road he takes a coin from a young chimney sweep, Petit Gervais, then recognizes too late what he has done. He calls after the child in anguish. The man he might become is now visible to him, and so is the man he still is.

Victor Hugo's *Les Misérables*, published in 1862, follows what happens when that new possibility meets the institutions and habits of nineteenth-century France. The novel moves across years, households, convents, streets, courtrooms, and barricades. Its five large parts bear the names Fantine, Cosette, Marius, the Idyll in the Rue Plumet and the Epic in the Rue Saint-Denis, and Jean Valjean. We will follow their continuous story. The book also stops for extended reflections on religion, Waterloo, Parisian slang, urban sewage, history, and moral philosophy. Those diversions are not mistakes in the plot. For Hugo, a person's fate can never be separated from the society that made the conditions of that fate possible.

Several years after the bishop's act, an industrious mayor has made the northern town of Montreuil-sur-Mer prosperous. Monsieur Madeleine brings employment, supports hospitals, and does not seem interested in his own wealth. He has devised improvements in manufacturing and used the proceeds to raise the lives around him. His achievement is not only charity from above: jobs and wages matter. The town admires him, though his reserve troubles the police inspector Javert. Javert trusts fixed categories. Once a criminal, always a criminal. Once a legal authority, always entitled to obedience. This inspector was born in a prison, and he has made allegiance to the law his answer to the instability of his own beginnings. He finds Madeleine's goodness suspicious because it does not match the official biography of the man Javert suspects him to be. He is correct about one thing. Monsieur Madeleine is Jean Valjean.

In the same town, a young woman named Fantine is struggling to survive. Her story begins before Madeleine appears, among students in Paris who enjoy playing at romance. Fantine falls in love with Tholomyès, a young man who will treat the relationship as an amusement and leave without taking responsibility for their daughter. When she must find work, Fantine cannot easily bring a child with her. She leaves little Cosette with an innkeeping couple, the Thénardiers, believing that their own children show they know how to care for the girl. The parents are actually opportunists who see Cosette as unpaid labor and a source of payments from Fantine. Their letters demand more money for supposed needs. They dress their daughters nicely and put Cosette to work. Fantine, far away, imagines that the wages she sends are protecting her child.

At Madeleine's factory Fantine finds employment, then loses it when her unmarried motherhood becomes known. The mayor has delegated authority; he does not know what his forewoman has done until the ruin has advanced. Fantine sells her hair, then her front teeth. She moves from precarious labor toward prostitution as the Thénardiers' demands continue. Hugo describes her descent with anger at a society that announces moral rules to the poor without supplying the means to survive them. An incident in the street brings her before Javert, who is prepared to send her to prison. The mayor intervenes. He hears enough to understand that the woman has been punished for circumstances in which the powerful have escaped accountability. He promises to bring her daughter to her. Fantine is gravely ill; she clings to the promise as a reason to stay alive.

Before he can fulfill it, Madeleine faces a decision that threatens everything he has constructed. Another man, a poor wanderer named Champmathieu, has been arrested as the escaped convict Jean Valjean. Authorities take the resemblance and the testimony of former prisoners as proof. Madeleine can remain mayor by allowing an innocent stranger to carry his old identity and suffer its punishment. Nobody outside his conscience requires him to speak. He wrestles with what disclosure will cost the people who rely on his factory and his protection, and with the possibility that his own good works might justify his silence. But the bishop's gift has made him responsible for more than appearances. He goes to the court, declares himself Jean Valjean, and gives evidence only he could know. The accusation against Champmathieu collapses. Valjean has chosen truth at the price of returning to danger.

When he returns, the hospital room is no longer safe. Javert has come to arrest him. Fantine, hearing the inspector insist that her protector is a convict, is terrified. She learns that Cosette has not yet been brought back. The shock overwhelms her; she dies without seeing her daughter. Valjean is seized and imprisoned. This failure is central to the novel. Doing the right thing does not mean saving everyone in time. Fantine's life cannot be restored by a better moral explanation of events. The promise to her daughter survives the mother's death, and Valjean must now decide whether to make that promise the organizing duty of his freedom.

The story moves south toward the Mediterranean and then north again. Back on the galleys, Valjean finds a way to escape after rescuing a sailor during a dangerous maneuver; his apparent disappearance allows him to be treated as dead. He makes his way to Montfermeil, to the Thénardiers' inn, on a winter evening. Cosette is now a child accustomed to fear. She scrubs and sweeps while the innkeepers' daughters play. When the house needs water, she is sent into the dark forest with a bucket too heavy for her. She is afraid of the shadows and of the anger waiting at home. On the road back a stranger lifts the bucket and carries it for her. The stranger is Jean Valjean.

The scene seems almost too small for a novel of revolutions and armies: a man takes a child's load. Yet it is the promise made to Fantine translated into an action the child can feel. At the inn Valjean sees Cosette tremble, obey, and glance at a doll she is not permitted to touch. He purchases an extravagant doll for her and negotiates with the Thénardiers to take her away. They calculate the profit, change their terms, and attempt to extract more. Valjean pays, and in the end the child leaves with him. She has no clear conception of who this new guardian is. He has little experience of living with a child. Their bond begins not with explanations but with the astonishing fact that she can fall asleep without waiting for punishment.

They travel to Paris and live quietly. Cosette is learning affection; Valjean is learning what it means to be needed without being feared. But Javert's pursuit resumes. Valjean leads the girl through the city at night, over a wall, and into the protected enclosure of a convent. There he encounters Fauchelevent, a gardener whom Madeleine once rescued from beneath an overturned cart. Fauchelevent returns the earlier kindness by helping the fugitives find a place in the convent's life. Cosette receives schooling among the nuns. Valjean, working as a gardener, appreciates the quiet, though the convent is also an enclosure where people have withdrawn from the world's difficulties. He has found safety for the child, but the novel has not finished testing what safety will mean when she grows old enough to choose a life beyond him.

Now Hugo leads us toward a young man whose history appears at first to belong to a different book. Marius Pontmercy has been raised by his wealthy, royalist grandfather, Monsieur Gillenormand. His father was an officer in Napoleon's army, and family politics have kept father and son apart. When Marius learns after his father's death how much love had been denied him, he rejects his grandfather's convictions and moves toward a precarious independence. He is proud, sincere, politically eager, and sometimes foolish. He is also caught between inherited stories: the grandeur of Napoleon, the legitimacy claimed by kings, and the republican hopes of his own generation.

One of his father's last wishes has a curious consequence. Colonel Pontmercy believed that an innkeeper named Thénardier saved his life at Waterloo. In fact Thénardier was scavenging among the bodies and happened to pull the wounded officer free while looking for valuables. The grateful man misunderstood the rescue. Marius has been instructed to honor the innkeeper if he ever finds him. Thus the name of the couple who mistreated Cosette reaches him not as a warning but as a family obligation. Hugo's long account of Waterloo, which interrupts the novel at the beginning of its second part, is not only a history lesson. It establishes how accident is later converted into legend, and how an apparent moral debt can rest on a false account of events.

Marius, now living in modest lodgings, becomes involved with young republican students, the Friends of the ABC, who debate the future of France. Their leader Enjolras is devoted to an ideal larger than personal happiness; other members bring humor, scholarship, ardor, and ordinary disagreement to their meetings. Marius is not identical with them. His political imagination is still pulled toward Napoleon, and his feelings are just as capable of leading him away from public affairs as into them. A visit to the Luxembourg Garden changes his life. There he notices an older gentleman and a young woman who walk together. The young woman is Cosette, no longer the frightened child with a bucket. In successive encounters Marius falls in love with her, and Cosette gradually notices him too. They have hardly spoken. Their attachment begins with looks and the long, unnerving speculation over whether those looks mean anything.

Another household is nearer to Marius than he realizes. In the dismal Gorbeau tenement where he rents a room, his neighbors live under the name Jondrette. He discovers that the family is the Thénardiers, reduced to desperate frauds and schemes. Their elder daughter Éponine, once indulged at Cosette's expense, now lives in poverty. She has intelligence, bravado, and an unreturned love for Marius. Her loyalty is never innocent of manipulation. She helps him find Cosette's address, but she is also drawn to concealment and despair when he is about to belong to someone else. The man she loves remains largely unaware of the cost of her attachment.

One night Marius watches through a gap in the wall as Thénardier prepares to trap a wealthy visitor who has offered charity. The visitor is Valjean; the daughter accompanying him has reminded Marius of the girl from the garden. The Thénardiers plan extortion and violence. Marius could summon the police, but he hesitates because the father he reveres believed Thénardier his savior. The police arrive anyway, led by Javert. Valjean manages to escape before he can be recognized, and the criminal family is arrested. Marius has now encountered a knot of mistaken identities, criminal histories, and inherited obligations, without yet knowing how they connect. The man he seeks as a father-in-law has been both a convict and a mayor. The benefactor who rescued his beloved once stood at trial to save another man's life. Marius knows almost none of this.

For a time the lovers find happiness in a secluded garden in the Rue Plumet. Marius and Cosette meet at night, speak of their feelings, and build the intimate world that seems to exist outside politics. Valjean senses that Cosette is changing and experiences a fear he knows is selfish: he may lose the person whose presence has given his life a purpose. He considers leaving Paris. His tenderness for Cosette is real, but so is the temptation to keep her dependent on him. Hugo does not make fatherly love immune to possessiveness. Valjean's greatest generosity may be to allow the young woman the future he cannot share in the same way.

Events in Paris interrupt the private story. The political climate after the July Revolution of 1830 has disappointed those who hoped for a thorough transformation. The restored monarchy has fallen, but the new July Monarchy has not ended poverty, police power, or republican dissent. General Lamarque, admired by many opponents of the regime, dies during a cholera-stricken spring. His funeral in June 1832 becomes the occasion for an insurrection. The uprising in the novel is not the great Revolution of 1789 and not the revolution of 1848. It is the smaller, doomed June Rebellion, whose barricades will close around the friends Marius knows.

In the streets the students and their allies seize an opportunity, bring weapons and materials into place, and make a barricade near the wine shop of the Rue de la Chanvrerie. The city is no longer merely a place of gardens and quiet meetings. Ordinary pavement is torn up; furniture and carts become a fortification. The men speak as if history can be changed by a sufficient act of courage. Enjolras thinks in principles. Grantaire doubts nearly everything, yet remains attached to those who act. The child Gavroche, born into the Thénardier family but largely abandoned to the streets, moves among the fighters with jokes, reckless expertise, and an eye for practical danger. These lives are not interchangeable illustrations of heroism. Each carries a different relation to hope, poverty, and the risk of dying.

Marius comes to the barricade in despair. Cosette is apparently leaving Paris, and he imagines no future without her. The battle offers him a place to die that can be called meaningful. His despair is not the same as Enjolras's politics, though it leads him to the same stones. Éponine, disguised as a young man, has reached the barricade too. She has maneuvered to keep Marius near her, and when a soldier fires she puts herself in the way of the bullet. Dying in his arms, she confesses her love and gives him a letter from Cosette that she had withheld. There is no happy justice in this confession. Éponine's sacrifice is bound up with the jealousy that helped put Marius in danger, and the love she finally names will not be returned. Marius kisses her forehead as she asks, and she dies.

One of the insurgents has already captured a spy. It is Javert, who had entered the barricade to report to the authorities. The students bind him and plan to kill him when the fight can spare someone for the task. Valjean, meanwhile, has found the letter Marius sent to Cosette and learned where the young man is risking his life. The guardian who feared losing his daughter now goes to the place where her lover may be killed. He enters the fighting and helps the defenders. When he is offered the chance to execute the prisoner, he takes Javert away from the other men and quietly releases him, giving him an opportunity to escape. The act is as difficult for Javert to understand as the bishop's silver once was for Valjean. The law has demanded Valjean's body for years, and yet he refuses to take the life of its most relentless representative.

The barricade cannot hold indefinitely. The defenders are isolated, ammunition runs low, and the government's forces press forward. Gavroche goes into the street to gather cartridges from fallen soldiers and is shot. The death is unbearable not because the boy was safe before, but because his vitality has persuaded us for a moment that the city might be his playground rather than his executioner. One by one the defenders fall. Marius is wounded and collapses. Enjolras, at the end, faces the soldiers. Grantaire wakes from a drunken sleep and chooses to stand beside him; they are shot together. The rebellion is crushed. Hugo has given the insurgents individuality and eloquence, but he does not falsely invent a victory for them.

Valjean finds Marius unconscious and carries him away. Escape through the streets is impossible, so he descends into the Paris sewers. The novel changes from public battle to a desperate passage through mud, darkness, foul water, and narrow channels. Valjean carries a young man who may die and whose love for Cosette threatens to leave Valjean alone. He cannot know whether his effort will succeed. The sewers are more than a clever escape route: Hugo has spent pages considering how cities hide the consequences of their own prosperity and waste. Here the hidden city becomes the way one person may save another. At a locked exit Valjean encounters Thénardier, who believes he is seeing a criminal transporting a corpse. Thénardier helps him through for money and secretly tears a piece from Marius's coat, intending to use it as evidence for later blackmail. Even now he misreads the scene.

Outside waits Javert. The inspector has resumed his pursuit. Yet he has also been released by the very man he is appointed to catch. Valjean asks permission to take Marius to his grandfather's house, and Javert allows it. Then he lets Valjean return home briefly instead of arresting him on the spot. These are acts Javert cannot fit into the law he has made his universe. He does not know how to live with a criminal who is morally better than the law's account of him, or with an officer who has failed to obey his duty as he understands it. Alone that night, he writes a note proposing practical improvements to prison administration and then throws himself into the Seine. His final act is not an argument that the law and morality were never related. It is the tragedy of a man who could not survive their divergence.

Marius recovers slowly at his grandfather's house. For a time he does not know who carried him out of the barricade or how close he came to death. He and Cosette marry. The ceremony draws together a grandfather, a beloved young woman, a young man whose political passions have been tested, and an older guardian whose happiness depends on their happiness. Valjean provides Cosette with a substantial dowry while declining to make the celebration a monument to himself. He has come as far as the bishop's silver could carry him, and farther. Yet this is not the end of the story, because he believes he must tell Marius the truth about his past.

After the wedding Valjean confesses that he is a former convict, that the name he used was not his birth name, and that Cosette was not his biological daughter. He tries to explain without praising himself. Marius, who cannot yet place the confession alongside Valjean's conduct, concludes that a dangerous man has entered the family. He does not know the story of the bishop, of Champmathieu, of Fantine, or of the sewer. He begins to make Valjean's visits to Cosette shorter, colder, and eventually impossible. The very household he has helped create is being closed to him. Cosette knows only that her father seems to be withdrawing; the reasons are hidden from her. Valjean accepts the distance because he believes her new life should not be endangered by his identity.

Then Thénardier arrives to sell Marius what he thinks is damaging information. He has learned enough of Valjean's past to believe he can profit. He boasts of a body he saw being carried through the sewers and produces the torn scrap of the coat as proof of murder. Marius recognizes the material. It belonged to the clothes he wore at the barricade. The supposed murder victim was himself, and the supposed murderer his rescuer. Other pieces of Thénardier's story reveal that Valjean did not steal the wealth he gave Cosette. Marius at last sees how profoundly he has misjudged the man. The same petty criminal once credited with saving his father at Waterloo has inadvertently exposed the greater rescue that Marius owes to the man he has shunned.

Marius and Cosette hurry to Valjean. He is dying, diminished by isolation and by a life whose injuries have never ceased to cost him. The silver candlesticks remain with him. They began as an accusation overturned, then a gift, then a standard by which he would measure his conduct. Now they are ordinary objects in a room containing the people who most matter to him. Cosette and Marius arrive in time for reconciliation, though not for recovery. Valjean can hear their love and respond to it. He speaks of Fantine, whose promise has finally reached its destination, and of Cosette, who has become an adult capable of choosing happiness. He dies. The book closes not with a courtroom clearing every record, but with the knowledge that his grave will be nearly anonymous and his life's meaning largely held by those who knew him.

What remains after so much suffering? Fantine was not saved. The June insurgents did not win. Javert could not change. The Thénardiers continue to pursue profit. Even the people who love Valjean misread him for years. Hugo refuses to reduce a social catastrophe to the rescue of a few worthy individuals. Yet the story of the candlesticks insists that a different act was possible at each turning: the bishop's welcome instead of exclusion, Madeleine's confession instead of a stranger's condemnation, Valjean's decision to take Cosette from the inn, his release of Javert instead of revenge, his carrying of Marius instead of abandonment. Each action changes the next person's possibilities without repairing the whole world. The title *Les Misérables* is plural. The novel asks what happens to everyone whom a society has pushed out of its account of respectable life, and what it would take to bring them back into view.

## Text and evidence companion (not spoken)

### Textual basis and scope

This is an original, continuous, spoiler-complete major-arc telling of Victor Hugo's five-part *Les Misérables* (first published 1862), using the complete 1887 Isabel F. Hapgood English translation as the named navigable witness, checked against the French five-part organization. The account does not reproduce Hugo's extended Waterloo, convent, slang, sewer, and historical digressions in full; those are addressed as essential features of the novel's form rather than excluded as irrelevant. Scenes and dialogue are independently composed as explanatory narration, not a performed translation.

### Primary narrative loci

- **Part I, Fantine, books 1–2:** Bishop Myriel, Valjean's release, stolen silver and candlesticks, Petit Gervais.
- **Part I, books 3–8:** Fantine and Tholomyès, Cosette at Montfermeil, Madeleine's factory and mayoralty, Javert's suspicion, Champmathieu's trial, Fantine's death.
- **Part II, Cosette, books 1–8:** Waterloo and the Thénardier encounter; the galleys and escape; Cosette's forest bucket, rescue and Paris life; Fauchelevent and the convent.
- **Part III, Marius, books 1–8:** Gavroche; Gillenormand and Pontmercy; Friends of the ABC; Luxembourg Garden; Gorbeau, Éponine, Thénardier's trap and Javert's raid.
- **Part IV, The Idyll in the Rue Plumet and the Epic in the Rue Saint-Denis, books 1–15:** 1830–32; Valjean's fear and Marius/Cosette's courtship; Éponine's fate; the June barricade, Gavroche and imprisoned Javert.
- **Part V, Jean Valjean, books 1–9:** the barricade's fall; the sewer passage and Thénardier's blackmail evidence; Javert's crisis; marriage, disclosure, estrangement, recognition and Valjean's death.

### Editorial boundary

Novel events are described as fiction, not as a documentary history. The June Rebellion depicted in the story took place in **1832**, not 1789 or 1848. Valjean's fictional criminal treatment and Javert's psychology are readings of Hugo's characters. The narrative reports the book's class and gender politics without treating one exemplary man's kindness as a sufficient reform of poverty and punitive law.
