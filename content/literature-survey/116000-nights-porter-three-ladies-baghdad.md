# One Thousand and One Nights — The Porter and the Three Ladies of Baghdad

A complete **selected tale cycle** with its nested stories and its conclusion, placed within Shahrazad's frame. The controlling English witness here is John Payne's 1882–84 *The Book of the Thousand Nights and One Night*, Volume I, consulted via Wikisource; earlier catalog lectures `074000`–`075000` used Burton Volume I. The shared early cycle is recognizable across the two witnesses, but the translation versions are not interchangeable texts.

One morning in Baghdad, a porter waits beside his empty basket for someone willing to pay him to carry a load. He has no idea that his day's work will end in a house where a royal command is powerless, three beggars are lost princes, a pair of black dogs are women, and nearly everyone present is hiding a story capable of saving a life.

The customer who approaches him is a richly dressed woman. She tells him to follow her, and the two begin a journey through the markets. She buys fruit and meat, sweet pastries and flowers, wine and scented oils, enough to furnish a feast for a large company. Each time the basket grows heavier the porter makes a complaint or a joke; each time the woman adds something else. The day's work ought to end at the door of a grand house. But there are two more women inside, and the porter is so enchanted by the household's beauty and its marvelous provisions that he lingers, reluctant to return to the street.

The women have planned a gathering for themselves. One attends the door, another has bought the food, and the eldest commands the household. They have no need for the porter once he has unloaded his basket, but he argues merrily that good company becomes better when it is complete. The women laugh and agree to let him stay on one condition. There are things in the house that he must not ask about. The principle is expressed in a warning above the door: whoever speaks of a matter that is not his business may hear an answer he will not like. The porter accepts, and for a while the evening is precisely what a tired laborer might have dreamed of. He eats, drinks, and joins the jokes. For a few hours someone who has carried the purchases of the rich is permitted to enjoy them.

The first knock comes from three wandering mendicants, called kalandars in the story. They are strikingly alike: each has one eye, and each has shaved his face in the fashion of a religious wanderer. They ask for a night's hospitality. The women admit them after repeating the condition of silence. The second knock brings three merchants, or so they claim. In fact they are the caliph Harun al-Rashid, his vizier Ja'far, and his swordsman Masrur, wandering the city in disguise. The caliph wishes to know what happens among his subjects when they are not standing before a throne. He too must agree that, inside this house, curiosity has a price.

The company eats, sings, and talks. Then the eldest woman orders two black dogs brought into the hall. They are led forward, and she beats them, though they do not threaten her. A moment later she weeps and embraces the animals. The act is so contradictory that the guests can hardly believe their eyes. Before they can recover, another of the women sings a lament. The portress, overwhelmed, tears at her clothing and faints, showing scars left by terrible beatings. She is revived; the lament and collapse occur again. The guests begin whispering. The caliph is outraged at being asked to observe such things in silence. Ja'far reminds him of the promise, but authority is a habit hard to suspend. Finally the men press for an explanation.

The eldest woman has warned them. Now she summons armed servants. The guests are seized, and the entertainment threatens to end with their deaths. Before the swords fall, she offers terms: each man must explain who he is and how he came to be here. Whoever can give a satisfactory account may go free. The porter begins at once. He tells the story we already know: he was hired to carry a basket, followed the purchases from shop to shop, entered the house, and stayed to dine. His story is so short and accurate that the women laugh. He may leave. But he wants to hear what becomes of the others, and remains.

The first kalandar begins. He was not born a beggar, but a prince whose father ruled a city. As a young man he visited his cousin, another prince. The cousin asked him to help conceal a secret meeting by leading a richly dressed woman into a tomb and sealing a passage beneath it. The prince obeyed and later regretted the favor; he could not remember the tomb he had closed. Then disaster struck his own kingdom. His father's vizier seized power. This was an enemy the prince had once injured by accident, putting out his eye with a stray shot. The usurper now deliberately destroyed the prince's eye in revenge and ordered his execution. A headsman spared him, and he fled back to his uncle's city.

There he and his uncle found the hidden tomb. Inside lay the cousin and the woman, burned black in their illicit refuge. The uncle's judgment upon his son was fierce. The surviving prince had escaped one revenge only to discover another household destroyed by secret desire. Before he could recover, the usurper's armies overran the uncle's city as well. Deprived of family, country, and one eye, he shaved himself and wandered until he reached Baghdad. The tale tells us why his face bears the mark of loss. It tells us also that a man may be born a prince and still become a stranger who has to beg to be heard.

The second kalandar has a different history. He too was a royal son, educated in letters and admired for his learning. While traveling to another court, he was attacked and forced to flee. He found refuge with a woodcutter, who showed him an entrance underground. Beneath the earth lived a woman held captive by a powerful jinni. She had been concealed there for years, unable to leave. For a time the prince and the captive enjoyed the relief of companionship. But their safety depended upon not provoking her captor. The prince's recklessness betrayed them. The jinni returned, killed the woman, and made the prince suffer through an enchantment that left him in the form of an animal.

Even this disaster does not end the story. The enchanted prince is taken into a household where his strange intelligence is noticed. The daughter of a king understands how to undo the spell and engages the jinni in an exhausting contest of magical forms. She defeats him but dies from injuries sustained in the combat; the prince recovers his human shape but loses an eye. The king, mourning the daughter who saved the stranger, sends him away. The caliph's guests have already heard one account of an eye destroyed in revenge. Here another eye is lost in an attempt to set a captive woman free, followed by a rescue that costs the rescuer's life.

Within this second story another moral example has been told, about an envious person and the person he envies. The victim, given a chance to retaliate, chooses mercy. His mercy stands beside the jinni's violence and the prince's carelessness as one possible answer to injury. We need not follow every turn of that embedded parable to hear why Shahrazad includes it. In the royal bedroom where she tells these tales, a powerful man has also interpreted an injury as permission to punish others. Each act of forbearance adds another imaginable response.

The third kalandar begins his adventure on the sea. A magnetic mountain draws ships to destruction. A strange device involving a mounted figure helps him escape the curse, but one misfortune follows another. He discovers an underground refuge where a boy has been hidden because astrologers have foretold that the prince will kill him. The prince knows that he is the person named in the prophecy. He resolves to protect the boy through the appointed period of danger. They become companions. At the very last moment, through a terrible accident involving a knife, the prophecy is fulfilled. The man who meant to save the child has caused the child's death. The tale does not make intention sufficient to undo an act.

Driven onward, the prince reaches a palace inhabited by forty women. They welcome him and grant him a life of pleasure. When they must leave for a fixed interval, they entrust him with keys to a hundred chambers and warn him not to open the last one. He discovers gardens, treasures, delights, and marvels behind the first ninety-nine doors. He has more than enough to see and enjoy. Yet the forbidden room draws his attention until he can think of little else. He opens it, and a magical horse carries him into the air. The journey ends with the loss of his eye and the loss of his place among the women. He too wanders to Baghdad. Each kalandar has arrived by a different road, yet all have learned the cost of trying to force a secret to yield its answer.

The women now permit the company to leave. Harun al-Rashid, who has been listening disguised among the merchants, is not satisfied. He cannot forget the two dogs or the scars. The next day he orders the women and the kalandars summoned to his court. There, the balance of authority changes. Inside the women's house, they could threaten him because he had entered as a guest and accepted their rule. In the palace, the caliph can command an explanation. The women tell him their histories.

The eldest explains that the two black dogs are her older sisters. Their father died and left the daughters portions of his property. The elder sisters married badly, lost their fortunes, and returned in poverty. Their younger sister welcomed them, clothed them, and shared her wealth. Yet gratitude did not last. On a trading voyage, the younger sister showed kindness to a man who had appeared in humble circumstances but was not what he seemed. Her sisters, angry at her good fortune, plotted to kill her and threw her into the sea. The man she had helped was connected with an enchanted being who rescued her. The sisters were transformed into black dogs. The younger woman was charged with punishing them, although she did so in tears. The cruelty in the hall was thus entangled with an earlier betrayal and an imposed obligation.

The portress explains her scars. An older woman had persuaded her to enter an apparently respectable house, where a powerful young man courted and married her under strict conditions. During a visit to a market, a merchant offered beautiful fabric as a gift in exchange for a kiss. Urged by an older companion, she agreed despite having promised not to allow such contact. But the merchant bit her cheek viciously. When her husband saw the wound, he assumed a breach of faith and ordered her beaten so brutally that the marks remained. He did not kill her, but he sent her away. The woman who had spent the earlier evening singing and laughing had also been carrying the evidence of a man's sense of ownership over her body.

The caliph orders the return of the jinniyah who had transformed the sisters. She restores them to human shape. The caliph also learns that the husband who punished the portress was his own son. The tale ends with pardons, gifts, and marriages arranged through the authority of the court: the sisters and kalandars are provided for, the beaten woman is returned to her husband under a renewed contract, and the caliph himself takes a bride from the company. In the logic of this version, a powerful ruler has resolved every loose thread. Yet a listener is entitled to notice that the ending returns a woman to the man who beat her and turns several complicated lives into marriages decided by other people. The resolution is the story's resolution; it need not become ours.

And now we return to the royal chamber where Shahrazad is telling it. For night after night, she has held Shahryar's attention with other people's unfinished accounts. Here the trick becomes visible inside a single house. One secret produces a warning not to ask. The warning is broken. The punishment is delayed while the guests tell their histories. Those histories produce still more stories. When the guests have earned their freedom, a ruler orders another telling because he cannot live with an unanswered question. Shahrazad has given her dangerous listener a model of himself, without ever having to tell him directly that he is the one being judged.

The porter came into the house because he wanted to be paid. He remained because he wanted to hear more. The caliph came because he wanted to know what his subjects did when nobody was looking. He remained, and then summoned them back, because no command could make the mystery disappear. The story understands a temptation more powerful than wealth: the wish to know what happens next. Inside Shahrazad's frame, that wish is the narrow opening through which another dawn can arrive without a death.

## Text and evidence companion (not spoken)

- **Controlling narrative witness:** John Payne, *The Book of the Thousand Nights and One Night*, vol. 1 (1882–84), [The Porter and the Three Ladies of Baghdad](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/The_Book_of_the_Thousand_Nights_and_One_Night/The_Porter_and_the_Three_Ladies_of_Baghdad), with separately linked First, Second, and Third Calender stories, the story of the Envier and the Envied, and the Eldest Lady and Portress. Payne's account includes the court conclusion. The previous overview episodes use [Burton's 1885 Volume I](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/3435), a separate historical English translation of related Arabic textual material.
- **Full-cycle scope:** this narration contains the gathering, the taboo and its violation, all three calender life histories, the eldest woman's dogs, the portress's scars, Harun al-Rashid's inquiry, magical restoration, and the marriages. Some long episodes within the calenders' memoirs are condensed, especially the second calender's nested moral exemplum; this is an overview, not a translation.
- **Critical context:** [Marzolph, narrative constructions of Harun al-Rashid in the *Nights*](https://brill.com/display/book/9789004340541/B9789004340541_004.pdf) (court authority and the disguised-caliph motif). [Wikipedia: One Thousand and One Nights](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/One_Thousand_and_One_Nights) orientation only.
- **Interpretation boundary:** the supernatural events and the domestic abuses are claims of this received tale, not independently documented history. The normative approval of the final marital settlements inside the tale is **not** adopted by the narrator. The tale's internal stories are distinct narratives embedded within Shahrazad's overarching frame.
