# Kumulipo — Guided Reading I: From Deep Darkness to Day

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

How does a creation poem begin if it does not begin with a command? Not with a ruler saying that light must exist, and not with a craftsman setting out materials on a workbench. It begins in a darkness that is already full of possibility. The world is hot, the heavens are moving, the sun's light is diminished. There is moisture and the beginning of substance, but nothing yet resembles the crowded land of people and animals that we know. Then the chant names a beginning: Kumulipo, a male presence, and Pōʻele, a female presence, emerging in the night. Birth follows birth. A living order unfolds through kinship.

We are entering the Hawaiian *Kumulipo*, a cosmogonic genealogical chant, a mele koʻihonua. It is more than two thousand lines long in the form in which it has been published, arranged into sixteen major periods called wā. We will not attempt to perform the chant, and we will not merely recite every name in an English list. Instead, we will follow its construction from the first birth through the emergence of daylight, listening for the relationships that give its opening movement force. Our principal English witness is the translation published in 1897 by Queen Liliʻuokalani. The words and choices in any English rendering are not identical to the original Hawaiian performance tradition.

At first the chant moves through the sea. The birth of the coral organism stands near the beginning. From this small life come other forms of coral; then worms, star-shaped creatures, shellfish, sea urchins, and further marine beings appear. There is a repeated sense of one being arriving with or giving rise to another. Read a few entries and it is tempting to turn the poem into a primitive biology textbook. Read more carefully and you discover that the list is doing something else as well. It is establishing relations among the creatures. The world grows because beings are connected by birth and correspondence.

Try to hear the structure rather than count the species. In Liliʻuokalani's translation, an inhabitant of the sea can be paired with something in the forest. A seaweed has a land plant as its counterpart. A marine form and a plant growing above the water may share a name, a pattern, an appearance or an association. That pairing is not necessarily what a modern zoologist means by biological descent. It is poetic classification made through relationship. Water and land do not sit apart as categories without bridges. The chant joins them with repeated language.

One of the most striking qualities of this opening is that it makes a tiny marine being capable of carrying enormous significance. Coral appears before kings and before the kinds of human characters who ordinarily command a story. This does not imply that a chief and a coral polyp occupy the same place in social hierarchy. It means that the genealogy of the chief will ultimately have to pass through a world that was alive before the chief was born. The origin of political authority cannot be fully separated from the origin of other life.

Listen for another refrain: the persistence of night. Darkness is not presented only as an enemy to be defeated. It is the condition in which a world becomes possible. Births can happen there. The poet returns to the language of night after a succession of forms, keeping the listener aware that all of this abundance is arriving before the full presence of the human daylight world. The repetition creates rhythm and expectancy. What begins as apparent emptiness becomes increasingly inhabited.

The sea is not only full of the animals a fisher might recognize. It is associated with vegetation, with material that grows and spreads, with plants whose counterparts are found ashore. A reader who strips out the repeated formulas to make a short list may understand the taxonomy more quickly, but will miss the way the poem makes connections feel inevitable. The point is not merely that such-and-such a plant came after such-and-such an animal. It is that each arrival calls for another; the name of one being reaches outward to a related being somewhere else.

As the periods proceed, different classes of plants and creatures enter the field of attention. The familiar world is assembled through pairings. But there is no reason to imagine that the composer had a printed chart on which every species was filed into a modern scientific category. The chant works with knowledge of the environment and with associations that are cultural, linguistic, ecological and genealogical. A plant may be significant because it resembles a marine creature, because it has a ritual relationship to another being, or because its name resonates in the pattern of the verse. Translation can communicate some of that and conceal other parts.

The emergence of land animals and birds does not bring an immediate conclusion. We encounter a world in which animal behavior, forms of life and the movements of named powers remain entwined. In one section, the pig becomes a figure around which earth, mounds and ancestry gather. Elsewhere, short lists of bodily appearances and temperaments suggest the fertility of the poem's language: an entity arrives, and a name turns into the description of a particular quality. It would be a mistake to pretend that these are all anonymous fossils lined up in a historical sequence. The poem is making kinds of beings available to thought through the act of naming them.

Naming itself becomes an action. In an ordinary historical genealogy, one may read a name and ask who that person was, which territory they ruled, and what date they lived. In this opening, names also carry a different burden. They connect one form of life to another. They give presence to things that might otherwise pass unnoticed. The coral, worm, shell, plant, pig and bird are not scenic background. They are among the ancestors and relations from which the later inhabited world will emerge.

It is possible to hear these passages as a statement of kinship with place. The Hawaiian word ʻāina means land, but a merely real-estate understanding of land cannot account for a song in which the natural world is arranged as a chain of births. In later Kanaka Maoli interpretation, belonging to land is a genealogical relation, not just a matter of occupying a place. This helps explain why the poem's marine opening is not an irrelevant preface before the human material begins. It establishes what the human material will depend upon.

The chant also demands that we resist our most convenient comparison. We can admire its sophisticated attention to relationships among organisms without calling it a prediction of Darwin's theory of evolution. Evolutionary biology makes particular claims about heredity, variation, populations and mechanisms over time. The *Kumulipo* makes poetic and genealogical claims in its own conceptual system. That system deserves to be understood as a work of thought, not praised only by saying it anticipated a later European science.

Now move toward the eighth wā. The world is no longer an apparently endless nocturnal succession of marine and earthly beings. A different threshold is reached. In the 1897 English text, Lailai is born as a woman; Kii is born as a man; Kāne and Kanaloa appear as gods. The ending of the passage marks the arrival of day. It is a dramatic change in the character of the genealogy, but it is not an abrupt deletion of everything that has come before. The beings of daylight emerge through the long structure of related births that the night prepared.

Lailai matters because the human and divine lines begin to meet around her. She is not simply 'the first woman' in a universal account that we can substitute for any other creation narrative. She belongs to a specific Hawaiian genealogical poem with its own named powers and relationships. The source's account has subtleties, and different translations and commentators interpret particular passages differently. Our purpose is to recognize the threshold, not to flatten those differences into one unchallengeable schematic family tree.

Notice, too, that daylight arrives with drums and other names carrying cultural life. A world with people is a world with movement, sound, social distinctions and practices. The poem does not say, in a prosaic sentence, that the biological portion of the story is over and culture now begins. It performs the change through a shift of named presences and the rhythm of its declaration. After the long beginning in darkness, day can be spoken.

We have reached a meaningful stopping point without reaching the end of the chant. The first movement did not tell a self-contained adventure with a villain or a rescue. It took us from deep darkness to a living sea, from sea to land, from repeated pairings to human and divine genealogies. To appreciate it, we have to change what we expect a story to accomplish. A sequence of names can create suspense when every name expands the world and makes another relationship possible.

The day has come, but the genealogy has only begun. The later portions will carry us through named ancestors and chiefly lines. Those sections are not a tedious list appended after the interesting creation. They are one of the reasons the creation was sung. To understand why, we must follow the chant beyond the first dawn, toward the people whose political and familial lives made the preservation of the genealogy urgent.

## Text and evidence companion (not spoken)

### Textual and cultural boundary

An original, non-quotational English guided reading of the opening seven wā and the daylight transition in wā 8, principally following Queen Liliʻuokalani's published 1897 English translation. This is **not a performance, complete reading or authorized replacement of the Hawaiian mele**. The Hawaiian-original 1889 edition and later Beckwith translation (1951) are distinguishable witnesses; no exact equivalence of all biological and ritual terms between witnesses is claimed. The first night/day division is more subtly described in some scholarship: the eighth section contains the transition, and scholarly descriptions sometimes group it differently. The text says daylight at the end of that section.

### Source locators

- Liliʻuokalani (1897), First Era: Kumulipo and Pōʻele, first marine beings, land/sea correspondences, repetitions of night.
- Era 2–7: successive classes of plant and animal life, pig imagery, named characteristics.
- Era 8: Lailai, Kii, Kāne, Kanaloa, and the mark of daylight.
- Katrina-Ann R. Oliveira, *Ancestral Places*, opening discussion: mele koʻihonua as land–genealogy; royal use and transmission.
