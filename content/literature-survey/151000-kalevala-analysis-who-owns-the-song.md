# The Kalevala — Analysis: Who Owns the Song?

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

There is a competition in the Kalevala that explains more about the work than almost any battle. A young man, Joukahainen, challenges the old Väinämöinen to a contest of knowledge. The younger singer can name things he knows; the elder can sing in a way that makes the world change. His opponent begins sinking into the marsh. It is an exhilarating image of language as power. It is also uncomfortable. The great singer wins by making a weaker person helpless, and that person's desperate bargain sets in motion the fate of Aino, a young woman who does not want the marriage promised on her behalf. In the Kalevala, song can create and song can wound. Its deepest interest is not simply that words are magical, but that their use has consequences.

The book known as the Kalevala has a complicated relation to the people who originally sang its materials. Its best-known form was published in 1849, the second major edition assembled by Elias Lönnrot, a Finnish physician, language scholar, and collector. The earlier Kalevala had appeared in 1835. The later text contains fifty runos, or poems, and more than twenty-two thousand lines. Lönnrot and other collectors worked with materials preserved by singers in Finland and Karelia. Those singers possessed repertoires of narrative poems, charms, laments, and other verbal art. Lönnrot did not discover a single intact epic that everyone had simply forgotten how to read. He selected, joined, rearranged, and sometimes revised poems to make a large literary whole. Recognizing his editorial role does not mean that the underlying oral traditions were fictional inventions.

It matters which form of the work we say we are reading. The 1849 Kalevala is an authored compilation shaped by Lönnrot's decisions. A runo sung in a Karelian village, the lines printed in the first 1835 version, and the corresponding passage in the expanded edition are related but not interchangeable objects. The names of individual tradition-bearers matter too. Lönnrot recorded performers including Arhippa Perttunen, whose knowledge and artistry were not just raw material for someone else's genius. Later scholarship and archives have made it possible to study the singers and their varying contexts more carefully. A nation can celebrate a book without making the people from whom its materials were collected disappear behind its title page.

Why did such a book matter so much? In the nineteenth century, questions of language and cultural identity were inseparable from political life. Finland had been transferred from Swedish rule to the Russian Empire in 1809 and existed as an autonomous grand duchy. Finnish was widely spoken, but the languages of administration, elite education, and high literature had other institutional histories. The prospect that Finnish-language oral poetry could sustain an epic of great scale had enormous cultural force. The Kalevala helped demonstrate the resources of Finnish literary expression and became part of movements that imagined a nation through its language. Yet the central geography of collecting also included Karelia, much of it on the Russian side of borders. A national symbol built from shared borderland traditions cannot be explained adequately by a simple story of an ancient, already unified Finnish nation speaking with one voice.

The word epic can itself be misleading here. Homer has battles, journeys, councils, and funerals, and the Kalevala has corresponding grandeur. But Lönnrot's book is also filled with instructions, charms, descriptions of agricultural labor, and the origins of apparently ordinary things. To forge iron, heal a wound, or build a boat, one may need to know the words that tell how the thing first came to be. Language is not a report about making; in the poem it is often part of making. The origins of iron are relevant to a wounded knee because knowledge about the substance can help control its harm. That is a narrative logic different from modern technology and from the fantasy convention in which someone merely announces a spell.

The poem's prevailing four-beat rhythm and its pattern of parallel lines also shape its listening experience. A phrase returns in another form, a thought develops by sounding again with slightly altered terms, and a singer finds room to extend an action without adding new plot events. An English translation can communicate the images and much of the repetition, but a version composed in a different meter will not reproduce every feature of Finnish or Karelian oral performance. The widely available nineteenth-century translation by John Martin Crawford is a useful historical reading witness, with old-fashioned spellings and interpretive habits of its time. It is not transparent access to the original songs. Readers seeking the sound of the tradition should distinguish the printed Finnish epic from archival transcriptions and living performances.

The Sampo is a perfect example of the challenges of interpretation. Ilmarinen forges an object that produces desirable goods; Louhi stores it in Pohjola; three men from Kalevala steal it; it breaks in the ensuing struggle. What, exactly, is it? A mill, a magical engine, a source of prosperity, a symbol of cosmic order, and an object interpreted in many other ways. A responsible overview can explain what it does in the narrative without pretending that one modern interpretation has recovered the single secret meaning known to every singer. The conflict is unmistakable even if the object is not. Who deserves the wealth that extraordinary labor has brought into being? The craftsman who made it, the household that commissioned it, or a broader community? The book refuses to distribute rewards as smoothly as a moral fable.

This question is connected to the poem's portrait of making. Ilmarinen is an astonishing smith, yet when he tries to forge a replacement wife from gold, he produces something cold and unresponsive. His skill has reached the wrong problem. Väinämöinen, who knows words of origin, can likewise fail spectacularly in human judgment. He treats Aino as an appropriate bride and learns only after her death what his desire has ignored. Neither craft nor wisdom gives a character authority over another person's consent. It would be a mistake to call the epic a consistent modern argument about autonomy; much of its language about women and household duties belongs to other social worlds. But these narrative failures make it possible to ask how its characters misunderstand the limits of their power.

The long wedding songs sharpen the issue. A bride leaving her family is greeted with celebration and admonition. The songs recognize that marriage reorganizes belonging, labor, and obligation. The woman's sorrow is not a footnote to the man's success. And later, Kullervo's story turns a household relationship into an instrument of destruction. When Ilmarinen's wife hides a stone in the enslaved boy's bread, the immediate injury is a broken knife. The larger injury is the contempt through which a household abuses someone who has almost nothing left. The boy's revenge is horrendous, not redemptive. Kullervo then commits further harm, suffers the revelation of an incestuous encounter, and eventually kills himself. The epic gives us powerful causes without requiring us to excuse their effects.

Kullervo's cycle has reached well beyond Finnish literature partly because it offers a tragedy distinct from the usual heroic conquest. A young man can survive many attempts to kill him and still be unable to live. His strength does not free him from the patterns of violence that made him. It is worth hearing the whole narrative before turning it into a symbol of rebellious masculinity or national suffering. He is not a liberator who wins the final battle. He is someone who destroys the enemy and then discovers that revenge has not created a home.

The epic also gives us a different account of heroic importance through Lemminkäinen's mother. The son undertakes adventures and dies. The mother receives the sign of his death, searches for the remains, and puts him back together. In one sense the story satisfies an old narrative desire: death need not be the end. But it also transfers decisive agency to a character who has been warning against reckless violence from the beginning. It is the mother's fidelity, not the young man's prowess, that allows the tale to continue. The Kalevala's world contains heroic actions whose value cannot be measured by who struck the final blow.

The music of the kantele is another alternative to conquest. Väinämöinen makes the first instrument out of a giant fish's bones. Listeners gather from across the natural world, and the power of the scene lies in being held together by sound. After the first instrument is lost in the Sampo struggle, he constructs another from birch. The new instrument cannot restore the whole ruined mill, but it renews something the mill could not provide. Where the Sampo seems to manufacture useful things, the kantele makes an audience. For a text produced out of song traditions, that may be its most suggestive image of cultural power.

Later artists took the Kalevala into new mediums. The composer Jean Sibelius drew repeatedly on its characters and scenes; painters, most famously Akseli Gallen-Kallela, made images of Väinämöinen, Aino, and the Sampo struggles. These works helped turn selected scenes into widely recognizable emblems. They can also conceal the multiplicity of oral forms underneath the printed book. A national gallery painting of a heroic singer is not the same thing as the repertoire of a Karelian tradition-bearer. One enriches the other when we keep track of who made each work and when.

Finally the book ends with Marjatta's miraculous child, who speaks against the old singer's judgment. Readers have long noticed the Christian associations of the berry-conception and the baptism of a new ruler. Yet the ending does not simply provide a victory parade for Christianity over a defeated pagan world. Väinämöinen sails away leaving his song and instrument, and the story allows the possibility of his return. This is a literary negotiation over succession, not an impartial transcript of an ancient religious conversion. It is fitting that a nineteenth-century work made from older songs ends with a scene of one voice leaving and another beginning.

The Kalevala's enduring importance rests on two achievements that should be held together. It is a remarkable literary epic in its 1849 form: humorous, strange, formally persistent, and capable of tragedy as well as marvel. It is also a major episode in the history of collecting, editing, and nationalizing oral culture. Its first question is how the world came to be. Its hardest question may be who gets to tell that world into being now: the singer in a village, the collector at his desk, the nation that adopts the result, or a listener who hears the competing voices still alive inside the book. An excellent reading makes room for all of them.

## Text and evidence companion (not spoken)

### Distinctions and evidentiary boundary

The 1849 *Kalevala* is the specific literary text under discussion. It is distinct from the 1835 edition, from manuscript/archival oral poetry, and from contemporary folk performance. The historical claims about Lönnrot, Karelia, the singers, and editorial synthesis are grounded in the Finnish Literature Society and Kalevala Society. Crawford 1888 is a public-domain translation witness, not evidence for the sound of oral delivery. Interpretations of the Sampo, consent, technology, and musical gathering are presented as critical questions rather than as statements of an agreed original mythological system. No copyrighted modern translation is quoted.
