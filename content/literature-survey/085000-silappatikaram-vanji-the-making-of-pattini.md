# Silappatikaram III: Vanji and the Making of Pattini

Book III of the received Tamil epic, Vañcikkāṇṭam, episodes 24–30. The focus shifts from Kannaki's grief to the public religious and royal memory made in her name.

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

What does a kingdom do with the memory of a woman whose accusation destroyed another kingdom's capital? That question begins the last movement of *Silappatikaram*. The first book belonged to Puhar, a city of maritime trade and music. The second took us to Madurai, where a goldsmith's false accusation and a king's reckless judgment led to Kovalan's death and Kannaki's terrible curse. The third moves to the Chera country. Kannaki is no longer in the royal court confronting a living ruler. Her story is carried by other people. It will become a narrative told to a king, a reason for a journey, an image made from stone, and a focus of worship. The form of the epic itself changes as remembrance moves from household loss to political institution.

In the hills, people encounter a remarkable woman, grieving yet surrounded by signs that exceed ordinary life. The mountain communities tell of her transformation and departure with her husband into a realm beyond human reach. Their report travels to the Chera ruler Senguttuvan, who hears it in the company of his queen and advisers. A poet or learned companion recounts the events that have led to this moment: Kovalan's faithlessness and ruin, the move to Madurai, the execution, the broken anklet, and the consuming fire. We have heard the story from inside its immediate suffering; the Chera court now hears it as something that has already happened elsewhere and must be interpreted. What meaning should a ruler take from another ruler's failure?

The queen's counsel is decisive. The court considers the dead Pandya queen and Kannaki, whose faithfulness and extraordinary power have now become famous. Kannaki is to be honored with an image and a shrine. This is not a casual addition to the poem's ending. It gives the Chera monarchy a means of presenting itself as responsive to a righteousness that a rival king failed to recognize. The story of a woman wronged by state power is becoming an argument about what rightful state power should do. We can see the attraction and the ambiguity at once. A ruler honors Kannaki instead of condemning her; but the act of honoring her also draws her independent anger into the order of kings.

The ministers discuss where the stone for her image should come from. The poem gives the Chera king a northern ambition. He chooses to obtain a sacred stone from the Himalayan region and to purify it by the Ganges. This requires a long expedition across lands outside the immediate setting of the first two books. Rival kings enter the narrative; royal valor and military prestige come forward in a way that may at first seem disconnected from a woman's grief. But the very connection is the point. The new shrine is to be the achievement of a king who can command armies and move objects across an immense landscape. The memorial of Kannaki becomes an undertaking that demonstrates the reach of Chera authority.

The expedition encounters resistance and wins victories in the poem's telling. The names and humiliations of rival rulers belong to a literary account of royal triumph. It would be a mistake to convert this entire journey into a verified campaign itinerary merely because its cities and rivers are real. The historical dating and compositional layers of *Silappatikaram* remain debated, and later attempts to fix a precise chronology from its references to foreign kings cannot by themselves establish all of its events. Within the narrative, however, the movement of the stone is exact in meaning. A mineral taken from a northern mountain and ritually washed in a sacred river travels south to become the public image of a Tamil woman. The object joins widely recognized forms of holiness to a locally grounded tale of justice.

The king returns and prepares the consecration. The focus shifts to the craftsmen, rites, gatherings, and public acknowledgments through which an image becomes more than a stone. Guests attend; rulers are associated with the ceremony; the heroine is no longer remembered only as Kannaki, the wife who carried a pair of anklets into exile. She is honored as Pattini, a figure of fidelity, moral force, and protection whose worship will develop different histories in Tamil and Sri Lankan communities. The poem presents a beginning for that sacred status. A historical study of Pattini worship must examine the wider evidence of practices and later transmission rather than assuming every element of its origin can be recovered from one epic.

The transformed heroine can now speak, bless, and become an object of devotion within the poem's world. We should not hurry past the cost of this transformation. In Puhar, Kannaki's husband deserted her. On the road she offered the last family valuables for a new livelihood. In Madurai she saw him killed by a state that had not investigated the accusation. Now kings speak of her virtue, and other people create the formal means by which she may be remembered. The conversion of grief into worship offers a kind of justice after death, but it does not restore the possibility of a life that was taken from her. The epic leaves the listener with both consolation and an irreversible absence.

The last episodes also look back toward those who survived the central tragedy. The families and companions of Kannaki and Kovalan must absorb a news that has transformed their relations to the world. Madhavi, whose artistry and love were so important in the first book, belongs to this longer aftermath, though the independent story of her daughter Manimekalai will be developed in a different Tamil work. The received *Silappatikaram* does not end by pretending that the story of one couple has produced a harmonious city. It turns instead toward the social power of memory. The names of the dead, the practices of renunciation, the cultivation of virtue, and the royal use of commemoration are now part of the narrative landscape.

The work is traditionally attributed to Ilango Adigal, whose own presence is woven into its literary framing. Modern scholars do not agree on the precise historical date of its composition or on every feature of its textual growth. We can responsibly say that the received epic is structured in three books and thirty episodes and that it carries a sophisticated account of poetry, music, dance, social life, legal error, and sacral kingship. We cannot responsibly say that its narrated royal expedition or every named guest at the shrine establishes an independent chronology. Reading the work well requires allowing it to be literature in full, rather than reducing it either to a court archive or to a simple cautionary tale.

Look back at the first image of the series: a wealthy household in the sea-facing city of Puhar, with an anklet still only an ornament. Nothing in that domestic object announces its final career. First it is something a married woman possesses. Then it is the couple's last capital. Then its contents expose a judicial murder. Finally it helps create a goddess's public memory in a different kingdom. The movement across the three Tamil capitals mirrors the movement of the object through those meanings. The scale of the work widens from desire to law and then to worship.

The conclusion does not require us to choose whether to admire Kannaki as a faithful wife or as a woman who publicly shamed a king. She is both within the poem, and the tensions are part of its power. The epic also asks what kings must do when truth arrives from outside the palace in the hands of someone they have ignored. In Madurai, a king knew too little and acted too quickly. In Vanji, another king hears a story and chooses to make its moral claim permanent. Whether that permanence liberates the heroine's memory or absorbs it into royal glory remains a question the text makes possible. *Silappatikaram* is therefore not only the story of an anklet. It is the story of how an ornament becomes evidence, evidence becomes catastrophe, and catastrophe becomes a tradition that successive communities continue to interpret.
