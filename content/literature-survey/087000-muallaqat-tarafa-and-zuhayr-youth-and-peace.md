# Mu'allaqat II: Tarafa and Zuhayr — Youth, Defiance, and the Price of Peace

Two independent pre-Islamic Arabic odes are heard beside each other. Their speakers move through the shared techniques of the long *qasida* but arrive at sharply different ideas about time, risk, and responsibility.

One young poet sees the remains of a woman's campsite and remembers a relation now lost. Soon he is describing a camel as though no other living creature could show so completely what endurance looks like. Later he speaks of the brief span of a human life and refuses to let other people tell him how to spend it. Another poet also begins with an abandoned dwelling. But he goes on to praise men who settled a deadly conflict and to warn his listeners that war destroys people long after the first boast of courage has faded. The poems are attributed to Tarafa ibn al-Abd and Zuhayr ibn Abi Sulma, two of the seven famous voices gathered in the *Mu'allaqat*. Placing them beside each other allows us to hear the long ode as a form capable of disagreement with itself.

Tarafa's poem opens with Khawla's deserted dwelling. He sees traces of the camp that remain visible like marks left on a hand. Already the image is doing two things. It offers a comparison to help a listener see the ground, and it suggests that memory can be carried on the body even after its cause is absent. The poem soon turns to the caravan and especially to the speaker's she-camel. A substantial stretch of the ode is given to the animal's body, legs, speed, endurance, and movement through difficult terrain. To a hurried reader this can look like a delay before the emotional message. But the description is itself an argument about the possibilities of travel. The speaker has been abandoned by people or fortune; his camel remains a means by which he can move through the world despite the pressure of grief and danger.

Tarafa is deeply interested in the relationship between bodily vitality and the knowledge that vitality ends. He boasts of pleasure, generosity, and the refusal to hoard life against a future that no one can guarantee. The drink, the social gathering, and the willingness to help a person in distress become parts of a recognizable persona. This is not simply a call to reckless hedonism. In the poem, the speaker also complains of relatives who question him, exclude him, or fail to understand the principles by which he lives. His chosen expenditures may look to them like waste; to him, failure to use wealth for friendship, courage, and pleasure would be a greater waste. There is pain beneath the self-assertion because his own kin have become judges from whom he feels estranged.

The tradition surrounding Tarafa's short life tells stories about courtly offense and violent death. Those biographical stories cannot be made identical with the voice of the poem. We do not know how much of its reported personal history can be recovered from later anecdotes. The ode itself is sufficient to give us the crisis: a man who knows he will die insists that he must live according to his own estimation of what is worth doing. The desert route, the camel, the quarrels with relatives, and the speaker's own finite body belong to one world of pressure. He cannot postpone existence until his critics approve of him.

Zuhayr's ode makes a different choice after a similarly mournful opening. He recognizes the remains of a dwelling associated with Umm Awfa. The traces are faint, and the people of the camp have gone on. The *nasib* performs its work, creating the feeling that time has passed and that what once seemed stable is now only a readable mark on the land. But Zuhayr will use that consciousness of loss to discuss a public calamity rather than primarily his private appetites. The ode turns toward a conflict between groups named in the poem and praises men who have acted to bring the fighting to an end.

The praise is not vague. Peace has required intercession, money, and a willingness to accept responsibility for the consequences of killing. The peacemakers are celebrated because they understand the human cost of a cycle in which every death becomes the reason for the next. The poem speaks from the aftermath of war, not the innocence of someone who has never seen it. When the speaker warns against rekindling violence, his imagery is severe. War is not something one can admire from a safe distance and then put away after the spectacle. It is an activity that grinds down those who participate and generates injuries their children may inherit.

Zuhayr's reflections on age give further weight to these admonitions. He is an older voice in the poem's received persona, one who has seen enough to distrust simple declarations of honor. He does not deny the importance of reputation. Indeed, he is praising men whose reputation rests on keeping peace. But he asks that honor be measured by the prevention of suffering as well as by the ability to inflict it. The old poet and the defiant young poet are not mechanical opposites. Tarafa also cares about generosity and human obligation; Zuhayr also uses a proud and formal art to celebrate his patrons. Their contrast matters because they assign different weight to the facts of mortality and social life.

The *Mu'allaqat* collection is not a debate written by one author. It is a later grouping of independent poems with different histories of transmission. Nor should every word ascribed to each poet be treated as a documented personal confession. The formal resemblances among the odes make the differences more audible. Both begin with traces. Both value generosity, endurance, and speech worthy of being remembered. Both know that wealth and life can vanish. Tarafa's answer is to insist on the need to live intensely before that happens. Zuhayr's answer is to ask men to stop making one another's lives shorter.

In Zuhayr, the movement from praise to reflection gives the poem the texture of judgment accumulated through experience. It honors those who have undertaken the difficult work of restoring relations among enemies and then warns that the greatest danger may come from people who romanticize the violence that negotiation has ended. The powerful images are not detachable moral sayings. They grow out of the specific conflict and settlement the ode commemorates. What a later reader may quote as a proverb was first part of a public argument addressed to an audience that knew names, alliances, and injuries.

Tarafa's long description of the camel can now be heard anew. The animal carries its rider past the memory of a deserted camp, across the distance that separates desire from opportunity. He will not be trapped by those who refuse him a place or by the inevitability of death. Zuhayr's peacemakers, meanwhile, try to keep whole communities from being trapped inside an inherited war. These are distinct forms of resistance to fate. Neither offers a universal law for human conduct. Read together, the odes show what a major poetic tradition could do: hold grief, bodily experience, generosity, political memory, self-defense, and ethical warning within a single powerful voice, and let neighboring voices differ about the meaning of courage.
