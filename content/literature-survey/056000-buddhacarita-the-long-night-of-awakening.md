# Buddhacarita, Part Two: The Long Night of Awakening

Aśvaghoṣa's Sanskrit cantos VIII–XIV, stopping at the point where the original Sanskrit text breaks; the completion of canto XIV is reported as a reconstruction supported by Chinese and Tibetan transmission.

The palace has not yet understood the distance between a horse's hoofbeats and a son's decision. Chandaka returns without Siddhārtha. Those who see the empty saddle must learn that it is not a temporary errand. The prince they expected to govern their city has gone into the forest as a renunciant. His family will not summon him back by giving him a more luxurious room. The scene belongs to the eighth canto of Aśvaghoṣa's *Buddhacarita*, and it forces us to hear what a spiritual departure sounds like from the house left behind.

Chandaka's grief is mixed with shame. How can he explain that he helped the prince leave? Why did the horse obey? In the palace, the women cry out, and King Śuddhodana's distress is unhidden. Aśvaghoṣa does not make this a story in which the prince is virtuous because everybody else's pain vanishes. Those attached to him have suffered a real loss. The poem asks how a person can pursue a goal believed to benefit all beings while inflicting grief on those closest to him. It does not provide an easy family settlement before moving on.

The king sends counselors to bring Siddhārtha home. They find him away from the splendor that once defined his role. They speak of his obligations: to father, family, lineage, and polity. A prince must not mistake one part of life for the whole of it. People inherit responsibilities; a ruler cannot simply set them aside. The argument has weight because the men carrying it are neither foolish nor malicious. Siddhārtha answers that he has not left out of boredom or contempt. He has been confronted with aging, disease, and death. He cannot promise himself to a household's future while leaving that universal question unanswered.

The envoys urge compromise. Return now, they suggest, and perhaps seek release later, once a son has secured the royal line. Siddhārtha replies that death does not offer a postponement schedule. A person may die young. He cannot be certain that the day he imagines for renunciation will ever arrive. The ministers' plea has exposed a problem deeper than parental authority: all plans assume a length of life they do not control. He does not blame his father for wishing him back; he cannot accept that desire as a reason to abandon his search.

News of the wandering prince reaches Śreṇya, the king of Magadha, often known as Bimbisāra. The ruler meets Siddhārtha and sees what a remarkable renunciant he has become. He offers him a share of his realm. The scene is a second chance for the protagonist to enter politics by a different door: if he will not return to his own father, he may rule beside a powerful neighbor. The young man refuses. A crown, even one freely offered, cannot protect the wearer from the force that first sent him away.

Their conversation is not merely an inventory of life's misfortunes. Siddhārtha asks what it means to govern desires when their fulfillment only makes one vulnerable to losing what has been gained. He discusses the way wealth, beauty, and status excite new hunger. Aśvaghoṣa's poem is capable of celebrating rich cityscapes, yet it allows its protagonist to question what such beauties can finally do. The king speaks from within the necessary business of sustaining a state. Siddhārtha speaks from a quest he believes must take precedence over the rewards of ruling.

The prince now seeks teachers whose disciplines promise understanding rather than mere renown. He visits the philosopher Arāḍa. Here the narrative slows into a conversation about what a person is, how bodily and mental events relate, and whether liberation can be gained through refined meditative separation. The teacher is respected and learned. Siddhārtha listens carefully. But he does not consider the proposed analysis enough to explain the ending of suffering. A refined state may be difficult to achieve and still leave the root problem uncut. He seeks further instruction, including a teaching tradition associated with Udraka, and again finds that the goal he is looking for has not been reached.

He goes to a place where extraordinary austerity is practiced. He reduces food; he presses the body toward the point where it is barely able to sustain him. Some fellow ascetics admire his endurance. But weakness is not the same as knowledge. The prince looks at his own condition and recognizes a limit in the strategy of making the body suffer. If the mind cannot think clearly, torment may become another form of attachment, this time attachment to the prestige of suffering itself. The poem moves toward the idea that extremes of indulgence and deprivation are both poor guides, though its surviving Sanskrit structure and later continuations supply different details of the transition.

As the decisive night approaches, Siddhārtha prepares a place for meditation beneath a tree. A great opponent emerges in the poem: Māra, who represents the forces that prevent awakening. Māra does not simply stand for an ordinary enemy with a sword. He deploys a theater of fear and seduction. There are monstrous shapes, threats, flying weapons, unsettling sounds, and promises of pleasure. The confrontation is staged at the scale of cosmic combat, while its central physical action is a person refusing to abandon a seat. This is not a battle won by becoming the stronger tyrant. It is a victory in which the forces of agitation fail to move someone from clarity.

Aśvaghoṣa describes Māra's followers trying different methods. Terrifying bodies crowd the field. Weapons are hurled. Where violence cannot dislodge Siddhārtha, temptation attempts to accomplish what terror could not. The prince remains composed. His resistance is not a claim that other people who feel fear are deficient. It is the carefully intensified climax of a literary life in which every kind of conventional pressure has been applied: love, family, wealth, beauty, state power, ritual austerity, and now supernatural opposition.

Māra withdraws defeated. The next canto begins with the prince entering meditation in search of the ultimate truth. Through the watches of the night he contemplates former births and the wandering of living beings through repeated existence. The poem describes increasing knowledge rather than a sudden lucky guess. It turns from the visible city roads of the early chapters to an immense moral landscape in which actions lead to consequences and birth does not end the story. Compassion becomes stronger because Siddhārtha now sees suffering on a scale no single ruler could relieve by redistributing riches or improving a road.

As the night progresses, he examines the conditions through which ignorance, craving, becoming, birth, aging, and death arise. The causal insight is crucial. If suffering had no conditions, it would be impossible to transform. If its conditions can be understood, it may be possible to end the chain that produces it. The insight does not erase the pains of the people who loved him or the deaths he saw from his chariot. It changes the kind of answer he believes can be given to them. The goal is no longer to secure a privileged person against pain, but to understand a path by which beings can cease to be bound by craving and ignorance.

We have to be precise at this point. The transmitted Sanskrit text breaks early in canto fourteen, after the account of the first two watches begins. A commonly printed conclusion to the canto is supplied from other language traditions, especially material compared by E. H. Johnston. Some editions also include much later Sanskrit verses supplied by Amṛtānanda. A recording that simply declares the entire conclusion to survive in Aśvaghoṣa's original Sanskrit would falsify the evidence. The story of awakening can be told from the broader transmission; the manuscript history must travel with it.

In the completed narrative tradition, the prince awakens as the Buddha and turns outward. He has learned what he sought but must now decide whether the truth can be taught. The discovery is subtle; people are absorbed by familiar pleasures and fears. Yet if even some listeners can understand, silence would leave them with no help. That tension brings us to the next stage of the poem. The renunciant who left a family now faces the responsibilities of a teacher. His question has become an answer, and the answer itself makes a new demand.

Aśvaghoṣa was writing an ornate Sanskrit poem, not a modern itinerary assembled from independent travel records. We need to hear both the conversations and their poetry. The father and envoys have good human reasons to call Siddhārtha home; the kings and teachers offer real alternatives; Māra's army externalizes a struggle for steadiness; and the watches of the night turn a moral problem into an inquiry into causes. The prince's great journey is not measured by how far he has ridden. It is measured by the distance between his first frightened question about an old man and his decision to teach a world in which everyone ages.
