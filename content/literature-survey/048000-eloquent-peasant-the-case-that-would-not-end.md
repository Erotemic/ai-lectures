# The Eloquent Peasant: The Case That Would Not End

An original narration of the ancient Egyptian *Eloquent Peasant*, following the composite Middle Kingdom textual tradition: the robbery, nine petitions, the royal decision to collect the speeches, and the judgment preserved near the damaged conclusion. The rhetorical argument is part of the action, not an interruption of it.

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

There is a strip of land beside a river just wide enough for a traveler to pass. Water lies on one side, a field of barley on the other. If you were carrying goods to market, you might hardly notice the place. A man who controls the land beside it notices everything. He has worked out how to turn a public road into a trap.

The traveler is Khunanup, a peasant from the Field of Salt. At home he has a wife named Meret and children to feed. He has measured the grain left in their household, setting aside provisions for the family and enough bread and beer for his own journey. His donkeys carry the saleable things of his district: natron and salt, reeds, plant products, skins, and other goods that people elsewhere may need. The trip has a practical purpose. He is not seeking an audience with the king, or setting out to become a famous speaker. He wants to make an exchange and bring food home.

The man beside the road is Nemtynakht, attached to an estate belonging to Rensi, a powerful high steward. Nemtynakht sees the loads and wants them. He has no right to confiscate them, so he creates a reason. He orders a cloth spread over the narrow path. Its edge nearly touches the water; the other edge lies against the field. A traveler who continues straight will be accused of treading on the cloth. A traveler who steps aside will be accused of trespassing on the barley. The river gives no third route.

Khunanup tries to pass. Nemtynakht tells him not to step on the cloth. When the peasant moves toward the field, the man objects to that too. Khunanup points out that the road is a road, and a traveler must be permitted to use it. Then one donkey lowers its head and takes a mouthful of barley. Nemtynakht seizes the chance. An animal has eaten a little grain; he declares that the animal must be taken as compensation.

One mouthful of barley has suddenly become the price of a donkey. Khunanup protests the disproportion. He invokes Rensi, the man who owns the estate and, he says, punishes robbers. The reply is a declaration of local power: the steward may be great, but here and now Nemtynakht is the person who decides. He strikes Khunanup with a stick and drives away his donkeys and goods. The peasant cries out. Nemtynakht tells him to be quiet, because the landing place is associated with a lord of silence. Even his lament, after the theft of his livelihood, is treated as a trespass.

Khunanup spends days demanding restitution from the man who robbed him. Nothing happens. At last he takes his complaint to Rensi, whose name he has already used as a promise that law must exist above the road-side bully. Rensi is approaching his official boat when the peasant finds him. The high steward has servants, advisers, and other business. Khunanup has only his story. He asks for someone to hear it and reports what has been done.

Rensi's officials are not impressed. They describe the quarrel as a minor dispute involving a poor man and an estate worker. Perhaps the man who took the goods should be made to pay them back. The powerful men discuss the case at a distance from the person whose property is gone. Rensi does not give Khunanup a decision. Silence now comes from the higher officer as well as the thief.

But Khunanup can speak. His first great petition addresses Rensi as a man whose duty is to keep a course through danger. He turns an ordinary case into an image of a vessel steered across the waters of right conduct. A magistrate should carry those who depend on him safely to shore. He should be a protector of people who have no protector. If the high steward possesses an office associated with justice, then justice is not a favor he may choose to confer. It is what the office is for.

The language is elaborate, but the demand is not. My donkeys were taken. The man who took them beat me. I have come to the person responsible for seeing that such power is not used without restraint. Hear the case and act on it. The more Khunanup speaks, the more clearly the scale of the offense emerges. The theft of his possessions is also an attempt to make him accept a world in which a rich man's agent can convert a trick into a legal judgment.

Rensi is struck by the speech. He brings news of the eloquent petitioner to the king, Nebkaure. Now a second story begins inside the first. The king wishes to hear more of these extraordinary words. He instructs Rensi to withhold his response so that the peasant will return and speak again. Scribes are to preserve the petitions. Food is to be supplied to Khunanup without his knowing its source, and provisions are also to reach his family.

From one point of view, the king is providing for the man and preserving a remarkable literary performance. From the peasant's point of view, the silence means something different. He is still without his donkeys. He cannot see that his words are being collected as treasure. He has asked for justice, and the court has decided that his need makes good material for listening. This is the tale's central cruelty, and it is not a cruelty inflicted by Nemtynakht alone.

Khunanup petitions again. He measures Rensi against the standards of his office, describing the judge as a person who should balance what has gone wrong and set things upright. A scale is useful only when it is not secretly weighted. A guide is useful only when he does not turn the people following him toward danger. The official who takes an oath to protect truth cannot treat truth as a sound that pleases him while leaving its speaker in distress.

The second and third appeals return to the same injury, but they do not merely repeat the first. The peasant circles the problem the way a skilled speaker explores an opponent's defenses. If Rensi knows what justice is, why is he not acting? If he cannot act, why does he hold office? If he has the power to redress the injury, then silence itself has become a choice. With each return, the peasant's eloquence becomes more pointed. We hear a mind seeking a way through an obstruction as deliberate as the cloth spread across the path.

Other petitions use the language of food, household duty, river travel, and the proper ordering of the land. A person who distributes justice should be as reliable as a measured portion of grain. A person who judges should not let hunger, greed, and favoritism choose the result. The metaphors matter because Khunanup is no abstract philosopher invited to talk about fairness. He is a man whose family depends on goods that were taken from him. Balance and nourishment have material weight.

Rensi continues to hear without answering. At one point Khunanup's indignation provokes a beating by the high steward's guards. It is an important worsening. The peasant first suffered violence from the thief on the road; now violence enters the place where he has come to report that theft. If the official charged with stopping abuses allows new abuses, an appeal to authority can reproduce the very wrong that made it necessary.

As the petitions continue, Khunanup becomes more severe. He warns against greed that destroys the position it hopes to secure. He distinguishes the duty of a judge from the appetite of a collector. He addresses Rensi as someone who still has the chance to be the man his title promises, but he no longer speaks as though fine titles themselves were evidence of virtue. The longer the hearing lasts, the greater the distance between the court's admiration of his words and the justice those words ask for.

There are nine petitions in the received text. The number is not nine separate crimes; it is nine attempts to make one original wrong visible to a power that refuses to move. We need not recite every image to hear the change from hope to rebuke. The man who began by flattering a great officer becomes an accuser of an officer who can hear injustice eloquently described and still leave it unresolved. The collection of speeches shows that a petitioner may be praised for his skill while his actual claim is postponed.

In the final petition, Khunanup speaks of words as instruments by which people measure what is right and wrong. He urges Rensi to avoid falsehood, delay, and favoritism. He is nearing the end of his willingness to appeal. If the steward will not answer, the peasant will take the matter elsewhere, even into the realm of death. The appeal to Anubis is not a theatrical flourish detached from the problem. It signals that the earthly channel of justice has failed so completely that the speaker imagines addressing another authority.

Rensi orders that he be brought back. Khunanup fears punishment. Men who had beaten him before are agents of the same household, and he has just spoken with dangerous boldness. But the high steward tells him not to fear. The speeches have been preserved and shown to the king. The ruler has admired them and has returned the case to Rensi for judgment.

The late surviving lines lead to an inventory of Nemtynakht's property. The possessions of the wrongdoer are transferred to the peasant; the account then breaks off in damage. The case that began with a donkey's mouthful of barley ends, as far as the surviving text permits us to say, not simply with repayment for a small loss but with a reversal of material power. Nemtynakht attempted to use his position to take a poor man's goods. The decision strips him of property instead.

That ending should not erase the delay. The king does not discover that Khunanup has a claim only after hearing nine fine performances. He already knows of the robbery when he orders the petitions prolonged. Rensi does not learn how to receive a complaint only at the close; he has known from the beginning that the man is asking for redress. The story makes justice arrive through the very delay that has made justice more urgent. It can be read as a celebration of righteous kingship, and also as a question about the uses powerful people make of a vulnerable person's suffering.

The preserved work comes from the literary culture of Egypt's Middle Kingdom, and its written witnesses are not a stenographic report of a particular trial. The eloquence is crafted. It gives a peasant a command of language that compels the highest offices to listen, while showing that command alone cannot force a judgment. We should not turn the story into an uncomplicated handbook of Egyptian legal practice. It is literature about what justice ought to be, and what happens when the administrators of justice prefer the sound of a complaint to its answer.

Imagine again the road beside the water. Nemtynakht's cloth has disappeared from view, but the shape of the trap remains through the entire story. First Khunanup cannot move without being declared at fault. Then he cannot receive judgment without continuing to produce more words. The first snare is spread by a local thief; the second is made by men whose business is to judge thieves. The peasant's speech tests them both. By the end his goods are no longer merely goods to be sold for food. They have become the measure of whether the court will do what its office requires.

There is a difference between preserving a voice and listening to what that voice says. The Egyptian story preserves Khunanup's voice with extraordinary care. It also lets us ask why he had to raise it nine times. That question is one reason the tale can still be read as more than a clever contest of speeches. It is the story of a traveler who wanted to go home with food, and a society that admired his demand for justice before finally providing it.

## Textual basis and source notes (not spoken)

- **Primary narrative witness:** Composite Middle Kingdom Egyptian text, with major witnesses Papyrus Ramesseum A, Berlin 3023 and Berlin 3025. See the Thesaurus Linguae Aegyptiae-based composite described at https://en.mouseia.org/texts/eloquent-peasant . The line numbering varies between manuscripts/editions.
- **Named translation consulted:** R. B. Parkinson, *The Tale of Sinuhe and Other Ancient Egyptian Poems, 1940–1640 BC* (1997), pp. 58–75, available in a British Museum teaching handout at https://www.britishmuseum.org/sites/default/files/2022-10/Hieroglyphs_unlocking_ancient_Egypt_Eloquent_Peasant_full_text.pdf . This lecture independently paraphrases narrative and rhetorical progression rather than reproducing the protected English translation.
- **Narrative locators:** Opening voyage and blocked road; complaint to Rensi; first petition and king's private directive; second through eighth petitions, including corporal punishment; ninth petition; return to Rensi and inventory of the thief's property. Final manuscript lines are damaged.
- **Scholarship and material object:** British Museum, papyrus EA 10274, https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/object/Y_EA10274 ; literary account of elite readership at https://artsandculture.google.com/asset/papyrus-with-part-of-the-tale-of-the-eloquent-peasant/pQGeats9MSEXww . Do not equate the narrated royal judgment with independently observed real litigation.
