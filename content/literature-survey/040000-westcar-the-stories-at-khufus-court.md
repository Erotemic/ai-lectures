# Westcar: The Stories at Khufu's Court

A scene-first retelling of the surviving frame and episodes in Papyrus Berlin 3033, the Westcar Papyrus. The beginning and ending are damaged or lost. The accounts are literary scenes, not verified reports of actual Fourth- or Fifth-Dynasty court events.

A king has summoned his sons. Somewhere outside the room there are workshops, granaries, officials carrying messages, and the enormous machinery of a royal household. Inside, a more difficult enterprise is underway: someone must tell the king a story that he has not heard before.

The king is Khufu, called Cheops in Greek histories, whose name later generations would associate with the Great Pyramid. The storytellers are princes. They speak about extraordinary events that are supposed to have taken place during earlier reigns. The names of kings lend their tales the air of history, but the scenes belong to literary imagination: a magician creates a crocodile from wax; another parts the waters of a lake; a third refuses to demonstrate a terrible experiment on a human prisoner. The work survives on a damaged Egyptian papyrus now called the Westcar Papyrus.

We enter the frame late. The original opening is missing, and the first surviving tale is too broken to recover securely. It concerned an earlier royal court, apparently that of Djoser. We have enough of the frame to know the pattern. A prince relates a marvel from the past. Khufu is impressed, and orders offerings in honor of the earlier king and the learned person associated with the deed. The listeners are being shown how to honor the dead through remembered acts, but also how to entertain a living ruler.

One story comes from the reign of King Nebka. Its magician is a chief lector priest, often called Webaoner or Ubainer, a man whose professional authority includes sacred words and ritual knowledge. His wife has taken a lover. The narrative does not offer a calm private conversation in which they resolve the betrayal. It gives the priest a marvelous instrument of punishment.

He fashions a little crocodile out of wax and places it in the hands of a servant. At the proper time the servant drops the figure into the water where the wife's lover goes to bathe. What entered the pool as a small crafted object becomes a living crocodile of terrifying size. The creature seizes the man and disappears below the water. For days it keeps him there, alive in the grasp of a being that has crossed the boundary between a model and an animal.

The magician then summons the king to witness what has happened. Here the tale has the rhythm of a demonstration. First comes the story of the offense. Then comes the crocodile carrying its captive back from the water. The king beholds the monstrous animal, and the priest stoops to grasp it. In his hand it is only wax again. The shift is immediate, as though the world has folded around a sentence correctly spoken.

The king orders punishment. The crocodile descends once more with the man, and the wife is put to death. The episode is harsh. It makes the priest's skill and the king's judgment look formidable, while leaving us little access to the wife's perspective. A reteller should not soften those events into an innocent prank. The story's picture of royal justice is precisely part of what lets us hear how distant its assumptions can be from our own.

Another prince tells a different kind of wonder, set in the reign of Sneferu. This time no one needs to die. The king is listless. He searches for a diversion in his palace, room after room, but cannot find what will restore his pleasure. His lector priest, Djadjaemankh, proposes a day on the water with a beautiful rowing party. A boat will carry the king across the palace lake, and the rhythm of oars and the sight of the banks may lift his heart.

The voyage begins as prescribed. The rowers move together. Then one of the women drops a small fish-shaped ornament of turquoise into the lake. She stops rowing; the rest of her side stops with her. The amusement collapses over one tiny object. The king offers a replacement, even an ornament more costly than the lost one, but she wants the very thing she has lost. That request changes the problem. Wealth cannot solve it. Skill might.

Djadjaemankh speaks his words over the lake. A mass of water is folded aside, so that the bed of the lake lies exposed. Down on the bottom the turquoise ornament is found. It is returned to the woman, and the water settles back into its place. Only then can the company row on. The scale of the marvel is enormous, but its purpose is modest: the recovery of one woman's own possession.

This is a useful contrast with the crocodile tale. In one, magical command brings a violent judgment. In another, the same sort of extraordinary power is used to make a day pleasant again. The prince who tells it has offered Khufu not merely an example of might, but an example of attention. The king of the old story has to listen to a person who refuses his easy substitute.

The next prince, Hordjedef, changes the arrangement of the game. Why keep recounting powers demonstrated long ago, he asks in effect, when the king could see a marvel during his own reign? There is a man named Djedi living away from the court. He is very old, but he is still vigorous, and his skill is celebrated. He is said to know the number or whereabouts of certain hidden chambers belonging to the sacred place of Thoth. He can restore a severed head to a living creature.

Khufu orders Djedi brought to him. Hordjedef himself travels to the old man's home. The invitation is an honor, but also a summons no subject can lightly refuse. At court, Khufu wants a spectacle. He proposes a condemned human prisoner as the subject of the beheading and restoration. Djedi refuses. Whatever the rules of the story permit a king to command, the magician will not demonstrate the act on a human being.

Instead the attendants bring birds. A goose is beheaded, and its head is laid at a distance from its body. Djedi speaks. The body stirs; the head approaches; the two become one again, and the bird stands alive. The demonstration is repeated with other birds. The magician's refusal is not a failure of the marvel but part of the marvel's moral shape: the king's curiosity has met a boundary he did not set himself.

The scene turns toward secrets. Khufu wants information connected with the mysterious chambers of Thoth. Djedi cannot simply produce the knowledge at command. He explains that the person who can bring it is not yet born. A woman called Reddjedet carries three children of the sun god Re, and they are destined for kingship. Djedi's prediction moves the tale out of the realm of courtly entertainment and into succession, the question that no ruling house can treat as a harmless curiosity.

For Khufu the revelation is unsettling. The descendants who will rule are not the immediate continuation he may have expected. Djedi reassures him that his own line has time remaining: Khufu's son and grandson will rule before the promised children. The listener can hear both the comfort and the threat. What sounded at first like a puzzle about secret rooms has become news about a future dynasty.

Now the narrative shifts from the royal court to the house of Reddjedet. She is in labor, and the birth is difficult. The god Re sends divine help: Isis, Nephthys, Meskhenet, Heqet, and Khnum go to the house in disguise as traveling musicians or performers. The women enter and attend her while Khnum assists. One by one the three boys are born. The deities pronounce futures upon them, futures of royal distinction.

The household does not immediately become a palace. Its occupants have to manage children, food, supplies, and the problems of ordinary service. The disguised goddesses leave behind a sack containing the sound of future royal celebration. Reddjedet hides it. From within, the music of kingship can be heard, as if an entire accession festival has been compressed into something that can be lifted and stored in a room.

Then a quarrel breaks out between Reddjedet and a maidservant. The servant threatens to inform Khufu of the extraordinary births. Her threat matters because the prophecy has made the children politically dangerous long before they can even speak. She goes to her brother, but he refuses to help her. In the surviving version he strikes her. She goes to draw water, and a crocodile seizes her. The creature recalls the earlier tale without making the two crocodiles identical actors in one connected episode.

Her brother returns and reports the event to Reddjedet. The papyrus breaks off in the ensuing conversation. We do not have the rest of the family story or an intact closing scene in which the princes finish their entertainment and Khufu answers. We know the three sons are associated by the narrative with the founding of a new line of kings, but the missing ending cannot be restored merely because later Egyptian royal history supplies familiar names.

The work has a striking architecture. At first, the king listens while others tell him marvels about his predecessors. Then a living magician enters his court, capable of giving him a marvel of his own. The magician's greatest gift, however, is not a trick. It is a prophecy that points beyond Khufu's authority and makes the future partly independent of his wishes. Finally, the story leaves the palace and watches that future arrive in a woman's house, with divine assistance and domestic quarrels.

A court entertainment has quietly changed its center of gravity. A king who can command a magician cannot command the history that the magician announces. A woman who has just given birth has become the bearer of a future the court may fear. A small ornament lies at the bottom of a lake; a small crocodile becomes enormous; a song of kingship is folded into a bag. Size and power change places again and again.

The text does not permit us to claim that these episodes were observed in Khufu's actual court. The preserved papyrus is a later literary witness, and the rulers it imagines belong to a still earlier past. But fiction can make historical questions vivid without being a chronicle. What does a king expect from a skilled storyteller? What is a magician allowed to refuse? Does royal greatness mean punishing offenses, restoring what is lost, or hearing news that kingship will one day pass elsewhere?

Our last surviving image is not the answer. It is a frightened household after a servant's attempt to report a dangerous prophecy has ended at the water. Then the writing stops. The missing leaf is still missing. We leave Khufu's court with the feeling that a very ancient story knew how to end one scene and hold its audience on the edge of another.

## Textual basis and source notes (not spoken)

- **Witness:** Papyrus Berlin P. 3033, conventionally the *Westcar Papyrus* or *Tales of King Khufu's Court*. Both outer ends are lost; the first extant scene is already damaged, and the extant final episode ends abruptly.
- **Direct texts checked:** Bibliotheca Alexandrina, Digital Library of Inscriptions and Calligraphies, Westcar Papyrus, English text and transliteration: https://inscriptionslibrary.bibalex.org/Presentation/Monument.aspx?INS_ID=13&Lang=en&MON_ID=2857 ; Robert Barnhart, segmented hieroglyphic transliteration and English with explicit restoration notes: https://rhbarnhart.net/Westcar_trans_web.html . The former is a research institution's text; the latter is an independently prepared reading, not a substitute for a critical edition.
- **Episode locators:** lost opening and fragments before col. 2; Webaoner crocodile, cols. 2–4; Sneferu rowing party, cols. 4–6; Djedi summoned by Hordjedef, cols. 6–9; Reddjedet's labor, hidden musical sack, angry maidservant and lost continuation, cols. 9–12.
- **Editorial boundaries:** No invented finale, no assertion that the chief magician was the historical Imhotep, no treatment of the tales as verified biographical reports from the Old Kingdom. Names and details vary in translation; “Webaoner” and “Ubainer,” and “Reddjedet” and “Ruddjedet,” are alternate transcriptions.
- **Orientation:** https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Westcar_Papyrus ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Khufu ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fifth_Dynasty_of_Egypt .
