# The Book of Dede Korkut — Story I: Names, Captives, and the Courage to Return

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

At the feast of the Oghuz lords, the tents are not all alike. A ruler without a son is seated where everyone can see his humiliation. A man who has won battles may find himself helpless before the judgment of his own household. A child who can defeat an enormous bull still has no name until the old storyteller gives him one. This is the world of the Book of Dede Korkut: a cycle of heroic stories in which feats of arms matter, but the questions that stay with us are often about parents, children, wives, brothers, and the cost of holding a community together. Its stories are not chapters in a continuous novel. Heroes return, relationships overlap, and events can be told in different ways. We will follow the first cluster of tales, leaving the rest, including the violent ending of the cycle, for another telling.

Bayındır Khan holds a great feast. His guests are arranged according to whether their houses have sons, daughters, or no children. Dirse Khan is childless, and finds himself dishonored. He leaves angry, but his wife asks him what has happened. When he tells her, she does not suggest that he threaten the other lords. She suggests generosity. Feed hungry people, give clothes to those who have none, release burdens, and pray. The story describes a son being born after that change in the household's conduct. The boy grows strong. During another festival, a dangerous bull breaks loose. The child faces it and wins, and the company sees that an anonymous youth has done what grown warriors could not. Dede Korkut comes forward to give him the name Boğaç, recalling the bull. Naming is not merely decoration here. Until the feat is recognized and pronounced, strength has not become public standing.

Boğaç's rise should be a father’s triumph. Instead, the men who surround Dirse Khan become jealous. They tell the father that his son has turned against him. On a hunt, the father believes their lies and wounds his own child with an arrow. The boy is left as if dead. But his mother is not willing to accept an absence as an answer. She goes searching and finds him; healing comes in the tale through his mother's care and extraordinary help. Meanwhile the slanderers, terrified that their scheme will be exposed, seize Dirse Khan himself. The son they tried to destroy has a choice. Let his father's enemies keep him, or rescue the man who shot him. At his mother's urging, Boğaç gathers the strength to free Dirse Khan. The tale could have ended with a ruined family. It ends with the family restored, though restoration has passed through a betrayal that cannot simply be wished away.

Another feast, another hunting expedition, and another household left exposed. Salur Kazan, one of the great Oghuz leaders, rides away with his warriors. While he is gone, an enemy force attacks his camp. Livestock are driven away. His mother, his wife Burla Hatun, and his son Uruz are taken prisoner. In a heroic song the loss of a camp is a military disaster; to the captives it is a crisis of the body and home. A shepherd stands against the raiders with a sling, refusing to abandon what he has been charged to watch. When Kazan learns of the raid, pride alone cannot guide him to his family. He needs news of the tracks and roads, the memory of people left behind, and the loyalty of someone who was not sitting among the great lords at the feast.

Kazan follows the raiders. The story gives him the scale of a warrior from legend, but Burla is not a silent prize waiting at the far side of the battle. In captivity she and her son must navigate their enemies' threats. Her son resists being used to coerce her, and the threat of disgrace is as sharp as the danger of the sword. The shepherd's courage and the captive family's refusal to yield become part of Kazan's victory. Allied Oghuz fighters strike the enemy force, the prisoners are recovered, and the camp is restored. The cattle can come home, and the tents can rise again. Yet this is one of the cycle's recurring ironies: the most splendid banquet has almost nothing to say about who will protect the household when the celebrated warriors ride off together.

The next story begins with parents wishing for children and with an arrangement between two families. A boy, Bamsı Beyrek, and a girl, Banı Çiçek, are promised to one another. When they are grown, the agreement alone is not enough. They must meet as people, and the story makes Banı Çiçek a formidable participant in that meeting. She tests the young man in the arts of riding and combat; she is not persuaded by a title that has never met resistance. Beyrek proves himself, and Dede Korkut must still navigate the demands of her family. The old bard is an envoy as much as a singer. He carries words between households that can easily turn a marriage negotiation into a fight.

The wedding seems ready to complete the story, but the world interrupts. Beyrek is captured by enemies and carried away to a distant fortress. His friends and family grieve; years stretch into a long absence. Banı Çiçek is placed under pressure to marry another man, Yaltajuk, who benefits from the story that Beyrek will never return. By now the hero's problem is not simply to escape prison. He has to cross the distance between the life he left and the life other people have built around his presumed death. When he finally escapes, he comes back disguised, testing what remains recognizable after so many years. The return of a lost lover is a scene of joy, but it is also a scene of accusation: who believed, who lied, and who found it convenient for the missing person to stay missing?

The tale lingers over the moment of recognition. Beyrek has survived exile, but he cannot simply proclaim that the old promise is his to take. The people who loved him must see him for themselves; the deception that sustained his rival must be exposed. The rival flees. Beyrek reunites with his people and his intended bride, and the delayed marriage can be celebrated. The story offers an ending full of restored names and places, an ending an audience might want to carry home. Yet the book has a longer memory than this wedding. Beyrek will return much later, not as the invincible survivor of a romantic adventure but as a man caught between divided loyalties. For now, the tale grants him the return for which everyone has waited.

Salur Kazan's son Uruz, meanwhile, is troubled by something a young man in this world can hardly admit: he has not yet won renown in battle. His father takes him out into danger, intending to teach him the life of a warrior. At the moment when the lesson becomes real, the roles reverse. The son is captured while his father survives. Kazan must take news of that failure back to the household. Burla, Uruz's mother, will not accept that the fate of her son belongs to the men alone. She joins the rescue. The victory, when it comes, is not an isolated son's rite of passage. It is an action by the parents and their companions, and it gives Uruz another kind of education: he learns what his claim to honor costs the people who love him.

There is a different kind of warrior in the story of Deli Dumrul. He builds a bridge and demands payment from travelers. Even those who avoid his bridge are not permitted to escape his demands. His fame depends on having someone to defy. Then he sees a young man die and learns that an angel, Azrael, has come for the man's life. Dumrul thinks he can challenge this force as he would challenge an enemy rider. He calls for the angel and imagines a combat in which stronger muscles might settle the matter. But death does not answer to his rules. Faced with the power of God, he discovers that courage cannot mean refusing to recognize any authority above oneself.

Dumrul is offered a possible reprieve: someone else may give a life in place of his. He goes to his father and mother. They love their son, but neither will surrender their own remaining years. Dumrul then speaks to his wife. The tale does not make her bargain a reward for his boastfulness; it is the measure of a relationship that the boastful hero did not know how to measure. She offers her life. Dumrul is shaken into a prayer different from his earlier challenge. He asks not to preserve himself at the cost of the woman who chose him. The ending grants the couple a long life together, while his parents are taken. Listeners may find that ending severe. It belongs to a story whose central question is not whether the swaggering hero can strike down death, but what he discovers when another person freely offers to die for him.

Then comes Kan Turalı, a young man who wants a bride who can match his own courage. His father searches for such a woman and hears of Selcan, a princess whose hand is guarded by impossible trials. A bull, a lion, and a camel must be defeated before the suitor can claim her. Turalı undertakes the contests, each one giving the audience the sight of a man whose skill is genuine. But trials established by a hostile court are not necessarily an honest contract. After he has done what was demanded, soldiers are sent against him. The promised marriage still has to be defended on the road away from power.

Selcan is not a spectator during that attack. She fights to protect Turalı. He is wounded, and for a moment her bravery stings the pride of a man who imagined he would always be her protector. This is a dangerous moment, different from the danger of beasts in the arena. A marriage can fail even after the monsters have been defeated if one partner cannot bear the other's strength. Turalı comes to terms with the woman who has saved him. The pair returns, and a wedding can follow. The two stories of Beyrek and Turalı sit close together: one asks whether love survives a long absence, the other whether love can survive the humiliation of being helped.

The old bard's voice moves through these stories as a recurring kind of authority. Dede Korkut names a child, negotiates a marriage, speaks blessings over the living, and reminds listeners that the force of a strong arm does not preserve anyone forever. These first tales bring captives home, fathers and sons back into relation, and lovers to their weddings. They are full of victory, but the victory is always dependent on more than the hero. A mother searches. A shepherd stands his ground. A bride refuses to be a helpless prize. A wife offers what a parent will not. The next group of tales will take those same ideas into darker territory: a one-eyed devourer, brothers who do not recognize each other, and finally a division within the Oghuz that no enemy army needs to create.

## Text and evidence companion (not spoken)

**Narrative witness:** The twelve-story Dresden manuscript of the *Book of Dede Korkut* (*Kitāb-ı Dedem Korkud*), stories 1–6 for this lecture. Episode checks use the custodial Saxon State and University Library Dresden [story-by-story synopsis](https://www.slub-dresden.de/en/explore/manuscripts/kitab-i-dedem-korkut/dresdner-handschrift/inhalt) and the 1972 Faruk Sümer, Ahmet E. Uysal and Warren S. Walker edition, [publisher's contents](https://utpress.utexas.edu/9780292707870/). English text of the 1972 edition was not independently collated in full; the episode-level narrative is an original paraphrase guided principally by the repository's published manuscript synopsis and secondary documentation, not a substitution for that translation. The modernized name spellings are approximations for voice narration; scholarly transliterations differ.

**Episode map:** Dirse Khan, Boğaç and the slanderers (Dresden tale 1); Salur Kazan, Burla, Uruz and the shepherd (tale 2); Beyrek and Banı Çiçek, capture and recognition (tale 3); Uruz's capture and parents' rescue (tale 4); Deli Dumrul, Azrael, parents and wife (tale 5); Kan Turalı and Selcan (tale 6). In some translations events are compressed or ordered differently in English; this script is not a performative reconstruction of a medieval storyteller.

**Historical and cultural boundaries:** Oghuz heroic material accumulated over centuries, and surviving written witnesses are not recordings of a single ancient performance. The Dresden manuscript contains a prologue and twelve tales, whereas the Vatican witness transmits fewer. This account preserves the cycle's Christian–Muslim border-war idiom as the outlook of its literary world, not a reliable description of historical religious communities. The narrator's observations on family power and honor are present-day literary interpretation.
