# The Rubáiyát — Guided Reading: The Garden at Dawn and the Empty Glass

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

Imagine arriving at a garden while the night is still dark enough for the stars to be visible. A voice is calling for the day to begin. A tavern door should open; the sleepers ought to rise. The hour is short, the speaker insists, and the people who are gathered here will not have another morning exactly like it. This is how Edward FitzGerald's first English Rubáiyát begins. Not with the birth of a hero, the summons to war, or the opening of a royal court. It begins with the disturbing fact that time is already being spent. The sun rises whether we have worked out what life means or not.

This lecture is a guided passage through the seventy-five quatrains of FitzGerald's first version, published anonymously in London in 1859. A quatrain is a four-line poem. The Persian rubāʿī is an established poetic form; hundreds of quatrains have been attributed over the centuries to the eleventh- and twelfth-century scholar Omar Khayyam, but their authorship and arrangement are historically complicated. FitzGerald chose, reshaped, combined, and arranged material from Persian manuscripts into a single English poetic sequence. So the garden we are entering is, in one important sense, his. It is not an authenticated account of one day in Khayyam's life, nor the original order of a Persian book. For a listener, this distinction makes the experience more interesting, not less. We are hearing a great Victorian poem built through an encounter with Persian verse.

At dawn, the first command is to wake. The tavern's patrons call for the door to be opened. We have so little time to stay, they say in effect, that lingering outside is foolish. The scene has the gaiety of a gathering and the urgency of a warning. One could enjoy it as a song about drinking. But notice how quickly the drinking acquires another meaning. The drinkers are not merely thirsty; they are mortal. To open the door now is to admit that we cannot arrange for an infinite number of future mornings in which to begin living.

The year turns to spring. Trees flower. A nightingale sings among roses, and the old empires of Persian legendary kings are gone. Jamshid's great court has fallen silent. Kay Qobad and Kay Khosrow, whose names evoke the epic world later familiar to readers of the Shahnameh, are not present to answer the birds. Yet the vine continues to produce grapes, and the garden continues to bloom. The old rulers thought themselves important, and perhaps they were. The rose is unimpressed. The contrast is not simply between joy and grief. It is between the immense stories people tell about their power and the repeating, impersonal life of the world around them.

The speaker turns from kings to the living companion beside him. Bread, wine, verse, the shelter of a bough, and another human voice seem sufficient to make even a bare stretch of wilderness feel like paradise. The scene invites us to imagine a small island of affection separated from wealth and rank. The greatness of a sultan's palace has suddenly become less attractive than a few necessities freely shared. We need not turn the wine into a single fixed symbol to hear this. It can be wine, the pleasure of being alive, an image of release from fear, or a challenge to more anxious forms of piety. The point is that the speaker prefers a fragile pleasure that can be touched to a greatness that can only be promised.

Then the poems grow colder. The rose opens and scatters its petals. The hopes of ambitious people, whether fulfilled or disappointed, vanish as quickly as snow on desert sand. The grand kings become dust. The speaker imagines ruined palaces occupied by animals and flowers whose roots may be nourished by the bodies of people once celebrated for beauty or power. This does not mean that a rose literally remembers a dead emperor. It is a way of making the cycle of death and new growth impossible to look past. The spring garden is also a graveyard, though its visitors have come here to be glad.

Now the companion is asked to pour another cup. The drink would sweep aside regret for yesterday and dread of tomorrow. Friends have already died. Those still living sit where they once sat. Someday another gathering may sit where these people are now. To move into the future is to move into the place the dead have left, and then to leave such a place for people who have not yet been born. The speaker calls on us to make use of what remains. This could sound like shallow indulgence if we heard it once. Because the sequence keeps returning to graves, it sounds instead like an argument uttered by someone who cannot bear to have life wasted in hesitation.

There are voices of religious and philosophical certainty in the surrounding world. They speak of reward, salvation, judgment, and the proper way to understand the universe. The poem's speaker has visited learned people and listened to their elaborate disputes. He remembers leaving as perplexed as when he entered. This is not a carefully organized treatise against one school of theology. It is an expression of exhaustion with explanations that have failed to remove the fact of death. He does not say that a person should never ask difficult questions. He keeps asking them with remarkable persistence. But his questioning grows angry because the answers have not arrived.

The speaker imagines being carried through the heavens and seated at Saturn's height, solving many intellectual puzzles and still failing to untangle the central one: why a human life begins and where it goes when it ends. The cosmos may contain complex patterns that human intelligence can describe. None of those patterns necessarily grants a private answer to a frightened mortal. The poem's question is not whether anyone can calculate an orbit. It is whether discovering how the universe behaves tells us why we must vanish from it. The voice comes back to locked doors and veils, to a conversation between two people that lasts a moment and then breaks off.

A curious change occurs around the middle of the sequence. Instead of demanding an explanation from the sky, the speaker turns to an earthen bowl. Its lip touches his; he imagines the vessel answering. It too was once something alive. Perhaps its clay was part of a person who kissed and laughed and asked what the world meant. In the potter's marketplace the speaker watches wet clay being beaten into form, and imagines the clay begging its maker to be gentle. The metaphor is almost frighteningly physical. A clay vessel is both an ordinary thing to fill with wine and an image of the living body transformed into matter. The same object can hold the drink of the present and the memory of death.

The potter image pushes the question beyond mortality toward justice. If a maker shapes an imperfect vessel, can the vessel fairly be blamed for the form it was given? Why should a creator who controls the conditions of existence punish creatures for being the creatures he made? These challenges will become explicit later, but they are already in the marketplace where clay is struck into shape. What begins as an invitation to enjoy a cup is turning into an argument with the power that made human beings finite, vulnerable, and dependent.

And yet the speaker does not settle permanently into accusation. Again there is wine. Again there is the garden. He imagines the world as a theater of passing shadows, and human figures as brief shapes moving across its light. He tries to laugh at cosmic arguments and returns to the nearest things: a shared drink, a companion, flowers growing beside the water. The movement is repetitive on purpose. One quatrain does not deliver a final proof. Each offers a new angle on the same inescapable conditions. The voice can be defiant, tender, scornful, playful, and frightened within a few turns of the page.

In the later part of the first edition, the speaker directs an especially sharp question upward. If the road of life was set with snares, and human beings have not chosen the trap into which they were born, how can their maker condemn them for falling? A related quatrain recalls the creation of Adam and the serpent in Eden, then throws the language of forgiveness back toward the creator. There is a theological argument beneath the jest. A doctrine of divine justice becomes difficult when the speaker also imagines a divine power capable of arranging every step. Rather than translating this into a settled philosophical position, we should hear it as the pressure of unanswered questions inside a poem.

At this point the poem gives us a small dramatic scene, a chorus of pots in a shop at the end of Ramadan. The moon is not yet visible. Rows of vessels wait in the gathering darkness. Some remain silent. Others begin to speak. One asks who exactly is the potter and who is the pot. Another cannot believe a maker would shape something with care merely to smash it later. A crooked vessel asks why its maker let it emerge crooked and then subjected it to ridicule. A more hopeful speaker imagines that the creator is not as cruel as people say. These are not separate characters who will go on adventures or resolve a plot. They are philosophical positions given the voices of fragile objects.

A single image keeps changing its force. Earlier the vessel was a reminder that human life becomes dust. Now the vessels have become a community arguing about their maker. We see why the material world of this poem matters. Bodies are made of substances, and substances change hands, shapes, and uses. A rose may grow where a ruler was buried. A bowl might hold the memory of a lost lover. A pot's uneven wall may become evidence in a case against the potter. Nothing stays safely in the category of mere decoration. Every ordinary object can ask the speaker the question he was trying to avoid.

The pots' conversation breaks off when the new moon appears and preparations begin for the end of the fast. The poet then returns to the desire to be buried near the garden, to have the scent of the grape rise from his grave. The speaker acknowledges that wine and song have damaged his reputation in the eyes of others. He has repented before and broken his repentance. Spring itself seems to tear up solemn promises. Whatever position we take on the spiritual significance of wine, there is genuine self-mockery here. The speaker is not presented as a perfectly consistent teacher. He knows his habits and his failures, and he laughs at the distance between what he declares and what he does.

Near the end, spring begins to vanish. The roses of the first morning are going. The nightingale that sang in the branches is silent, or has flown where the speaker cannot follow. He imagines breaking the whole arrangement of the world and remaking it closer to the desires of the heart. That is a startling proposition. It is not merely a request for another glass or another season. It is the wish that a universe designed to end friendships, youth, and beauty might have been made differently. The earlier arguments with fate have arrived at their most ambitious form: if the world cannot be justified, perhaps it should be rebuilt.

But the poem does not end with an improved universe. The moon rises over the same garden it has crossed before, and the speaker imagines that it will rise there again long after he is gone. The human wish for continuity is defeated, or perhaps transformed into an act of tenderness. He turns to the companion who may return to this place in the future. Among the guests, when that person reaches the spot where the speaker once sat, there should be a gesture of remembrance: turn a glass upside down because he is absent. No marble monument, no immortal kingdom, no final philosophical theorem. Only an empty place marked by a cup.

That is the emotional journey of FitzGerald's 1859 Rubáiyát. It begins by calling sleepy people toward the day, grows restless with the silence of kings and sages, argues with fate, listens to clay pots dispute the justice of their maker, and closes by asking to be remembered at a gathering. As Persian literary history, it is a transformed and contested witness. As an English poetic sequence, it possesses a deliberately made path from dawn to absence. The final cup answers the first door: come in while you can, for soon enough someone else will have to open it without you.

## Text and evidence companion (not spoken)

### Edition and authorship boundary

This guided narration follows the sequence and numbering of Edward FitzGerald's **first, 1859 English edition**, 75 quatrains, in the public-domain facsimile transcription linked below. The first edition is a designed English literary work, not a verbatim translation of a single Persian collection or evidence that Omar Khayyam ordered poems into a dawn-to-dusk narrative. The original Persian rubāʿī is a stand-alone verse form; attribution of the larger body to Omar is disputed. Quatrains are paraphrased in original prose rather than reproduced or treated as the poet's autobiography. The chronological garden/day frame describes **FitzGerald's sequence**, not an authenticated medieval performance.

### First-edition quatrain map

- **I–VIII:** dawn, tavern, spring, historical kings, cyclic renewal.
- **IX–XXII:** celebrated rulers and living companions, bread and wine, vanished power, friends buried and remembered.
- **XXIII–XXXVI:** impending death, learned disputation, cosmological limits, doors, veils, clay bodies and potter.
- **XXXVII–LVIII:** time and wine, skepticism toward certainty, theater of shadows, creator and justice, predestination and responsibility.
- **LIX–LXVI:** *Kūza-nāma*, the potter-shop sequence of speaking vessels, placed at the end of Ramadan.
- **LXVII–LXXV:** burial, repentance, vanishing spring, recasting the world, moonlight, and the final empty glass.
