# Tristram Shandy — Analysis: The Art of Losing the Thread

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

What would it mean to write a completely accurate autobiography? Start at birth and you have already omitted your parents, the country they lived in, the language in which they learned to think, and the accidents that shaped the house you entered. Start with your parents and you must explain their parents. Introduce a friend and you have to say how the friendship began. Describe one incident and another incident, three years earlier, changes its meaning. A life-story that includes everything cannot begin. A life-story that begins has already made a selection. Laurence Sterne built one of the funniest novels in English around that dilemma.

*Tristram Shandy* is frequently described as a novel that anticipates much later experiments in fiction. That is a useful observation if we do not allow it to erase the eighteenth-century book itself. Sterne's work was published in nine volumes between 1759 and 1767, during a period when English prose fiction was still testing the relationship between history, memoir, correspondence, satire, and invented narrative. Samuel Richardson's letters, Henry Fielding's narratorial interventions, and other contemporary strategies had already made the storyteller's presence a live question. Sterne pushed the question so far that the act of telling became the main adventure.

Tristram is constantly announcing the kind of book he intends to write, defending the order in which he presents things, criticizing readers who demand too much speed, and addressing people he imagines holding the volumes. We are not simply given his memories; we are made participants in the management of them. He points to a detail, withdraws, returns, apologizes, boasts of his freedom, and suggests that the reader's impatience is itself part of the joke. The novel does not eliminate the narrator. It makes the narrator almost impossible to escape.

The first remarkable result is a different experience of time. The clock in the conception scene seems to promise regularity. A clock's hands move ahead at a predictable rate. Tristram's pages do not. An event may receive a sentence; the explanation of that sentence may occupy chapters; an incident of a few minutes may spread over hundreds of pages. In ordinary realistic fiction, length often suggests duration or importance. Here, length reveals the narrator's fascination. Time in the book is not measured by what a clock does, but by how memory and attention refuse to keep pace with it.

That contrast is most visible in the birth. The title promises the life and opinions of Tristram, yet before he can enter his own tale we hear about a marriage settlement, a midwife, military engineering, names, medical instruments, a clergyman, and a sequence of family theories. The long postponement makes two claims at once. Our beginnings depend on people we did not choose. And the material of identity arrives already interpreted. Tristram inherits not only accidents, but Walter's conviction that each accident has damaged him.

Walter Shandy can be read as a parody of systematic thought. He reasons carefully from a premise and scarcely notices when the premise is absurd. Having decided that noses determine fortune, he cannot treat a compressed infant nose as merely an injury. Having decided that names have destiny in them, he cannot endure Tristram's baptismal name as an ordinary error. His elaborate Tristrapaedia is another case. While Walter writes the perfect account of how a boy ought to be educated, the boy advances beyond the age the book was meant to address. Reality is not refuting every idea Walter has. It is showing that a method of living can become a way of never attending to life.

The novel's play with the association of ideas belongs to a contemporary intellectual world in which John Locke's account of human understanding was widely discussed. Sterne does not offer a faithful philosophical exposition. He makes habits of association socially visible. A clock summons sex. A word about a wound summons a diagram of a siege. A mention of a nose summons an entire mock-learned tale. A reader laughs because the connection exists in one person's mind and not another's. Communication breaks down not because people are stupid, but because the same sentence reaches different private systems of association.

The hobby-horse gives those systems a particularly sympathetic name. Walter has one; Toby has one; Tristram himself rides the most energetic horse of all. It would be easy to treat the word as a synonym for foolish obsession. But the book's interest is subtler. Toby's military reconstruction may be eccentric. It can also be a way of dealing with what he suffered in battle. The maps allow him to return to pain without giving it the last word. They preserve a soldier's sense of order in an event that damaged his body. We are free to laugh at the little fortifications on the bowling green, but Sterne asks us to recognize why they might matter to the man who builds them.

That is why Toby is morally central to a book ostensibly about somebody else. Unlike Walter, he does not make his private framework a rule that other people must obey. When Le Fever is dying, Toby's attention turns outward. He helps the father and cares about the son. His soldier's loyalty enlarges into kindness. The episode does not need a philosophical treatise. Its power comes from the gap between a man who can hardly explain his wound and the clarity with which he responds to another person's suffering. Many of Sterne's jokes seem to say that human communication is hopeless; Toby's actions suggest that communication is not the only path to understanding.

Sterne's sentimentality is therefore inseparable from his comedy, not a later correction of it. The black page mourning Yorick and the treatment of Le Fever are not accidents in a playful novel. They make us notice how a life can exceed its description. The narrator may argue with his audience and conceal a bawdy meaning behind a dash. But some events call for a silence, a mark, or the refusal of a clever phrase. Emotion becomes stronger because it emerges among apparently frivolous materials.

The page itself is one of the novel's instruments. Sterne includes a black page, a marbled page, blank space and other typographical games; he varies punctuation, draws the wandering lines taken by his story, and sometimes provides a space whose use he imagines the reader completing. This matters to a course heard as audio. A listener can be told that a page turns black in mourning. A narrator can describe how colored marbling varies from copy to copy. But no spoken sentence can make the physical volume behave exactly as the printed object does. *Tristram Shandy* is literature aware that books are material things. Its jokes live partly in the difference between words, marks, paper and the reader's own actions.

The marbled page, in particular, makes randomness visible inside a work controlled by an author. A printed novel is ordinarily reproduced as if each reader encountered the same surface. Marbling implies the opposite: no two impressions need be alike. It is an emblem of a narrative that resists the appearance of predetermined order. Yet Sterne has deliberately positioned that emblem. Even a carefully staged joke about chance remains an artful construction. This tension is at the center of his method.

His learned references perform another sort of mischief. Sterne was an educated clergyman who drew on satire, sermon, classical anecdote, medical discourse, and works such as Rabelais and Cervantes. The book contains extravagant pseudo-scholarship and episodes whose erudition can overwhelm the small event they supposedly explain. In a conventional moral treatise, learned authority stabilizes a claim. Here it is often a means of postponement. An explanation can become so comprehensive that the thing being explained is nearly lost. The joke is not that learning is worthless. It is that learning, like any hobby-horse, can be ridden away from its purpose.

The novel also depends on people who are not allowed equal control over their stories. Mrs. Shandy has her own wishes about childbirth, but the men surrounding the event talk far more than she does. Susannah's delivery of a message becomes decisive for Tristram's name; the servant can be blamed for the failure of a scheme she did not invent. Widow Wadman's curiosity is reasonable, but she must navigate the social rules that prevent a woman from asking a direct question about a prospective husband's body. And Bridget and Trim conduct a parallel courtship in the shadow of their employers. These figures have wit and agency, yet the narrator's attention is distributed unevenly. It would be a mistake to claim that Sterne simply liberates every person from the conventions he mocks.

Consider Widow Wadman from her own vantage point. A man she may marry has been wounded in a part of his body he never discusses without a map. She needs practical knowledge. He thinks the location on the battlefield is the question. The scene exposes how manners and gender expectations can make intimate truth difficult to reach. It also exposes the limits of a supposedly universal male language of military precision. Toby knows coordinates and trenches; he does not understand what the widow is trying to learn. Neither language is sufficient on its own.

There is a further tension about illness and disability. Toby's wound is a source of laughter through the constant failure to state what happened. But Toby is not a joke because he is wounded. The book makes a distinction between the narrator's delight in misunderstanding and the character's dignity. Toby remains one of its most admirable people, not because he somehow overcomes a physical injury and becomes a model of triumph, but because he is capable of affection and care throughout the life the injury has changed.

An autobiography also raises a question of authority. Why should Tristram, whose name and body have been determined by the mistakes of others, be the only person permitted to interpret the family? He is charming and unreliable in a very particular way. He constantly admits that he is managing the reader. We may trust him about a person's kindness while distrusting the explanation he gives for a misfortune. We can laugh at Walter as Tristram invites us to and still suspect that the older man is more than the caricature the son needs for his story. Tristram's account is always one performance among possible ones.

The last pages make this problem unavoidable. By any ordinary standard, the narrator has failed to deliver the life promised on the title page. He has advanced only a small way toward his mature existence. The final joke about a cock-and-bull story seems to announce the failure. Yet the reader is left with something more durable than a complete chronological summary: a set of distinct and unforgettable minds, each difficult to contain in an orderly explanation. The novel says, in effect, that the digressions are not preventing us from learning who these people are. They are the way we learn.

When critics connect *Tristram Shandy* with later experimental fiction, the connection is genuine at the level of techniques: direct address, unstable chronology, self-conscious narration, typographical play, and the collision of high argument with low comedy. But later writers did not have to wait for modernism to discover that stories can become conscious of their own telling. Sterne belonged to a longer history of literary experiment. His achievement was to make the mechanics of thought and recollection feel both ridiculous and intimate.

Here is the lasting puzzle. The more thoroughly Tristram explains himself, the farther he travels from the promised self. The farther he travels from that self, the more clearly we see his people. In a lesser book this would be mere delay. In Sterne it becomes a claim about the nature of human experience: to know someone is not necessarily to possess a completed account. It is to learn the particular turns of mind, habits of care, comic misconceptions and sudden grief by which a person inhabits the world. What we call a digression may be the most direct route toward that knowledge.

## Text and evidence companion (not spoken)

### Scope and cautions

An original literary analysis of the complete nine-volume novel. The distinction between close textual observation and interpretation is maintained. Terms such as *self-reflexive* and *experimental* describe reading effects, not proof that Sterne intended to invent twentieth-century postmodernism. Material page devices are described in speech but require viewing a printed/facsimile text to experience fully. No modern copyrighted scholarly prose is reproduced.

### Evidence loci

- Vol. I, chs. 1–4 and 21–25: conception, association, hobby-horses and the narrator's defense of digression.
- Vols. I–III: Yorick's black page; Dr. Slop, birth and nose; the marbled page.
- Vols. IV–V: naming and the Tristrapaedia; Bobby's death; sash-window accident.
- Vol. VI: Le Fever and Toby; visual diagrams of narrative progress.
- Vol. VII: traveling through France while fleeing Death.
- Vols. VIII–IX: courtship, injury, gendered indirection and the bull's closing joke.
- Textual history: 1759–1767 serial publication; different editions include intentional and unintentional changes to physical page effects.
