# La Galigo — Analysis: A World Too Large for One Book

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

If someone asks how long *La Galigo* is, the answer usually comes in the form of an extraordinary number. Thousands of pages. One of the longest epic traditions known. An immense work surviving in scattered manuscripts. The scale is real, but a number cannot explain why the work has lasted. A manuscript can be long because its author refuses to stop. *La Galigo* is long partly because its world repeatedly generates a reason to tell another story. A marriage creates a journey. A journey reaches another family. A family produces a child. A child needs recognition, ritual and a future marriage. The process can expand almost without limit, yet each new episode remains intelligible because listeners know the families and the rules by which they are connected.

We need to begin by correcting a common picture. *La Galigo* is not a single ancient book that one author wrote and that survives in a uniform copy. It is a corpus of Bugis-language epic poetry from South Sulawesi, with substantial oral and written histories. Texts are preserved in different manuscripts, most written much later than the likely emergence of the poetic tradition. The Leiden manuscript, twelve volumes and more than twenty-eight hundred pages, is exceptionally important. But it is not the entire work. The UNESCO nomination says that no known manuscript contains the whole epic. Even the longest surviving opening provides only part of it.

The scholar Sirtjo Koolhof argued that the growth of this poetry is inseparable from the nature of the society that transmitted it. Its episodes may fit together into a broad genealogical and narrative sequence while different tellings vary in length and wording. In that sense, the epic behaves less like a finished printed novel and more like a world with recognized families, settings and narrative possibilities. A composer could expand an episode by making an encounter, ceremony or negotiation more elaborate without necessarily breaking the listener's sense that this belongs to the same tradition.

Calling it an encyclopedia is helpful, as Koolhof does, provided we understand the metaphor. This is not an alphabetically arranged book of neutral facts. Its remembered knowledge is embedded in exemplary and sometimes deeply flawed aristocratic lives. Marriage rules, ritual observances, rank, visiting arrangements, seafaring and ceremonial positions all enter the poem through things people do. Where a modern handbook might explain how a community marks a child's coming into the world, *La Galigo* might tell the passage of a royal child through an elaborate sequence of ceremonies. The form permits knowledge and narrative pleasure to occupy the same space.

Consider the opening scene of the Middle World being inhabited. A purely theological account might tell us which gods created which lands. Here, beings from above and below establish lineages through marriage. Their children are not anonymous occupants. They will receive rites and become relatives of people in another court. This helps explain the epic's sense of politics. Authority is not imagined as a lonely individual appearing with a crown. It is tied to descent, alliance, ceremony and the acknowledged place of others in a network.

The geography is equally distinctive. Luwuq and Cina matter as political and familial settings. The sea between them is traversed by heroes whose ships have their own narrative importance. There are also routes between the Upper, Middle and Lower Worlds. This does not mean the poem is a straightforward report of the historical map of fourteenth-century Sulawesi. It means the epic imagines a connected cosmos through which travelers can carry both power and obligations. Its countries are settings in a mythic geography, sometimes bearing names that continue to resonate historically, but not interchangeable with the modern locations that happen to share their spellings.

Now consider Sawérigading, the great sailor. He can be made to resemble an epic hero familiar from the Mediterranean: a traveler, fighter and courtly figure whose adventures give the audience a way to see a large world. Yet the likeness has limits. His decisive voyage is entangled with his forbidden desire for his twin sister and her refusal to marry him. Her counsel sends him toward another woman, Wé Cudaiq. His conduct at the second court includes disguises, conflict, coercive maneuvers and complicated relationships with other wives. The ethical problem is not solved by calling him a hero. The tradition is interested in what the powerful do, which is not the same as declaring everything they do admirable.

Wé Tenriabéng is a particularly important test of how we listen. In a superficial summary she might become only an obstacle to her brother's happiness: the forbidden beloved who must be replaced by a permissible bride. But she is not passive. She rejects the marriage, advises a course of action, and later exercises influence from the Upper World. Her choice redirects a major portion of the epic. The story is not necessarily a modern argument about autonomy, but it allows us to see that the decisions of women are part of the causal structure of the poem, not merely a decoration around male adventures.

Wé Cudaiq presents a different problem. One version of the narrative includes her refusal after a marriage arrangement, a dispute over payment, and war. If a teller calls that a romantic victory, the listener may lose sight of how the hero's status changes the consequences of a personal wish. The poetry's viewpoint is aristocratic; it assumes a world in which dynastic marriage is part of political life. Our analysis can examine that assumption while remaining faithful to the episode. We do not have to invent private speeches of resistance to recognize that refusal, negotiation and force are different actions.

The generation after Sawérigading shows why the epic is not simply his biography. I La Galigo's birth is followed by trouble and displacement, not a promise that the celebrated child will be sheltered from every difficulty. He travels and joins the arrangements around his sister's bissu initiation. A bissu is part of an Indigenous South Sulawesi ceremonial tradition with its own concepts of ritual expertise and gendered or androgynous religious roles. English labels help a newcomer orient themselves, but they can mislead if treated as perfect translations. Nor should such roles be collapsed into a single current social category that the original texts never use.

The distinction between a narrated past and the societies that keep the story is important here. The epic is commonly described as containing pre-Islamic cosmological material. That description concerns the imagined world and the formation of its tradition; it does not imply that the Bugis people of recent centuries have lived outside Islam or that performance and interpretation stopped changing after Islam's arrival. The stories could continue to be remembered and written in communities whose religious lives had changed. A preserved old element and a later living practice are not enemies by definition.

Then there is the issue of who preserved the text we can read. The Leiden manuscript was transcribed in the nineteenth century in work associated with Colliq Pujie, also known as Arung Pancana Toa, a royal Bugis woman who collaborated with the Dutch scholar B. F. Matthes. Institutional accounts differ in how they describe the exact distribution of transcription and direction, but her involvement is central. It would be a serious mistake to present her simply as a passive intermediary between a European collector and an anonymous local heritage. The surviving artifact is part of a history of skilled Bugis literacy, royal knowledge and the pressure of colonial scholarship.

That same history makes preservation morally complicated. The Leiden volumes are housed in the Netherlands; another UNESCO-recognized manuscript remains in Makassar. Other manuscripts are in local collections, including private hands in South Sulawesi, and in institutions elsewhere. It is valuable that a manuscript has been digitized and made accessible for research. It does not follow that digital access settles who has cultural authority, what can be represented in performance, or how to care for languages and practices still maintained by communities. A scan preserves marks on pages. It does not, by itself, preserve the ability to chant the verse well.

Language matters because *La Galigo* is poetry. UNESCO emphasizes its strict meter and distinctive Bugis vocabulary. The distances that a translator has to cross include rhythm, repeated formulas, social titles, and names with inherited resonance. An English synopsis can tell us that a ship was built or a woman refused to marry. It cannot reproduce the full effect of performance, variation in formula, or the way an expert listener recognizes one genealogical strand returning. Our narrative lectures are therefore guided introductions supported by named witnesses, not claims to have rendered the poem in full.

There is an additional reason to avoid the phrase 'the original complete story'. In a tradition that could grow by composing new episodes, the idea of a fixed original may be precisely what we have the least ability to reconstruct. Scholars can compare manuscripts, study the language, identify sequences and analyze historical layers. They can describe some episodes with confidence and place others more tentatively. What they cannot do is simply take the most familiar modern adaptation, add missing connective scenes, and declare it the lost complete medieval epic. Intellectual honesty about the evidence preserves the pleasure of the actual stories.

How, then, should a modern listener approach *La Galigo*? First, learn the central relationships, not every name. Batara Guru's arrival; Wé Nyiliq Timoq's ascent; their descendant Batara Lattuq; his children Sawérigading and Wé Tenriabéng; the voyage toward Wé Cudaiq; the arrival of I La Galigo. Next, listen for repeated social events. Who hosts, who travels, who marries, who is excluded, who must be ritually recognized? Third, remember that the narrated courts stand inside an imaginative cosmology, not in a historian's reconstruction of actual palace events.

This kind of reading changes what we mean by importance. A text need not be globally influential through the same route as a European printed book to be consequential literature. *La Galigo* is a monumental poetic world whose relevance has been sustained by Bugis people and whose manuscripts document complex practices of knowledge, memory and artistry. Its importance is not only that it is very long. It is that its length gives us an elaborate account of how lives are bound together, how an imagined cosmos can be inhabited, and how a community might keep returning to a great inherited story to make sense of another generation.

At the start, the Middle World waited to be populated. At the end of our short survey, that world is full of marriages, ceremonies, ships, children, competing claims, spirits and voyages. The ultimate continuity does not reside in the victory of one sailor. It resides in the possibility that, after he arrives at one shore, someone else will have a reason to set sail. That is why a poem like this can be so much larger than any one of its manuscripts—and why a careful introduction should leave the listener eager for actual Bugis-language performances and texts rather than satisfied with a supposedly definitive English summary.

## Text and evidence companion (not spoken)

### Source and interpretive boundaries

Original English analysis, using the UNESCO registration and nomination, Leiden manuscript inventory, and Koolhof's specialist 1999 article. The narration distinguishes *Sureq Galigo* as performed/written tradition from the specific nineteenth-century Leiden NBG-Boeg 188 witness. Terminology for bissu is a cautious introductory gloss, not a substitute for South Sulawesi practitioners' own descriptions. Do not imply a single complete original, a full English translation, or an uncomplicated relationship between colonial collecting and preservation. The account of Colliq Pujie's role reflects differences in institutional phrasing: associated with or directing transcription, not singular modern author of the whole epic.
