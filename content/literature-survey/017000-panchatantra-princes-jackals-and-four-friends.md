# The Panchatantra, Part One: Princes, Jackals, and Four Friends

This manuscript follows Books I–III of Pūrṇabhadra's 1199 Sanskrit recension as translated by Arthur W. Ryder (1925). That is a **named medieval recension of a much older Sanskrit story tradition**, not the recoverable text of a single original author from antiquity. The narrator called Vishnusharman belongs to the collection's literary teaching frame. Selected embedded fables are retold while the principal frames and their endings remain intact.

A king has three sons, and they are hopeless students. They have wealth, a palace, teachers, and every reason to prepare for rule, but they have learned almost nothing about how people behave. The king is frightened by what will become of his kingdom in their hands. He calls his advisers and asks how the boys might gain judgment before it is too late. Ordinary instruction, he fears, will take years. A teacher named Vishnusharman offers a bolder plan: give him the princes for six months, and he will teach them what they need through stories.

The collection that follows is called the Panchatantra, the Five Treatises or Five Books. Its stories have circulated through many languages and textual forms. We are following one clearly identified form, the Sanskrit recension compiled by Purnabhadra in 1199 and translated into English by Arthur Ryder in 1925. Many of its component tales are far older than that version, but we cannot identify one unchanged ancient manuscript from which every later reader descended. The stories survive by changing hands, changing language, and being retold. Our job is to hear their particular intelligence without pretending that we have recovered the one first telling.

Vishnusharman does not begin with a sermon about being kind to strangers. He begins with two friends who die because someone else wants power. The first book is called The Loss of Friends. It opens with a bull called Lively, who is separated from a merchant's caravan and left behind in a forest. Lively survives, recovers his strength, and begins to bellow. Nearby rules a lion called Rusty. He has come toward the water when the strange sound makes him hesitate. The forest's king is afraid of a noise he does not understand.

Two jackals, Cheek and Victor, once connected to court office, observe the lion's discomfort. Cheek thinks it wiser not to intrude into the ruler's affairs. Victor sees an opening. Whoever can explain the unknown danger may become indispensable to the frightened king. Their argument immediately branches into another story, about a monkey who meddles with a carpenter's wedge and suffers for it. The warning sounds sensible. Victor, however, is not looking for safety. He is looking for advancement.

He approaches Rusty, measures his fear, and investigates the noise. A bull is not a supernatural enemy. Once Victor knows that, he can present himself as the servant who solved the mystery. He arranges a meeting between lion and bull. Rusty discovers that Lively means no harm; Lively finds a powerful protector. The two become companions. They talk so much that the lion begins to neglect the hunting and customary life of his court. Victor, who had hoped to rise through his usefulness, finds himself displaced by the friend he helped introduce.

There is the cruelty of the plot. Friendship itself is not the problem. The problem is that it has changed who can speak to power, who receives attention, and who has a secure livelihood under the ruler. Victor decides to destroy the relationship. He tells Rusty that Lively is conspiring against him. He tells Lively that Rusty intends to eat him. He offers each supposed evidence that can be made to look convincing. Fear will do the remainder of his work. The more each friend prepares to defend himself, the more suspicious he looks to the other.

The book repeatedly pauses for nested fables as the jackals argue over what to do. A jackal hears a tremendous booming noise from a war drum battered by branches, and discovers that an alarming sound may come from an empty object. A rabbit saves weaker animals from a lion by persuading him that another lion waits in a well; the furious predator leaps at his own reflection. A blue-dyed jackal deceives the animals into treating him as a special ruler until a familiar howl betrays him. These are not decorative stories placed between the important events. They are the instruments of persuasion with which ambitious advisers try to make particular courses of action seem unavoidable.

Finally the lion and the bull meet as enemies. Rusty springs at Lively, who answers with his horns. Cheek protests that Victor has achieved a wicked and foolish result. A counselor's task should be to prevent a ruler's ruinous anger, not to manufacture it. But the damage is done: Lively is killed, Rusty's confidence has been poisoned, and Victor has demonstrated how power may be seized through control of information. The book's title is exact. A friendship has been lost, not because either friend originally wished to end it, but because an intermediary understood their fears better than they understood one another.

The second book, The Winning of Friends, offers a different answer to the dangers of life. A crow named Swift spots a hunter laying a net with grain. He warns the other birds away, but a flock of doves led by Gay-Neck comes down to feed and is trapped. If each dove beats its wings alone, the hunter will collect them one by one. Their leader tells them to act together. The entire flock rises, carrying the net off the ground. The hunter chases, astonished, until they are beyond his reach.

That is only the first rescue. The doves fly to a mouse called Gold, whose sharp teeth can sever the cords. Gold emerges, recognizes Gay-Neck, and starts to chew through the bindings. The king asks him to free the others first. The episode gives the lesson a memorable shape: in danger, the leader's safety must not be purchased by abandoning the flock. The mouse releases them all. Then Swift the crow, who saw the rescue, approaches Gold and asks to become his friend. Gold is wary. Crows eat mice. Swift replies that even natural enemies may form a wise alliance when they have reason to trust one another.

They become friends, and soon move to another place where a turtle named Slow joins them. A deer called Spot will complete the group. Nothing about these four animals suggests obvious fellowship. One flies, one gnaws, one runs, and one moves slowly through the water. They belong to different worlds and face different predators. Yet that is precisely what makes their partnership possible. Each can do something the others cannot.

The collection is too shrewd to say that friendship means every danger vanishes. The deer is caught in a hunter's net. The crow sees what has happened and brings the mouse to cut the bindings. The turtle, anxious for his friend, arrives slowly and exposes himself to capture. The hunter, having lost the deer, catches the turtle instead. Now the four friends devise a second rescue. Spot lies as though dead by a pool, and Swift pretends to peck at the body. The hunter drops the turtle and runs for the apparently easy prize. Gold cuts the turtle loose, the deer springs away, the crow flies up, and the mouse disappears into cover. The hunter finds himself with nothing.

The shape of the second book is not that one clever animal defeats all opponents. The first rescue makes the second necessary. Concern for a friend creates vulnerability; then the combined abilities of the group answer it. The same book contains tales about misjudgment, greed, and lost wealth, including the two-headed Bharunda bird whose heads refuse to cooperate although they share one body. Its frightening little parable says the opposite of the four-friend story: if the parts of a life treat each other as enemies, they destroy what supports them all.

The third book moves from the forest and pool to political war. It is called Crows and Owls. A king of crows, Cloudy, lives with his flock in a vast tree. His enemy is an owl king named Foe-Crusher, whose followers attack the crows. The owls have a stronghold in a mountain cave and use their advantage in night fighting to terrorize their opponents. Cloudy asks his advisers what to do. One recommends peace with a strong enemy. Another recommends fighting. Another advises withdrawal. A fourth argues for holding their home behind defenses. A fifth proposes an alliance. Each has quotations and examples proving that his course is the wisest.

Then Cloudy turns to an older adviser, Live-Strong, who has listened to them all. The elder explains that each strategy might be reasonable in its proper circumstances, but the present danger calls for deception. He proposes to enter the owls' fortress as a seeming traitor. To make the story credible, he allows the crows to appear to injure and abandon him. The owls find him, apparently betrayed by his own side, and debate what to do with a supplicant who claims to hate their enemy.

The debate among owls echoes the debate among crows. Some fear that sheltering an enemy is foolish; others think a defector can be useful. The owl king and his courtiers accept him. Live-Strong is permitted to remain near the cave and gradually builds up dry fuel. He watches the fortress, learns its habits, and waits. The owls depend upon their protection inside the cave during daylight. When the crows return with fire, that protection becomes a trap. The cave is burned and the owl host destroyed.

It is a dark victory. The counsel that saves one political community works by annihilating another. We should not soften the story into a children's lesson that cleverness is always good. Nor should we assume it speaks in one simple moral voice. The book stages rival arguments about diplomacy, fear, alliance, retreat, spies, and the ethics of deceiving enemies. The outcome shows the efficacy of Live-Strong's stratagem, but the narrative also gives the reader ample reason to distrust anybody who can use friendship as a costume.

At the start, a teacher promises to educate three princes through animal tales. After three books, what have the boys heard? They have heard a lion manipulated into killing a friend. They have seen a dove flock, mouse, crow, deer, and turtle survive by caring for one another. And they have watched a war end through infiltration and mass death. These are not three versions of one rule. They are situations in which a ruler must ask what kind of relationship is possible, what an adviser wants, when fear is justified, and whether the apparent solution will destroy something worth preserving.

The Panchatantra is famous for its animals, but its subject is not zoology. A jackal can be a courtier, a crow a diplomat, a mouse a rescuer, a lion an anxious king. The animals make human institutions strange enough that we can examine them. The princes are meant to leave their schoolroom able to tell a friend from a flatterer and to understand why being clever is not identical to being wise.

## Textual edition and selections

- Sanskrit tradition: *Pañcatantra*; **Pūrṇabhadra's recension completed in 1199 CE**, translated by Arthur W. Ryder, *The Panchatantra* (University of Chicago Press, 1925); open [Ryder table of contents](https://franpritchett.com/00litlinks/panchatantra_ryder/index.html) and [Wikisource's identified recension](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/The_Panchatantra_(Purnabhadra%27s_Recension_of_1199_CE)).
- Primary episodes: Book I, main lion/bull/jackal frame and *Jackal and War-Drum*, *Numskull and Rabbit*, *Blue Jackal*; Book II, *Winning of Friends* and *Spot's Captivity*; Book III, *Crows and Owls*, the owl-cave conclusion.
- [Wikipedia: Panchatantra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Panchatantra); [Kalila wa-Dimna](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kalila_wa-Dimna) — later transmission, not a source for the chosen Sanskrit version.
- **Selection boundary:** These are detailed frame narratives with selected embedded fables, not a claim to have retold all ~80 narrative units in this recension or the lost early exemplar.
