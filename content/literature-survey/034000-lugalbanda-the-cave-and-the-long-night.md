# Lugalbanda in the Mountain Cave: The Soldier Left Behind

A standalone narration of the surviving Sumerian composition *Lugalbanda in the Mountain Cave*, Oxford ETCSL c.1.8.2.1. The text is a modern scholarly composite of ancient manuscript witnesses, not an intact single tablet. Its final section is badly broken; the story is not given an invented reunion or military victory. The title, place names, and line intervals below identify this witness.

Before the army reached the mountains, it was so large that the dust of its marching seemed to rise into the sky. Men came from the city of Uruk, called Unug in the Sumerian poem, and from its district of Kulaba. Their king was Enmerkar. He had summoned them for a distant campaign against Aratta, a city beyond difficult country, a place of riches and divine power in the imagination of the poets who told his story.

There were distinguished warriors in the army, seven men brought up in privilege and accustomed to command. With them went an eighth, Lugalbanda. He is the figure we shall follow. But the story does not begin by setting him at the head of the attack. It begins by placing him among soldiers on a long march and then taking his strength away.

To understand what follows, imagine an expedition traveling out of the irrigated plain and into high, unfamiliar ground. Along the rivers there are fields, towns, and the reassuring signs of human work: canals, boundaries, roads that lead somewhere. In the mountains the roads are not reliable. The king may have a vast army, but neither a vast army nor a king's weapons can make a sick man walk.

The poem gives its march a ceremonial scale. The signal to muster sounds. The men pour out in such numbers that the movement resembles a flood or a bank of cloud. On the seventh day they enter the mountains. Then, partway through their journey, Lugalbanda falls ill. The text describes a violent seizure of his body. His limbs will not obey him; he can neither match another man's grasp nor lift his feet. He collapses into the dust while the troops continue to move.

The others confront a problem that has no good solution. They speak of taking him back to Unug, but how could they carry him so far? They speak of taking him to Kulaba, but that is no easier. To remain in the mountains with him would endanger the expedition. To abandon him is to leave one of their own facing death alone.

So they do something painful and practical. They bring him to a sheltered place, a cave, and make it as livable as they can. They prepare a bed or shelter like a bird's nest. They set beside him provisions that sound almost like a feast: dates, figs, cheese, fat, eggs, oil, bread, sweet food, beer, and water. They leave aromatic substances and a weapon. The list matters. The men are not indifferent to him. It is precisely their care that makes the abandonment terrible. They can give him everything they have carried except the company he needs and the ability to leave the cave.

His companions depart. Some are his brothers, in the language of the poem, whether by kinship or the close fellowship of the troop. They weep and mourn as they turn away. They leave enough food for someone expected to live, but the arrangement is also like an offering laid out for someone expected to die. The poem holds both possibilities together.

Then there is silence. The expedition has gone. No signal horn can be heard, no friend steps into the cave, and the mountain that had been difficult terrain for an army becomes the entire world of one exhausted man.

Two days pass, and part of a third. Lugalbanda is still ill. Evening comes, bringing a little relief but not recovery. In the poem the coming and going of light are never mere changes of weather. Day and night are governed by divine powers. When human help has failed, Lugalbanda turns toward them.

He calls on Utu, the sun god. Utu looks down upon a world where roads and travelers, judgments and livelihoods, all depend on his light. Lugalbanda does not address him as a victorious hero demanding a reward. He asks to stop being sick. He reminds Utu how far he has traveled from family and city. No mother is there to mourn him, no father to advise him, no neighbor to come to the doorway. He is not simply frightened of death. He is frightened of disappearing where no one who loves him will know what became of him.

The plea is given long, careful attention. It asks us to stay beside him rather than hurry to his rescue. A person lost in unknown mountains may be harder to find than an animal gone missing from a familiar herd. Lugalbanda imagines his own end as a body cast down in a place without a name. He asks the sun not to let that happen.

Utu hears him. The poem says the god accepts his tears and gives him heart. But there is no instantaneous cut to the hero standing on his feet. Darkness comes and the ordeal continues. Inana, the goddess associated with Venus, shines on the cave. Lugalbanda calls to her. He imagines the home he misses: the city where his mother bore him and the people who might still be living their ordinary lives while he lies beyond them. His broken speech is partly preserved and partly lost. We can follow the force of the appeal without pretending that every damaged line can be reconstructed.

Inana answers with encouragement and sleep. The night brings another divine presence. The moon god, Suen or Nanna, takes his place in the sky. Lugalbanda's petitions stretch across the hours. He has called to the sunset, looked for help in the stars, and waited for day. A later age might describe this as a single feverish night, but the Sumerian poet makes it a journey through the divine order of the heavens. Every light becomes a possible witness to his loneliness.

At last dawn returns. Lugalbanda speaks to Utu again. The sun clothes poor people and rich people alike; it gives a traveler a way to proceed and makes the world visible. In his appeal, even someone without a human companion may have a companion in the sun. His praise is not detached theology. It grows from the problem of being unable to walk out of a cave by himself.

The narrative at this point begins to change. Lugalbanda's protective powers gather about him. The sickness gives way. He emerges from the cave, and the poem describes life-saving plants and water coming to his aid. He eats from the plants and drinks the water. The man who could not lift his feet now moves with the extraordinary speed of a mountain animal. It is a transformation in both body and circumstance: from a man placed on a bed like a corpse to a man moving alone over land that had defeated him.

Yet the return of strength does not mean the return of certainty. His companions are gone, and the road through the mountains is not suddenly clear. The poem follows him making food and fire, ordinary acts that suddenly appear as feats. He brings provisions from the cave, strikes stones for sparks, kindles coals in the open, and makes cakes without an oven. The scene is given more attention than a modern action story might give it. Someone who was left to die can now gather fuel and prepare a day's food. That is itself a victory.

The poem moves into a remarkable account of a wild bull. This is not a tournament in which Lugalbanda must defeat an enemy king. It is an encounter with the animals and divine energies of the remote land. The young man watches and prepares an offering. The gods are invited to share in what he has made. The mountain is no longer only a place of neglect; it has become a landscape in which he must learn to live, give thanks, and negotiate his place.

This is also where the surviving witness becomes difficult. Description expands into images of altars, animals, dangerous powers, and supernatural beings. Some lines are damaged; other lines have variant readings. A large stretch of the poem's later section is missing altogether. Its remaining descriptions of spirits and battle forces are vivid, but they do not tell us exactly how Lugalbanda next finds the army. We must not fill that silence with a convenient march and triumphant reunion as though the poem had preserved one.

A second Sumerian composition, usually called *Lugalbanda and the Anzud Bird*, takes up the hero's isolation in the mountains and eventually tells how he rejoins the army. It belongs to the same legendary world, and it is natural to listen to the stories together. But they are two received compositions, not pages preserved intact from one continuous novel. The broken end of the cave narrative remains broken.

That break is worth remembering, because the clearest dramatic movement of this story has already occurred. An army came into the mountains full of confidence. One man fell, and no command or military strength could save him. His brothers left provisions, wept, and went on. He survived by speaking to the powers he believed governed the sun, the night, and the life of the earth, and by recovering enough strength to work and travel again.

The poem's great scenes are not scenes of kings winning territory. They are scenes of a soldier asking for life, making food, and standing where he had lain helpless. It is a story about the limits of collective power and about care that is real but insufficient. The army's departure is neither an easy act of cruelty nor a happy rescue. Its men leave a brother whom they love because the mountains make every alternative seem impossible. After their footsteps fade, Lugalbanda must find a path no one else can walk for him.

For the moment, keep him there in the high country, carrying what remains of the food his friends prepared, the fire small in the darkness, the first strength returning to his legs. The wider campaign against Aratta has not ended. The returning of one man's life is the center of the poem we actually possess.

## Textual basis and source notes (not spoken)

- **Primary witness:** Oxford ETCSL *Lugalbanda in the Mountain Cave* (c.1.8.2.1), composite Sumerian text with English translation: https://etcsl.orinst.ox.ac.uk/section1/tr1821.htm . The English text is used for plot checking and line locators, not reproduced in the spoken narration.
- **Locators:** lines 1–70 expedition and elite; 71–140 illness, cave, provisions, departure; 141–239 prayers to Utu, Inana and Suen; 240–299 recovery, travel and cooking; 300–393 bull and divine offering; 394–499 partly damaged descriptions; unnumbered loss after line 499; proposed Segment B may be another composition.
- Do not conflate the separate ETCSL c.1.8.2.2 *Lugalbanda and the Anzud Bird* (https://etcsl.orinst.ox.ac.uk/section1/tr1822.htm). There is no documented victory over Aratta or fully recoverable ending in this witness.
- Context: Oxford ETCSL literary catalogue https://etcsl.orinst.ox.ac.uk/catalogue/catalogue1.htm ; Wikipedia orientation https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lugalbanda ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aratta . Historical dates of manuscript copies do not directly date legendary events.
