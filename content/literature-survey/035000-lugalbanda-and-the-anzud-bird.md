# Lugalbanda and the Anzud Bird: The Gift of Speed

A standalone retelling of the extant Sumerian composition *Lugalbanda and the Anzud Bird*, Oxford ETCSL c.1.8.2.2. It follows the poem's Anzud episode, reunion with Enmerkar's army, journey to Inana and surviving final instruction. The poem's concluding action is not expanded beyond the received text.

A man who has survived alone in the mountains has a question that matters more than honor or wealth. Where has the army gone? Somewhere beyond the ridges, the men from Uruk are marching under their king Enmerkar toward Aratta. They left Lugalbanda behind when he fell dangerously ill. Now he can walk again, but he is still a person without a road. He cannot follow an entire army by wishing to find it.

The Sumerian poem tells us what he does next. He seeks help from a bird so formidable that even the mountains seem to tremble when it cries. The bird is Anzud, sometimes written Anzu. In later Mesopotamian traditions this name belongs to a creature of enormous mythic power. Here the important thing is not an abstract description of what Anzud represents. It is that the bird has a home, a mate, and a child, and Lugalbanda has noticed it.

High in the mountains grows a great tree associated with the gods. Its roots reach into hidden waters; its branches shelter a nest. Anzud hunts wild bulls. The poem imagines it with fearsome teeth and talons, carrying animals that would dwarf an ordinary bird's prey. The nest is not a safe place for a human visitor. Anzud might destroy anyone it found near its young. Lugalbanda approaches anyway.

He does not plan to capture the chick. Instead he brings food, sweet cakes prepared with honey and rich meat. He feeds the fledgling, arranges it comfortably in its nest, paints its eyes with kohl, perfumes its head with cedar, and leaves the setting transformed. What might have seemed an intruder's act now looks like lavish hospitality. The human guest has offered care to the one creature most precious to the terrifying guardian of the mountain.

Lugalbanda withdraws and waits. The poem lingers over the return of the parents. Anzud approaches carrying prey, calling to its nest. At first there is no answer. Then another unanswered call. In a creature large enough to frighten gods, the absence of a small voice causes immediate terror. The bird fears that someone has taken its child. Anzud's partner cries out as well, and the sound spreads fear through the divine beings of the hills.

The parents arrive and find the child alive and splendidly tended. Food has been placed before it. The nest has been treated with care. Their sorrow turns to delight. Anzud announces that whoever has done this will be granted a great destiny. Now Lugalbanda can step forward.

He approaches the bird with extravagant praise, but the praise has a purpose. He recognizes both Anzud's overwhelming power and the protection of its family. He tells the bird that he has trusted himself to its keeping, and that its mate can be like a mother to him. There is a delicate reversal. The man abandoned by his companions has become the protector of another being's child. Through the act of caring for a household that is not his own, he obtains the chance to enter a household of protection.

Anzud receives him with pleasure and begins to offer rewards. The first is a glorious return to Uruk, like the arrival of a richly laden trading boat. Lugalbanda does not accept it. Another offer concerns strength in battle and terrifying arrows. He refuses that too. The bird offers protective armor and powers associated with war. Again he will not be satisfied. There is even an offer of abundance from a divine dairy, the rich butter and milk of the world's fertility. None of this is what the solitary man wants.

The series is almost comic. A creature that can bestow extraordinary fortune presents reward after reward, and the human beneficiary is unmoved. In most heroic stories the opportunity to acquire invincible weapons would be an obvious climax. But Lugalbanda knows the problem that actually stands before him. Without movement, without the power to cross distances, all the armor in the world could leave him stranded on the wrong side of the mountains.

At last Anzud asks what he chooses. Lugalbanda asks for tireless legs and strong arms. He wants to go where he looks, reach the places he desires, move with the speed of sunlight and lightning, and never be stopped by weakness on the road. The request arises directly from the experience that opened his adventure. Once he could not stand. Now, given a choice between riches and motion, he chooses motion.

Anzud grants the blessing and warns him not to reveal it to his brothers. Some good fortune, the bird suggests, is safest when not publicly displayed. Then the bird rises into the air while Lugalbanda takes the ground. From above, Anzud can make out the army. Lugalbanda sees the dust it has stirred. A great creature of the air has shown a human traveler the direction he could never have discovered from inside his cave.

He comes down into the ranks of his old companions. They are astonished. To them he was nearly dead and then lost. They ask what he has eaten, how he crossed the mountain rivers, how he survived lands that are fatal to someone traveling alone. Lugalbanda does not explain Anzud's gift. He answers in a strange, exuberant language of animal speed and wild food. The men embrace and kiss him, welcome him with food and drink, and have him once again in their company.

The reunion is not the end of the poem. In a few lines a much larger military problem reappears. The force from Uruk has reached Aratta and is struggling beneath its walls. Stones and missiles pour down from the fortified city. Mountains and thorny terrain make retreat difficult. Days become months, and the turn of a year passes. The great expedition that once seemed unstoppable has become an army fixed in place.

Enmerkar needs a messenger to go back to Uruk. He asks the men around him. No one volunteers. The road they have taken together is too dangerous for any one person to retrace alone. The king asks again. No one speaks. Only Lugalbanda rises. He offers to return to Kulaba and insists that he must travel alone.

Notice how the old crisis has changed shape. When the young man was sick, his companions could not carry him home. Now the entire army cannot send anyone home, and the person they once left behind is the only one who can do it. The gift Lugalbanda asked of Anzud was not a luxury. It has become the one form of help the expedition needs.

Enmerkar gives him a message for Inana, the goddess associated with the city. The king's complaint is complicated. He recalls his own connection with her and the work of making Uruk a place of walls and settled life. He asks why a goddess who once called him into that city now seems to have turned away from him. If she loves the city, why bind its fate to a king she will not support? If she loves him, why leave him trapped before Aratta? The speech is an appeal made from fear as much as pride.

The others question Lugalbanda's decision. They warn that no ordinary person can cross the mountains alone and live. He takes his supplies and goes. The song no longer needs to treat this journey as an impossible miracle at every step. We have already seen how he earned the power that makes it possible. He crosses range after range, and by midnight he is back in Kulaba, in the presence of Inana.

She receives him with affection and surprise. How has he come from Aratta without companions? He repeats the message. The king's long account of his relation to the city and goddess is now spoken through the mouth of the one soldier who could cross the distance. In a story about heroic speed, the journey matters because it carries words rather than a weapon.

Inana's answer is not simply that she will send an army of gods to win the siege. She describes a river landscape, the fish that live there, and a particular tamarisk growing near the water. The instructions concern the cutting of that tamarisk and the preparation of offerings associated with the river and the strength of Aratta. The details are strange to a modern listener, but they are important precisely because they do not resemble conventional tactics. The goddess describes an act that joins ritual, landscape, and warfare. Enmerkar will have to act on knowledge received from a place he cannot currently reach.

The surviving poem ends with a brief description of Aratta's bright walls and the stones from which its buildings are made, followed by praise of Lugalbanda. It does not give us a scene in which he carries Inana's answer back to the besieged king, nor an account of the final fall of Aratta within this composition. Those events are not ours to supply. There are other Enmerkar and Aratta traditions, but to splice their conclusions onto this poem would hide the shape of the text that survived.

What has happened is nevertheless substantial. Lugalbanda started as a man whose life the army could not save. In the mountains he found a family more frightening than any soldier. He treated its youngest member with kindness, received its gratitude, and asked not for the things heroes usually ask for, but for the ability to move. When the army itself was immobilized, he became its messenger. He did not conquer the mountains by striking them down. He learned how to pass through them.

This is why the Anzud episode is more than a tale of a clever man tricking a powerful beast. The bird's grief for its child mirrors the grief of the companions who once left Lugalbanda in a cave. In a difficult landscape, the recognition of another family's need changes what is possible. Care brings protection. Protection gives speed. Speed carries the word of a king to a goddess. The fortunes of an entire army now depend upon a gift that began with a hungry chick in a nest.

At the end the walls of Aratta still stand in the poem before us, rich with shining stone. The king still needs the knowledge carried back to him. But Lugalbanda has crossed the impossible road and reached the goddess. In one of the oldest surviving hero narratives, the decisive power is not the strength to kill a monster. It is the strength to go on living, to find another creature's trust, and to carry a message where no one else can go.

## Textual basis and source notes (not spoken)

- **Primary witness:** Oxford ETCSL composition c.1.8.2.2, conventional title *Lugalbanda and the Anzud Bird* (page heading abbreviated to *Lugalbanda*). English composite translation https://etcsl.orinst.ox.ac.uk/section1/tr1822.htm . Not a verbatim performance of any single ancient tablet.
- **Locators:** lines 1–49 isolated hero, nest, Anzud's attributes; 50–110 feeding the chick and father's fear/joy; 111–202 requested blessing of speed, secret and its grant; 203–250 reunion; 251–289 siege and volunteering; 290–344 Enmerkar's message and departure; 345–387 visit to Inana and repetition; 388–417 Inana's river instructions and final praise.
- The bird grants movement, not physical flight to Lugalbanda; Anzud flies while Lugalbanda walks. There is no witnessed return trip to Enmerkar nor complete battle resolution in the surviving ending.
- Parallel text *Lugalbanda in the Mountain Cave*, ETCSL c.1.8.2.1 https://etcsl.orinst.ox.ac.uk/section1/tr1821.htm ; scholarly catalogue https://etcsl.orinst.ox.ac.uk/catalogue/catalogue1.htm ; Wikipedia orientation https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lugalbanda ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anz%C3%BB .
