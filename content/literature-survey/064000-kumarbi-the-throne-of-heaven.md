# Kumarbi: The Throne of Heaven

A source-limited retelling of the Hurrian succession myth preserved in Hittite as the *Song of Kumarbi*, often called *Kingship in Heaven* or *Song of Emergence*, CTH 344.

The king of heaven sits on his throne. A god stands before him with a cup. It is the smallest possible picture of a hierarchy: the one who drinks, and the one required to serve. In this story, however, the standing figure will become a rival, and the one on the throne will be cast down. Another servant will rise, and he too will attack the king. The cup passes from hand to hand across generations, but the seat of power does not belong securely to any of them. Even the body of a defeated ruler may contain a weapon against his conqueror.

We know this narrative through fragments of a Hittite-language composition found among the tablets of Hattusa, reflecting a tradition rooted in Hurrian myth. Modern scholars call it the *Song of Kumarbi*, *Kingship in Heaven*, or the *Song of Emergence*. These are names for a damaged ancient poem, not three independent epics with separate plots. Several passages can be read, while others are broken or require reconstruction. The myth is related to other songs about Kumarbi and the Storm-god, including the *Song of Ullikummi*, which we have treated separately. It does not follow that all these surviving songs once formed a single continuous book with every transition preserved.

The first ruler in the surviving succession is Alalu. He sits in heaven, while Anu, a powerful divine figure of the sky, waits on him and serves his drink. Alalu holds the kingship for nine years. In the ninth, Anu fights him and wins. Alalu flees downward into the dark earth. His descent is both the end of a reign and an image of the displacement of a heavenly authority. The new king Anu now sits where Alalu sat. And before Anu stands another servant: Kumarbi, linked by descent to the defeated Alalu. For nine years, Kumarbi hands the cup to the ruler who supplanted his predecessor.

The repetition is deliberate. The verses almost invite us to expect that in the ninth year the servant will rebel. Kumarbi does. He fights Anu, who flees upward like a bird. Kumarbi catches him by the feet and pulls him down from the sky. This overthrow is more violent than a new king taking a seat. Kumarbi bites off Anu's genitals and swallows them. He thinks the mutilation has settled the succession. In the logic of the tale, if he has deprived his rival of the means to produce descendants, he has protected his own future. He laughs. But his laughter lasts only until Anu explains what has happened inside him.

Anu says that the swallowed seed has impregnated Kumarbi. Three extraordinary beings have been placed in the body of the victorious god: the Storm-god, called Teshub in the Hurrian tradition; the mighty river Aranzah, identified with the Tigris; and the god Tashmishu. The very act meant to end Anu's descendants has given them an unexpected place to grow. Kumarbi has won the encounter and become the bearer of his own challengers. A later reader may recognize a broad resemblance to Greek tales about divine generations and fathers threatened by their children, but this composition has its own sequence and exact verbal logic. We cannot simply replace its gods with Uranus, Cronus, and Zeus and announce that every detail matches.

Kumarbi's triumph begins to curdle. He has not defeated lineage; he has swallowed it. The poem's next preserved pieces describe argument, threats, and the practical problem of birth. What can be born from a body not imagined as the body of a mother? In some passages the text discusses possible exits or openings and a release that strains the ordinary language of childbirth. Modern retellings sometimes settle on a particular spectacular method of birth. The surviving lines and their editorial restorations are less secure than such a polished story suggests. We can tell that the Storm-god emerges, but not turn every gap in the tablets into a confident anatomical scene.

One especially memorable episode involves a stone offered as though it were the infant. A diorite object becomes a substitute for the god Kumarbi wishes to consume, and the stone injures or breaks his teeth. It is a cruel reversal of his first victory. When Kumarbi seized Anu, he used his mouth as a weapon and swallowed the seed that would defeat him. When he tries to use the same mouth to prevent the next generation's rise, he encounters stone. The story makes eating, birth, succession, and violence parts of a single chain. The winner cannot secure the throne because his own means of victory carry the next danger within them.

The Tigris is not merely listed among abstract gods. As a powerful river given divine life, it makes the landscape part of the extraordinary birth. Tashmishu likewise enters the lineage of powers associated with the rising Storm-god. Their appearance enlarges the event beyond a father and one child. Kumarbi has taken in a network of rival forces, and he cannot undo their kinship by refusing to name it. The text's broken condition makes their exact sequence of births and the dialogue surrounding it harder to reconstruct than the initial struggle with Anu. We should resist the temptation to write a family conversation not supplied by the tablets.

By the time the poem approaches its more damaged conclusion, a young Storm-god stands in the field of conflict. The story points toward a change in kingship, but the detailed circumstances of the Storm-god's final ascent are not preserved in the text as a continuous concluding scene. Elsewhere in the body of Kumarbi songs, the Storm-god is already paramount and Kumarbi devises new ways to challenge him. *Ullikummi*, the stone giant placed against the sky, depends upon that later situation. Such a relation among compositions is meaningful, but it is not permission to use the later poem to fabricate the lost last page of this one.

Consider again the opening picture: a ruler drinking, a subordinate standing. The apparent order of heaven is undone by time. Alalu is king; Anu serves. Anu becomes king; Kumarbi serves. Kumarbi takes kingship through a violence that makes the next contender possible. The nine-year rhythm exposes the fragility of any authority that thinks its own rule is the natural endpoint of history. Perhaps no god in the tale can possess the throne without becoming the figure another god is waiting to overthrow. That interpretation comes from the structure the preserved narrative gives us, not from a recovered final speech.

The myth's kinship with other ancient stories is worth exploring, but comparisons can conceal a composition's particular force. Its repeated cupbearers, the sky and dark earth, the swallowed seed, the river born as divine power, and the stone that frustrates devouring belong to this Hittite-preserved Hurrian tradition. We should also remember the material labor that lets us hear it at all. Clay broke, lines vanished, bilingual scholarly interpretation made the script intelligible, and editors pieced witnesses together. When the readable words stop, the story becomes a question rather than a license for invention. The throne has changed hands; a new struggle begins; the ancient poem that reached us cannot tell every final step.

## Source companion (not narrated)

- Primary surviving witness: *Song of Kumarbi* / *Kingship in Heaven* / *Song of Emergence*, Hittite tablet fragments catalogued CTH 344, notably KUB 33.120+. [Frankfurt TITUS, Hittite text corpus, CTH 344 German translation](https://titus.uni-frankfurt.de/texte/etcs/anatol/hittite/cthtr/cthtr122.htm), especially lines 9–50 for the two nine-year reigns, the overthrow of Anu, and the three pregnancies; subsequent lines are less complete.
- Independent academic comparison: Hans Gustav Güterbock, "Kumarbi" traditions and Harry Hoffner's *Hittite Myths* (2nd ed., 1998), and Gary Beckman's discussion of Hittite literature in *From an Antique Land* (2009). These contextual works are not reproduced or quoted here.
- Lacuna boundary: Kumarbi's pregnancy and the Storm-god's emergence are attested; the exact birth scene, stone-substitution episode, and concluding royal succession involve damaged passages and editorial readings. Avoid asserting a full preserved triumph scene.
- Orientation: [Wikipedia, Kumarbi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kumarbi); [Hittite mythology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hittite_mythology). A Hurrian-rooted myth in Hittite transmission is not automatically the same as a surviving Hurrian-language manuscript.
