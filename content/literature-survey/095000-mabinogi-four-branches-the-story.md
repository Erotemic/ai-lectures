# The Four Branches of the Mabinogi — The Story: Four Turns of Fortune

A single continuous retelling of the four connected medieval Welsh prose branches, from Pwyll and Rhiannon through Branwen, Manawydan, and Math. The scope is explicitly the **Four Branches**, not all the stories that later editors grouped under the name *Mabinogion*.

A prince is hunting in a forest when he hears a pack of hounds and sees a stag brought down. The dogs standing over it are not his. He drives them off and lets his own hounds eat. That small act, which might be dismissed by a man accustomed to taking what he wants, changes his life. The owner of the strange hounds appears and tells him that he has broken a courtesy owed between rulers. The prince is Pwyll, lord of Dyfed, in the west of Wales. The hunter he has offended is Arawn, ruler of Annwn, the Otherworld.

Arawn gives Pwyll a means to make amends. For a year they will exchange appearances and places. Pwyll must go to Arawn's court, live as Arawn, and defeat Hafgan, Arawn's enemy, when the appointed time comes. Pwyll agrees. He enters a household whose people believe him to be their own lord, and shares a chamber with Arawn's wife but does not assume that a change of appearance grants him a husband's rights. In public he performs his role; in private he observes a restraint that his host has not forced him to observe. When the day of combat arrives, Pwyll strikes Hafgan down and refuses to strike a second time, following Arawn's instructions. He returns to his own body and court with a new friendship and the name of a man who has dealt honorably with the Otherworld. From the beginning the tale treats kingship less as the right to command than as the capacity to keep an obligation when a simpler path is available.

One day, at a mound in Dyfed, Pwyll sees a woman riding a shining horse. His men pursue her, but her gentle pace carries her beyond the strongest horse they can mount. Pwyll rides after her himself. She does not speed up; he only exhausts his horse. At last he calls for her to stop, and she stops. Her name is Rhiannon. She has come because she wishes to marry him, not the man selected for her. She also knows how to design the next move. They arrange a wedding, but at the feast a stranger asks Pwyll for a boon. Pwyll grants it too readily. The stranger is Gwawl, Rhiannon's rejected suitor, and his request is for Rhiannon herself. The careless word has trapped the prince.

Rhiannon devises a counter-trick. At the next feast Pwyll returns in poor clothing carrying a bag that cannot be filled. He asks only enough food to fill it. When the guests cannot satisfy the request, Gwawl is persuaded to step into the bag himself. Pwyll closes it, and his companions beat the captive in the grotesque game of 'badger in the bag.' It is a cruel scene, comic to the storytellers and frightening to the person inside. Gwawl is released only after agreeing to abandon his claim and renounce revenge. Rhiannon and Pwyll marry. The bargain appears finished. Generations later the tale will show how a publicly remembered humiliation may refuse to stay buried.

A son is born to Rhiannon. On the night attendants are meant to keep watch, the infant disappears. They panic at the prospect of blame and smear blood on the sleeping mother's face, accusing her of eating her child. Rhiannon denies it, but her word cannot overcome the evidence the frightened servants have manufactured. She is condemned to sit by the gate and offer to carry guests on her back, telling each stranger the crime she is said to have committed. Pwyll does not undo the judgment. A woman whose intelligence saved the marriage is made to rehearse a false confession before visitors who know nothing of the event.

Elsewhere, a lord named Teyrnon watches over a mare whose newborn foals have repeatedly vanished. This time, when a foal is born, he keeps vigil and strikes at a great claw that enters to seize it. Then he discovers a baby. He and his wife raise the found child, naming him Gwri. As the boy grows, his likeness to Pwyll becomes apparent. Teyrnon brings him to the court of Dyfed. The truth emerges. Rhiannon's son is restored, and the boy is named Pryderi, a name linked to the care and worry surrounding his disappearance. His mother can lay aside the unjust burden. The first branch ends with the promise that the lost child will grow into the world that almost destroyed his family.

Many years and many miles away in the second branch, the giant king Bendigeidfran, also called Bran the Blessed, receives the king of Ireland, Matholwch. A marriage is proposed between Matholwch and Bran's sister, Branwen. It should bind two kingdoms. But Efnysien, a half-brother who was not consulted, responds by mutilating the Irish king's horses. His insult nearly wrecks the alliance. Bran offers enormous compensation, including a cauldron able to restore dead warriors to life, though those restored cannot speak. Matholwch accepts the gifts and takes Branwen across the sea. For a time she is honored; she bears a son, Gwern.

In Ireland, people continue speaking of the humiliation done to Matholwch. The king allows the debt to be transferred to his wife. Branwen is sent to the kitchens, made to perform hard work, and abused by those who should protect her. The original insult was another man's act; she bears its punishment. She finds a starling, trains it to carry a message, and sends word to Wales. Her brother hears and gathers an army. He is so large he walks through the sea while ships sail alongside him. The Irish watchers think they see a forest coming over the water, and a moving mountain beside it. Branwen knows what those terrifying shapes must be.

Matholwch offers a settlement. A great hall is prepared, and Gwern, the child of the two houses, is put forward as a symbol of peace. Yet a plot has been hidden in the hall, with armed men concealed in sacks. Efnysien discovers and kills them. For a moment the danger seems to have passed. Then this same man, whose first grievance broke the alliance, seizes the small Gwern and throws him into the fire. The act is almost beyond comprehension. The child who might have made peace is murdered by his own uncle. Battle breaks out in the hall, and every hope that noble gifts or elaborate agreements might resolve the quarrel is consumed.

The cauldron makes the fighting worse. Dead Irish warriors are placed in it and rise again, alive but speechless, able to return to battle. Efnysien sees the destruction he has helped unleash and at last chooses to undo something of it. He hides among the dead, is thrown into the cauldron, and breaks it from within, dying in the effort. The war does not reverse. Bran is mortally wounded. Only seven of his company survive, among them Pryderi, the child of the first branch now grown. Bran tells them to sever his head and carry it home. It speaks to them, and for years they feast in enchanted chambers where the memory of loss is strangely held at bay. Eventually the head is buried at the White Hill in London. Branwen returns to Wales but dies of grief after witnessing what her marriage and her rescue have cost. The great political alliance has left nearly every household broken.

The third branch begins among the survivors. Pryderi has inherited Dyfed. Manawydan, brother of Bran and Branwen, has returned from the war with little prospect of regaining what he has lost. Pryderi offers him a place in Dyfed and marriage to his widowed mother Rhiannon. They live together with Pryderi's wife Cigfa. After the destruction in Ireland, this small household offers a rare peace. Four people eat, hunt, govern, and find companionship. It is the most ordinary happiness these tales have allowed for a long time.

Then a mist descends. When it clears, everyone else in the land has disappeared. The towns are silent, the fields empty, the animals gone. The four remain, with buildings and possessions but no people with whom to conduct the usual business of life. They survive by hunting for a time, then travel into England and learn trades. Manawydan makes beautiful saddles. The local saddlers resent his competition. He takes up shield-making; the shield-makers resent him. He makes shoes so fine that the shoemakers organize against him. Again and again his skill produces prosperity, and prosperity brings the threat of violence from those whose livelihoods he disturbs. The four leave rather than fight and finally return to the deserted land.

A mysterious fort appears where none had stood before. Pryderi enters and finds a splendid bowl, which he touches. He cannot move or speak. Rhiannon enters to rescue him and is caught in the same enchantment. The building vanishes. Manawydan and Cigfa are left alone. Manawydan does not charge blindly at the empty countryside. He cultivates wheat. Three fields grow beautifully, but each is destroyed by strange mice at night. He guards the last field and catches one mouse, slower than the rest. He announces that he will hang it. Cigfa and a series of visitors urge mercy. He will not release it without terms. His firmness is comic on the surface: a great lord preparing a gallows for one little mouse.

The truth turns the comedy into negotiation. The mouse is a transformed woman, wife of the magician Llwyd, who has enchanted Dyfed to avenge the humiliation of Gwawl by Pwyll long ago. The grievance of the first branch has reached the next generation. Manawydan bargains with Llwyd. The captives must be returned, the people restored, the spell lifted, and the household protected from renewed vengeance. He does not need to kill the mouse to win. He needs his opponent to make and keep an enforceable promise. The land fills again with life, and the third branch ends with survival achieved through patience and a shrewd demand for repair.

The fourth branch turns north, to Gwynedd, where Math son of Mathonwy rules under a strange condition: except in wartime, his feet must rest in the lap of a maiden who serves him. His attendants include his nephews Gwydion and Gilfaethwy. Gilfaethwy desires Goewin, the woman holding their uncle's feet. To make her available, Gwydion engineers a war. He visits Pryderi and tricks him out of a herd of pigs received from the Otherworld, trading magnificent-looking animals and horses that will soon dissolve into nothing. Pryderi pursues the fraud, and war takes Math away from his court. During his absence Gilfaethwy sexually assaults Goewin. The magical war was a plan to open a room where a woman could not be protected.

Math learns the truth. He takes responsibility for Goewin's standing and condemns his nephews, turning the two men into pairs of animals, first deer, then boars, then wolves, making them live through changing sexes and generations before he restores human form. Pryderi has died in single combat with Gwydion. The boy restored to Rhiannon in the first branch, the survivor of the Irish destruction, the ruler who welcomed Manawydan, has been killed in a quarrel engineered to hide another man's violence. His death connects the four branches more cruelly than any celebration of noble descent could do.

Math needs a new maiden to hold his feet. Gwydion proposes his sister Arianrhod. She is subjected to a test and unexpectedly gives birth, under circumstances the tale leaves morally troubling and mysterious, to two sons. One enters the sea and is known as Dylan. The other is taken into Gwydion's care. Arianrhod refuses to grant him a name, arms, or a wife of human origin. Gwydion meets each refusal with a trick. Through deception he causes her to speak the name Lleu Llaw Gyffes, and later to arm him. With Math he fashions a wife from flowers: Blodeuwedd. The beautiful creation seems the solution to a problem that has been treated as one of making a woman's consent unnecessary.

But Blodeuwedd has desires beyond the role for which she was made. She takes a lover, Gronw, and together they plan to kill Lleu. Lleu is protected by extraordinary conditions, and she persuades him to disclose the special arrangement by which he can be wounded. When the conditions are staged, Gronw strikes him with a spear. Lleu becomes an eagle and vanishes. Gwydion searches, finds the wounded creature, and restores him to human form. Blodeuwedd is transformed into an owl, condemned to be shunned by other birds. Gronw is killed by Lleu despite attempting to shield himself behind a slab of stone. The fourth branch ends with the return of the injured hero, but not with a reconciliation of the people whose desires brought the injury about.

Four branches, then, but not four unrelated adventures. The first asks whether a ruler can keep faith with someone he has wronged, then shows a mother punished by a lie. The second turns a wedding alliance into a catastrophe through injured pride and retaliatory violence. The third restores a land not through another spectacular massacre but through negotiation and patience. The fourth repeats the misuse of cleverness for sexual access and leaves the reader with a beautiful woman made for a man, then punished for wanting another life. Pryderi, born, recovered, endangered, and finally killed, passes through all four stories like a fragile thread. The last time we see him, we remember the child once returned to his mother. Magic is everywhere in this world, but so are promises, debts, family loyalties, and injuries that outlive those who first inflicted them. No single king or wizard is powerful enough to ensure that his choices will end with him.

## Text and evidence companion (not spoken)

**Textual scope.** Anonymous Middle Welsh *Pedair Cainc y Mabinogi*, the Four Branches: **Pwyll Pendefig Dyfed; Branwen ferch Llŷr; Manawydan fab Llŷr; Math fab Mathonwy**. This is a complete plot telling of those four linked works, not of the eleven stories in the broader modern *Mabinogion* grouping. The episode sequence follows [Lady Charlotte Guest's public-domain translation, Project Gutenberg #5160](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/5160), used as an identified English witness, with manuscript context checked against the [National Library of Wales's White Book of Rhydderch](https://www.library.wales/discover-learn/digital-exhibitions/manuscripts/the-middle-ages/white-book-of-rhydderch/).

**Narrative checkpoints.** Pwyll's exchange with Arawn; Rhiannon and Gwawl; the stolen child and Teyrnon; Branwen's marriage, Efnysien's insult, the speaking cauldron, Gwern's death and Bran's head; Manawydan's marriage, the empty land, crafts, the golden bowl, the mouse and Llwyd; Gwydion's deception of Pryderi, the assault on Goewin, Math's punishment, Arianrhod, Lleu, Blodeuwedd and Gronw. Names vary in transliteration and translation; the narrative does not invent an ancient continuous Celtic scripture behind the medieval manuscripts.

**Textual-history boundary.** The White Book of Rhydderch and Red Book of Hergest are later manuscript witnesses to tales whose earlier oral and written histories are debated. Episode claims here are claims about the narrative. They are not documentary evidence of a historical Welsh ruler, the Irish invasion, or mythic persons.
