# Klamath: Llao, Skell, and the Heart at Crater Lake

A source-attributed narrative introduction to the Klamath story of Llao and
Skell, and to a **separate** Klamath account of volcanic fire and Giiwas. The
first account follows a Klamath-language narrative recorded from **Robert
David** and published by M. A. R. Barker, as mediated through published
scholarly summaries; Barker's original pages were **not directly available**
for this draft. The second account follows a **named contemporary Klamath
speaker**, Perry Chocktoot. This is original educational narration, not a
community-authorized performance or an invented synthesis of the versions.
The companion identifies the distinct witnesses, their access limits, and the
sources for each substantive claim.

A heart is being thrown back and forth on a playing field beside Crater Lake.

The players are no ordinary company. They belong to the beings who inhabit a Klamath story about the time when the world was being shaped. The heart does not belong to an animal killed for food, or to a warrior buried after battle. It is the heart of Skell, a powerful figure who ought to have been the last being anyone would dare to treat as a plaything. But Skell has been overcome. His enemies have taken the heart out of his body, and they are passing it from one to another.

Somewhere among the spectators are those who have not accepted his death. They are watching the game for an opening. What they need to recover is not a weapon or a crown. It is the one thing that might bring Skell back to life.

The struggle that follows will end with another body cut into pieces, another terrible mistake, and the head of a defeated being left in the lake as an island. It is one of the Klamath traditions connected with the landscape now known widely as Crater Lake, in southern Oregon.

But before we follow the heart, we need to know whose story we are hearing. The Klamath people are not figures who disappeared when this story was written down. The Klamath, Modoc, and Yahooskin Paiute people together constitute the Klamath Tribes today. Their histories and stories are held by living communities. The story of Llao and Skell has many versions, and even the names in English vary. In one recorded Klamath-language account, Llao appears as Le-w, and Skell as Sqel, a figure also identified with Old Mink or Old Marten. An English-speaking audience often knows them by the spellings Llao and Skell, so we will use those names while keeping their variants visible.

The Klamath narrator Robert David told a version that linguist M. A. R. Barker published in nineteen sixty-three, with Klamath and English on facing pages. Later researchers summarized that account and compared it with other tellings. We have those scholarly comparisons, though not the pages of Barker's book in front of us. The story we can responsibly tell here follows the sequence those researchers identify; where an older English reteller adds an episode, we will say so rather than quietly placing it in Robert David's mouth.

## The visitor and the stolen heart

Skell is not simply a strong combatant on one side of a war. In accounts of the Klamath world, the figure identified as Mink or Marten is a transformer, a being associated with preparing the world for human life and teaching people ways to live within it. He is a maker of conditions, not only a destroyer of enemies. His companion Weasel also has a role in the tale.

Across from them stands Llao, a monstrous inhabitant of the lake, with other beings under his command. His followers include creatures described as giant crawfish. The water is their domain. These are not animals placed in the story merely to add local color; they are the family and forces that make Llao dangerous.

The opening of the heart-recovery narrative turns on a visitor. Llao has a daughter. In the recorded strand summarized by later scholars, she wins the confidence of Mink, or in a variation, that of Weasel. Her beauty is part of the enticement. Her purpose is hostile. Once she has gained her advantage, she takes Mink's heart and carries it away to her father's people at Crater Lake.

There is little point in making up a courtship conversation for her, or pretending to know precisely what Mink thought when she approached. The surviving descriptions of the versions do not give this lecture a license to supply that missing dialogue. What matters for the action is the change in the balance of power. One moment a world-shaping figure has an ally beside him. The next, an enemy has reached the place in his body on which his life depends.

It is an especially hard form of defeat because the heart is not hidden or treated as sacred by the victors. Llao's people make a game of it. The object passing between the players is the evidence of their triumph. For Skell's friends, every throw is the possibility that the thing needed to restore him will be lost again.

The companion Weasel survives to seek help. He goes to Gmokamc, the creator figure named in Klamath traditions. Gmokamc advises him; allies take part in the effort to bring back the stolen heart. The narrative does not end in an easy victory of strength over strength. It becomes a problem of getting close enough to the enemy at the one place where the enemy has made himself careless: the game.

The companions enter the occasion at Crater Lake. There are too many opponents to overpower by force. There is an advantage, however, in the enemies' confidence. They have already won once. They see the heart as a trophy that can be passed around for entertainment. The people of Skell watch the exchanges and wait until it can pass into friendly hands.

Then the heart is taken from the field. The opportunity is brief, because Llao's followers can pursue. But a chain of helpers carries it out of reach. The enemy's apparent victory has produced the very chance needed to reverse it.

Skell lives again.

That bare statement is the hinge of the tale. It would be a lesser story if resurrection ended the danger. Llao still has power; the lake is still crowded with his followers; and being restored once does not make Skell invulnerable. The balance has shifted, but the conflict is not finished.

## What the later English version makes visible

A version printed by O. C. Applegate in the early twentieth century gives the ball game a sharper chase. This is an English record made by a non-Native agent; it is not interchangeable with Robert David's Klamath-language telling. Yet it preserves a vivid way of presenting the same decisive event, and it is worth hearing on its own terms.

In Applegate's account, Skell's followers mix with the assembly at the ball field while the victors throw the heart. One of Skell's allies, Antelope, finally carries the heart away with extraordinary speed. When the pursuit presses close, it passes to Eagle, and then through other helpers. A distant call announces that the heart has gone where Llao's followers can no longer reclaim it.

Think about the structure of that rescue. No single hero reaches the end of the journey alone. The strength of one runner has its limit. The next receives what the first cannot carry any farther. The enemies have treated the heart as something to be tossed about without consequence; Skell's friends turn passing it from one being to another into an act of collective care.

An even more elaborate English retelling, by Martelle W. Trager and reproduced in a National Park Service historical study, describes a challenge on the playing field and a flight that involves Fox, Antelope, Eagle, and the call of Dove. In that telling, Coyote goads the players until the heart is hurled far enough for a hidden helper to seize it. These details belong to the later English retelling of nineteen thirty-nine, not to a text this lecture has personally checked against Robert David's exact Klamath words. The broad episode is shared; its choreography changes.

## The children beneath the water

The heart is back, but Llao is not yet defeated. In the strand associated with Skell and Mink, Llao overpowers his rival again, carries him toward Crater Lake, and intends to cut him up. His children, the enormous crawfish in the water, are to eat the pieces.

This second capture changes the story's direction. Skell has already regained his life through the courage and ingenuity of others. Now he must save himself from a stronger opponent who believes the outcome is settled. He cannot rely on the enemies' game a second time. He must find an opening while actually in Llao's hands.

The story's surviving summaries agree on the outcome: Skell outwits Llao, kills him, and turns the planned dismemberment back upon the one who devised it. The later English version published by Martelle W. Trager supplies a particular trick. As Llao carries Skell toward the water, Skell complains of a small creature biting him and asks for enough freedom to scratch. Llao, so certain that his prisoner is helpless, loosens his grip. Skell uses the opportunity to strike. That is Trager's staging of the reversal; it should not be mistaken for an independently verified transcript of every telling.

Now it is Llao whose body is divided. Skell throws the pieces into the lake. But he does something still more cunning: he leads Llao's children to believe the pieces are Skell's own remains. The crawfish devour them. The father who intended to feed his rival to his children is himself consumed by them, because they do not know what they are being given.

The horror of the tale lies partly in how long the deception holds. Pieces go into the water. The children eat. Pieces go in again. The recognition that should stop them never comes. The story gives Llao no grand funeral and no final opportunity to gather his followers for revenge. His own command has made his children instruments of his destruction.

Then Skell throws in the head.

This time the crawfish recognize what has been cast down. They will not eat it. The head remains where it falls, above or in the waters of Crater Lake, and in the telling this is the origin of Wizard Island.

A present-day map calls it a volcanic cinder cone. The story names it as the surviving head of the being who ruled the lake. We need not pretend those sentences are competing versions of the same scientific textbook. One accounts for a place within a living tradition of beings, relations, consequences, and remembered landforms. The other describes the geological processes by which an island formed after an eruption. Each is asking a different kind of question of the visible island.

The ending also makes the story's moral discomfort difficult to evade. The return of the heart saves Skell, but the victory is not gentle. A trick defeats a trick. The appetite that Llao imagined would destroy an enemy becomes the means of destroying him. It is not a modern tale arranged so that everyone learns a tidy lesson before going home. It is a narrative of transformations in which intelligence can be as decisive as force, and in which the form of a landscape carries the consequences of a struggle.

## Another story: when fire reached the people

There is a different tradition associated with Crater Lake that should not be pasted into the heart story as though it were the next chapter of one fixed book. In this second strand, the mountain itself breaks under a conflict that brings fire down toward the human community.

One publicly shared account comes from Perry Chocktoot, who served as director of the Klamath Tribes' Culture and Heritage Department and told a writer for Travel Oregon a version he had heard as a child. Here the creator is called Gmo'Kam'c. In Chocktoot's telling, the creator comes through the mountain and makes rivers, trees, animals, and people. A spirit named Llao later looks out through an opening in the mountain. He sees a woman and seeks to marry her, sending a messenger to the family.

The woman refuses. She does not wish to go into the underworld with Llao. Her father refuses the request as well.

The consequence, in this telling, is not simply Llao's disappointment. His anger grows, and the violence reaches far beyond the people who answered him. He bursts out and throws fire. The surrounding forest burns. People flee toward the waters of Klamath Lake and stand in the water to escape what is coming across the land.

Here the danger reaches people who have no way to strike back at a being who can make the mountain burn. The lake is not yet the peaceful blue landmark on a tourism poster. The story's landscape is full of danger and movement. The human response is to survive the fire while the forces of the world contend around them.

Chocktoot describes several religious specialists, called kyoks, going toward the mountain to offer themselves in an attempt to end the destruction. Gmo'Kam'c intervenes. He judges the people to have lived with restraint: they have not stripped every nest of its eggs or every bush of its berries. Their way of taking what the land offers matters to the outcome. The creator fights Llao and forces him back beneath the mountain. The mountain closes. Water comes into the opening, and the new lake is called Giiwas.

The woman who refused Llao is important. Without her decision, the conflict becomes merely two supernatural powers fighting because an exciting eruption is needed. In Chocktoot's telling, it begins with someone refusing a dangerous demand, then reaches the larger question of how the people have treated their country. The intervention is connected not to arbitrary favor but to conduct that leaves room for other lives to continue.

There is also an older English fire-and-collapse narrative that circulated widely after it was written down by William Colvig. Colvig said that he had heard a story from the Klamath chief Lalek in the nineteenth century, but he also said the original record had been lost. Later versions were written from memory and altered across publications. In these accounts the rejected marriage and the conflict of powerful beings return, sometimes with Skell rather than Gmo'Kam'c defeating Llao. The earth shakes, fiery material crosses the sky, and the great mountain falls in on itself.

The resemblance is striking, but the texts should not be forced into agreement. The heart-game story explains the fate of Llao and the presence of an island. The fire-and-collapse accounts attend to a mountain transformed and to people surviving a catastrophe. Even where characters share names, we cannot use a missing manuscript as a blank space in which to invent one master version.

## Two kinds of evidence at the same mountain

The landforms give another way to tell the history of this place, without replacing the stories people hold about it.

The volcano geologists call Mount Mazama stood here before there was a Crater Lake. Over hundreds of thousands of years, successive eruptions built a large volcanic mountain. About seven thousand seven hundred years ago, a devastating eruptive sequence expelled enormous quantities of ash and pumice. As magma emptied from beneath the mountain, the summit area collapsed into a deep basin. The geological term is a caldera: a depression formed when the support beneath a volcanic summit gives way during major eruptions.

The collapse did not immediately produce the finished lake. Rain and snowmelt gradually collected in the basin. Smaller eruptions continued within it. One of those volcanic centers grew into what we now call Wizard Island. This distinction is important: the island's formation was part of **post-collapse** volcanic activity, and the lake and island took shape over time. The mountain did not simply explode one night and leave the exact modern scene behind in the morning.

The age of the eruption comes from geological evidence, including dated deposits and the relationships among layers of volcanic rock. It is not an age measured by counting how many times an individual Klamath tale was recited. Conversely, the old accounts are not merely mistaken scientific reports awaiting correction. They are narratives transmitted within a people and tied to their understandings of Country, beings, proper behavior, danger, and memory.

There is good reason to take seriously the possibility that oral knowledge retains memories of great events. People lived in the broader region when Mazama erupted; archaeological materials lie beneath volcanic deposits. Klamath accounts recorded before modern scientific explanations were well established speak of fire, a disrupted mountain, and a changed landscape. Yet we cannot date the precise words of one version to the eruption, nor claim that a ball-game episode is a literal observational record of a specific geological process. The historical question of what has been remembered and transformed across generations remains a question, not a completed proof.

Some place names make that distinction especially interesting. Giiwas is a name associated with the lake in Klamath usage; it is not a nickname bestowed by a park authority. Llao Rock, on the northwestern rim, bears a name associated with the story, but is also a geologically identifiable older lava flow. Wizard Island is a tourist and map name for the feature associated with Llao's head in the heart-game tradition. The names are not transparent windows into one fixed text. They show that a single landscape can be named and understood through more than one history.

## A sacred place and a living people

For generations, outsiders repeated that Klamath people were too afraid of Crater Lake to approach it. That statement is misleading. Klamath and other Indigenous people treated the place as powerful, and there were rules and expectations about who went there, when, and for what purposes. The Oregon Historical Society records its role in seeking visions and in religious life, as well as hunting and gathering nearby. A sacred place is not the same as a place never visited. A restriction on ordinary or casual entry is not proof of absence.

The difference mattered politically. As settlers and promoters sought to claim the lake as a scenic attraction and then as a national park, some repeated the claim that Native people had effectively abandoned it. Historical research has shown how that claim worked against the continuing relationships of the tribes to the lake. Turning sacred caution into a story of Native fear allowed outsiders to treat an inhabited cultural landscape as an empty one.

The people who held the stories continued to face pressure far from the rim. The Klamath Tribes' own history describes the treaty of eighteen sixty-four, when they ceded vast areas of land while reserving rights that continued to matter to them. It recounts how, despite their opposition, Congress adopted a law in nineteen fifty-four to terminate federal recognition and the reservation system. Tribal government and identity did not vanish because the federal government had declared an end to its relationship. In nineteen eighty-six, federal recognition was restored after decades of work by tribal members. The loss of land was not simply reversed.

This is not an unrelated political epilogue added to a fascinating legend. It helps explain why keeping the names of narrators, the language of a telling, and the place of a story matters. There is a history in which outsiders collected the stories while also asserting power over the landscape to which those stories belonged. The fact that books and field notes preserved words is important; so is the fact that the people who shared them lived through dispossession and continued to speak as a people.

A Klamath Tribes language booklet acknowledges Robert David and other elders who worked with Barker and other linguists. That recognition is a reminder that the word *collected* can conceal the person who knew the language, remembered the story, and made the recording possible. Barker deserves to be credited for his linguistic work, but the storyteller did not become anonymous because a scholarly book was published under the researcher's name.

We should also recognize the limits of the evidence available for a new narrated work. Robert David's full pages in *Klamath Texts* were not directly checked for this manuscript. Published scholarly descriptions make the central sequence clear: an enemy's daughter, the stolen heart, a ball game, its recovery, Skell's revival, the defeat of Llao, the crawfish, and the head that remains as an island. They do not authorize us to perform unrecorded dialogue or to claim a word-for-word retelling. The later English accounts carry additional episodes, and this lecture has explicitly treated them as additional versions.

## The island and the heart

Return to the playing field where we began.

The heart travels from hand to hand. To Llao's people it is the spoil of a victory, so secure that it can become a game. To Skell's allies it is a life that has not yet been lost for good. The difference between those two ways of seeing the same thing allows the rescue. Those who think the heart is a toy have made it possible for those who know its meaning to take it back.

Then the story turns one last time. The lake beings swallow the wrong body, and when Llao's head enters the water they recognize what they have done. The remains become a feature of the place itself. A visitor today can look from the rim toward Wizard Island and see the same shape that generations of tellers and listeners have understood in the story, while also learning the geological history of the cone beneath the surface.

The story is not ours to make complete by adding speeches the sources never gave us. Nor does it belong to the distant past alone. Its language, narrators, variants, and the living community connected with Giiwas are all part of what it means to encounter it carefully.

The heart was recovered. The head remained. And the water, deep within the broken mountain, is still there.
