# The Song of Roland — The Story: The Horn and the Rear Guard

A complete, spoiler-containing telling of the received Oxford-text epic, including the false peace, ambush, the horn, Charlemagne's return, the war with Baligant, and Ganelon's trial. The battle is narrated as the poem tells it, not as a documentary record of the events of 778.

There is one city left unconquered in the poem's Spain. Charlemagne has fought for seven years; the emperor's great army has taken castles and towns, and now Saragossa stands beyond his reach. Inside it, King Marsile confers with his advisers. They propose not a decisive battle but an offer that will make the Franks leave: promise tribute, hostages, and future submission, then abandon the promise when the army has gone. The messenger will be received with solemn words. A false peace may succeed where armies have failed.

At Charlemagne's camp the offer arrives, accompanied by gifts and carefully chosen assurances. The emperor is experienced enough to suspect deceit, but he also commands an army that has spent years away from home. A council of barons must decide how to answer. Roland, the emperor's nephew and the most dazzling of his fighters, urges them not to trust Marsile. He remembers earlier treachery and demands that the war be finished. Others argue for accepting the offer and returning to France. An embassy must carry their answer into Saragossa, and everyone knows that an envoy sent to an enemy who has killed messengers before may never return.

Roland proposes his stepfather, Ganelon. It is a disastrous choice. Ganelon hears not a contribution to council business but a personal attack. Roland has named him for a dangerous errand before assembled peers, and now he must go. He speaks of Roland with a hatred that continues growing after the court disperses. A quarrel over who will carry a message becomes the seed of a catastrophe. The epic has its armies, its angels, and its great ceremonies, but its war will be decided first by an injured man insisting that his grievance must be paid.

Ganelon goes with the Saracen messenger Blancandrin. On the road they talk. Their conversation begins in guarded courtesy and discovers an alignment: each would benefit if Roland were removed. Marsile wants the invader's most dangerous warrior destroyed; Ganelon wants revenge on the man he blames for his humiliation. At the Saragossa court, Ganelon performs the emperor's envoy with audacity. He survives the danger of the embassy, gains the king's confidence, and offers a plan. Let Charlemagne believe that peace is secure. As the great army marches back through the mountain passes, its rear guard will be commanded by Roland. Strike there, with enough force that even Roland cannot survive. Without its champion, the emperor's great power may fail.

Ganelon returns bearing a story of agreement. He presents gifts and assurances, and Charlemagne begins the retreat. It is not cowardice to go home after a long campaign; it is a decision made on false information. Ganelon's advice and the expectations of the court place Roland in command of the rear guard. Oliver, Roland's trusted companion, stays with him. Archbishop Turpin rides among the warriors. The main force moves toward France through the Pyrenees while the smaller company follows behind. They are proud of the responsibility, unaware that the man who suggested it has already described their position to the enemy.

From a height, Oliver sees what Roland does not yet acknowledge: enormous enemy forces gathering, their helmets and lances multiplying until the mountains seem full of them. Oliver urges Roland to sound his great horn, the oliphant. Charlemagne is not yet beyond hearing; the emperor could turn back, and the rear guard need not be crushed alone. Roland refuses. To call for help before combat would seem to him a confession of fear. He does not want the songs of France to say that he failed his duty at the first sight of danger. Oliver argues that real duty is to preserve the army. Roland hears an appeal to shame. The difference between them is terrible because neither is a coward, and one has mistaken the form of courage for its purpose.

The enemy attacks. The rear guard holds astonishingly, and the poem gives its warriors great names and brilliant deeds. Roland bears the sword Durendal. Oliver fights beside him, skilled and lucid under pressure. Turpin, a bishop in armor, encourages and fights. They answer one charge after another. The action is described as a sequence of collisions, broken lances, falling riders, prayers, and recognitions. At first the Franks appear capable of winning any struggle by the intensity of individual valor. But the opposing force is too large, and new companies arrive. Even a heroic warrior cannot be in all places at once. Men whom the poem has named lovingly begin to fall.

The battle is both triumphant and grotesque. A warrior is praised for the blow that crushes another man's armor, and a moment later he may be killed by someone the poet has only just introduced. The same names recur over bodies. Feudal loyalty is visible in the decision to hold formation around a commander who will not sound his horn. There is no quiet exit for the fighters who remain. Their courage becomes a kind of enclosure, defining who they are and cutting off the possibility of rescue.

Oliver challenges Roland again. By now the question is almost impossible: to call the emperor back would be prudent, but Roland has spent the crucial hour refusing. The companions quarrel in words that bear the pressure of affection and despair. Roland eventually resolves to sound the oliphant, not to save an intact army but so the emperor may return, honor the dead, and avenge them. Turpin agrees. The horn's cry crosses the mountains. Roland blows so powerfully that blood comes from his mouth and his temples throb. Charlemagne hears it. Ganelon tries to explain the sound away, as he must; the emperor finally recognizes the distress and orders the army to turn.

They cannot arrive in time. The rear guard is dying. Turpin offers confession, absolution, and the assurance that their deaths have meaning in God's sight, then fights until he can fight no more. Oliver is wounded and struggles through the confusion; in the fog of battle, unable to see clearly, he strikes Roland by mistake. Roland does not answer with anger. The companions know that they are nearing the end. Oliver dies. The poem has spent so long presenting their friendship that the loss seems more intimate than any number of enemies killed.

Roland remains among the dead and dying. He looks for the bodies of his peers and drags them into Turpin's presence for blessing. He fights on, then senses that death has reached him too. He tries to break Durendal so that the sword, with its treasured associations and relics, will not become a trophy for the enemy. The blade will not shatter. He lies down facing the direction from which the enemy came, his sword and horn beside him. He offers his glove to God, and an angelic presence receives the sign of submission. The greatest fighter in Charlemagne's army has died not in the emperor's sight but in a mountain pass reached too late by those who loved him.

When Charlemagne comes upon the field, the victory he had imagined as a safe return has been exchanged for grief. He recognizes Roland and the other dead. The poem gives this formidable emperor the right to weep and lament. He does not simply record casualties, because the loss is bound to his household, his honor, and his responsibility. The pursuing Franks strike the retreating enemies, and the narrative makes divine aid part of the pursuit: the natural day is extended so that vengeance may continue. To its own audience the miracle confirms heaven's approval. To a reader outside its religious commitments it reveals how completely the poem wants one army's grief to become a moral claim upon the whole world.

The story is not finished when Roland falls. Marsile is alive, maimed and desperate, and a greater ruler from abroad, Baligant, comes to support him. The poem sets up a second, immense confrontation, as though it must move from the intimate disaster of the rear guard to a war encompassing empires. Charlemagne gathers his forces. Baligant's army bears elaborate banners and names, and the two sovereigns become mirrors of worldly power. Their armies clash; champions fall; the fight narrows at last to Charlemagne and Baligant themselves. Charlemagne kills his rival and the opposing host breaks. The Franks take Saragossa. The poem reports the destruction of places of worship it attributes to its imagined enemies and mass baptism, presenting these acts as a completion of righteous victory. Its account of Muslim worship is a Christian literary fabrication, not a description of Islam.

With the enemy city taken, Charlemagne returns toward his own capital. Roland's body and the bodies of his companions are carried back and buried with honor. Victory has not changed the basic fact of their deaths. Aude, Oliver's sister and Roland's betrothed, comes before the emperor. He offers her the possibility of a different husband within his family. She hears that Roland is dead and collapses. She too dies. The epic is stern enough to allow only a moment of life to the woman whose future has been destroyed by the battle, but that moment extends the loss beyond the military roll of honor. It is not only warriors who pay for the honor of warriors.

There remains the man whose decision made the ambush possible. Ganelon is arrested and brought to trial. He refuses to be described as a simple traitor. He admits hatred of Roland, insists that he sought private vengeance for an insult, and claims he did not betray the emperor as emperor. His defense rests on a distinction between a feudal man's right to answer injury and his obligation to the ruler. Many nobles hesitate to condemn him; they would prefer peace among powerful families. If the court can label the catastrophe a private quarrel, its political structure may be spared an uncomfortable reckoning. Charlemagne demands judgment, but he too is bound to the procedures of his world.

Thierry, a relatively uncelebrated knight, challenges Ganelon's case. If a man causes a commander to be killed while serving the emperor, then he has betrayed the office and the realm, however personal his resentment. Ganelon's champion Pinabel fights Thierry in judicial combat. The encounter decides what council argument alone has not resolved. Thierry wins. Ganelon is condemned and executed with brutal violence; his relatives who backed the wrong side suffer punishment. The poem presents the verdict as justice, but its mechanism is also a reminder that the poem's world determines truth through oath, status, divine judgment, and the body of a fighter.

Queen Bramimond, brought from Saragossa, eventually accepts baptism and receives a Christian name. The story treats her conversion as a closing sign that the victorious order has reached even the defeated court. Yet there is almost no space in the poem for her account of the war. Her people's voices are speeches constructed by the enemy poet; their devotion is caricatured; their defeat is made into the victor's theological proof. We can understand the poem's designed ending without treating it as a neutral account of what anyone in Spain believed.

And even now the emperor cannot retire. Gabriel calls upon him to go to another threatened place, and Charlemagne laments the hardship of a life that demands still more war. This is the closing sound: not a satisfied empire, not a lover reunited with a beloved, not even the prolonged fame of Roland, but an old ruler who has buried his best men and is told there will be no rest. The horn that should have been sounded in time has sounded too late. The trial has named a guilty man. No verdict can unmake the dead, and the poem knows that a just cause, as it understands justice, does not guarantee an end to grief.

## Text and evidence companion (not spoken)

**Controlling narrative.** The Oxford tradition of the Old French *Chanson de Roland*, accessed in the public-domain [C. K. Scott-Moncrieff English translation, Project Gutenberg #391](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/391). Narrative events are grouped into one continuous retelling, not into separate laisses or episodes. The account is explicitly of the **poem's** Muslims and Frankish holy war, not of Islam or the historical eighth-century battle.

**Plot checkpoints.** Marsile's offer and the council; Ganelon's hostility and mission; betrayal plot with Blancandrin and Marsile; rear-guard assignment; Oliver's request and Roland's refusal; battle, delayed horn, Turpin and Oliver's deaths; Roland's death with Durendal and glove; Charlemagne's return; Baligant's defeat and Saragossa; Aude's death; Ganelon's claim of private vengeance, trial by combat, and punishment; Bramimond and Gabriel's closing summons. The medieval poem's great set-piece battle is not evidence of army numbers or faith practices.

**Textual and historical orientation.** David Raybin, [“The Song of Roland,” Oxford Bibliographies in Medieval Studies](https://doi.org/10.1093/obo/9780195396584-0207) (2016), on the importance of Oxford Bodleian MS Digby 23 and the chanson de geste tradition. [Wikipedia: The Song of Roland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Song_of_Roland) supplies general orientation only. Consult manuscript scholarship for exact Old French wording; no modern English translation is quoted verbatim here.
