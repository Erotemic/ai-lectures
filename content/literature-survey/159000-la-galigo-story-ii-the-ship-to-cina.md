# La Galigo — Story II: The Ship to Cina and the Child of the Epic

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

By the time Sawérigading is ready to sail, the Middle World has acquired a difficulty its first founders could never avoid: kinship creates both intimacy and limits. The son of the royal house of Luwuq has returned from journeys through extraordinary places, and the person he desires is his own twin sister, Wé Tenriabéng. His desire cannot make the marriage permissible. His sister does not consent to become the solution to his longing. What happens next will carry him across the sea, draw other kingdoms into conflict, and eventually bring into the story the child whose name is used for the entire great cycle: I La Galigo.

This telling stays close to the connected episodes summarized for the Leiden manuscript's later volumes, especially seven through twelve. It is not the ending of the whole *La Galigo* tradition. Think of it as a sustained narrative from one surviving sequence of an immense work, in which the hero's search for a bride becomes a story about other people's choices and the costs of ignoring them.

Wé Tenriabéng advises her brother to seek Wé Cudaiq, a princess of Cina. This Cina belongs to the geography of the Bugis epic, not a simple modern reference to China as a state. The prospect is alluring because the princess resembles the sister Sawérigading cannot marry. But resemblance is already a warning. If the hero travels to escape a forbidden desire while expecting to find the same beloved elsewhere, will he really have changed his understanding of love? Or will the next woman enter his story as a substitute imagined before he meets her?

The voyage requires a ship. In the tradition, a great tree, often named the wélenréng, becomes the timber for Sawérigading's vessel. It is one of the enduring images attached to his journeys. Think of the practical problem of seafaring raised to epic scale: one needs a material body strong enough to cross water, but the means of making that body belongs to an animate, storied world. A ship is not merely a prop waiting beside the shore. Building it becomes an episode connecting power, landscape and movement.

The sister's role does not end when she gives the advice. The accounts describe her ascent to the Upper World, and from there her influence continues across the boundaries that earlier generations crossed in establishing the Middle World. She has refused one role—wife to her twin—and taken up another, as someone whose knowledge and power can help to redirect the story. The heroine of the ship's departure is therefore not simply the princess the sailor is going to meet. She is also the sister who makes the journey necessary and whose powers remain important when the hero encounters difficulty.

Sawérigading sails and fights. The voyage is not a quiet progression from port to port. Sea crossings in *La Galigo* are opportunities for displays of power and for confrontations with other rulers. The hero's standing at home does not guarantee obedience abroad. Every shore is another court with its own loyalties and claims. The ocean connects these courts, but connection does not mean peace. In epic time, a journey may expand into several battles before the traveler reaches the person he seeks.

At Cina, the hero does not begin by presenting a simple, transparent self. In one episode he assumes a disguise, passing as a merchant and using a mask associated with another ruler to obtain access to the palace. The details matter because they cast a shadow over what could otherwise be told as a tale of bold courtship. Sawérigading wants to see the princess before the public terms of marriage are settled. He uses performance and deception to achieve the encounter. We should not replace this with an invented love-at-first-sight conversation. The information the manuscript summaries give is enough to establish both his initiative and the troubling imbalance in his approach.

The marriage negotiation is complicated. A proposal is accepted, and a substantial bride-price or dowry arrangement occupies the story. Time passes as obligations are met. Then Wé Cudaiq refuses the marriage. Here we reach the episode's crucial test. The hero has crossed seas, fought opponents and secured what appears to be an agreement. Yet the woman he desires does not become his merely because he has fulfilled what other people demanded. Refusal is an action, even when the narrative surrounds it with expectations and pressures.

The arrangements for returning the marriage payment become an occasion for renewed dispute. The people of Cina claim the repayment is complete when it is not; Sawérigading responds with war and prevails. One could narrate this as a man's victory restoring the course of a thwarted romance. But such a telling would hide the sequence's moral problem. The breakdown of a marriage negotiation has become a military conflict. The princess's rejection has not vanished because the soldier is victorious. There is no need to invent the princess's inner thoughts to see that the conflict involves more than offended heroic pride.

The epic does not confine Sawérigading to the role of a patient suitor. He enters Wé Cudaiq's space by supernatural means, taking the form of a breeze. This is another movement across a boundary that the person on the other side has not opened. It is striking beside the story of his sister. At Luwuq, a woman close to him sets a limit and advises another route. At Cina, he tries to find ways around a woman's refusal. There is a large difference between recounting what happens in the episode and celebrating every action of its hero.

Wé Tenriabéng's power again reaches the world below. In the Leiden summary, she is associated with bringing down the land and palace of Malimongeng from heaven. The story's logic is not that magic makes people free of consequences. Magic multiplies the possible rearrangements of the social world. Palaces and territories can be moved, while the essential problem of relationships has still not been solved.

At this point the family picture becomes more crowded. Sawérigading marries another woman, I Wé Cimpau, a queen of Lémpang. Yet relations with Wé Cudaiq are eventually resumed. The Leiden account places a reconciliation several months later, followed by visits. The result is not the neat fairy-tale pattern in which one rejected match gives way to one stable new marriage. In the epic's world, several unions, competing households and different lines of descent coexist. Each adds a new claim to recognition and a new possibility of jealousy.

Wé Cudaiq becomes pregnant and gives birth to a son, I La Galigo. But the event that sounds, from a distance, like the triumphant arrival of the title character is immediately unhappy. The mother rejects the infant and he is sent away. He is later brought back, and the wider households become entangled. Once again the birth of a royal child does not guarantee a harmonious family. This cycle began with the ceremonial honoring of a newborn son in Luwuq; now it confronts us with a son whose place at the center of a household is insecure.

Sawérigading's other marriage also produces a daughter. Wé Cudaiq's hostility toward her rival becomes part of the story. Such episodes are difficult to package as the simple private failings of a few characters, because each union is attached to a court, a royal lineage and other people who will be affected by the conflict. The families have political weight. A quarrel in a bedroom is connected to the status of children, the inheritance of power and the stability of alliances.

Then the focus shifts to another member of the next generation. Wé Cudaiq bears daughters, including Wé Tenridio, whose story becomes bound to a bissu role. In the Leiden narrative she is marked by the presence of a powerful spirit and is to undergo an initiation. The bissu belong to a distinctive religious and ceremonial world in South Sulawesi. We cannot reduce the role to an English equivalent such as priest, shaman, or a modern gender identity without losing what makes this institution local. Nor should a very short overview pretend to reconstruct a ritual the manuscript summary does not fully describe.

I La Galigo, now more than a newborn name, participates in making this ceremonial future possible. He travels to Luwuq to obtain special attributes for his sister's bissu initiation. He also marries in the course of his journey. A house is prepared for the initiation after his return. Think about the motion of the narrative: Sawérigading sailed because of a conflict over marriage; his son journeys for an occasion that ties kinship to religious and ceremonial responsibility. The same sea that carried a troubled courtship can carry the arrangements for another person's recognized place in the world.

This is where we stop our account of the Leiden manuscript's opening collection, not because all the adventures of the epic are over, but because it gives a meaningful boundary. A voyage has brought a hero to a foreign court, entangled several families, produced children and connected a new generation with ritual office. It has carried us from the impossible desire of a brother toward his sister to a daughter being prepared for a very different social role.

Some other surviving manuscripts preserve episodes outside this sequence. One important fragment in Makassar, also recognized in the UNESCO registration, concerns a journey by Sawérigading and I La Galigo to Senrijawa for a wedding and the initiation of a bissu. It is another example of how these people keep traveling for acts that combine ceremony, kinship and obligation. But the story as preserved there is its own manuscript episode. We should not simply paste it into the end of the Leiden telling and call the result an original, complete novel.

A story this wide changes the meaning of what it is to finish a story. Individual voyages may conclude; a marriage may be negotiated; a ceremony may be performed. The larger poetic world remains open to another departure, another child, another dispute over status and another song. Sawérigading's fame is genuine, but he is not the only maker of events. Wé Tenriabéng's refusal and intervention, Wé Cudaiq's opposition, the disputes among households, and the younger generation's new responsibilities move the narrative as powerfully as his sword or ship.

The ship to Cina has reached land. The sailor has won battles and made a family. Yet the most revealing event may be that the family did not become simple once the voyage was completed. The ocean was large, but the network of obligations waiting on either side was larger. That network is what the poem keeps exploring, again and again, as if the world could never exhaust the number of stories that kinship makes possible.

## Text and evidence companion (not spoken)

### Textual boundary

Original English narrative survey of Leiden NBG-Boeg 188, primarily volumes 7–12, with contextual references to the earlier volumes and the separate Makassar fragment described in the UNESCO nomination. **This is neither a complete *La Galigo* retelling nor a verified translation of the Bugis text**; English narrative events are bounded by institutional manuscript synopses and named scholarship, not copied dialogue. The Leiden sequence ends at a manuscript boundary, not the work's global ending. The term *Cina* is local to the narrated geography, not automatically a reference to a modern sovereign nation.

### Source locators

- NBG-Boeg 188, vol. 7: Sawérigading returns and falls in love with Wé Tenriabéng.
- Vol. 8: sister's counsel to court Wé Cudaiq; ship timber from the wélenréng tree; voyage and heavenly departure of Wé Tenriabéng.
- Vols. 9–10: merchant disguise, approach to Cina court, bride-price, refusal, disputed repayment, fighting, breeze episode, rival union and later reconciliation.
- Vols. 11–12: births of I La Galigo and daughters, divided households, Wé Tenridio's bissu role and I La Galigo's voyage for ritual attributes.
- UNESCO nomination, Museum La Galigo manuscript 2610/07.114: separate journey to Senrijawa, wedding, bissu initiation, ritual and cockfight motifs; **not** contiguous with Leiden sequence.
