# Setne Khamwas: The Book from the Tomb

A full story-first narration of the surviving *First Tale of Setne Khamwas* (Demotic Cairo Papyrus CG 30646), with the missing opening explicitly reconstructed only as an introduction, and the embedded story of Naneferkaptah, Ahwere, and Merib preserved.

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

In the dark of a tomb, a living man is about to take a book from the dead. He has been told that its words can command things no ordinary reader can command. Its pages promise knowledge of birds, fish, gods, and the concealed life of the world. But the tomb is not empty. The book belongs to someone who died because he wanted it. A dead man's wife has a story to tell the intruder, and she intends to tell it before he reaches for the treasure.

The intruder is Setne Khamwas, the literary version of a son of Ramesses the Second. A historical prince named Khaemwaset really did live in the thirteenth century BCE and became famous for his interest in monuments and the past. This story, however, survives in a much later Demotic Egyptian papyrus from the Ptolemaic era. It uses a remembered historical name to make a fictional adventurer: a learned prince whose love of old writing may be greater than his judgment. We should not date the story's manuscript to the reign of Ramesses merely because the hero is called his son.

The opening pages of the papyrus have been lost. Editors reconstruct the premise from what follows: Setne has searched for the book of the god Thoth and entered the Memphite tomb of Naneferkaptah, where the precious volume is kept. His foster brother Inaros accompanies him. Within the burial chamber are the dead prince and the spirit of his wife Ahwere. Their little son Merib belongs to the same destroyed household. In the surviving tale, Ahwere explains why the book is here and why its possession is a curse. This is the story inside the story, and it is the warning Setne will refuse to hear.

Ahwere begins far back in her life, in the royal house where she grew up with her brother Naneferkaptah. They wish to marry. Their father the pharaoh imagines other marriages that will enlarge their family's alliances, but Ahwere pleads for the union she desires, and eventually it is permitted. The tale does not ask us to accept ancient dynastic marriage customs as modern ideals. It shows us a family brought together by strong affection, so that the devastation to come will be felt as the loss of actual people rather than a magical punishment written on a diagram.

A child is born, Merib, and the household prospers. Naneferkaptah's love is not merely for his wife and boy. He is consumed by the study of writing. He wanders among monuments and tomb inscriptions, reading what others have left behind. One day during a procession for Ptah, a priest challenges him. If he truly desires knowledge, the priest says, there is a book written by Thoth himself. One spell would allow its reader to perceive and command hidden features of the visible world. Another would reveal the sun and moon and divine company in their courses. The claim appeals to everything the scribe thinks he has been seeking.

The priest demands payment and offers directions. The book is hidden in the waters near Coptos, enclosed in a succession of boxes made of different precious and durable materials. Around them are snakes, scorpions, and a great serpent that seems to guard the whole treasure. The layered boxes matter. Each one is another boundary between ordinary desire and forbidden knowledge. To reach the innermost writing, a seeker must cross water, creatures, metal, wood, ivory, and gold. But Naneferkaptah hears the description as a map to success, not a warning about a boundary that should not be crossed.

Ahwere begs him not to go. She understands the priest's disclosure as the beginning of their misfortune. Naneferkaptah asks the pharaoh for a boat and prepares to sail south. He takes Ahwere and Merib. The family travels up the river to Coptos, where the prince carries out magical preparations and makes his descent in search of the book. The scene has the form of a heroic expedition, but its companion is not glory. It is the apprehension of a woman who has already tried to keep the voyage from happening.

Naneferkaptah reaches the place of the boxes. He overcomes the serpents guarding them and strikes at the powerful creature that returns again after being cut. Through magical skill he passes the defenses, opens the nested enclosures, and takes the divine book. He reads the spells. The world becomes transparent to his knowledge: the creatures and heavenly powers named by the priest are suddenly accessible. Then he makes a copy of the words, dissolves the writing into liquid, and drinks it, so that the book's contents may live within him. His success seems complete. But the god whose book he has taken has not agreed to the theft.

Thoth brings his grievance before Ra. The consequences strike the family on the return voyage. Merib falls into the river and drowns. His body is recovered for burial near Coptos. Ahwere, overcome by the loss, also falls into the water and dies. Again the grieving prince retrieves the body for burial. Naneferkaptah is left alone on the ship with the book and the terrible knowledge that his accomplishment has destroyed the people who went with him. He cannot bear to return to the pharaoh and explain that he brought his family south and is coming back without them.

He binds the book against himself and goes into the river. His body is recovered with the book still upon it and carried to Memphis. The pharaoh orders the volume concealed, and Naneferkaptah is laid in his tomb. Ahwere and Merib remain buried in Coptos, separated in death from the husband and father they loved. In the tomb, their spirits can appear together, but the bodies remain apart. This is where the outer tale begins again: a learned living prince stands in the burial chamber of a learned dead prince, being told exactly how a book ruined a household.

Setne has heard every word. He still wants the book. He tells Ahwere he will take it if it is not freely surrendered. Naneferkaptah rises from his bier and challenges him to a game of draughts. If Setne thinks that learning makes him the better man, let him show his ability. They play. Naneferkaptah wins the first game and, by a spell, drives Setne into the ground as far as his legs. He wins again and pushes him deeper. A third loss sinks Setne nearly to the ears. The dead man controls the rules of his own tomb, and the living intruder has not learned even from humiliation.

Setne calls out to Inaros, who goes for powerful amulets and magic books. With their aid Setne escapes the spell, grabs the book, and leaves the tomb. Ahwere mourns the removal of the light the volume gave the chamber. Naneferkaptah says that Setne will return with a forked stick and a brazier upon his head, the signs of a humiliated bearer. The living prince reports his adventures to the pharaoh, who tells him to return the book now. Setne ignores him. He begins to unroll the stolen writing and read it before anyone who will listen. The scholar has won his object and lost the capacity to hear advice.

Soon Setne sees a beautiful woman at the temple of Ptah. Her name is Tabubu, and she is said to belong to a distinguished priestly family associated with Bastet. Setne desires her at once. He sends a servant with an offer of money in exchange for a private encounter. Tabubu treats the offer as an insult to her status. If he wants her, he must come to her own house, where she will set the conditions. He follows. The home is furnished with lavish materials and prepared for feasting. Desire has drawn the learned man away from the book, but the movement is not a change of subject. His inability to hear a warning is about to be tested in another form.

Tabubu demands that he sign over his possessions. Setne agrees. She then requires his children to consent to the transfer so that they cannot dispute it. He agrees again. Finally she demands the children's deaths so that no future claims will remain. Setne orders the act. The tale describes his children thrown out and devoured by animals while he continues to pursue his desire. This is a brutal scene. It should not be turned into a harmless courtship test merely because we will soon learn it belongs to a magical deception. The hero's willingness is what the scene exposes, and it is horrifying.

At the final moment the vision collapses. Setne awakens naked, humiliated, and exposed where others can see him. The pharaoh appears and tells him his children are alive. He has suffered the terror of a crime within a crafted experience, and he can no longer pretend that his intelligence automatically makes him wise. The deception resembles the earlier magical contest in the tomb. When Setne tried to take what belonged to another, he was thrust down into the earth. When he tried to command another person's body through money and rank, he was led through a series of choices that showed his willingness to sacrifice those most dependent on him.

He returns to Memphis and embraces the children whose deaths he believed he had permitted. The pharaoh again commands him to return the book. This time Setne obeys. He descends to the tomb with the forked stick and brazier, exactly as Naneferkaptah had predicted. Ahwere receives him, and the dead prince laughs. The humiliating signs make his lesson public. The book is returned to the people who had already paid for it. But one thing remains unsettled. Ahwere and Merib are still buried in the distant city of Coptos. Naneferkaptah asks Setne to bring them home.

Setne sails south. He works with priests, searches among tombs, and reads inscriptions. The request recalls the scholarly activities that first drew both men toward the book, but now his inquiry serves other people. At first the search fails. An old priest appears, claiming inherited knowledge of the burial place beneath a building. Setne suspects a private grudge. The priest stakes himself on the truth of the claim, and the grave is found. The old man then reveals that he was Naneferkaptah in disguise. For once Setne's questions and his skill at finding hidden things have led him not to steal a secret, but to reunite a family.

Ahwere and Merib are brought north and laid in the same tomb as Naneferkaptah. The burial chamber is closed over them. This ending matters as much as the frightening episode with Tabubu. The tale began with a book drawing people away from the obligations of love. It ends with a man giving labor and learning to restore a household's resting place. Setne has not solved the question of how the god's words work. He has learned that possessing dangerous knowledge is not the same thing as having earned its use. The final task is not a new magical spell. It is the bringing home of the dead.

The surviving papyrus is from the Ptolemaic period and its first two pages are absent. Modern editions reconstruct the opening from the visible plot; that reconstruction is an aid to understanding, not a recovered page. The story draws on the memory of a much earlier prince, but its urban setting, literary imagination and concerns about writing belong to its own later reception. It is Egyptian fiction about Egyptian antiquity. And it leaves us with a sharp question that travels beyond its setting: what is the worth of being able to read the words of a god, if you cannot listen when another person tells you to stop?

## Source companion (not narrated)

- Received Demotic witness: **Cairo Papyrus CG 30646**, Ptolemaic period; manuscript's first two pages missing. Narrative reference: [Attalus, *First Tale of Setne Khamwas*, Miriam Lichtheim translation](https://www.attalus.org/egypt/setne.html), with explicit brackets for missing/reconstructed text; location by papyrus pages 3–6 and headings *Setne takes the book*, *Setne and Tabubu*, *Setne returns the book*.
- Additional independent scholarly assessment: [*The Journal of Egyptian Archaeology* review of Goldbrunner's Setne edition](https://www.academia.edu/1620957/Review_S_Goldbrunner_Der_verblendete_Gelehrte_Der_erste_Setna_Roman_P_Kairo_30646_JEA_95_2009_), discussing the missing columns, magical book, and narrative structure; [World History Encyclopedia on Setna I](https://www.worldhistory.org/article/1056/setna-i-a-detailed-summary--commentary/) for orientation.
- [Wikipedia, *Setne Khamwas and Naneferkaptah*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Khaemweset) for orientation only; if unavailable use [*Khaemwaset*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Khaemweset). The historical Khaemwaset is not assumed to have undergone this fictional adventure. The Tabubu scene is a magical humiliation and moral exposure, not confirmation of actual child murders in the frame narrative. This is a new prose paraphrase, not reproduction of Lichtheim's copyrighted translation.
