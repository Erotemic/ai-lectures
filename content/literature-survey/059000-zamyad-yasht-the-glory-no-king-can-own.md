# Zamyad Yasht: The Glory No King Can Own

A guided telling of the legendary scenes in *Yasht* 19, the Zamyad or Zam Yasht, from the Avestan liturgical tradition. This is a hymn composed of invocations and episodes, not a lost continuous epic or the medieval *Shahnameh*.

Before there is a king, there are mountains. The first verses name them one after another, ranges rising in a world known through sacred geography. A listener encounters a procession of heights, passes, and named places whose sounds carry their own authority. Then the hymn begins to speak of something that is not a mountain, a crown, or a weapon, but can make a ruler great: a radiant glory known in Avestan as *khvarenah*, often spelled *khwarenah* and sometimes rendered as royal fortune or divine splendor. The king may appear to possess it. The stories that follow make the opposite point. It can depart.

The text is *Yasht* nineteen, called the Zamyad Yasht or Zam Yasht, from the Avestan religious corpus associated with Zoroastrian tradition. The name evokes the earth, but much of the hymn celebrates the glory conferred on divine beings, heroes, and rulers. It is not a novel with a single narrator following one child into adulthood. It calls up different episodes like lights placed along a long road. A later Persian epic, Ferdowsi's *Shahnameh*, will give famous kings and heroes fuller lives, but we should not quietly take the medieval poem's elaborate events and pretend the ancient hymn already contains them. The hymn tells us what it tells us.

The divine glory is first associated with Ahura Mazda and with powers that sustain an ordered world. Again and again the liturgical language returns to the qualities of strength, protection, excellence, and rightness. A ruler who possesses those powers is not simply rich. His authority has a relation to moral and cosmic order. It can be praised, but it can also be lost. That is why the hymn's recurring invocations matter to its narrative. They give a shared framework to stories that would otherwise look like an unrelated list of names.

The hymn remembers early rulers such as Haoshyangha, whose greatness appears in acts of overcoming hostile powers, and Takhma Urupa, associated with subduing demons. These are compressed celebrations, not the long biographies that appear in later books. Their deeds appear in relation to the glory that sustains them. A name comes forward, performs a memorable act, and passes into the sequence of figures in whom the splendor has been seen. Each glimpse raises a question that becomes the center of the next episode: what would make this power remain with one ruler, and what would cause it to abandon him?

Yima is the decisive example. For a time the legendary king is surrounded by an abundance that later kings can only remember. In broader Avestan tradition his reign is associated with protection from hardship and extraordinary prosperity; in this hymn he possesses the kingly glory. His story might seem to invite the simple claim that the greatest ruler must deserve the greatest permanence. But the hymn moves toward a rupture. When Yima takes delight in a lie, the glory no longer stays with him. His magnificent station is made fragile not by an enemy's larger army, but by a failure in the relation between speech and truth.

The splendor leaves Yima in three movements, imagined in birdlike form. The withdrawal is a marvelous image precisely because nothing physical could keep it. A king could lock a gate or guard a treasury. He cannot bar the passage of a power whose presence depended on his fitness to hold it. Other beings are associated with the departing glory, and the hymn moves from the failed holder to those who will carry strength into new conflicts. Yima is not erased; he remains a remembered king. But remembrance of his splendor is inseparable from remembrance of its flight.

One of the successors to glory is associated with Thraetaona, the heroic figure who confronts the monstrous Aži Dahāka. The creature is a force of destructive power, later familiar to Persian listeners in other literary forms as Zahhāk. Here the contrast is pointed: an extraordinary strength must be used to overthrow a being that threatens life and order, not merely to enlarge a hero's possessions. The hymn gives the deed in concise heroic form. It does not provide the complete courtly tragedy, lineage, and later political succession that a reader of the *Shahnameh* might expect.

Another figure is Keresaspa, remembered for terrifying combat with monsters. The hymn evokes the ordeal of confronting overwhelming creatures, including serpentine and predatory powers. Again the heroic image is brief. What makes it memorable is the disproportion between one human champion and the danger he faces. To defeat a monster is to become part of a cosmic contest whose scale exceeds the ordinary rivalry of kings. Yet the hero is celebrated within a liturgical sequence; the monsters are not material for a fabricated chain of quests connecting every name in the hymn.

The poem also tells of Frangrasyan, an antagonist associated in later Iranian epic with Afrāsiyāb. He wants to seize the glory. He approaches the waters of the sea Vourukasha, and the scenes of his attempts have a repetitive force. He dives or tries to seize the elusive power, then fails. It is a different image from Yima's flight. Yima had the glory and lost it. Frangrasyan wants it but cannot make it his by an act of force. The two episodes belong together because they show opposite failures of possession. One ruler cannot keep what his conduct has forfeited; another cannot steal what is not his to hold.

The waters are not an incidental backdrop. They dramatize a power that can be approached, pursued, and invoked but cannot be reduced to plunder. Frangrasyan's anger does not give him greater access. The obstacle is not simply that he has chosen the wrong place to dive. The hymn's moral landscape is built so that a hostile claim cannot acquire legitimacy through intensity of desire alone. The distinction will sound familiar to anyone who has heard an epic about a usurper, but here it is presented in a concise ritualized account whose repeated failure is itself the point.

Other royal names follow: members of the Kayanian line, including Kavi Husravah. Their appearances extend the glory through a history imagined as a succession of holders, conflicts, and victories. The hymn is not a court chronicle. We cannot read its sequence as a dated list of reigns. Yet its royal genealogy has a political imagination: a ruler's right to govern must be understood in relation to powers older and larger than his individual will. The greatest kingdom is always provisional. The glory belongs to an order it may illuminate for a time; it is not the private property of the palace.

The horizon of the hymn then expands. Zarathushtra and his patron Vishtaspa enter its field, linking heroic kingship to the tradition that preserves and uses the hymn. Its vision finally looks toward a future renovator or savior, the Saoshyant, through whom a world transformed beyond its present suffering is anticipated. A listener who began among the mountains has been carried into the far future. The poem moves from landscape to ancient victories, the corruption and loss of kings, the work of religion, and the expectation of renewal. That is why its structure matters. The list of figures becomes a claim about the history and destiny of the world.

Different words and spellings are used for these names in modern translations. The Avestan *khvarenah* is often called *farr* in later Persian usage. Yima appears as Jamshid in later stories, Thraetaona as Fereydun, and Frangrasyan as Afrasiyab. Knowing those connections can help a listener cross between early Iranian narrative and Ferdowsi's famous epic, which our library already introduces. But a similarity of names is not a license to import every later incident. The older hymn is a religious poem with its own order, recurring offerings, and selection of deeds. Its silences should be allowed to remain silences.

The word glory can be misleading in modern English. It may sound like fame, applause, or the bright decoration of a conqueror. The hymn's concept is closer to a potent splendor that can sustain legitimate authority, while placing that authority under judgment. The verses make a case through stories rather than a philosopher's formal proof. Why does the beloved king lose greatness? Because he has become false. Why does the grasping rival fail? Because power is not acquired by an unworthy hand. What end might justify the long series of heroes? A world renewed rather than simply conquered.

To hear *Yasht* nineteen as a continuous adventure would make the stories more convenient and less accurate. The hymn's movement is a ceremony of remembrance. We meet the mountains, the glory, the heroes, the liar, the defeated aspirant, the sacred patrons, and the promised future. No one person owns the entire sequence. The listener is invited to remember a difficult proposition: authority may shine on a person, but a person cannot make authority true by calling himself its owner. A king can command armies to defend a throne. He cannot command the glory to stay when it has taken flight.

- Context and name crosswalk: [Avesta.org glossary](https://www.avesta.org/zglos.html). [Wikipedia, *Yasht*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yasht) and [*Khvarenah*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Khvarenah) are orientation only.
- This lecture is *selected narrative traversal of a liturgical hymn*, not an exhaustive line-by-line translation, not a fictional continuous epic, and not an unqualified history of real royal dynasties. The *Vendidad* story of Yima's refuge, which occurs in another Avestan text, is deliberately **not** retold as an episode of Yasht 19. Source spelling reflects translation traditions.
