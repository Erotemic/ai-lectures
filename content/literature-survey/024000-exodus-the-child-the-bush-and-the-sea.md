# Exodus, Part One: The Child, the Bush, and the Sea

An original, chapter-grounded narrative of the received Book of Exodus, chapters 1–15. The source boundary is the Hebrew Bible/Torah as translated in the public-domain King James Version for reference, not a reconstruction of Israelite migration from archaeological evidence. The account includes religious and ethical claims made by the text, presented as claims within that text.

The story opens by naming a family. Jacob and his sons once came to Egypt, where Joseph, sold into slavery by his brothers, had risen to power and saved many people during a famine. But Exodus begins after Joseph has died, and after the people who knew his work are gone. His descendants have multiplied. The family has become a people. A new king rules Egypt, and the story says that he does not know Joseph. What the previous generation regarded as a refuge, the new government begins to regard as a danger.

The king fears that the Israelites, numerous and increasingly visible, might join Egypt's enemies in a war. So he forces them into labor. They build store cities for Pharaoh, and their lives become bitter with burdens imposed by overseers. His strategy fails to diminish their numbers. Fear hardens into a more intimate violence. The ruler orders Hebrew midwives to kill newborn boys while allowing girls to live.

Two midwives, Shiphrah and Puah, refuse to carry out the order. The narrative says that they fear God more than the king, and when Pharaoh asks why male infants are still alive, they give him an explanation that does not betray the families they have protected. These are among the first named characters in the book. Before the birth of the famous lawgiver, two women have already resisted a murderous decree. The king responds by commanding that Hebrew baby boys be thrown into the river.

A woman from the tribe of Levi hides her son for three months. When she can no longer keep him concealed, she makes a small basket, waterproofs it, and sets it among the reeds beside the Nile. The child's sister waits at a distance. Pharaoh's daughter comes down to bathe, sees the basket, hears the infant crying, and has compassion on him despite recognizing that he is one of the Hebrew children. The sister offers to find a nurse. The child's own mother is brought to nurse him, and the princess later raises him as her son. He is named Moses.

The story has placed a threatened child inside the household that ordered his death. But no simple triumph follows. As a grown man Moses goes out and sees the forced labor being imposed on his people. He sees an Egyptian beating a Hebrew and kills the Egyptian, hiding the body. When he later tries to stop two Hebrews from fighting, one asks whether Moses intends to kill him too. His act has become known. Pharaoh seeks his life, and Moses flees to Midian.

There, far from the royal court, he helps the daughters of a priest water their flock after local shepherds drive them away. He becomes part of that household and marries Zipporah. They have a son. The shepherd's life may appear to have closed the earlier conflict, but the people in Egypt still groan under labor. The book says their cries rise to God, who remembers the covenant with Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob. It is the promise from Genesis returning inside a new kind of story: the family promised a future is now a people held under power.

One day, while tending sheep near Horeb, Moses sees a bush burning without being consumed. He approaches and hears a voice calling him by name. The ground, the voice says, is holy; he must remove his sandals. The speaker identifies himself as the God of Moses's ancestors and declares that he has seen the oppression in Egypt. Moses is to return, confront Pharaoh, and lead the people out.

Moses does not immediately accept. Who is he to challenge a king? What name should he give if the Israelites ask who has sent him? The reply concerning the divine name, often rendered in English as 'I am that I am', has provoked centuries of translation and interpretation. In the story it is not merely a philosophical riddle. It comes with a promise of presence: Moses will not return alone. He asks what if people refuse to believe him. He is shown signs involving a staff transformed into a serpent, a diseased and restored hand, and water changed to blood. He protests that he is not eloquent. His brother Aaron is appointed to speak with him.

The return journey is not made comfortable. One brief, disturbing passage describes an encounter in which the Lord threatens Moses, and Zipporah uses a flint to circumcise their son, averting the danger. Exodus gives the event without the background that would make every motive clear. A retelling should leave that difficulty visible rather than making up an explanation. The man called to bring a people to safety is himself caught in a moment of frightening uncertainty.

Moses meets Aaron, and together they come before Pharaoh. Their message is that the God of Israel commands the people to go into the wilderness and hold a festival. Pharaoh refuses. He asks why he should heed this God, and he makes the labor harder. Workers must produce the same quantity of bricks but gather their own straw. Their overseers beat them for failing impossible quotas. The Israelite foremen blame Moses and Aaron: what has this intervention accomplished except misery? Moses turns the complaint toward God. The rescue he was promised has begun with more suffering.

The contest then becomes a sequence of signs, judgments, refusals, and renewed demands. Aaron's staff turns into a serpent before Pharaoh; the Egyptian specialists perform a similar wonder, though Aaron's swallows theirs. The river water is struck and turns to blood. Fish die and the water cannot be drunk. Frogs invade houses and beds. Then come swarms of small biting creatures, a plague whose insect name is rendered differently in English versions; flies; a disease among livestock; boils on people and animals; hail and fire that devastate the fields; locusts consuming what remains; and a thick darkness over Egypt.

These are not simply obstacles in a children's game that grows more difficult at each level. The narrative presents a conflict between the powers Pharaoh claims to control and a divine command he will not recognize. Its repetitions are deliberate. Sometimes Pharaoh appears ready to relent while suffering is acute, and then retracts the concession when the danger passes. Sometimes the text says Pharaoh hardens his own heart. At other points it says that God hardens Pharaoh's heart. Those statements create questions about responsibility and divine action that interpreters have argued over for a long time. The lecture need not solve them by pretending the text says only one thing.

In these scenes, suffering extends far beyond the ruler who makes the decisions. Egyptian households, agricultural workers, and livestock experience the cost of Pharaoh's refusal. Some Egyptians heed warnings about hail and shelter their servants and animals; others do not. The story's moral horizon is not contained by Moses and Pharaoh in the throne room. The human consequences of a political conflict are written across an entire country.

The final plague is announced with special dread: the death of the firstborn. Before the event, the Israelites are instructed to prepare a meal. Each household is to take a lamb, mark its doorposts with blood, roast the meat, and eat with unleavened bread and bitter herbs, ready to leave. The book links the meal to a recurring observance, the Passover, through which later generations are to remember the liberation. The instructions to remember are woven into the story before the actual departure happens. A future community is being told how to keep this night present.

At midnight the narrative says that Egypt's firstborn die, from the royal household to the poorest home and even among animals. There is a cry throughout the land. The suffering is enormous, and it should not be recounted as merely a clever final move in Moses's argument. Pharaoh finally summons Moses and Aaron and tells the people to go. Their departure is hurried; the dough has not had time to rise. People carry what they can and leave the country where their ancestors once found food and their descendants found bondage.

Exodus does not let escape end at the border. Pharaoh changes his mind and pursues them with chariots. The Israelites reach the sea and see the Egyptian force approaching. A wilderness lies around them, and water blocks the way ahead. They panic and accuse Moses of bringing them out to die. Moses tells them to stand firm. The narrative describes a divine wind and a path opening through the waters. The people go forward with water on either side, while the Egyptians follow. Then the waters return and overwhelm the pursuing force. The passage is traditionally known in English as the crossing of the Red Sea; the Hebrew name is often discussed as the Sea of Reeds. Naming that translation question is not the same as locating the crossing on a modern map.

The last scene of this movement is a song. Moses and the Israelites sing about the victory, and Miriam, named as Aaron's sister, takes a timbrel while women follow with music and dancing. The book has changed register from fear and commands to public celebration. But even the song looks forward. The liberated people must still be led to a dwelling place; the journey is not finished when the water closes behind them.

Exodus has begun with the state treating a population as a threat to be controlled. It has passed through the defiance of two midwives, a princess's compassion, a fugitive's hesitation, the repeated collapse of negotiations, an ancient meal that becomes ritual memory, and a sea crossed between terror and hope. None of these events, by being vividly told, becomes independently established archaeological history. They are the received narrative through which communities have understood liberation, divine fidelity, public law, and the peril of power.

And the most difficult part of being free still lies ahead. A people can be removed from a ruler's grasp in a night. It takes much more to discover how that people will live together, how it will judge, and what it will do when fear returns without Pharaoh standing before it.

## Received text and independent history

- Primary: [Exodus chapters 1–15, KJV text](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Exodus%201-15&version=KJV), using chapter and verse as locators, not an invented modern reconstruction.
- Independent literary introduction: [Yale Bible Study, *Exodus* overview](https://yalebiblestudy.org/courses/exodus/lessons/introduction-study-guide/); [Wikipedia: Book of Exodus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Book_of_Exodus).
- Scenes: 1 Shiphrah/Puah, forced labor; 2 infant Moses, flight, Midian; 3–4 bush, name, objections, Zipporah; 5–6 Pharaoh and increased quotas; 7–10 successive plagues; 11–13 firstborn, Passover, departure; 14 crossing; 15 victory song and Miriam.
- The composition and historical setting of the Book of Exodus are subjects of scholarship. The lecture tells the received account without asserting that all named individuals, plagues, or locations can be independently established by archaeology.
