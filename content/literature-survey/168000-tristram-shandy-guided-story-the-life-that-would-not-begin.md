# Tristram Shandy — Guided Story: The Life That Would Not Begin

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

A man sits down to write the story of his life. He intends to tell everything. Not merely what happened to him, but why it happened; not merely what people said, but what they meant; not merely how he came into the world, but what sort of world was waiting. His name is Tristram Shandy. It will take him an astonishingly long time to get himself born.

Most autobiographies begin with a sentence about childhood, or with a memory of a parent, or with an adult looking back over the years. Tristram begins before birth. His parents are together on the night when he is conceived, and his mother interrupts the moment to ask about the household clock. She has made a connection between the winding of that clock and another regular domestic obligation. His father is furious. A life, Tristram suspects, can be disordered by an interruption before its owner has even drawn breath.

This is the opening of Laurence Sterne's *The Life and Opinions of Tristram Shandy, Gentleman*. There are nine volumes. It was published over the years from 1759 to 1767. We are going to follow the central lives and stories, but there is no way to convey the experience by pretending the novel is simply a straight sequence of events. Tristram's digressions are part of the story. Whenever a new person enters a room, he wants to explain a private history. Whenever a word has two meanings, he wants to pursue both. Whenever an event needs no explanation, his family produces several. If our telling takes a turning here or there, it will be because a straight path would misrepresent the work.

First, the family. At Shandy Hall there is Tristram's father, Walter Shandy, a man who has formed theories about nearly everything and has a terrible time living among people who will not obey them. He regards names as formative forces; he has strong convictions about education, noses, obstetrics, and the management of human life. His conviction is sincere, but it is no protection against accident. His brother Toby is another kind of thinker. Uncle Toby is gentle, modest, and obsessively interested in military engineering. He was wounded during the siege of Namur, and the wound left him with pain, disability, and a story that everyone wants him to explain more directly than he can.

Toby has a loyal companion, Corporal Trim, whose army service has bound him to his old captain. While Walter constructs elaborate theories, Toby and Trim construct miniature fortifications. They want to understand where a battle happened, how trenches advanced, where a battery stood, why men took one position instead of another. On a bowling green they recreate siegeworks in the earth. Walter sees wasted effort. Toby sees a world he can grasp: a world of embankments and angles and little wooden implements, where the chaos of war can be reduced to a scheme.

Their enthusiasm is something Tristram calls a hobby-horse. Everybody, he suggests, has one: an absorbing private way of organizing experience. It may look foolish to everyone else. To the rider, it offers sense and comfort. Walter rides his theories. Toby rides his sieges. Tristram rides the sentence that keeps carrying him away from his own biography. The notion is comic, but it is also generous. One cannot really know another person by dismissing what that person loves.

Consider Uncle Toby's wound. Walter would like a clear anatomical explanation. Toby insists that the damage cannot be properly described without understanding the fortifications at Namur. He reaches for his map, traces the route of a trench, explains where he stood when the battle turned. The questioner tries again. Where, exactly, were you wounded? Toby begins at the beginning of the siege. He is not trying to deceive. To him, the location of an injury is part of its cause, its setting, and his identity. The joke continues because both parties think the other has missed the relevant fact.

Around the family move other figures. The servant Obadiah rides out on errands and returns with complications. Susannah attends Mrs. Shandy and is entrusted with messages whose wording matters too much. Doctor Slop, who practices the new art of male midwifery, becomes involved in a delivery that will repay neither his skill nor Walter's confidence. The clergyman Yorick is witty, outspoken, and vulnerable to people who choose to mistake his jokes. His story ends in death, and Sterne marks it with a black page. A novel that laughs so much has room for a page that refuses language.

We have not yet reached Tristram's birth. Walter and Mrs. Shandy have a marriage agreement with peculiar provisions. She is determined to give birth according to her own preference. Walter prefers reasoned arrangements and professional assistance. Debate spreads through the house; the men discuss obstetric instruments while the woman in labor is elsewhere, doing the actual work. Doctor Slop arrives, and his forceps promise a modern intervention. Their use helps turn the delivery into a disaster for the infant's nose. Walter, who believes a substantial nose makes an important difference to a human future, is devastated.

It is not enough that Tristram will be born under unfortunate circumstances. His father has already developed a theory about names. Some names bring grandeur; others drag a life downward. He has settled on Trismegistus, a name with the sort of historical and mystical associations that please him. But during the confusion surrounding the infant's baptism, the desired name is lost in transmission. What emerges is Tristram, a name Walter detests. The new child has scarcely arrived and already, by Walter's reckoning, has suffered a ruined conception, an injured nose, and a fatal label.

Walter reacts to the name with the weight of a philosophical catastrophe. Another person might shrug and continue with the household business. But Walter has made a system, and systems make accidents feel like universal proof. He argues, laments, and asks what a world has come to when such things can happen. His son, writing decades later, is still trying to make a life-story out of these claims. We cannot know whether his misery comes from events or from the theories that make the events inevitable. The comedy is built on that uncertainty.

The birth itself arrives only after many departures into other business. One of those is the strange treatment of noses. A learned, foreign-sounding tale by Slawkenbergius grows around the subject, full of suggestion, scholarship, misreading, and confusion between literal and figurative dimensions. Sterne's readers are never allowed to remain innocent of double meanings. The respectable discussion is liable to become bawdy at the smallest shift of emphasis. Walter can pronounce on noses with a philosopher's gravity; the language around him refuses to stop making another joke.

A book that behaves like this can seem to be hiding a conventional plot just beyond the next interruption. But the interruptions repeatedly disclose something worth knowing. When Tristram devotes pages to the way a story should be written, he is showing how a human thought develops. One memory calls up another, which recalls a friend, which raises the question of a conversation years earlier. If he hurried past all these associations, the account might be shorter, but it would no longer resemble his experience of remembering.

At Shandy Hall another loss interrupts the household: Walter's elder son, Bobby, dies away at school. Walter, who has a theory for grief as well as for everything else, attempts to turn the blow into an occasion for grand moral reflection. His eloquence does not supply an easy answer. Toby responds in his own way. Trim, with his soldier's gestures and plain feeling, says and does what Walter's learned arguments cannot. Sterne is capable of mocking a man at the precise moment he is also asking us to pity him. Walter's language cannot make the death smaller, but the effort to speak is part of his distress.

Now we can understand why one of the tenderest stories in the novel belongs not to Tristram but to Toby. At an inn, an army officer named Le Fever is dying. Toby and Trim learn about his situation and come to help. Le Fever has a young son, and the kindness offered to father and child comes without a lecture about deserving it. The old soldiers understand the cost of service. When the father is gone, the boy will need care. Toby's actions answer a question Walter's system of life has not properly asked: what ought one person to do when another needs him?

There is a scene of astonishing restraint in the midst of the book's theatrical comedy. Toby can be made ridiculous by his maps, his toy cannon, his circular explanations. Yet he is never ridiculous for kindness. He treats the weak and the defeated as human beings. When his hobby-horse meets a suffering person, it need not become an instrument of domination. It can become a door to compassion.

Walter, meanwhile, undertakes the education of his son with comparable determination and very different results. He develops a grand plan called the Tristrapaedia, a work meant to encompass the principles by which a child should be raised. The writing of this system consumes time. Every day that Walter plans the future, the boy grows older in the present. A tutor that arrives too late can educate only the child who no longer exists. Here Sterne's joke is almost cruel: a parent determined to do everything properly may overlook the child actually waiting beside him.

The child's body is no safer from accident than his future is from theory. A sash window falls, causing a painful injury and another embarrassment involving the region of the body that adult conversation cannot discuss without innuendo. Walter's ideas about boys, heredity, and the future of the Shandy name meet again with the unmanageable world of things that slip and break. The novel teases a reader who wants the precise, forbidden details. It teases Walter for finding an intellectual disaster in them. And it allows Tristram to make the injury part of the family legend rather than simply reporting a mishap.

One might reasonably ask: where has Tristram himself been while all these stories unfold? Often he is the infant or child who has been the object of other people's speeches. Sometimes he is the grown narrator, supervising the arrangement of the book. Sometimes he is the invisible son of people whose personalities predate him. What has been promised as an autobiography is becoming a family portrait, and perhaps the portrait of a certain kind of human weakness: the inability to think about one thing without being captured by another.

By the later volumes the narrator gives us a new urgency. Death is pursuing him. Rather than sit obediently and await it, Tristram travels in France, moving with a speed his writing has rarely shown. He passes through places that demand description, but even travel does not free him from interruption. He can turn a journey into an argument about how journeys should be told, then turn the argument into another journey. His quick movement through space contrasts with the slow advance of his life on the page. It is as though writing has become the means by which he holds mortality away for another sentence.

And the novel turns decisively toward Uncle Toby's romance. Widow Wadman lives near the Shandys. She is interested in the gentle veteran, and the veteran is not incapable of affection. But the courtship is shaped by an embarrassing uncertainty. Toby's wound has been described in military terms; what exactly did the injury do to his body? The widow wishes to know. Her question is not merely idle curiosity. It bears on the possibility of marriage and intimacy. Yet to ask it openly would be a breach of the manners by which everyone is trying to conduct the affair.

What follows is one of Sterne's longest exercises in communication gone sideways. Toby hopes he understands her hints. The widow attempts to learn without stating. Trim gives advice and serves as interpreter, though his own experience and his own entanglement with the widow's maid Bridget complicate matters. The more carefully everyone behaves, the more difficult a simple answer becomes. Toby can describe precisely which work he was besieging when he was hurt. That description cannot give Widow Wadman the information she needs. The machinery of politeness and the machinery of military explanation both keep the encounter from reaching the point.

The novel spends a remarkable amount of time preparing for a courtship it can hardly complete. Toby's diffidence matters as much as the joke. He is gentle enough to be shy of causing pain and innocent enough not to hear all the implications. The widow is not merely an instrument of comic appetite; she is trying to assess a serious future through a social language designed to conceal the question. It is a marriage plot whose obstacle is not a rival, not an inheritance, not a villain, but the practical difficulty of asking someone what has happened to his body.

Tristram could end his book with the resolution of this affair. He could return to his own adulthood. He could tell us how the nose, the name, and the childhood window affected his fortunes. He does none of these in the manner expected. Instead the last pages move to another domestic argument, another misunderstanding, and a bull. Walter has a problem with a bull whose performance threatens the orderly management of the household's breeding arrangements. The language suggests that the problem of reproduction has been with us from the very beginning. Yorick brings the exchange to a close by calling it a cock-and-bull story.

And that is where the novel ends: not with the conclusion of the life its title promised, but with an expression that describes a rambling or improbable tale. The narrator has made the refusal of completion into the last joke. It is tempting to say that nothing has happened. But a great deal has happened. We have watched two brothers misunderstand each other and remain affectionate. We have seen a father turn his son into a theory while a gentle uncle turns a map of war into a language of care. We have followed a widow trying to learn whether she may safely love a man. We have seen grief put into grand sentences and into quiet acts. We have watched the narrator discover that the business of explaining life has no natural stopping point.

Sterne makes the last sentence a kind of invitation. If a life can never be fully told, why pretend it can? If the people closest to us remain mysteries, why hide their strangeness behind orderly biographies? If all stories are shaped by the speaker who chooses where to linger, perhaps the most honest story will show us its choices. The book began with a clock being wound. It ends without announcing that time has been mastered. Between those two moments, Tristram has found a way to make each interruption another reason to go on telling.

## Text and evidence companion (not spoken)

### Scope and edition

An **original whole-work guided telling** of the central family, birth, childhood, travel, and Toby–Wadman arcs of Sterne's nine-volume *The Life and Opinions of Tristram Shandy, Gentleman* (published 1759–1767). The nonchronological design is deliberately retained; this is not a chapter-by-chapter synopsis or a reproduction of Sterne's exact prose. Some details are elided, including digressive sermons and embedded tales. The main witness is a publicly accessible mixed-edition scholarly transcription; original editions vary in typography and page effects.

### Primary textual loci

- Vol. I: conception and clock; family; Walter's names; Toby's wound and military hobby-horse; Yorick and the black page.
- Vols. II–III: Dr. Slop, Trim, obstetrics, birth and damaged nose; inserted sermon; nose narratives; marbled page.
- Vol. IV: baptism and Trismegistus/Tristram; Walter's lament and additional discourses.
- Vol. V: Bobby's death; Walter's rhetoric; Tristrapaedia; sash-window incident.
- Vol. VI: Uncle Toby and Le Fever; paths and the narrator's drawings.
- Vol. VII: Tristram travels in France while fleeing Death.
- Vols. VIII–IX: Toby, Trim, Widow Wadman and Bridget; concluding bull argument and Yorick's summation.
