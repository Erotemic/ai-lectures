# Sir Gawain and the Green Knight — Story: The Axe and the Green Lace

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

A rider enters King Arthur's hall carrying a holly branch in one hand and an enormous axe in the other. The holly says peace. The axe says something else. He has no helmet and no shield. His horse is green, his clothes are green, and so is his hair, down to the beard that falls over his chest. This is Christmas at Camelot, and the young court has been celebrating for days. Arthur likes to hear of a marvel before he sits down to eat. A marvel has arrived, but it will not be content simply to entertain him.

The stranger asks where the famous king is. When Arthur invites him to dismount and join the feast, he refuses. He has come, he says, not for battle but for a Christmas game. Any knight may take the axe and strike him once, without resistance. The challenger will keep neither shield nor armor between himself and the blow. In exchange he asks only the right to return the same stroke in a year's time. It sounds like the easiest victory a knight could win and the worst bargain he could make. The court is silent. The Green Knight mocks their reputation. Arthur's face reddens. He reaches for the axe himself.

Then Gawain stands. He is Arthur's nephew, a knight celebrated for courtesy, but he asks to take the challenge by calling himself the least valuable person in the hall. If anyone must risk his life to protect the king's honor, let it be him. The court agrees. Gawain takes the axe. The enormous visitor lowers his neck, gathering his long hair clear of the blade. Gawain swings once. The head falls and rolls across the floor. This should settle the matter. Instead the headless man walks calmly among the feet of the guests, retrieves his head, and mounts his horse. Holding the severed face toward Gawain, he tells him to find the Green Chapel and present himself for the answering stroke. Then horse, rider, and talking head depart.

The feast resumes because Camelot knows how to turn disaster into ceremony. Arthur laughs and orders the terrifying axe hung where the guests can see it. Gawain cannot laugh the year away. He has promised not merely to be brave in an instant but to travel, voluntarily, toward the hour in which another man may cut off his head. Summer comes, flourishes, and passes. Autumn gathers its grain. As the year grows old, the promise grows nearer. The poem takes time to notice the passing seasons; Gawain's terrible future continues to approach while the whole country goes about its life.

Before he rides, the court dresses him for the journey. His armor gleams; his shield carries a golden five-pointed knot, the pentangle. Its unbroken lines stand for interlocked virtues: the reliability of his senses and fingers, devotion to Christ's wounds, reliance on Mary's joys, and five qualities that should make a knight worthy of trust. Generosity, fellowship, purity, courtesy, and compassion are not detachable ornaments. They are supposed to support one another. Gawain carries this pattern into a world where doing what one virtue requires may make another much harder to keep.

His horse Gringolet carries him away from the warmth of Camelot. The road crosses bleak moors, wooded hills, rivers, and a winter landscape where cold is as dangerous as any armed enemy. There are fights and creatures in the wilderness, but the poem does not turn them into a string of tournament victories. Gawain is hungry and tired. He seeks shelter on Christmas Eve, and asks the Virgin Mary to help him find a place where he can hear Mass. He looks up and finds a castle rising magnificently beyond the trees, as if his wish has become stone.

The castle's lord welcomes him as an honored guest. This man is large, cheerful, richly dressed, and wonderfully at ease in his own house. He is the lord of Hautdesert, a name we will come to know only later. The lady of the castle is beautiful, quick-witted, and thoroughly capable of deciding how a conversation will go. With her sits an elderly lady, richly honored by the household. Gawain is washed, fed, rested, and celebrated; he seems to have wandered out of death's road into a place built expressly to comfort him. He tells his hosts about the Green Chapel. The lord laughs and assures him that it is close enough to reach on New Year's Day. There is no need to search the country. Gawain can spend the remaining days as his guest.

The lord then proposes a game of his own. He will go hunting each day, and whatever he wins in the forest he will give to Gawain when he returns. Gawain, meanwhile, will stay in the castle, and whatever comes into his possession he must give to his host. Both promise to be faithful to the exchange. Gawain has already agreed to one game involving an axe and a future reckoning. Now, because he is in a generous household, he agrees to another. The terms seem playful. We will discover that the promises have been fitted together with extraordinary care.

On the first morning, the lord's hunters drive deer through the woods. The poet watches the chase closely: the running animals, the hounds, the horns, the cutting of the carcasses after the kill. Inside the castle, Gawain has been permitted to sleep late. He wakes to find the lady standing beside his bed. She has entered his chamber and trapped the celebrated knight in a difficulty no shield can settle. A man famed for courtesy cannot simply insult his host's wife and order her away. A knight pledged to chastity and loyalty cannot accept every invitation she makes. She teases him about his reputation and suggests that a proper Gawain would be much bolder in love. He answers with elaborate politeness, turns compliments aside, and avoids betraying either his conscience or his host. At last she asks for a kiss, and he gives her one.

That evening the lord comes home with the day's deer. The lord presents his prize; Gawain gives him a kiss in return. The host jokes about how fine a reward Gawain has obtained. Gawain does not tell him where it came from, because the agreement requires the exchange of winnings, not an explanation of each event. So far he has kept the letter of his bargain. Yet the poem lets us hear the uncomfortable sound of a private encounter becoming the currency of a public game.

The second morning sends Bertilak after a fierce wild boar. The animal turns on the pursuing dogs and men, fights from its hiding places, and resists the hunters' efforts until the lord faces it himself. In Gawain's chamber the lady returns. Her compliments sharpen into questions about love. She asks why a knight of such fame never takes what a lady offers. He must again find a way to be gracious without becoming unfaithful. The conversation is a duel of implications, with each courtly phrase capable of being understood another way. This day she gains two kisses. That night Bertilak gives Gawain the slain boar. Gawain gives Bertilak two kisses. The games still fit together.

On the third day the hunting party pursues a fox. It twists, doubles back, and seeks ways through the undergrowth that will confuse the hounds. At the castle the lady makes her boldest effort. She finds Gawain in bed, presses him with talk of love, and wins three kisses. Then she offers a costly ring. Gawain refuses it. He cannot accept so valuable a gift; he has no way to repay her. She tries another gift, a green silk girdle, or lace, that can be tied around his waist. He dismisses it at first as a small, ordinary thing. Then she says it possesses a special power: a man who wears it cannot be killed. A stroke that should take his life would fail.

Remember what Gawain is supposed to do tomorrow. He has come voluntarily to kneel beneath an enchanted axe. He has been promised no miracle of survival, and no one has told him that his courage will make the blade harmless. Now a woman in a warm room offers him a way to live. Gawain changes his mind and accepts the girdle. She makes him promise to keep it secret from her husband. They exchange their three kisses, and she leaves. This is the decisive moment. The flaw is not that he reaches greedily for treasure. It is that he wants his life, and consents to hide what he has been given, though he has promised his host every day's winnings.

Gawain goes to confession before the New Year appointment. He receives absolution and prepares for what he thinks will be death. But when his host returns with the fox, the knight gives him three kisses and no girdle. For the first time the host has not received the full day's winnings. Gawain can speak honestly to a priest and still evade the demands of the exchange game; the poem has placed two forms of truthfulness painfully close together. The guests feast again, and in the morning Gawain leaves the castle wearing the green lace concealed under his clothing.

The lord sends a servant to guide him. The winter landscape is grim, and when they approach the Green Chapel the guide tries to turn Gawain back. The creature waiting there, he warns, is merciless. It does not matter whether a man is noble or humble. If Gawain wishes, the guide will tell no one that he fled; he can escape alive and keep his honor, at least as far as the world will know. Gawain refuses. To save himself by breaking the promise now would make the long journey a lie. He rides on alone and finds not a glittering chapel but a strange hollow mound and cleft in the earth, a place that looks more like the entrance to a cave than a church.

He hears a grinding noise. The Green Knight is sharpening an axe. Gawain calls out; the giant emerges, enormous and green as before, and welcomes his guest for keeping the bargain. Gawain kneels and bares his neck. The axe rises. But at the first feint Gawain flinches. The knight stops and laughs at him. Where is the courage of the man who cut off another's head at Camelot? Gawain is ashamed, steadies himself, and declares he will not move again. The blade rises a second time, then checks before it touches him. The Green Knight says that this time Gawain remained steady. On the third swing the edge falls, barely nicking his neck. Blood runs, but his head remains on his shoulders.

Gawain leaps up, takes his sword, and announces that the terms are fulfilled. He has accepted the answering stroke. No more are owed. The Green Knight, far from offended, laughs and explains what has happened. The first two swings did no harm because Gawain kept faith with his host during the first two days of the exchange. The third draws blood because on the final day he withheld the green girdle. And the hunter who was away among the deer, boar, and fox? He is standing in front of Gawain now. Bertilak of Hautdesert and the Green Knight are the same man.

The lady's invitations had been part of a test devised with her husband's knowledge. Behind the whole adventure stands the older woman Gawain saw at the castle: Morgan le Fay, the powerful enchantress who is Arthur's half-sister and has learned magic from Merlin. The explanation given by the Green Knight is that Morgan sent him to Camelot both to test the court's proud reputation and to frighten Guinevere. It is a revelation that explains the mechanics without removing the discomfort. Gawain was not told the test's rules; his host placed him in a morally dangerous situation while disguising the stakes. Gawain nevertheless considers the hidden lace his own failure.

Bertilak tells him that the wound is small because the fault was limited. He did not conceal the girdle for greed or in order to betray the lady. He kept it because he loved his life. The Green Knight is ready to forgive him. Gawain is less ready to forgive himself. He condemns his own cowardice and covetousness, then erupts in bitter language about the dangers women have brought upon famous men. The examples that come to his mind are the biblical stories of Adam, Samson, David, and Solomon. The outburst shifts some blame away from his own decision, even as he names the decision a shame. The poem does not finish by giving the hero a perfectly composed moral speech.

He keeps the green girdle, not as a triumph but as a reminder that he has fallen short of the perfect truth represented by the pentangle. He returns to Camelot. The king and court greet him with joy, eager to hear how he survived. Gawain shows them the cut on his neck and explains the lace. He believes the strip of green should testify against him. Arthur and the knights respond with laughter, affection, and a decision: they too will wear green bands for his sake. What Gawain treats as a private badge of shame becomes the court's shared emblem of fellowship.

The final difference is not neatly resolved. Does the court understand his failing better than he does, or does it turn a moral crisis into another agreeable fashion? Perhaps both. Gawain learned, at the edge of an axe, that a man can genuinely value honor and still want desperately to live. The court learns that its most courteous knight has returned, wounded but alive, and refuses to let him stand alone. The poem closes with a blessing and a reference to the order of the Garter, a link to the world of later chivalric ceremony. But its most enduring picture is simpler: one green strip of silk that means something different to the man who wears it and the people who love him.

## Text and evidence companion (not spoken)

### Narrative witness and edition boundary

This is an original English retelling of the complete four-fitt narrative of the anonymous late-fourteenth-century alliterative poem, not a translation. Plot order was cross-checked against Jessie L. Weston's public-domain 1898 prose retelling and the Stanford Arthurian literature outline. Weston sometimes omits technical hunting detail and makes editorial interpretations; for manuscript and literary-form claims prefer the British Library's Cotton Nero A X/2 catalog and Middle English scholarship. The Green Knight's final revelation reflects the received poem, not an independent historical event. Narration is not divided into poem line-by-line commentary.

### Scene and source guide

- Camelot, the beheading bargain, the head carried off: **fitt I** (the Green Knight's challenge and Gawain's pledge).
- The year, Gringolet, pentangle, winter journey and Hautdesert: **fitt II**, including the fivefold pentangle passage.
- Three hunt and bedroom pairings, kisses, gift of girdle and exchange-game violation: **fitt III**.
- Guide's escape offer, Green Chapel, three swings, nick, Bertilak's identity, Morgan le Fay, and green girdle at Camelot: **fitt IV**.
- The narrative details and the literary theory of Gawain's failings are distinguishable; the ending's laughter admits more than one reading.
