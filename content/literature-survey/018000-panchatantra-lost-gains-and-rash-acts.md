# The Panchatantra, Part Two: Lost Gains and Rash Acts

An original narration from Books IV–V of Pūrṇabhadra's 1199 recension, based on Arthur Ryder's 1925 translation. This concludes the five-book selection begun in 017000. The manuscript treats the monkey/crocodile and barber stories as separate book frames and chooses important nested cautionary fables without conflating other recensions.

A monkey sits in a tree above the sea and throws fruit to a crocodile. The crocodile likes the fruit, and the monkey likes the company. They become friends. It is easy to picture the visits: the crocodile resting at the edge of the water, the monkey in the branches, the conversation renewed day after day. The danger is not visible in the scene, because it does not arise from the monkey's actions. It arises when the crocodile takes some of the sweet fruit home.

The crocodile's wife asks where it came from. He explains that a friendly monkey provides it. She reasons that anyone who lives on such sweetness must have a heart of astonishing sweetness. She wants to eat that heart and presses her husband to bring it to her. He refuses at first, protesting that the monkey is his friend, almost a brother. But his wife persists until he gives way. This is the frame of the fourth book of the Panchatantra, called Loss of Gains. Its central question is how a person may throw away something valuable through a foolish demand or a badly chosen loyalty.

The crocodile returns to the tree and invites the monkey to his home. The monkey cannot travel underwater, he objects. The crocodile offers to carry him on his back. The monkey trusts him and climbs aboard. Only after they are far from shore does the crocodile confess that he is taking his guest to be killed. It is a deeply unpleasant betrayal because the animal's honest enjoyment of friendship had seemed genuine. The crocodile is not pretending that he never cared. He is choosing a different demand over the life of the friend who welcomed him.

The monkey is frightened, but he does not let panic dictate his answer. He says that of course he would have brought his heart, had he known it was wanted. Unfortunately he left it in a hollow of the tree. Why had his friend not mentioned the need earlier? The crocodile, delighted that the problem can be solved, turns back toward shore. Once close enough, the monkey springs up into the branches. Only then does he explain that no one leaves a heart behind in a tree. The crocodile asks him to return, now claiming that the danger was a joke. The monkey refuses. Friendship can recover from embarrassment or error; it cannot recover at once from a deliberate attempt to kill a guest.

The monkey and crocodile exchange more fables. One concerns creatures who misjudge a former acquaintance after trust has been broken. Another tells of an ass disguised in a tiger skin. The farmer who owns the ass wants the disguise to frighten people away from their fields while the animal grazes. It works until the ass hears another donkey and brays. The sound reveals him, and he is killed. The image is sharp: a powerful appearance can be borrowed, but the creature beneath it still has a voice. What appears to be a successful fraud may contain the means of its own exposure.

The book is not simply suspicious of friendship. The monkey genuinely benefited from the friendship while it was honest. The crocodile lost the friendship because he could not keep a boundary between competing obligations. Another tale concerns a donkey lured repeatedly toward a lion by a jackal's promises. The victim mistakes the bait for an opportunity even after having barely escaped the predator. The collection keeps asking who profits from advice and why someone who has almost suffered disaster might believe the same adviser again.

The fifth book, Ill-Considered Action, begins in a town with a merchant who has lost his wealth and fallen into despair. In his sleep he has a dream promising that a monk will come to his house and, when struck, turn into a quantity of gold. The next day the extraordinary event occurs. A barber witnesses it. What the barber sees is enough to fill his mind with the prospect of sudden riches, but not enough to understand the event. He concludes that striking monks on the head produces gold.

He invites monks to his home, promising help with their needs, then attacks them with clubs. The result is not gold but injured and dying people. The authorities arrest him. His excuse is that he saw the merchant do something similar. The tale is disturbing and absurd at once. It shows the difference between observing an action and understanding the conditions under which it occurred. A pattern inferred from one event becomes deadly when applied without thought. The fifth book turns that mistake into a series of stories about haste, false analogy, and decisions made before someone has looked carefully.

One of the shortest is the story of the loyal mongoose. A woman and her husband are caring for a baby and a mongoose raised within their household. The mother is anxious that the animal might harm the child, yet the creature is devoted to the family. One day both adults are absent. A snake approaches the baby's bed. The mongoose attacks and kills it, saving the child. When the mother returns, the mongoose runs toward her with blood on its mouth. She assumes it has killed the baby and, before examining the room, strikes the animal dead. Inside she finds the baby alive beside the dead snake. Her protection of the child has turned into the destruction of the protector.

It is almost unbearably clear in retrospect. The woman has acted on the danger she feared rather than on the evidence she needed. The blood was real, but its meaning was not what she thought. The story is not an argument that parents should be careless about danger. It is an argument that an urgent feeling is not identical to knowledge, and that some actions cannot be undone after a mistaken conclusion.

Another fable concerns four seekers of treasure. They are given objects that will fall and indicate places where wealth lies hidden. The first finds copper and is satisfied. The second continues to silver. The third reaches gold. The fourth cannot bear to stop: if the amounts keep increasing, perhaps something greater than gold waits ahead. He goes on alone into a scorching, desolate region and encounters a man tormented by a spinning wheel fixed to his head. When the traveler asks about the man's condition, the wheel transfers onto him. His pursuit of the next reward has brought him to a punishment from which he cannot leave by his own effort. The one who stopped at gold eventually finds his friend and hears what has happened. More may not mean better. To know when to stop is also a kind of intelligence.

The fable of the lion-makers tells of several men rich in formal learning. They find the bones of a dead lion and decide to display their knowledge by reconstructing the body and restoring its life. One companion, possessing sound sense rather than the same impressive scholarship, warns that a revived lion will be dangerous. The others dismiss him. He climbs a tree while they finish their demonstration. The lion comes alive and devours the men who brought it back. The survivor is not the most learned; he is the person who knew that a technical accomplishment is not automatically a good thing to do.

The difference between knowledge and judgment runs through these stories. The barber saw a transformation but failed to understand its cause. The mother saw blood but failed to learn whose it was. The treasure-seeker saw a sequence of increasing riches and assumed it must continue safely. The scholars saw the chance to prove a skill and ignored the living creature whose teeth the skill would restore. A few seconds of further thought might have saved lives. Yet the collection also warns that too much delay, or the wrong kind of caution, may ruin opportunity. The princes are not being handed a single rule like 'always hesitate.' They are being taught to examine what matters in a particular situation.

The five books make an interesting pair of opposites. The first says that a friendship may be destroyed by the calculated malice of an adviser. The second shows friends coordinating their very different powers. The third shows how a ruler may win a conflict through deception. The fourth explains how gains, trust, and opportunities may be thrown away through carelessness. The fifth asks what becomes of intelligence when it outruns judgment. Together they form something more difficult than a moral picture book. They suggest that our minds are crowded with conflicting impulses: trust and suspicion, speed and patience, loyalty and self-preservation, ambition and restraint.

These tales were made to be told inside other tales. A prince asks how a policy can be justified; an adviser answers with a fable. One character challenges a claim and receives another example. Stories become portable pieces of reasoning, some sound, some opportunistic. The collection invites us not only to admire a clever ending but to ask who used the tale, at what moment, and for whose benefit. Victor's deceptions in the first book prove that a person who quotes wise sayings is not necessarily wise. Words of advice may themselves be part of an attack.

There is a literary history beyond the teaching frame. Sanskrit versions traveled through Middle Persian into Arabic and later into many other languages. The family of narratives associated with Kalila and Dimna became a major channel by which fables circulated across Europe, the Middle East, and South Asia. That history is real, but it does not make every version identical. Names, story order, and political emphasis change. The specific animals we have heard today belong to the recension selected for these two lectures, not to an imagined universal script of the ancient world.

Return finally to the princes and their teacher. They were to learn in months what books of abstract advice had failed to teach them. Their teacher has not asked them to memorize one answer to every question. He has given them experiences they can carry without leaving the room: the lion whose trusted friend becomes his enemy, the trapped doves who rise together, the burning cave, the monkey leaping from the crocodile's back, the mother discovering the snake beside her child. We remember the situations, and then we ask what we would have noticed, what we would have believed, and what we might have done before it was too late.
