# Shiji III: Xiang Yu, Liu Bang, and the Songs at Gaixia

The conclusion of the Xiang Yu narrative in *Shiji* chapter 7, read beside Sima Qian's account of the Han founder in chapter 8.

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

The conflict between Xiang Yu and Liu Bang begins with a question that neither man's army can settle in a single encounter. Qin has collapsed, but there is no accepted rule for distributing the authority it once held. Xiang Yu has immense prestige as a commander and the power to allot kingdoms; Liu Bang has experience in receiving surrendered cities, working with advisers, and building a following that does not depend on one spectacular victory. Both are capable of making terrible decisions. The struggle Sima Qian describes is not a contest between a good man and a bad one. It is a study in how different strengths can become different kinds of weakness when an insurgency turns into government.

Liu Bang does not stay content with his assignment in the west. Under the counsel of Zhang Liang and other advisers, he gathers support and challenges the arrangement Xiang Yu imposed after Qin's fall. Sima Qian's chapters move through a dense geography of cities, rivers, roads, and grain routes. A place that looks like a minor detail on a political map can become vital because an army cannot eat a victory. Xiang Yu repeatedly demonstrates his ability to defeat forces in open battle. Yet Liu's coalition can retreat, replace commanders, gather allies, and return. The conflict becomes one of endurance as well as courage. Men who can afford to lose several battles sometimes outlast a man who feels he must win every one.

The Battle of Pengcheng, as the narrative remembers it, is a devastating example. Liu Bang moves into Xiang Yu's capital region while Xiang is occupied elsewhere. There is triumph and celebration among the Han-aligned forces. Xiang Yu turns with a smaller but mobile force and attacks suddenly. Liu's army breaks. The retreat becomes catastrophic, with large numbers killed or driven into the rivers in Sima Qian's description. Liu escapes and must rebuild. This should prevent us from reading the end backward as if the Han victory had been obvious all along. There are moments when Xiang Yu looks entirely capable of destroying his rival's project.

But a battlefield is only one arena in which the war is fought. Liu's generals cultivate and conquer other territories; Han Xin becomes especially consequential. Peng Yue threatens Xiang Yu's communications and supply. Alliances shift. In the *Shiji*, plans are discussed, rejected, and renewed; some victories produce political resentment as well as strategic advantage. Each side tries to weaken the other's network of loyalties. Xiang Yu can defeat a concentration of troops while finding that food and reliable allies have disappeared behind him. Liu Bang can suffer humiliations and yet retain the apparatus by which a new army is made. The book repeatedly asks us to attend to advisers and subordinates, not only the two famous leaders.

The siege of Xingyang and the struggles around Chenggao show what attrition feels like in a narrative normally remembered for dramatic encounters. Cities change hands, relief fails, transport falters. Men are rewarded, rebuked, suspected of betrayal, or abandoned. The war is not elegant. Sima Qian describes threats against Liu's family and verbal exchanges between rivals, details that make the struggle personal without turning it into an intimate rivalry detached from the people whose land is being fought over. An empire is forming through months and years of injuries to regions that will still have to feed and obey whichever leader survives.

At one point Xiang Yu and Liu Bang agree to divide their spheres along a boundary and suspend the fighting. It seems as though exhaustion may have produced a settlement. But Liu's advisers urge him not to let his opponent recover. The pursuit resumes. The coalition's final convergence depends on commanders who must be persuaded and on promised rewards for their part in the victory. Liu Bang is not simply collecting loyal followers by charm. He is bargaining over a world that has not yet settled into a single center. In Sima Qian's telling, even the final triumph requires a political talent for bringing independent ambitions temporarily into alignment.

Then comes Gaixia. Xiang Yu's force is surrounded, the food situation is desperate, and the Han troops sing songs associated with Chu. Hearing the sounds of his own homeland from the enemy camp, Xiang Yu wonders whether Chu has already passed out of his control. He drinks with his favored companion Yu and sings of his strength and his failure to find a way forward. The beloved horse and the human companion belong to a scene of farewell. The historian lets a man who has been almost impossibly powerful face the fact that personal might cannot restore a vanished position. There are later dramatic embellishments of Yu's final fate, and famous stage versions of the story. The *Shiji* scene itself must be distinguished from all of those later additions.

Xiang Yu breaks through with a small group of riders. His escape becomes another extraordinary sequence, at once a last display of military skill and an account of narrowing options. He drives off pursuers and kills opponents, insisting that Heaven has destroyed him and that he has not been defeated through lack of skill in battle. That claim is revealing. The military talents by which he understands himself remain intact in his own account, yet the world they were meant to master has slipped away. He reaches the Wu River, where a boatman offers to carry him across. South of the river he might have found refuge among his old people. Xiang Yu refuses. Too many of the men who left with him have died, and he cannot bear to return alone and meet the parents who entrusted them to him. He fights again and dies.

The closing scene is not written to make defeat shameful. Sima Qian grants the defeated leader grandeur, courage, and a heartbreaking awareness of loss. But his concluding judgment is critical. Xiang Yu trusted force, celebrated his achievements, and failed to recognize what his own decisions had done to his position. There is a severe literary justice in the placement of that judgment after the intimate songs and the final refusal of rescue. The historian allows us to feel sympathy for a man and then insists that sympathy cannot take the place of analysis. A ruler may die bravely and still have governed badly.

Liu Bang becomes the founding emperor of the Han. This is not the end of the human cost. Consolidating the dynasty means rewarding some allies, weakening others, controlling former rivals, and navigating the danger posed by generals who helped win the throne. The founder's biography in chapter eight offers its own mixtures of shrewdness, arrogance, humor, and political improvisation. In the whole *Shiji*, the winning house is not exempt from scrutiny merely because it eventually commissioned and inherited the world in which the historian wrote. The historian is interested in winners, but he does not write only their official triumphs.

The two figures remain compelling because Sima Qian refuses to reduce greatness to outcome. Xiang Yu achieves victories no one can forget, yet fails to build a lasting arrangement. Liu Bang endures defeats that ought to have ended his cause, yet succeeds in drawing people and resources into a durable political order. Their story gives no clean formula for success. It shows the gap between winning the next battle and creating a world that people can inhabit after the fighting. At the Yi River, another *Shiji* hero had gone to his death against Qin. At the Wu River, the man who helped defeat Qin refuses to flee from the consequences of his own war. Sima Qian makes both rivers places where public history and private decision suddenly become impossible to separate.
