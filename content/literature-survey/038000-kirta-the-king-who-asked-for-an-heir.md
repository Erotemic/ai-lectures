# Kirta: The King Who Asked for an Heir

An original narration of the surviving Ugaritic Kirta/Keret epic (KTU 1.14–1.16), using an accessible column-divided KTU English rendering and modern scholarly reconstructions. The tablets have lacunae; variant readings and conjectures are acknowledged. It ends at the preserved confrontation between Kirta and Yassib, not at an invented succession settlement.

The king is alone in his room, weeping. He has a palace and a throne, but neither can give him the thing he wants. His family has been cut down. Children have died, his marriage has ended in loss, and the line through which his house might continue appears broken. The throne of Kirta, also called Keret in modern books, may pass into the hands of strangers.

We know him from a set of broken clay tablets found at the ancient city of Ugarit, on the coast of what is now Syria. The tablets speak the language of Ugarit and were written by a scribe in the world of the Late Bronze Age. They preserve a poem about the desperate work of securing a family, the cost of a promise made to the gods, and an astonishing fight between a father and his son.

Kirta's grief opens the poem. It does not first celebrate a conquest or explain the splendor of his court. It counts losses. Family members who should have filled a great house are no longer there. He lies down crying and falls asleep. A god comes to him in a dream.

The visitor is El, the high god, whom the poem calls father of humanity. El asks why the king weeps. Does he want more wealth, more horses, more silver? Kirta's answer is that riches cannot replace children. He needs someone who will inherit, remember, and sustain the house when he is gone. He does not ask to be made immortal. He asks for continuity beyond his own life.

El tells him what to do. The divine instruction becomes a full plan for an expedition. Kirta must prepare provisions, assemble a force, and travel to a city called Udum. There a ruler named Pabil has a daughter, Huraya, who can become Kirta's wife. With her, El promises, Kirta may obtain the family he has lost.

The poem spends a great deal of time describing the mobilization. It is a procession of extraordinary size. Not only strong young men but people who might normally remain behind are called into the march. The list works like a poetic expression of completeness: all the resources and all the life of the land gather behind a single king's need. This is a strange military expedition. Kirta's army can threaten a city, but its purpose is not to take a treasure house or annex fields. It is to acquire a bride.

The dream's instructions are not forgotten at dawn. They are enacted. Kirta prepares food and orders the march. Ancient narrative poetry often repeats a command when it is fulfilled, and the repetition allows listeners to hear the power of the dream being made real. The route stretches through days of marching until the walls of Udum stand before the host.

But the journey includes a stop El's instructions did not fully explain. Kirta comes to a sanctuary of Athirat, also called Asherah, a major goddess. He makes a vow. If his mission succeeds, he promises her a magnificent payment. The vow turns his desire for a family into a debt that must be honored once the journey is over. The words are spoken on the road, before the city has surrendered and before Huraya has entered his house.

At Udum, the size of the army produces terror. The surrounding settlements face pressure. People and animals are threatened by the presence of a force that seems too large to resist. Yet Pabil cannot easily understand the demand. He can offer Kirta wealth, precious goods, perhaps political concessions. Kirta rejects the offers. The one gift that will make him withdraw is Huraya.

The siege itself becomes an argument over what a ruler may demand from another ruler, and over the fate of a woman whose marriage is negotiated through their power. The poem chiefly attends to Kirta's need and the divine promise, not to Huraya's consent in the terms a modern story would expect. We should not silently turn this into a courtship in which she chooses freely because the later family is important. The text presents a king using an army to obtain a wife and a father pressured by that force into giving up his daughter.

At last Huraya is brought to Kirta. He returns with her, and the poem turns from the hostile march to the building of a household. There is feasting and celebration. The gods gather to bless the new marriage. El promises children, an abundance of sons and daughters. The terrible opening image—a house emptied by death—seems to have been answered by a divine assurance that the house will be full again.

It is tempting to stop the story here. The lost family is replaced. The weeping king receives a wife, an heir, and a restored future. But the poem has been careful to plant a danger on the way to Udum. Kirta promised something to Athirat. A king who believes a blessing has been given to him may forget the obligation by which he sought it.

Time passes. Children are born, and one son, Yassib, becomes especially important to the succession. But the goddess remembers the vow. The poem links Kirta's later illness with the unfulfilled obligation. Precisely how the divine punishment works is partly a matter of interpretation, because damage to the text interrupts the sequence; what survives is enough to make the vow and the illness central to how the crisis is told.

Kirta falls gravely ill. Once more the palace becomes a place of fear and mourning. The man who had wept because he had no heirs now has children who fear losing their father. The king's body appears to be failing. There are scenes of concern and lament; his illness also threatens the order of a kingdom whose ruler should be able to protect the weak, hear disputes, and keep violence from becoming the common language of public life.

His daughter Thitmanit, in particular, belongs to the story of the family's distress. The names and relationships of the children appear amid broken passages and variant readings, but the essential movement is clear: the restored household is at risk of losing the person around whom its restoration was organized.

The gods take counsel. Baal is among the divine figures who respond to the king's plight, and El finally devises a remedy. From materials of the earth he makes a being named Sha'taqat, whose work is to drive away sickness. She travels to the palace and performs the healing act. The poet gives this cure a physical character: illness is expelled, the king is cleaned, and his appetite returns. To be hungry again, to eat, to rise from a bed and reclaim a throne—these are the signs that life is winning against death.

Kirta's recovery ought to bring another celebration. It does not. The next challenge comes not from Athirat's sanctuary or a foreign ruler, but from his own son.

Yassib approaches his father and says that a man who has been sick and inactive cannot remain king. He accuses Kirta of failing in duties of judgment and protection. While the ruler lay ill, the poor and the vulnerable suffered. A king must rule, not merely occupy the throne. There is force in that critique. We have heard of suffering at court and the weakness of an afflicted ruler. Yet the accusation is made by the heir who stands to gain from the father's removal. It is difficult to separate an argument about justice from an ambitious son's desire to rule.

We are suddenly back at the poem's opening question. Kirta wanted a son to save his house from extinction. Now that son places the father under pressure. The very inheritance for which Kirta marched and bargained has become a source of conflict. A family line is not the same thing as family peace. Creating an heir means creating someone who may one day want the throne while its present holder still lives.

Kirta answers with a curse. He invokes the gods against Yassib, calling for the son's head to be struck. In the surviving lines there is no gentle reconciliation and no convincing surrender of power to a righteous youth. The father who once pleaded for a child now speaks terrible words toward one. The scene ends on the edge of another family catastrophe.

Here we have to distinguish the poem from the conclusion a later reader might want. The surviving tablets preserve the confrontation and curse; they do not give us a securely recoverable subsequent history of Yassib, or a complete succession settlement. Scholars have offered proposals about what the king's words may mean for the younger members of his family, including Thitmanit. Those proposals are interesting, but they are not scenes to be narrated as though a lost tablet had been found.

There is another caution. Kirta is a legendary king within a poem. We have surviving manuscripts from ancient Ugarit, not a direct chronicle proving that a specific historical king assembled this exact army or stood before the walls of Udum to demand Huraya. The physical tablets are historical evidence. The events they tell belong to a literary world where gods speak in dreams and create healers to answer mortal prayers.

Within that world, the tragedy is tightly built. Kirta begins without an heir. El tells him to seek a wife. A threatening army goes to Udum, and a vow is made. The king gains children but leaves the goddess's claim unsettled. Illness enters the palace. Divine healing raises him from the bed. Then his heir confronts him with an accusation that being a father is not enough to make a man a good king.

The poem's rulers are bound by obligations that cannot be satisfied through power alone. Kirta can mobilize a formidable host; he cannot simply command the gods to forget a promise. He can acquire an heir through an expedition; he cannot command an adult son to remain grateful forever. He can be healed of an illness; he cannot easily heal the tension between retaining a throne and passing it on.

In this ancient story, the future of the royal household seems most fragile just after it has been saved. The man who wanted a son more than anything hears that son demand his crown. The story does not offer us a secure glimpse beyond the curse. It leaves the cost of succession before our eyes: a father and an heir facing one another, each claiming to defend the order that the other threatens.
