# The Homeric Hymn to Hermes: A Newborn Thief and a New Song

An original retelling of the long *Homeric Hymn to Hermes* (Hymn 4, lines 1–580), following its inventions, cattle raid, quarrel before Zeus, musical bargain and allocation of honors. Based on the H. G. Evelyn-White (1914) public-domain translation; dialogue is reported or newly phrased rather than copied.

A baby is born in a cave. Before the day is over, he has made a musical instrument, stolen his elder brother's cattle, invented an elaborate way of hiding his tracks, committed a strange sacrifice, and gone back to bed with an expression of innocence. His brother is furious. His father finds the whole affair difficult to take seriously. But the baby is serious about one thing: he wants a share of the world.

This is the long *Homeric Hymn to Hermes*, one of the funniest surviving Greek divine narratives. Its Hermes is not yet the familiar polished messenger of the gods, a young man with winged sandals who carries instructions from heaven to earth. He is newly born, fiercely inventive, and almost impossible to catch in a straightforward statement. The story shows him winning recognition by turning theft into a negotiation and raw cleverness into powers that the other gods will have to respect.

His mother is Maia, daughter of Atlas, who lives apart from the gatherings of Olympus in a sheltered cave on Mount Cyllene. Zeus visits her secretly. From their union comes Hermes. The hymn insists that his gifts appear immediately: he is a wily speaker, one who knows how to contrive and conceal, the sort of god who can make trouble even when shut inside a nursery.

The first object he encounters is a tortoise at the entrance to the cave. He is delighted. He addresses it as a marvelous discovery and carries it inside. Then the poem turns abruptly from affection to making. Hermes kills the creature, removes what he needs, hollows and fashions its shell, fixes a frame and strings, and invents a lyre. A life in the wild has become an instrument capable of new voices. The scene is comic in tone but materially precise enough to remind the audience that invention can be violent. The first sound of music comes through the work of hands upon a body.

Hermes tests his instrument and sings. He celebrates his parents and the circumstances of his birth. The newborn who will soon lie extravagantly has begun by telling, or at least singing, a version of his own origins. He has made a thing that can command attention without a weapon. It will be his strongest bargaining tool, though at first he treats it almost as a passing experiment. He sets the lyre aside because his stomach and his ambition now point elsewhere.

He leaves the cave and travels by night to the pastures where Apollo's cattle are kept. Apollo is the great god of archery, prophecy, and music, born to Leto and Zeus. In the divine family he is Hermes's elder half brother, already possessed of honors the newborn lacks. Hermes chooses the kind of property that announces an elder god's wealth: a large herd, carefully guarded and difficult to move unseen.

He drives the cattle away, but he does not simply leave tracks pointing to their destination. He manipulates their movement so that the hoofprints confuse anyone who follows them, making the animals seem to have gone in the wrong direction. For his own feet he fashions devices that leave unfamiliar marks. Everything about the route is designed to turn evidence against an investigator. The thief is also a producer of false narratives. The ground will tell one story while he and the cows have actually lived another.

An old man working in the fields sees something of the procession. Hermes offers him a compact lesson in discretion: witnessing an event does not mean you must make yourself its herald. The advice is delivered with the wit of someone who has never yet needed to live with the consequences of his own behavior. The old witness is not transformed into a companion on a quest. He is a practical problem in a scheme that depends on having no reliable observers.

At the place where Hermes conceals the herd, he separates two cattle and prepares a sacrifice. The poet dwells on fire, implements, and portions of meat. Hermes divides the offering among twelve portions associated with the divine assembly. This child wants to be counted in a company that has not yet given him a place. But the sacrifice poses an awkward question. Gods receive sacrificial honors; do they eat the meat like hungry mortals? Hermes is tempted by the smell, yet the poem leaves him not consuming the food. The episode mixes appetite with ritual, and a young god's improvised religion with his ambition for recognized status.

He hides the remaining cattle, conceals the signs of his fire, and goes back to Maia's cave. There he slips through an opening and climbs into his cradle, wrapping himself up as if he had never been anywhere. He holds a blanket close, and the lyre lies nearby. The audience knows that the baby has crossed an enormous landscape and acted like an accomplished criminal. His posture says, with comic extravagance, that he is helpless.

Maia is not fooled. She recognizes both the force of her son's intelligence and the danger of making enemies so early. Hermes answers as a child who has already developed a theory of family ambition. Why should they remain in a dim cave, deprived of honors, when the other immortals receive offerings and wealth? He is not content with the place allotted to a hidden child of Zeus. If necessary, he threatens to make his way to Delphi and steal even more from Apollo's sanctuary. Theft is his first argument for a promotion.

Meanwhile Apollo discovers the absence of his cattle. He follows the signs, questions people, and works toward the cave. Every clue becomes more aggravating. The tracks are absurd. The thief seems impossible to identify. Yet Apollo sees in the signs a deliberate intelligence and eventually arrives at Maia's dwelling. There is Hermes in the cradle, an infant who can barely be made to look old enough for a day's journey.

Apollo demands the cattle. Hermes feigns bewilderment. A newborn, he suggests, could not drive a herd across mountains; why accuse someone who has only just come into the world? He delivers his protest with the solemnity of an oath, while carefully bending the language of truth. Apollo is not persuaded. He has encountered an adversary whose tiny body hides a fully developed capacity for invention.

The brothers quarrel. Apollo threatens and pulls the child from his bedding. Hermes turns physical indignity into comedy, making a spectacle of helplessness and bodily embarrassment. His older brother wants a clean admission of guilt. Hermes keeps presenting a performance that makes the demand appear ridiculous. The result is more than a chase after stolen animals. It is a confrontation between different kinds of power: Apollo has rank, physical strength, and established authority; Hermes can make any straightforward accusation seem uncertain.

When they come before Zeus, the great father of gods and mortals becomes a judge. Apollo presents the facts. Hermes insists he is an innocent baby. Zeus sees through the defense, but the sight of the tiny defendant and his outrageous certainty amuses him. He orders the brothers to go together and find the cattle, and he tells Hermes to reveal where he has hidden them. The divine courtroom does not end the matter with a punishment proportionate to the theft. It becomes the setting in which Hermes's powers can be acknowledged and converted into a relationship.

As they travel, Apollo hears the lyre. A sound unlike the instruments he already knows emerges from the little thing Hermes has made with tortoise shell and strings. The angry elder brother stops to listen. His desire changes direction. Cattle that seemed indispensable a moment earlier can now be exchanged for something more valuable to him than livestock: music made through an art he has not previously possessed.

Hermes plays and sings a song that celebrates the gods. In some ways it repeats the beginning of his day. At first he sang his own birth in the cave, to an audience of almost no one. Now he performs before an elder deity whose approval can change his standing. Music makes an immediate social difference. The brother who had been hunting a thief now wants what the thief has invented.

The bargain begins. Hermes gives Apollo the lyre, teaching him how to play it, and Apollo yields control of the cattle to Hermes. The brothers return the herd, and Apollo receives his new instrument. The poem delights in the reversal: the god associated with music has acquired a central part of his musical identity from his youngest brother, while the thief becomes a legitimate keeper of animals. The old hierarchy has not been destroyed. It has been revised through an exchange.

The friendship still requires caution. Apollo recognizes that Hermes could steal the lyre back as easily as he stole the cattle, perhaps taking the bow as well. He asks for a pledge. Hermes promises not to rob him of what now belongs to him or intrude upon his house. Apollo, in turn, gives pledges of affection and grants further honors, including a fine staff. The language of the encounter moves from accusation to gift, from false evidence to an agreement that both sides can repeat publicly.

Hermes is still not allowed to possess everything Apollo possesses. He seeks a portion of prophecy, but Apollo distinguishes the oracle of Zeus's will from lesser forms of divination. The power to reveal the deepest divine purposes is not a thing he can surrender. Instead he tells his brother of other methods of learning signs, including prophetic bee maidens in a mountain setting, and describes how attention to omens can sometimes profit a traveler. A boundary remains even as friendship is made. Hermes has entered the divine community, but he is not another Apollo.

Hermes will become associated with messages, roads, trade, exchange, herds, and encounters across borders. The hymn's plot makes those roles intelligible. He is a god who finds loopholes in every boundary: the wall of a cave, the track left by an animal, the distance between one god's property and another's desire, the line between truth and a performance of truth. Yet his talent is not merely disorder. By the end he has made a musical art and helped two rivals discover an agreement neither could have imposed alone.

The story is not asking us to approve ordinary theft as a moral rule for human beings. Its divine world has its own comic scale, where the legal language of ownership collides with the exuberance of an immortal baby's self-promotion. The gods' honors explain why an ability matters and how it came to be allotted. Hermes's deception is genuinely deception, even while the poem invites us to enjoy it. Apollo's anger is genuinely justified, even while his stubborn insistence on catching a baby becomes amusing.

The most revealing exchange may be the simplest. Apollo begins by searching for missing cows. He ends by holding a lyre. Hermes begins by demanding an honor he has not been granted. He ends with his first recognized possessions and a place in the divine family. Neither gets exactly what he demanded at the start. What changes the world is the invention of something that makes a better bargain imaginable.

And all of this occurs within the newborn god's first day. He returns from his travels not as the innocent child he pretended to be, but as the figure his mother feared and his father finally recognized: a restless intelligence with the power to move between different orders of things. The hymn closes by praising a god whose talent is to make connections, even when those connections begin with a stolen cow and a very implausible story.

## Textual basis and source notes (not spoken)

- Anonymous *Homeric Hymn to Hermes*, hymn 4, lines 1–580, H. G. Evelyn-White (1914), public-domain translation, Greek/English Perseus-derived transcript indexed below.
- Lines 1–90: birth and tortoise-shell lyre; 91–209: theft, track trick, old witness, sacrifice, return to cradle; 210–339: Apollo investigates and confronts Hermes; 340–444: Zeus's judgment and the revealing of the herd; 445–580: lyre, exchange, pledges, staff, differentiated forms of prophecy and divine honors.
- The poem's discussion of divination and Apollo's retained knowledge should not be flattened into a claim that Hermes takes over the Delphic oracle.
- This is a divine origin comedy, not a biographical account of historical humans or a moral commendation of stealing.
- Orientation: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homeric_Hymns ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hermes ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_mythology .
