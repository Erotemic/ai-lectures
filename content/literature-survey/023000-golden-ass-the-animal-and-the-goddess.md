# The Golden Ass, Part Three: The Animal and the Goddess

Original continuous retelling of Apuleius's *Metamorphoses* Books VII–XI, following Lucius through changing owners and the temple of Isis. The novel's conclusion is religious, comic, and potentially ironical; the lecture describes its received form without flattening its ambiguity. Translation locators via Poetry in Translation's individual books.

Psyche's story ends with a wedding among gods. The donkey that listened to it remains in a cave with thieves. The contrast is merciless. A tale has promised that a person can pass through a sequence of impossible trials and be recognized for what she truly is. But Lucius is not recognized. He cannot speak to tell the robbers that he is a man. When he struggles, they call him a stubborn animal. When he runs, they beat him. What he wants most is a mouthful of roses, the ordinary flower that will cancel a magical mistake.

The next movement of Apuleius's *The Golden Ass* is a road of changing masters. In Book VII, a man joins the robber band with an account of daring adventures. He seems to be another thief and proposes a plan involving the captive Charite. In fact he is her beloved, Tlepolemus, come in disguise to rescue her. He makes the band believe they have found a capable new leader, gets the robbers drunk, and brings Charite out of danger. Lucius, the donkey, is also taken away. For a moment the world seems ready to reward both a faithful lover and the animal that carried the woman through danger.

But the narrator's restored fortune is fragile. Being bought, kept, or praised by someone sympathetic does not mean that an animal has become free. People still calculate how much he can carry and what labor might be extracted. His attempted self-defense may be read as obstinacy. Even a household that is kind to him can be overturned by others' cruelty. The novel repeatedly shows that his intelligence offers almost no protection once the people around him refuse to believe an animal has a human life inside.

The worst turn comes through a story about Charite. Another man, Thrasyllus, wants her for himself. His desire does not end when she marries Tlepolemus. During a hunt, he engineers the husband's death, then presents himself as a grieving friend ready to console the widow. Charite learns what has happened. She pretends to accept the murderer's advances, drugs him, and blinds him. Then, overwhelmed by the life that cannot be restored, she dies at her husband's tomb. Thrasyllus shuts himself away to die. We are far from the delicate palace of Cupid and Psyche. Charite's brief rescue from kidnapping did not guarantee a happy life. The novel allows romance to collapse back into a very human world of exploitation and violence.

Lucius is passed onward. One set of owners are attendants associated with the Syrian Goddess. They move from place to place performing religious displays and gathering money and food. The text portrays them with elaborate mockery, directing ridicule at their behavior, sexual life, and claims to authority. Much of the comedy depends on prejudices and stereotypes circulating in the Roman world. We can describe what the narrator sees without adopting his targets as our own. The same traveler who will later praise his service to Isis has observed forms of religion he portrays as corrupt. What separates authentic devotion from performance is one of the novel's unresolved pressures.

Among his new masters, Lucius continues to be hungry and beaten. He is driven from house to house, subjected to quarrels, thefts, and physical punishment. At times people think they are hiding behind closed doors, never suspecting that the donkey can understand them. He hears stories of sexual deception and domestic cruelty, and they become part of the book's entertainment. We learn a society through the things it lets an animal overhear. The lowly creature slips into rooms where a traveler of respectable standing might have been refused admission.

In Book IX, the donkey is put to work in a mill. The machines must turn, and slaves and animals turn them. Here the narrative stops its play of witty social embarrassment long enough to show bodies used up by labor: backs scarred, legs exhausted, faces covered in dust, men barely protected by rags. Lucius sees other beings reduced to functions. He had once studied magic for the pleasure of changing form. Now transformation is not a fleeting trick but a position in a work system. He shares that position with people who were not enchanted; they were simply enslaved.

The novel has no modern account of animal rights or labor equality waiting behind the page. Lucius is mainly outraged that he, a person who remembers being free and educated, has been treated as a beast. Still, his vantage point reveals what he could have ignored as a human traveler: the ordinary means by which affluent households and businesses make living bodies endure what is convenient for owners.

The book keeps changing its tone. In one episode an adulterous lover hides in a vessel, hoping to escape discovery. Another deception is uncovered by a sudden accident. Family quarrels become sharp little dramas in which suspicion, greed, and fear produce escalating punishments. Stories of betrayal move from one household to another, and the donkey listens like a witness who cannot testify. The outrageous anecdotes can be funny and can also be exhausting; that exhaustion is part of a novel in which neither luck nor good judgment permits Lucius to remain safe.

By Book X he has become a curiosity because he appears to enjoy human food and behave with human discernment. Those who discover his talents turn them into commercial opportunities. He is trained to perform for spectators, to make audiences laugh with the incongruity of an animal behaving as a person. He knows perfectly well that the person is real. The audience's pleasure depends on not knowing that. What began as his own desire to watch strange transformations has become a public theater in which his body is the show.

A wealthy woman becomes enamored of the remarkable animal. The novel treats the episode as a sensational comedy. Later, Lucius is expected to participate in an arena spectacle involving a woman condemned for grave crimes, part of an entertainment that is arranged to produce sexual humiliation and death. He is horrified by the prospect and fears the wild animals that will be released. It is not heroic principle alone that sends him fleeing. He wants not to be made the instrument of somebody else's punishment and not to die along with her. He escapes the handlers and runs until he reaches the seacoast at Cenchreae.

There, alone, he sleeps and wakes under the moon. The road has brought him to a limit. He cannot persuade a human owner. He cannot collect the roses he needs by ordinary means. He can only pray. He calls upon the great goddess under several names by which worshipers know her and asks for release from the animal life he has endured.

The goddess Isis appears in a vision. She tells him she has heard his suffering and sets out a specific instruction. The next morning there will be a procession in her honor. Among the worshipers a priest will carry a garland of roses. Lucius must approach that priest and eat the flowers. He will become human again, and in return he is to devote his life to her service.

The following morning, the festival approaches in a movement of light, color, ritual objects, and costumed worshipers. Lucius watches eagerly. The priest appears with the flowers. The donkey moves forward. The priest, prepared by divine instruction, offers the garland. Lucius eats the roses, and the change begins. Hair falls away, feet and hands take on their human shapes, the long face shortens, the animal's voice yields to a man's. The crowd sees a miracle; Lucius stands exposed, naked, astonished and free.

This is a kind of recognition scene, but its emotional meaning is different from Psyche's. She was threatened by her own desire to see the person she loved. Lucius has wanted people to see the person within the animal. The goddess's grace finally makes the inner and outer forms agree again. Human witnesses who treated him as livestock had no idea what they were handling. Their error is exposed in an instant.

The novel could end here. A story about a magical mistake has found the flowers that reverse it. But it does not end. Lucius becomes devoted to Isis, spends time with her priests, prepares for initiation, and receives access to a religious life the text treats with extraordinary seriousness. He explains that some rites cannot be openly described; he writes of approaching the borders of death and returning. The language is exalted and deliberately guarded. We do not possess from this novel a transparent set of instructions for reenacting a Roman mystery initiation, and an educational retelling should not invent its hidden ceremonies.

Later Lucius goes to Rome and undergoes further initiations connected with Osiris. These cost money. He must prepare himself, consult priests, and interpret dreams in which the gods give him instructions. He also begins to build an adult career. At the end, the former donkey is no longer a penniless beast driven from owner to owner. He belongs to religious institutions and to the social world of Roman public life. In a final turn, the novel makes a joke on his new dignity through the bodily signs of his religious office, including a shaven head, as if one visible marker has replaced another.

Readers have argued about how sincerely we should receive this ending. The religious vision is narrated with devotion; the same book has spent ten books exposing religious posturing, credulity, greed, and the human appetite for spectacles. Initiation brings Lucius a new life, yet the demands of the cult also bring expenses and another hierarchy of authority. We need not decide that Book XI is merely a parody or that it erases all the satire before it. Apuleius has written a novel roomy enough for wonder, mockery, and conviction to remain in conversation.

The man who began the book wanted to watch magic without being changed by it. Instead he was changed so radically that he could watch the human world only from below. His return is not a simple restoration of the curious traveler who first rode into Thessaly. He has seen things that traveler would likely have passed by: the mill's exhausted bodies, a captive bride's fear, the humiliations staged for the crowd, a priest's promise, a goddess's face. The donkey becomes human again, but the novel asks whether he can remain the same kind of human being.

At the seashore, the roses finally appeared. Yet the last book asks a harder question than how to undo a spell. How does a person make a life after the spell has been undone? The road from the thieves' cave to the procession of Isis is more than a journey through a string of owners. It is a journey through kinds of seeing: what a master sees in a servant, what a crowd sees in a performer, what a lover sees in disguise, and what a man discovers about himself when other people no longer recognize his humanity.

- Reference: [Brill, Apuleius Online](https://scholarlyeditions.brill.com/apo/); [Wikipedia: Golden Ass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Golden_Ass); [Fordham Ancient History Sourcebook, Isis passage](https://sourcebooks.fordham.edu/ancient/lucius-assa.asp).
- Episodes are told in novel order; selected subplots are summarized instead of represented as exhaustively recounted. The ending's sincerity and initiation's meaning are scholarly questions; don't use Apuleius as a ritual manual. The narrator's hostile representation of the Syrian Goddess's attendants is Roman literary satire, not an objective report about their community.
