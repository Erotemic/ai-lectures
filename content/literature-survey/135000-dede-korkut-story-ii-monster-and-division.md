# The Book of Dede Korkut — Story II: The Monster, the Brothers, and the Broken Oghuz

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

A child can know his father's name without knowing his father's face. In another of Dede Korkut's tales, Kazılık Koca rides against a stronghold and is taken prisoner, leaving a son too young to understand the loss. Years pass. The son, Yigenek, grows up among people who remember the captive man, while the family lives with a silence it cannot end. When he finally learns the truth, the knowledge becomes a command: a father waits in a foreign fortress, and the young man must decide whether to seek him. Warriors join Yigenek's expedition. At the fortress, the enemy champion has defeated earlier challengers, and the company is reminded that the rescue cannot be accomplished merely by declaring a noble purpose. Yigenek confronts and defeats the defender. The father is brought out, and the family line, interrupted by prison, is joined again. The victory belongs to a recognizable heroic pattern: a child becomes a rescuer when the parent can no longer rescue himself.

The repetition is deliberate. In these stories, fathers and sons repeatedly exchange positions. Salur Kazan rescues Uruz; later Uruz must rescue Kazan. The Oghuz are a world of lineages as well as individual warriors. To remove one father for years is to leave a story unfinished in a household. To bring him back is to make the old order visible again. But not all the dangers of the Oghuz come from enemy fortresses. The cycle now tells a story about an enemy raised inside the community, and here the repeated pattern of rescue is not enough.

In the tale of Basat and Tepegöz, the beginning is a violation. A shepherd assaults a supernatural woman. From that encounter comes an extraordinary child with one eye, a being whom ordinary weapons will not readily harm. An Oghuz lord takes him into his household. He is given care, but he injures and kills those appointed to tend him; he attacks other children, and at last the community casts him out. His mother has endowed him with powers that make him resistant to their weapons. When he returns, he is no longer merely a troublesome child. He becomes a devourer. The Oghuz can bargain with him only by offering people and livestock in tribute. A society that measures its strength in warriors is forced to count how many of its own people it must surrender each day.

Basat has himself grown up at the edge of the human world. In the story he is associated with a lion's care before being brought into Oghuz society, a reminder that belonging is not decided only at birth. When he hears what Tepegöz has done, including the death of his brother, he goes to face the creature. The monster's invulnerable skin makes ordinary courage insufficient. Basat must observe, wait, and discover where the giant is vulnerable. He blinds Tepegöz with a heated implement. Even then the struggle continues: getting free from a blinded monster who knows his own enclosure requires cunning as well as force. In versions of this encounter the hero passes through traps and tests before he can strike the final blow. He survives and kills Tepegöz, ending the tribute.

It is tempting to say this is simply another one-eyed-giant story, a Turkish answer to the Cyclops in the Odyssey. The resemblance is real, but Basat's drama has its own arrangement. Tepegöz is not met on an isolated island by strangers seeking food. He has been fostered among the people he now eats. The question is how a community's rejected, violent dependent became a power it could not control. Even the victor Basat is someone with a childhood outside ordinary society. The final defeat is therefore not just a contest between a man and a monster. It is an attempt to end a catastrophe that has grown from the group's own history.

Another tale brings us down from the monstrous to the painfully ordinary. Begil, one of the Oghuz guardians, has felt slighted by the great lord Kazan. He withdraws to his own land. A hunting accident leaves him with a broken leg, an injury that might have remained a household matter if neighbors had not learned of it. Enemies see a chance to strike a weakened frontier. Begil cannot ride out to meet them. His son Emren must stand where his father once stood. The young man goes into battle and, in the tale's religious language, receives divine aid. He defeats the opposing champion and saves his father's land. The enemy's change of allegiance and the final celebration restore the political boundary as well as the family honor. But the beginning should not be forgotten: the danger followed wounded pride inside the Oghuz. A commander's careless judgment helped isolate one of the men on whom the community relied.

The next family has a different secret. Egrek has been captured during a reckless expedition and held for years. His younger brother Segrek grows up without understanding what happened. When the truth is finally spoken, Segrek's first duty, as he sees it, is not to accept an arranged domestic happiness but to find his brother. He has a bride waiting, a family eager to keep him near, and a dangerous journey ahead. The tale does not present the conflict as a simple choice between love and adventure. Segrek knows that the marriage would be shadowed by a captive brother whose condition everyone had learned to endure.

He goes to the stronghold, and the defenses are formidable. At one point he sleeps, trusting to the watchfulness of his horse. The animal's alarm saves him from an attack; the hero's survival depends on a companion without words. Enemies then bring Egrek forward to fight the stranger who has arrived. For a moment a brother may become the instrument of the other brother's death. But recognition intervenes before combat can erase the family it is meant to defend. The captives and rescuers discover who stands before them, join forces, and escape. Segrek returns with Egrek. The wedding can at last be a celebration without the unspoken absent man.

Salur Kazan, the leader who earlier searched for his kidnapped wife and son, is himself taken captive in another adventure. A hunting journey, again, carries powerful men beyond the protection of their tents. An enemy lord catches him and confines him beneath a castle. Kazan is ordered to honor his captors. Instead he answers with a defiant song. A warrior's body can be forced into a well, the tale seems to say, but his public voice can remain beyond his captors' command. Years pass before Uruz learns of his father's imprisonment. The son leads a force to rescue him. The enemy attempts to use Kazan in the fighting, but father and son come to recognize one another. They defeat the captors and return. Kazan once redeemed his son from captivity. Now the return has come back around, and Uruz's adulthood is measured not by how splendidly he speaks of honor but by whether he can act when the older generation is helpless.

These rescues and weddings could have made an endlessly renewable cycle. A lord is captured, a young fighter wins a name, Dede Korkut speaks, and the camp celebrates. The final tale refuses that repetition. The Oghuz are divided into Inner and Outer groups, and a dispute breaks out over the ceremonial privilege of plundering Kazan's household. The terms may sound strange to us. In the tales the distribution of goods and public recognition at feasts can be political acts: to include a group is to recognize its place, and to exclude it is to risk making a feud. The Outer Oghuz believe they have been slighted. Their leader, Aruz, turns grievance into revolt against Kazan.

Aruz demands that Bamsı Beyrek join the rebellion. This is the same Beyrek who once returned from sixteen years' captivity, recognized his bride, exposed the false claimant, and regained his own name and household. He also has relationships on both sides of the division. Here is the crucial test. The man who endured prison for years can now save his life by changing his allegiance. But he refuses to betray Kazan. Aruz kills him. The tale gives the audience something that the preceding triumphs did not prepare it to accept: the hero who came home alive once will not come home alive a second time. There is no delayed recognition that can repair this killing and no wedding that can close it.

Kazan responds with force. He marches against the rebels and defeats Aruz. The clash ends, but a single victory cannot restore Beyrek to his friends or erase what made the revolt possible. A community has used the language of honor, loyalty, and proper division to murder one of its most celebrated men. The book closes its heroic sequence not with a foreign monster defeated, but with insiders killing an insider. A listener may recall the opening story of Boğaç, when jealous retainers almost destroyed a father and son. There the mother's intervention brought rescue and forgiveness. Here the consequences reach beyond one household. The quarrel grows large enough that the community's old capacity for return gives way.

Dede Korkut's presence is not the same as a guarantee of happiness. His blessings belong to the truth that lives end and their stories outlast them. In one tale a son receives his name; in another the strongest man is powerless against death; in another a creature raised by human hands preys on those who raised him; and in the last a hero who crossed a prison wall cannot cross a line of political betrayal. These are not twelve steps in a single hero's rise. They are a set of tests of a society that celebrates courage, kinship, generosity, and fidelity, while repeatedly showing how easy those goods are to put at war with one another. The Oghuz can fight giants and rescue captives. They are less secure when the quarrel is over who belongs, who has been honored, and who must choose a side.

## Text and evidence companion (not spoken)

**Narrative witness:** Dresden *Book of Dede Korkut*, tales 7–12. The original paraphrase follows episode boundaries checked against the [Dresden manuscript custodian's synopsis](https://www.slub-dresden.de/en/explore/manuscripts/kitab-i-dedem-korkut/dresdner-handschrift/inhalt). A critical English edition exists: Faruk Sümer, Ahmet E. Uysal and Warren S. Walker, *The Book of Dede Korkut: A Turkish Epic* (University of Texas Press, 1972), [contents](https://utpress.utexas.edu/9780292707870/). The full copyrighted translation was not collated. Modern transliterations and narrative emphases may differ between witnesses.

**Episode map:** Yigenek's rescue of Kazılık Koca (Dresden tale 7); Basat, Tepegöz and the child fostered among the Oghuz (8); Begil and Emren (9); Segrek and Egrek's recognition (10); Kazan's captivity and Uruz's rescue (11); the Inner–Outer Oghuz dispute and Beyrek's death (12). The cycles are narrated without adding scenes from parallel Oghuz legend as though they belonged in the Dresden text. For the monster's longer sequence of traps, the lecture summarizes rather than claims a complete translation.

**Care with comparisons:** The ancient *Odyssey* and the Tepegöz episode share a blinded one-eyed monster, but borrowing direction, chronology and common antecedents are scholarly questions, not settled here. The story contains ethnic-religious stereotypes and acts of conquest; such language is reported as literary testimony and not endorsed. An academic account of Dresden and Vatican manuscript differences is in the [Turkish Language Association journal](https://tdkbelleten.gov.tr/tam-metin-pdf/877/tur).
