# The Lusiads — Story I: The Sea, the Kings, and the Giant at the Cape

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

A fleet has crossed into the Indian Ocean, and the men aboard have no way of knowing that their voyage is already being debated on Olympus. Jupiter has assembled the gods to consider the Portuguese. Their ships are small, their passage long, and their courage evident. Venus takes their side, seeing in them a people worthy of favor. Mars supports them. Bacchus is afraid that if these newcomers win a place in the East, they will eclipse his own legendary fame there. So, in a world where Atlantic and Indian Ocean navigation can be discussed by Roman gods, a contest begins between the will to reach India and the powers determined to divert the ships. This is how Luís de Camões begins The Lusiads, a Portuguese epic published in 1572. It is a poem about a real voyage, but it is not a ship's log. Its hero is also a people, and its story continually changes scale—from a deck washed by saltwater to centuries of kings, from the wish of a sailor to get home to the impossible height of a giant made from a mountain.

The captain is Vasco da Gama. His destination is India by sea, around the southern tip of Africa. We meet the fleet partway through its journey along the East African coast. The men need pilots, water, food, directions, and enough trust to enter ports where they know little of local politics. Bacchus intervenes to poison those encounters. In Mozambique he stirs hostility toward the newcomers. An invitation or offer of assistance becomes a possible trap; the Portuguese attack and move on. Their next welcome at Mombasa is equally dangerous in the poem. The god takes advantage of strangers' dependence on intermediaries, while Venus rescues the fleet from disaster. Every harbor can look like deliverance until a host's intentions become clear. And because the poet makes the plot divine, the same coastal encounters carry two stories at once: the practical uncertainty of navigation and the moral certainty the epic wants to give its protagonists.

A safer welcome eventually comes at Malindi, which the poem calls Melinde. The local ruler receives the voyagers with hospitality and curiosity. Here the poem finds an ingenious way to slow down. If a captain is asked who his people are, how they came to be such sailors, and why they have taken so dangerous a road, his answer can contain a national history. Gama begins to recount Portugal's past. The ships remain at anchor while time turns backward. The listener is no longer watching a voyage alone, but hearing how the men aboard understand themselves as heirs to generations of struggle.

Gama's account begins with lands and peoples and the emergence of Portugal's kings. Battles are given the solemn dignity of epic. Afonso Henriques, the figure associated with Portugal's first kingship, rises in the narrative; Portuguese independence and warfare become part of the explanation for why a modest kingdom would later seek a world-spanning sea route. Gama offers to the king of Malindi a version of Portuguese history structured around exemplary rulers, hard-won victories, and proof of courage. In doing so, he turns a foreign audience into the imagined listener for an argument addressed to Portugal itself: our ancestors labored, therefore the present generation must be worthy of them.

Yet the history is not merely a parade of banners. The love and death of Inês de Castro interrupt the heroic rhythm. Inês loves Pedro, the heir to the throne, and the political consequences of their relationship become terrifying. The royal court fears the influence and succession troubles that could follow. Inês, a woman with children and no army, is sacrificed to the calculations of kingship. The poem allows her a plea for life, and then describes the killing that makes the royal story a tragedy. The grief of Pedro, afterward, can do nothing for the murdered woman. Camões famously makes the scene one of the emotional centers of his national history: the fate of a woman in a palace becomes as memorable as a military victory. If the epic is trying to prove that Portugal possesses greatness, it cannot keep every example from revealing what power does to the defenseless.

The succession crisis after King Fernando's death brings another test. Portugal's independence is contested, and a struggle with Castile draws the country toward catastrophe. The future King João I and the commander Nuno Álvares Pereira become central figures in the nation's survival. At Aljubarrota the Portuguese win the battle that fixes their place in the historical legend. The poem turns that victory into proof that a smaller people can resist a larger force when courage and leadership agree. But the chain of events also shows that the sea voyage is not the beginning of Portuguese ambition. By the time Da Gama sails, the kingdom has already developed a habit of imagining itself through dangers survived against powerful neighbors.

Now Gama's story reaches the age of ships and charts. Portuguese rulers have supported explorations south along the African coast. The hoped-for route to India is at first a possibility beyond known harbors, reached through trial voyages, imperfect information, and enormous cost. Under King Manuel the expedition is finally ordered. The men prepare their vessels, gather supplies, and make ready to leave the shore near Lisbon. Families come to see them go. The departure is heroic only from a distance. Up close it is the breaking of households: parents who cannot know whether they will see their sons again, spouses who may become widows, children who watch the ships take away people they love. The poem lets the cries on the beach rise against its language of glory.

An old man of Restelo watches the preparations and condemns the voyage. He asks what the appetite for fame is really purchasing. Why pursue distant power when grief and danger are already the likely price? Are those who speak of renown willing to carry the suffering that renown creates? His speech is not an instruction that cancels the expedition. The ships depart anyway. But the presence of the speech matters. A poet celebrating maritime empire has given a forceful objection a public place at the moment of departure. We should not pretend that he thereby becomes a modern opponent of colonial expansion; nor should we erase the objection because it complicates the celebration. The old man's words stay with the listener as the coast diminishes behind the fleet.

Da Gama's recounted voyage moves past unfamiliar shores and difficult weather. The sailors contend with fear, rough seas, and the loss of companions. The poet interweaves remembered stories of exploration with the dangers of the ongoing journey. A landing ashore can be no less threatening than a storm. One sailor, Fernão Veloso, ventures onto land and must flee back toward the boats; the moment has the rhythm of a badly judged adventure whose consequences almost turn deadly. Elsewhere the men encounter coastal peoples whose lives they do not understand. Camões's description carries the prejudice of an early modern European imperial voice. What we hear is what the poem makes of these encounters, not an impartial record of African societies.

Then, as the ships near the southern cape, a shape rises in the storm. It looks like an impossible mass of cloud and rock; it takes on the form of an enormous being whose anger fills the horizon. Adamastor is the monstrous spirit of the cape, a giant who speaks as though the sea's dangers had found a voice. He threatens the Portuguese with disasters to come, naming the capes and currents where ships will founder. He does not merely demand that the captain turn back. He tells him that the price of passage will continue to be collected in future lives. It is one of the great scenes in the poem because a geographical obstacle becomes a person who knows what human ambition would prefer not to know.

Gama refuses to be frightened into silence and asks who the giant is. Adamastor answers with a story of his own. Once he was a mighty being who loved a sea nymph, Thetis. His desire was answered with deception, and he became the rocky mass the sailors must pass. The monster is suddenly also a disappointed lover, his rage a grotesque expression of longing and punishment. The cape that terrifies navigators has a private history, though one made from divine fantasy. Camões has transformed the landform into an autobiography of frustrated desire. The fleet's passage is an act of navigation and an intrusion into an ancient grief.

The giant disappears, but the sea does not grow merciful. The ships still carry hunger, sickness, and weariness. When they finally approach safer eastern waters, the captain can tell the ruler of Malindi that they have survived. His account reaches the harbor where the telling began: a voyage has been narrated within a voyage. The two layers close together. Da Gama is no longer merely the captain standing before a hospitable king; he is the vessel through which the poem has brought the whole country to the edge of India.

Pause with the fleet at Malindi. It has not yet reached its destination. Bacchus is not finished; the gods are still divided. But the journey has already given us an important tension. The poem insists that the sailors' bravery deserves remembrance, and it also shows the grief of Inês, the warning of the old man, the desperation of relatives at the dock, and Adamastor's prophecy of drowned lives. Heroic fame and the suffering that makes it possible have become inseparable. That tension will follow the ships across the remaining sea. The next movement carries them to Calicut, where finding India proves very different from obtaining what they wanted there, and then to an impossible island where the poem tries to imagine what kind of reward a heroic history deserves.

## Text and evidence companion (not spoken)

**Primary literary witnesses:** Luís de Camões, *Os Lusíadas* (first printed 1572), cantos I–V; [Portuguese public-domain text](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/3333). For detailed plot and stanza checking consult Richard Francis Burton's 1880 public-domain translation and comments, [Volume I, cantos I–VI](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/77660/77660-h/77660-h.htm). The narration is a newly authored retelling, not a quotation from Burton or a substitute for the original octaves.

**Event locators:** Gods' council and Mozambique/Mombasa (I–II); arrival and hospitality at Malindi, beginning of Gama's historical account (II); Afonso Henriques, the Inês de Castro episode and Fernando (III); João I, Aljubarrota, preparations and the Old Man of Restelo (IV); African coast, Fernão Veloso, Adamastor, storms and arrival at Malindi in Gama's retrospective account (V). These are poetic episodes, not evidence by themselves for every historical claim they make.

**Historical boundary:** Vasco da Gama's first voyage to India was 1497–1499; Camões wrote decades later. The gods and Adamastor are literary inventions. The poem's depiction of African and South Asian peoples reflects sixteenth-century Portuguese imperial viewpoints. On epic form and expansionist tensions see [Columbia University's World Epics project](https://edblogs.columbia.edu/worldepics/project/os-lusiadas/), [University of Washington lecture on dissent and the Old Man of Restelo](https://classics.washington.edu/events/2018-04-30/pious-dissent-tradition-translation-and-subversive-allusion-luis-de-camoes), and [Wikipedia: The Lusiads](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Os_Lus%C3%ADadas) for orientation.
