# The Lusiads — Story II: Calicut, the Island of Love, and the World Revealed

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

After all the preparation, the arguments of kings, and the giant at the cape, the Portuguese fleet still has to reach India. The sea has not agreed to make this easy. Bacchus, who has already turned ports into traps, takes his struggle beneath the surface. He appeals to the gods of the ocean, warning them that human sailors are entering regions once governed by divine powers. Neptune's court answers the appeal, and a storm is prepared. The mariners do not see a council of gods; they see wind and water rising high enough to erase the horizon. In Camões's poem, the storm is part of the fleet's physical peril and part of a larger contest over whether these people will be allowed to make themselves known beyond their homeland.

The sailors try to keep their ships together. Orders are shouted through the wind. They know that the ocean does not care what kings may have promised or what chroniclers might later write. A ship can be broken in a single night. While the danger approaches, one of the men has been entertaining his companions with the tale of the Twelve of England, Portuguese knights who, in a chivalric adventure, defended the honor of English ladies. In the middle of a sea voyage the poem offers another story of overseas valor, as though the men require tales about the past to continue facing a future they cannot see. But stories do not calm a gale. Venus must intervene, sending her nymphs to soothe the furious winds. The weather changes. Morning brings the exhausted sailors the sight of a coast they have long pursued. They reach Calicut on the Malabar coast of India.

The relief of arrival is genuine. Yet reaching a destination is not the same thing as understanding it. Da Gama and his men are now visitors in a wealthy trading world that has existed without them for centuries. Ships come and go; goods, languages, religions, and alliances already connect the Indian Ocean. The Portuguese did not discover the people of India. They found a maritime route for themselves, and their arrival threatened to alter the terms of an existing commercial system. The poem itself does not speak in this modern historical register. It repeatedly presents the expedition as a heroic opening of the East to Portuguese fame. But its plot now reveals how difficult that ambition will be when the voyagers face a ruler with his own interests and advisers.

A local contact, Monçaide, approaches the newcomers and helps them navigate the port's language and customs. Da Gama goes ashore to meet the Samorin, the ruler of Calicut. The greetings are formal, the misunderstandings numerous, and the Portuguese captain must explain what his king wants. Trade and alliance are the goals he advertises. To the ruler, however, these new ships are one more uncertain factor in a complex regional world. The visitors' claims of greatness do not automatically make their cargo attractive or their intentions trustworthy. The poem's sympathies remain with Da Gama, but the very need to negotiate gives the scene a sharper truth than any simple claim that bravery makes all doors open.

Even painted banners become part of the negotiation. A local governor examines pictures displayed by the Portuguese, and Paulo da Gama explains the figures. The images lead to another account of Portuguese history, this time displayed as a gallery of ancestors and heroic deeds. The ship itself has become a portable monument. A foreign visitor can ask about a banner, and the answer will connect an emblem to a kingdom's ancient claims. Gama's men are trying not only to sell or exchange goods, but to make the Portuguese past legible to people who have no reason to know it. The scene tells us something about the epic's structure: historical memory is carried abroad as a form of political currency.

The encounter darkens. Rivals, intermediaries, advisers, and augurs stir suspicion around the fleet. Commercial interests fear newcomers who could disturb profitable routes. The poem attributes much of the hostility to the designs of Muslim merchants and the influence of Bacchus. It uses hostile religious language that we should recognize as the poet's viewpoint, not adopt as a reliable explanation for every real-world dispute in Calicut. Da Gama answers accusations, but he faces detentions and demands that threaten to leave the ships stranded. He has survived the cape and the sea, only to discover that a captain can be caught by a ruler's court as effectively as by a reef.

Monçaide helps reveal the danger. Da Gama secures the ships' freedom and departs with the news he came to seek: the route to India is navigable, and the Portuguese have reached an ocean of trade whose riches they hope to pursue. The business of establishing a durable political and commercial relationship has been far less successful. The fleet's withdrawal is not the triumphant treaty a reader might have expected from the poem's opening promises. Yet Camões will not end with frustration in a foreign port. He turns to the power that has aided the Portuguese all along. Venus prepares an extraordinary reward.

On the homeward voyage, the sailors find an island that seems made for delight. The setting is lush, carefully arranged, rich in living water, shade, flowers, and the beauty of an idealized world. Nymphs are there, and the weary sailors are welcomed into a celebration of pleasure. The island is neither a place marked on a trustworthy chart nor an ordinary stop mentioned in a captain's log. It is a poetic gift. For men who have lost comrades, survived storms, and gone hungry, the pleasures of food, rest, and love arrive with the force of paradise. The martial tale becomes an erotic one. The poem makes delight part of its definition of what heroism earns.

This change of register can surprise a listener. A Christian national epic has brought us onto an island orchestrated by Venus, a goddess from the classical pantheon. The nymphs reward sailors whose voyages the poet connects with Portugal's Christian kings. Camões knows he is combining systems of belief. His Roman gods are literary agents, functioning within an imagined machinery that praises a Christian monarchy and its ventures. The mixture is not an oversight. It makes the inherited apparatus of ancient epic available for a new history, and it allows the voyage to be celebrated in a language of desire that ordinary political chronicle could not supply.

The celebration continues into another kind of revelation. At the island's feast a prophetic singer describes Portuguese achievements that will follow the first voyage. The future governors and commanders of the Asian empire enter the poem before their time, as if the sea route already contains the conquests that later generations will make. For Da Gama the future is hidden. For the poet writing in 1572, much of it is history. The prophecy is a way of giving historical retrospect the thrill of foreknowledge. It is also one of the places where the poem's confidence in empire is most explicit: the people whose ships entered India with difficulty are promised a grand, expansionist future.

Then the sea-nymph Thetis leads Da Gama to a height and shows him a representation of the world. The Earth, the heavens, the spheres, the far shores and seas appear as a single order that can be contemplated. The captain who struggled with an uncertain coast now sees the planet as a whole. Geographic knowledge becomes an initiation. Adamastor, at the southern cape, once made the world seem hostile and unknowable. The vision on the island seems to reverse that encounter. The great distance across sea and earth can be understood, named, and imagined as part of a whole. But this is a cosmology of the poem's time, not modern astronomy. Its beauty lies partly in the way it stages a desire to comprehend everything while revealing how historically situated every such map must be.

The voyage ends with the fleet returning to Portugal. The men who left a shore filled with grieving relatives come back with news. They have reached India and crossed back across an ocean that could have killed them. Their return completes the story's outer arc, but the poem's final movement is directed not to those men alone. Camões turns toward his own country's rulers and the future of its honors. He has sung of deeds he thinks deserve remembrance; he has also complained that poets and worth are neglected, and that greed and other failures threaten the moral foundation of glory. The person who tells a national epic is asking whether the nation can live up to the image it has just heard.

So what has been won? The travelers return alive, a route has been established for Portuguese ships, and the epic imagines an empire coming into view. But we should not flatten the story into a single cheerful victory. The Samorin has not become a grateful subordinate; the hostile trading negotiations have exposed the limits of Da Gama's power. The sea has swallowed lives. The old man of Restelo challenged the entire project before the ships departed. Adamastor warned that more suffering lies ahead. Venus's island solves none of those real costs, though it gives them a radiant poetic answer. The work is capable of celebration and self-reproach because it is written not from the first voyage's uncertainty alone, but from a later century that already knows the ambiguities of success.

At the beginning, Jupiter and Bacchus argued about whether the small fleet deserved to enter history. At the end, the world has been opened before Da Gama's eyes and the name of Portugal has been carried across it. Yet one question remains beyond the divine machinery: what, precisely, should this success mean for the people who were already living along the route? The poem supplies the claims of its own time and sometimes the anxieties that unsettle them. It does not supply the full lives of Africans, Indians, and others described by the Portuguese voyagers. That incompleteness is part of reading the epic responsibly. We can follow the story's magnificent imaginative arc without confusing its vision of the world with the world itself.

**Primary witness:** Camões, *Os Lusíadas*, cantos VI–X. [Portuguese public-domain edition](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/3333). English plot and stanza checkpoints: Richard Francis Burton, *The Lusiads*, 1880, [volume I, canto VI](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/77660/77660-h/77660-h.htm) and [volume II, cantos VII–X](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/77661/77661-h/77661-h.htm). Original English narrative, no borrowed translation passages.

**Canto locators:** Bacchus, Neptune's council, storm, Twelve of England, Venus and Calicut sighting (VI); arrival at Calicut, Monçaide and the Samorin (VII); painted Portuguese heroes, Paulo da Gama, political intrigue and detention (VIII); departure and the Island of Love (IX); feast, prophecy of future commanders, Thetis's cosmological vision, return to Portugal and final address (X).

**Important qualifications:** 1497–1499 is the historical date of Da Gama's first voyage. The poem was published in 1572 and portrays trade and local communities from within a sixteenth-century Portuguese imperial framework. The Island of Love, divine debates and prophetic vision are inventions of epic poetry. For comparative reading see [Columbia World Epics](https://edblogs.columbia.edu/worldepics/project/os-lusiadas/), [Luís de Camões in Global Literary Context](https://cowlatinamerica.voices.wooster.edu/archive-item/luis-vaz-de-camoes-the-lusiads/), and [Wikipedia: Os Lusíadas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Os_Lus%C3%ADadas).
