# The Huarochirí Manuscript — Story II: The Dancers, the Dead, and the Llama in the Sky

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

At the mouth of an irrigation channel there is a stone. It is not merely a marker placed there after the work was done. The people tell that the stone is Chuquisuso, the woman whose fields were drying until she persuaded the powerful Pariacaca to make water flow to them. When the Cupara community cleans the canal, its labor has a story behind it. The channel is practical, but it is also remembered. A stone can be an ancestor, a reservoir can have a personality, and a mountain can be a power whom people visit to ask whether they will be able to plant.

The Huarochirí Manuscript, recorded in Quechua in the Andes around the year 1600, is full of such accounts. Its early tales introduce Cuniraya, who pursued Cahuillaca to the sea, and Pariacaca, whose violent arrival displaced the devouring huaca Huallallo. But the manuscript does not close when one power defeats another. It turns to the people who live with these powers. Different communities make claims about whose ancestors came first, who must conduct ceremonies, how water is distributed, and why the land contains the stones it does. There is no single hero to carry us through all of it. The connecting character is the country itself.

Begin with Chaupi Ñamca, a female huaca whose stories reach across the region around Mama. In one account she is the daughter of the wealthy Tamtañamca and the wife of Huatiacuri, the poor man who healed her father. In another, she is spoken of as Pariacaca's sister. The manuscript does not quietly resolve that conflict for us. The telling changes as people change places and families. Like Pariacaca, Chaupi Ñamca is associated with fivefold power. She has sisters whose names are remembered in varying forms; a five-armed stone belongs to her sacred presence. People address her as mother. Some describe her as a woman who has known many huacas as lovers before choosing Rucanacoto, a male huaca whose sexual power pleases her. In that account she settles into a stone near Mama. The stories place sexuality and fertility near the center of religious life, without making Chaupi Ñamca merely someone's consort.

Her celebrations are social events. People prepare ornaments, bring animals, and dance. Certain dances use the skins of pumas; others mark forms of prosperity or desire. One is associated in the manuscript with near-naked performance, and with Chaupi Ñamca's pleasure in seeing it. The account also reports observances around the time of Corpus Christi, the Christian festival that had become a major marker of the colonial calendar. We are hearing not a reconstructed, pristine pre-Inca ceremony but a written description of changing Andean practice, remembered and observed under Spanish rule. The dancers do not vanish from the story because the recorder disapproves of their gods.

Pariacaca's children, meanwhile, travel across the land. One, Tutay Quiri, becomes central to the Checa people. The manuscript says the Checa were treated with contempt by their older relatives, the Quinti. Tutay Quiri takes the slight seriously. He tells them that the position of the groups will change, and he leads campaigns into valleys already inhabited by people whom the text calls Yunca. The stories record victories, movement, and new arrangements of kinship. Some communities flee. Others are allowed to stay on condition that they accept a different place within the hierarchy and honor Pariacaca. The language of siblinghood makes political dependence sound like a family relation. A conqueror can call a people his brothers and still decide which brother must take the lower seat.

These movements are remembered not only in the names of groups but in their festivals. Following the route of Tutay Quiri, participants travel, camp, hunt, pray for rain, and return in procession. Those who remained at home greet them with drink. The dances connect places in sequence. Each stop has an obligation. A hill where the participants waited, a plaza with stones, a village preparing a welcome—these are not scenic details added for decoration. They are how a community knows where it belongs and why its ceremonial year follows a particular path.

There are stories of other communities too, some almost cruelly brief. Pariacaca comes to a gathering among the Colli and sits apart in poor clothing. Nearly everyone ignores him. One man gives him drink, then more drink, and coca when asked. Pariacaca warns the generous man to seize a tree when the danger comes. Five days later a great wind lifts the settlement away. The one who gave freely survives by holding fast. Pariacaca turns him into a stone, Capac Huanca, and commands that offerings continue to be made to him. The manuscript then says something that changes the air around the story. When Father Francisco de Ávila came, he and others broke and cast down that stone. A destruction that was once ascribed to an angry huaca has acquired another actor: a Christian cleric in the colonial present.

The past also contains rival accounts of where communities originated. One narrative says ancestors came from beneath the earth. Another speaks of a tree whose fruit became people. A further one remembers blood falling from the sky. These are not modern historians' competing hypotheses. They are traditions that locate the rights and relations of particular groups. A visitor to an unfamiliar valley might see farmland, a canal, a lakeside village. A local narrator can tell a far more densely populated story: here one lineage arrived, there another settled, here somebody was forced to flee, and at that stone a victory or bargain remains visible. Even when we cannot reconstruct a migration from such a tale, we can hear what the narration is doing for the community that keeps it.

Then the scale opens outward to the Inca court. The manuscript tells of an Inca ruler, Túpac Yupanqui, troubled by rebellions. His armies have not subdued the opponents. He has given treasure, food, and honors to huacas throughout his domains. Now he summons them to the great plaza in Cuzco and demands assistance. Powerful beings arrive in their litters. Pachacamac is among them. Pariacaca delays and ultimately sends a son, Macahuisa, to listen. The ruler speaks of the wealth he has bestowed and the people he has lost. Why should beings who receive such generous offerings leave their patron helpless?

The huacas are silent. Pachacamac says that if he unleashes his power, the earth itself may tremble and everyone, not just the rebels, could be destroyed. Macahuisa, however, volunteers. He is borne toward the conflict and becomes terrible rain. Lightning strikes; torrents sweep over the opposing forces. The rebel leaders die, though some ordinary people survive. The victory belongs in the manuscript to a local sacred power who acts on an imperial stage. The Inca offers rewards. Macahuisa does not care for ordinary food, and when special offerings are brought, the scene has the texture of one authority discovering that it cannot fully command another. He asks for the ruler's reverence in the form practiced by Pariacaca's people. The emperor can call the huacas to court, but the tale insists that a mountain's son has power the emperor himself needs.

It is tempting to treat this as a chronicle of a particular military campaign. The manuscript does not give us that kind of record. It gives us a story by which Huarochirí might remember a place in an Inca world: not as a nameless conquered province, but as the home of forces the emperor was obliged to respect. The story also admits a more difficult truth. Sacred power can grant rain and obliterate people with the same storm. The communities defeated by Tutay Quiri or Macahuisa have their own absence inside the victors' account.

The Spanish arrival appears not as the clean ending of all those relationships but as an intrusion into them. The manuscript speaks from a time when crosses, church calendars, forced resettlement, destroyed huacas, and disputes over religious observance have already altered the landscape. Sometimes the narrator calls a traditional being a demon, or compares a story with a Christian event; sometimes the text preserves a ceremony or a place-name with astonishing local specificity. There is no reason to imagine every line was dictated freely in an untouched mountain village. Yet it would be equally false to say the text contains nothing but the conquerors' opinion. The voices and names of Huarochirí survive within a record made under pressure.

Now consider a small story that reaches much further back. In a former age, when someone died, the family watched and waited for five days. The dead person's spirit, tiny as a fly, departed and could return. Relatives prepared food and drink, and the returning person was welcomed into the house. Then one man was late. He came back on the sixth day. His wife was furious that he had kept everyone waiting, and she threw a maize cob at him. He turned away. Since then, the account says, the dead no longer come home in that ordinary way. The whole condition of human mortality changes because of a moment of irritation in a household. Like the fox's tumble that settled the route of a canal, a small action has consequences for everyone.

People still remember and feed the dead. In one account they carry offerings, wait for signs, bring home a small stone, make a place for the spirit at the feast, and release it afterward. The colonial narrator notices food being left in church on All Saints' Day and interprets the practice in relation to older observances. It is a scene of continuity and change, not proof that two religions became identical. The mourners cook the food and make the journey. The deceased is not simply an idea; the family acts as though a relationship demands attention after the body has been buried.

Then the story lifts our eyes from earth to night sky. Across the dark spaces of the Milky Way moves Yacana, the great celestial llama. She is seen not as a line drawn between bright stars but as a dark form against a river of light, with a neck, eyes, and a young one near her. Yacana gives life and increase to earthly llamas. The account says a fortunate herder might encounter her as she descends to a spring; her wool could bring extraordinary growth to a herd. At midnight she drinks the ocean's water. If she stopped, the sea would flood the world. Remember the llama who warned of the first deluge? Here another llama prevents the next one, night after night. The stories are not presented as successive scenes of one plot, but each allows an animal to stand at the edge between a household's survival and a world-ending flood.

Other stars and dark shapes are named after birds and animals. Their appearance can forecast prosperity or hardship. A community may stay awake to observe them. Up in the sky there are relations among animals; below, in the fields, the same kinds of relations govern breeding, water, and seasonal hope. The celestial llama is not an ornamental metaphor pinned onto a landscape that would be otherwise inert. The text asks us to imagine a cosmos whose many parts actively sustain one another.

Near the end, we hear about springs and lakes again. At a spring called Porui, the huaca Anchicara directs water toward fields. A woman, Huayllama, arrives with claims of her own. Their disagreement over the flow of water becomes part of a relationship, and both become stones; the children associated with them remain in nearby water. The story remembers a ritual of canal cleaning. After wars, festivals, imperial assemblies, funerals, and visions of the night sky, the manuscript returns to an irrigation channel. That is not a failure to devise a grand conclusion. It is a return to what is at stake throughout: whose water will reach the maize, whose labor sustains the channel, whose names must be remembered when the work begins.

The Huarochirí Manuscript does not leave us a hero riding away from a finished adventure. It leaves a populated world. The stones at the edge of a field, the path of a pilgrimage, a cloud that gathers over a hill, a dead person's meal, and a dark shape against the stars each have a story. And the written record brings another layer: the people describing these places are living in a colonial world where their stories may be collected for use against them. The remarkable fact is that the narratives are still there, disagreeing with one another, naming the country with precision, and refusing to become just one story.

## Text and evidence companion (not spoken)

**Scope:** Selected connected communities-and-cosmos accounts from the *Huarochirí Manuscript* (not a linear sequel): Chaupi Ñamca and her rituals (chs. 10, 13 and related passages); Tutay Quiri, the Checa, and other local genealogies (chs. 11–12, 24); pilgrimage and cult observances (ch. 9 and later); the Colli, Capac Huanca, and Ávila's destruction of the stone (ch. 25); the Inca's call to the huacas and Macahuisa (ch. 23); death and feeding the dead (chs. 27–28); Yacana (ch. 29); spring and lake beings (chs. 30–31). This is a **selected survey** of the surviving 31 chapters and two supplements, not exhaustive event-for-event coverage. The manuscript changes narrators, locales and traditions.

**Edition and research boundary:** The episode references follow [Salomon and Urioste, *The Huarochirí Manuscript*](https://utpress.utexas.edu/9780292730533/) and [the JSTOR contents and episode descriptions](https://www.jstor.org/stable/10.7560/730526), checked against a public [Spanish text witness with modern transliteration](https://kuprienko.info/francisco-de-avila-dioses-y-hombres-de-huarochiri-fragmentos/). The latter is neither the early modern Quechua original nor a substitute for a scholarly collation; this original English prose script does not reproduce translated sentences. [Biblioteca Nacional del Perú, *Ecos de Huarochirí*](https://repositoriodigital.bnp.gob.pe/bnp/recursos/2/html/ecos-de-huarochiri/) provides modern literary reception and translation discussion.

**Ethical and historical caution:** The manuscript reports displacement and conquest from particular community positions; “Yunca” is a historically situated category in the text, not a timeless homogeneous ethnicity. The narrative claim that Macahuisa overcame armies with a storm is not independently established military history. Accounts of stones broken by Ávila must not be generalized into a claim that all Andean sacred practices disappeared or survived unchanged. Authors and informants have contested attributions, addressed in the analysis lecture. The name Chaupi Ñamca and her kinships differ in manuscript traditions. For orientation see [Wikipedia: Huarochirí Manuscript](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Huarochir%C3%AD_Manuscript).
