# One Thousand and One Nights, Part II: The Fisherman and the Sealed Jar

The *Fisherman and the Jinni* and its nested tales of the sage Duban, the enchanted prince, and the Black Islands, in the named Burton Volume I witness.

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

A fisherman goes out to the shore with a net and a rule. He will cast it only four times in a day, no matter how poor the catch. On the first cast he drags up something useless and rotten. On the second he finds more debris. The third brings only broken material. His children are waiting for food, and each empty net makes the rule seem foolish. On the fourth cast, however, there is a great weight below the surface. He hauls it up, expecting an enormous fish. It is a brass vessel, sealed with a mark he recognizes as connected with Solomon. The vessel is heavy and strange, but perhaps it contains something valuable. He breaks the seal and turns it over.

Smoke pours out. It rises until the fisherman sees the gigantic shape of a jinni towering above him. The creature has spent ages imprisoned in the jar. The fisherman expects gratitude for freeing him. Instead the jinni announces that he has resolved to kill whoever opens the vessel. At first, during his long confinement, he promised immense wealth to a rescuer. Later he imagined granting kingdoms. Still later he resolved to offer whatever a liberator could wish. As the centuries passed without release, his promises curdled into rage. At last he swore that whoever freed him would die, allowed only to choose the manner of death. The prisoner has mistaken his own suffering for a right to punish the first person he meets.

The fisherman argues that he has done the jinni a kindness. The jinni is unmoved. So the fisherman changes tactics. He pretends not to believe the creature could possibly have fitted in such a small vessel. The jinni, offended by doubt, turns into smoke and slips inside to demonstrate his power. At once the fisherman claps the seal back into place. The enormous being is trapped by the desire to prove his enormity. The scene is funny, but the reversal is exact: a man threatened with arbitrary death can survive by changing the terms of a conversation. He warns that he may throw the jar back into the sea where no one will ever find it. The jinni pleads for mercy and promises not to hurt him.

The fisherman does not accept the first promise. He tells a cautionary story of a king who killed the man responsible for saving him. This is the tale of the ruler Yunan and the physician Duban. Yunan had been afflicted with a disease no other doctor could cure. Duban devised a treatment connected with the king's exercise and restored his health without the familiar ordeal of medicine. The king rewarded the doctor. A jealous vizier whispered that a man who could cure by such subtle means could also kill by subtle means. The king allowed suspicion to outweigh what he knew of the doctor's service and condemned him to death. When Duban saw he could not turn the ruler from this unjust decision, he arranged a final book, whose pages the king would turn with moistened fingers. The poison passed to the ruler; the doctor died, and the king died from the book. That is the ending of the tale within the tale: fear makes a ruler murder his benefactor and then destroys the ruler himself.

Now the fisherman uses the story as an argument. Why should he release a being who has openly promised violence? The jinni insists that he will honor a new pledge. After negotiation, the fisherman releases him. The creature leads him to an extraordinary pool surrounded by hills and tells him to cast his net there. Four fish appear in four distinct colors. The jinni instructs him to present them to the king. Then he disappears. The fisherman's life, which had appeared to turn on a sealed jar, opens into another narrative. The gift is not yet wealth or safety; it is a way toward a mystery whose explanation will require more stories.

At the royal palace, the fish are taken to be cooked. A figure appears and addresses them. The fish reply and are destroyed in a bewildering event that the cooks cannot explain. The marvel repeats under different witnesses until the king decides he must discover where these impossible fish came from. The fisherman leads him to the pool. When the king examines the place, he notices that the surrounding country holds a secret. His search takes him to a dark, deserted palace. Inside he hears a young man lamenting as though every day were a fresh punishment. The stranger is a prince, but only the upper half of his body remains human. From the waist down he has been transformed into black stone.

The prince explains that his wife was a powerful sorceress and that she had a lover whose injured body she kept alive. When the prince discovered the affair and tried to intervene, she struck him with a spell. She transformed his body and devastated his kingdom. His capital became the lake; its people were changed into the four colors of fish according to the different religious communities the tale names. The sorceress has returned each day to beat the helpless prince before visiting the injured lover. The enchantment is not simply a spectacular piece of magic. It makes the imbalance of power visible in a body that cannot move and a city whose inhabitants can no longer speak as people.

The king decides to help. He goes to the place where the sorceress keeps her lover, kills the man, and takes his place. When she comes, he speaks in a disguised voice and persuades her to reverse the prince's partial transformation, claiming that the prince's suffering has disturbed the lover's rest. She restores the young man to his complete human form. The king then persuades her to undo the enchantment on the city and its people. She goes to the lake, works her spell, and the lost streets, buildings, and people return. Finally, when she comes back seeking the lover's favor, the king kills her. The power that held a community in the shape of fish is broken.

The recovered prince and the rescuing king speak of the distance between their cities and of what the prince will do with his renewed freedom. They travel to the royal court, where the events are received with wonder. The fisherman, whose fourth cast began the whole sequence, is rewarded lavishly. In the Burton version, the ending includes marriages linking the fisherman's daughters to the king and prince and gives his son a high post. The fisherman does not merely escape the jinni. His chance act leads to the return of an entire city from enchantment. Yet the ending belongs to the received tale, with its own conventions of wealth, marriage, and royal favor. It is not a historical report about a particular lost city.

The nested design deserves attention. The fisherman is threatened by a jinni, and counters with a story about a healer. The jinni bargains, and the fisherman is led to fish whose wonder summons a king. The king discovers a prince whose account explains the fish. Each tale changes how the listener understands the preceding one. The physician story shows the danger of repaying benefit with suspicion. The jar scene shows that cunning can defeat an apparently absolute power. The enchanted kingdom shows how private abuse can be expanded into public catastrophe. None of these themes needs to be reduced to a single tidy moral. The pleasure lies in the way a story breaks open and reveals another story inside it.

And another frame remains around all of them. Shahrazad is telling the fisherman story to a king who also believes his private injury authorizes the deaths of other people. As she tells of the jinni, the jealous ruler, and the prince's captivity, she asks Shahryar to listen to men whose power exceeds their judgment. If he understands any of their errors, he might recognize his own. But the book does not ask us to assume that he has already changed simply because he wants another installment. A story can make a night survivable without immediately making a court just. Shahrazad is building, night by night, a habit that may eventually outlast the danger of a single morning.

*One Thousand and One Nights* is so large and changeable that selecting two early narrative cycles does not amount to telling the whole work. Later installments should follow further identified stories and their distinct manuscript or printed histories, rather than promising an impossible single original edition. For now the fisherman returns from shore with more than his family could have imagined, and Shahrazad has won another dawn. Her reward is as precarious as the fisherman's was when he first opened the jar: the dangerous listener remains nearby, and the telling must continue.
