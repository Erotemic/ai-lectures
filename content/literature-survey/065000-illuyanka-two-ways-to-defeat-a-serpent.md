# Illuyanka: Two Ways to Defeat a Serpent

A separate telling of both surviving versions of the Anatolian Storm-god's combat with Illuyanka, CTH 321, preserving their divergent mortal consequences and damaged passages.

A storm god has been defeated by a serpent. That is the premise, and in the first telling there is no explanation of how a god who commands a sky of violent power could lose to a creature of the earth. The serpent, named Illuyanka, has already beaten him. If the god wishes to prevail, he must find a form of help that his own force could not supply. The answer will come from a goddess, a mortal man, and an enormous feast. In another telling of the same conflict, the answer will come from a marriage and the sacrifice of a son. The Hittite texts preserve both, and they should remain two stories rather than two halves of one repaired adventure.

The myth belongs to the religious and literary world of ancient Anatolia. It survives in Hittite-language witnesses grouped as CTH 321 and is connected in the text with the *purulli* festival, celebrated in a setting of prosperity and protection. The recorded story is attributed to a priest named Killa of the Storm-god of Nerik, although the weather deity fighting the serpent is specified in the text as the Weather-god of Heaven. Ritual context matters here, but it does not establish that we know every detail of how each scene was performed. The tablet gives words and sequence; it does not hand us a complete stage recording from a spring festival.

In the first account, the Weather-god and Illuyanka battle at Kiskilussa. Illuyanka wins. The Weather-god calls the gods together, and Inara responds. She is an Anatolian goddess associated with the plan that will change the outcome, not a merely decorative assistant. She prepares a great feast, filling vessels with wine and other drinks. The scene reverses what one might expect from a monster-fighting story. The defeated power will not win by being angrier or striking faster. Someone else must arrange circumstances in which the serpent can be held.

Inara goes to a place called Ziggaratta and finds a mortal named Hupasiya. She asks him to join her work. He makes their cooperation conditional upon intimate union with her; the goddess consents. The text's sexual bargain is direct and deserves not to be softened into a harmless promise of a kiss. Nor does the ancient text describe a love story beginning here. Hupasiya's role is instrumental to the plan, and the story will leave him under conditions shaped by the goddess's wishes. Their exchange places a human life inside a problem between divine powers.

Inara goes to Illuyanka's lair and invites the serpent and its offspring to the banquet. They emerge and drink until their bodies are too full to return to safety. Now Hupasiya acts. He binds the serpent so it cannot escape. The Weather-god comes with the other gods and kills Illuyanka. In a single sequence the plan has worked: nourishment becomes a trap; the human helper supplies the binding; the victorious deity strikes only after another's work has made the creature helpless. The narrative does not conceal how dependent the god has become upon a human and upon a woman's scheme.

One might expect Hupasiya to receive a simple reward and disappear. Instead Inara builds a house for him on a rock and settles him there. She tells him not to look out of its window while she is absent, for he will see his wife and children. The prohibition gives him a view of his old life precisely by forbidding it. Time passes. After twenty days he looks out, sees his family, and asks Inara to allow him to go home. Here the manuscript becomes damaged. Later summaries sometimes finish his story with a definite punishment, but the surviving lines do not justify a fully staged scene of his death. We are left with a man who helped a god win and now wants the human household he left behind.

The first version already complicates the notion of a heroic victory. The Weather-god's power is restored through cunning and collaboration. Hupasiya's service does not free him from the goddess's control. Inara may have made the victory possible, yet she has also placed the mortal under an order that separates him from his family. The broken end of his episode is not merely a nuisance to a modern storyteller. It is a reminder that we do not know how the text resolved the tension between divine gratitude and human freedom.

The second account begins again with defeat, but it does not reuse the feast. Illuyanka has taken the Weather-god's heart and eyes. These are not ordinary war trophies. Without them the god lacks the parts needed to act and perceive as before. He cannot simply gather the other gods and overwhelm the serpent. He must recover what belongs inside his own body. The solution is astonishingly domestic: the Weather-god marries the daughter of a poor human man, and they have a son. This son grows up and marries the daughter of Illuyanka. The victory will depend upon the passage of a bridegroom between hostile households.

When the son visits his wife's family, he follows his father's instruction and asks for the heart and eyes. The serpent's household gives them to him. He carries them back, and the Weather-god is made whole. An object that could not be recovered in battle has been recovered through kinship and a request made at the appropriate door. Here the serpent's daughter is essential to the plot, although the surviving account does not invite us to invent a secret conversation in which she betrays her father. The son's marriage creates access, and the requested organs are given.

The Weather-god then goes to fight Illuyanka again. This time his power is restored, and the serpent faces defeat. But the son is standing with the serpent. He calls to his father not to spare him. The god kills Illuyanka and his own son. The second version's ending is not a joyous celebration of a harmless victory. The father has used his child to recover the means of life and then destroyed that child along with his enemy. The text is terse. It does not provide an extended explanation of why the son refuses exemption. His words and the resulting deaths make the dilemma visible without solving it for us.

These are distinct versions, not competing modern fan reconstructions. Both survive within the CTH 321 tradition. In the first, feasting and binding undo a serpent's victory; in the second, marriage and return of body parts do the work. In the first, the mortal helper wants to return to wife and children; in the second, a son whose marriage served the plan dies in battle. Both stories show that the storm's victory depends on relationships outside the god's direct command. And both end by placing the cost of that victory on a human life.

Hittite scholars compare the story with other combats between a weather god and a serpent or chaos creature. Such comparisons can clarify a shared narrative problem—how ordered life survives when its defender is defeated—but the differences matter more than a single label like dragon-slayer. Illuyanka does not stand for every monster in every myth. The *purulli* setting also makes the story more than an isolated entertainment: words about land flourishing and being protected surround a tale in which prosperity depends upon overcoming an earlier loss. We should not confuse that ritual connection with proof that one exact sequence was enacted the same way in every Anatolian city.

The final image in one version is a house on a rock, from which a man looks toward a family he is told not to see. In the other it is a battlefield where a father can defeat his enemy only by refusing to protect his son. Either picture would be a strong ending for a tragic narrative. That the texts preserve both suggests that the tradition could explore the same divine conflict through more than one pattern of human obligation. The serpent falls, but the question remains: who bears the cost when a god restores his strength? The surviving versions do not ask us to solve the contradiction. They ask us to hear it twice.

## Source companion (not narrated)

- Primary witnesses: Illuyanka dragon battle, CTH 321, principally KBo 3.7 and related fragments. [TITUS, Hittite translation project, CTH 321](https://titus.uni-frankfurt.de/texte/etcs/anatol/hittite/cthtr/cthtr001.htm); [Petra Goedegebuure, *The Illuyanka Myth: A Hittite Saturnalia?*](https://linguistics.uga.edu/sites/default/files/2024-07/The%20Illuyanka%20Myth%20Hittite%20Saturnalia.pdf), with numbered paragraphs and alternative readings.
- Recension one: CTH 321 paragraphs 1–18 approximately; *purulli*, Inara, Hupasiya, banquet, binding, house and window; the ending for Hupasiya is damaged and should not be invented.
- Recension two: CTH 321, main witness KBo 3.7 column III, §§19–26 approximately; heart and eyes, marriage, son, organs recovered, both son and serpent killed. See the specialist textual discussion in Gary Beckman, *From an Antique Land* (2009) and Harry A. Hoffner, *Hittite Myths* (2nd ed., 1998).
- Orientation: [Wikipedia, Illuyanka](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Illuyanka). Avoid merging the two versions, or substituting later names from Hurrian mythology when the primary Hittite passage does not make that identification.
