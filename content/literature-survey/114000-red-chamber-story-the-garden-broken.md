# Dream of the Red Chamber — Story II: The Garden Broken

A conclusion to the *whole-novel* overview, beginning with pressures visible before chapter 55 and continuing into the ending of the **received 120-chapter print novel**. Major plot events after chapter 80 are explicitly attributed to that later textual stratum, not presented as a proven original ending by Cao Xueqin.

When a household has become very large, money is never just money. It is also the promise that the next festival can be held, that a servant will be paid, that a marriage can be arranged, that guests will not detect an embarrassment, and that a respected elder need never hear the word no. The Jia family has built its happiness upon those promises. Now the promises begin contradicting one another.

The Grand View Garden still appears magnificent. Baoyu still wanders among pavilions and people he loves. Lin Daiyu still has the power to turn a small hurt into a poem, or a joke into a quarrel, because she cannot be certain that her position is safe. Xue Baochai still understands more than she says. But outside their conversations the servants are disputing fees, relatives are seeking favors, and managers are borrowing time. Xifeng, who runs the domestic economy with formidable energy, is unwell and cannot be everywhere. No family can sustain a world simply by possessing one capable administrator.

For a time, part of that responsibility falls to Jia Tanchun, a young woman who has watched adults waste money and treats the crisis as an opportunity to change things. With Baochai and Li Wan assisting, she examines the garden's maintenance, sees where fees are being duplicated, and looks for ways to let people profit from the honest care of what they supervise. We can hear the intelligence in this reform: when someone has responsibility for a patch of land or a grove, that person may have a reason to preserve it. But we also hear the limits. A young unmarried daughter can reorganize household expenses; she cannot rewrite the inherited privileges and expectations of an entire clan. Her work reveals the problem more clearly than it cures it.

Baoyu's attention remains fixed on the personal cost of hierarchy. A servant called Qingwen is particularly vivid: proud, gifted, easily angered, and unwilling to disguise all her feelings as submission. When a valuable cloak is damaged, Qingwen, already ill, has the skill and determination to mend its intricate work. Such scenes make her memorable as a person with talent, not merely as part of the wealth on display around Baoyu. Another maid, Xiren, is disciplined and solicitous and has a quite different strategy for surviving. The household encourages the young master to receive this care as if it were naturally his. But the maids' security depends on the opinions of superiors, and opinions can be turned by rumors.

Not all the disasters begin with an impoverished economy. Some spring from men who are powerful enough not to think their choices matter. Jia Lian, Xifeng's husband, secretly arranges to take the young woman You Erjie as a secondary partner. Within the family's marriage arrangements, the act threatens Xifeng's position and also places You Erjie in danger. Xifeng discovers the scheme and responds with a terrifying mixture of social politeness and revenge. She brings You Erjie under her own control, manipulating household opinion and legal circumstances until the younger woman has almost no safe refuge. You Erjie loses the pregnancy that might have strengthened her position. Overwhelmed by humiliation and fear, she kills herself. We may understand Xifeng's anger at Jia Lian's betrayal and still see that her method of defense destroys someone with much less power.

Another younger woman, You Sanjie, faces a different ruin. She wants to choose whom she will marry, and she has developed hopes around a man called Liu Xianglian. He has heard compromising reports about the world around her and retracts his promise. She dies by suicide rather than endure another judgment upon her honor. The narrative does not ask us to count these deaths as equivalent, nor does it give every woman the same personality. It shows how hard it can be for a woman in this world to survive the stories other people decide to tell about her. Liu Xianglian, confronted by the consequence, withdraws from ordinary life.

At first the tragedies appear to be individual episodes: a younger wife trapped by an older wife's jealousy; a woman judged by gossip; another servant dismissed after an accusation. Then the whole household adopts the logic of suspicion. An intimate object associated with illicit sexuality is found in the garden. Instead of asking whether a society full of servants, kin, and visitors can reasonably keep every private act under surveillance, the elders decide that the garden itself must be searched. Senior servants move through rooms, containers, and personal belongings. The women who had once written verses together now see the private details of their lives treated as evidence.

Tanchun resists, aware of what the investigation means. The family is using its remaining authority against itself. The guilty and the innocent cannot be sorted by the ease with which they endure humiliation. A search justified as an effort to protect the household becomes one more way of breaking the trust that has held it together. The arrangement of the garden, which once allowed companions to come and go between buildings, now allows inspectors to enter every chamber. It becomes difficult to remember that these were the rooms meant to preserve a young girl's freedom from adult demands.

Qingwen is singled out by hostile reports and moral suspicion. Her spirit, which had once made her delightful to Baoyu, now makes her seem insolent to those who control the household. She is expelled while sick and dies in poor conditions. Baoyu visits too late to restore what he has lost. He responds with a lament that treats her not as disposable household labor but as a noble presence worthy of literary commemoration. Poetry could not save Jinchuan from the well; it cannot save Qingwen either. Yet Baoyu refuses to let the official verdict be the only surviving record of who she was.

The garden had seemed to possess its own seasons. After the search, autumn arrives as something more than weather. Daiyu, Baoyu, and their friends still live among flowers and buildings, but the invitation to believe in permanence has weakened. The old associations of flowers and fallen petals are no longer only private emotions. Everyone can see that the girls will be married away, sent into convents, dismissed from service, or exposed to a family scandal they did not create. Yingchun, gentle and ill-equipped to resist her elders, is married to a brutal man. Xiangling, once abducted as a child and later absorbed into the Xue household, endures additional suffering in a marriage environment where she has little authority. Even a household with great riches cannot provide safety for women whose legal position permits others to dispose of their lives.

Here the text reaches a boundary that matters. The earliest surviving forms associated with Cao Xueqin's work circulate through a first eighty chapters, with differing manuscripts and commentaries. The conclusion we are about to follow belongs to the received 120-chapter printed version that appeared in 1791 under Cheng Weiyuan and Gao E. It has become the story many readers know, but its exact relationship to Cao's unwritten or lost ending is disputed. The characters have a recognizable destiny within this received conclusion. We should not pretend that every detail is evidence of what the original author had completed.

In that conclusion, illness and misfortune accumulate. Yuanchun, once the household's hope at court, dies. Her imperial favor cannot be preserved like a family heirloom. Baochai's brother has long been a liability, and court connections no longer guarantee protection from consequences. Baoyu becomes increasingly unstable. His miraculous jade is lost, and without it his mind and body seem to fail. For a family that has spent years treating the stone as the source of its good fortune, its disappearance is an emergency. His relatives search for ways to restore him, but the remedy they choose will injure the person he loves most.

The elders believe Baoyu must marry. The acceptable bride is Baochai, whose family position and reputation make her a suitable match. Yet Baoyu's attachment to Daiyu has become difficult to ignore, and he is in no condition to acquiesce in a marriage he cannot imagine. The family devises a deception: Baoyu is allowed to believe that Daiyu will be the bride. When the wedding takes place, Baochai will be concealed beneath the bridal veil. This is not a romantic trick whose cleverness we should applaud. It is a demonstration that, within this household, the adults can use the rituals of marriage to govern the most intimate life of a child who has never learned to govern himself.

Daiyu learns what is happening. Already ill, she retreats into the company of her maid and the remnants of her private writings. She burns the poems and papers into which she has poured so much of herself. Outside her room, the house is making preparations for celebration. She is dying within hearing of a wedding. The novel has long taught us to hear sorrow in music and flowers; now the two soundtracks of this household, ceremony and grief, become impossible to separate. Daiyu's long repayment of tears is nearing its end.

Baoyu lifts the veil and sees Baochai. The ceremony has joined him to a woman who has not invented the rules by which she was chosen. It has severed him from the woman he wanted to marry. He discovers that his relatives have deceived him, and news comes that Daiyu has died. Baochai's situation is no victory. She must now inhabit a marriage founded on a false promise and a grief that cannot be treated as an insult to her character. In the account that has circulated to generations of readers, the love triangle is tragic because no one in it can win: Daiyu dies, Baoyu loses the person he loves, and Baochai becomes his wife under conditions that almost exclude the possibility of mutual happiness.

The wider family soon faces a judgment it cannot conceal behind a curtain. Authorities arrive to investigate and confiscate property associated with its offenses. Titles and offices are vulnerable; the riches that supported so many ceremonies can be inventoried and removed. The garden, which had cost an enormous sum to build, offers no protection. Some possessions are eventually returned and parts of the family's status are restored, but that limited recovery is not the same thing as returning to the old world. Money has escaped through too many openings. People have died. Servants have scattered. The most intimate places have become haunted by memories of people who will not come back.

Grandmother Jia, once able to make the world bend around her preferred grandson, grows old and dies. Her trusted maid Yuanyang, who had resisted being taken as a concubine by an older master, does not find a secure future after the grandmother is gone; she dies by suicide. Wang Xifeng's ingenuity cannot overcome sickness, debt, loss of authority, and the consequences of her past choices. She too dies. It would be tempting to call these deaths merely symbols of a clan's fall, but the book has spent too long giving these women habits and voices for that. Grandmother Jia has loved and been partial. Yuanyang has defended herself. Xifeng has been admirable, cruel, resourceful, and trapped. Their absence makes the old house a different place.

A remembered kindness now changes the future of a child. Xifeng's daughter, Qiaojie, becomes vulnerable to relatives who would profit by disposing of her through a coerced marriage or sale. Granny Liu, the countrywoman whose visits to the estate once amused the rich, returns in a position to help. In the received ending she assists Qiaojie's escape. The woman whom the Jia household treated as a curiosity possesses knowledge, loyalty, and mobility that the remaining aristocrats cannot command. This is one of the novel's sharpest reversals. Charity offered as patronage returns as protection when the original patron has lost almost everything.

For Baoyu, the remaining path appears to be the one his father desired all along: he must complete his education and sit the examinations. His family hopes that scholarly success can replace lost honors and repair what it can of the clan's position. He studies and eventually takes the examination. He succeeds. The family's conventional hopes are apparently justified at last. But the young man who has accomplished this has seen the people of the garden die, leave, and suffer. The official degree cannot restore them or make the marriage that saved appearances into a happy one.

After the examinations Baoyu disappears. There is no ordinary return to celebrate, no comfortable explanation to offer his wife, no fatherly satisfaction in a son prepared to assume office. He has followed the way out of the world that the monk and the Daoist had long ago opened for the stone. In the received ending, his father later sees a figure standing in the winter snow, barefoot and shaven-headed, wearing a striking red cape. The figure bows, and the monk and Daoist take him away. It is Baoyu, but he is no longer available for the roles the family built around him. The stone's journey through earthly life is done.

The conclusion is not free of discomfort. Baochai remains, with a child expected and a marriage that has turned into absence. Family fortunes are partly repaired, yet the repair comes too late for many who paid the cost. What is the value of enlightenment if the people one leaves behind must bear its consequences? What is the value of success if it arrives after the destruction of those for whom the success was supposed to matter? The book refuses to make every account balance. Even the red cape in the snow can be heard as an image of release or as another disappearance.

Return in memory to Daiyu gathering fallen petals. She did not know the details of the search, the wedding, the confiscations, or the last winter road. She knew that a flower was fragile and that love could not prevent the passing of the season. Her thought was not too small for the great story around her. It was the story in miniature. And the family, which had spent its wealth trying to make a beautiful world last, had failed to learn from the girl who understood from the beginning that beauty is no form of ownership.

## Text and evidence companion (not spoken)

- Narrative basis: [*Honglou meng* composite Chinese text](https://zh.wikisource.org/wiki/%E7%B4%85%E6%A8%93%E5%A4%A2), especially ch. 55–80 and 81–120, the latter treated **as the received 1791 printed continuation**, not as securely Cao Xueqin's writing. For the 1791 Chengjia witness see the [120th chapter](https://zh.wikisource.org/wiki/%E7%B4%85%E6%A8%93%E5%A4%A2%EF%BC%88%E7%A8%8B%E7%94%B2%E6%9C%AC%EF%BC%89/%E4%B8%80%E7%99%BE%E4%BA%8C%E5%8D%81).
- Textual scholarship: Ann Waltner, [Controversy Over the Last 40 Chapters](https://open.lib.umn.edu/redchamber/chapter/controversy-over-the-last-40-chapters/). There is no consensus that all events of the received ending were originally written by Cao, or that they follow his lost outline exactly.
- Scene audit: Tanchun's reforms (ch. 55–56); Qingwen mends a cloak (ch. 52); You Erjie/Sanjie (ch. 64–69); garden search (ch. 73–74); Qingwen dismissed and Baoyu's lament (ch. 77–78); Yingchun's marriage (ch. 79–80); Yuanchun's death and jade lost (ch. 94–95); marriage substitution and Daiyu's death (ch. 96–98); confiscation (ch. 105–107); Grandmother Jia and Yuanyang (ch. 110–111); Xifeng's death and Qiaojie's escape (ch. 113–119); examinations and Baoyu's disappearance (ch. 119–120).
- A few complex outcomes are summarized rather than exhaustive: the novel's legal allegations, family branches and court rewards vary in treatment; separate deep dives should check individual episodes rather than project an invented unified downfall.
- [Wikipedia: Dream of the Red Chamber](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dream_of_the_Red_Chamber) for orientation; no modern copyrighted English translation is quoted.
