# La Galigo — Story I: When the Middle World Was Inhabited

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

Imagine a country that has not yet become a country. There is a shore, there are seas, there is a world between worlds, but no courtly history to tell of it. High above lies the realm of the gods. Beneath lies another realm, also inhabited by powerful beings. Between them is the Middle World, the place where a chain of marriages, births, journeys, ceremonies and quarrels will produce the ancestors of kingdoms. The beginning of the Bugis epic called La Galigo is concerned with an astonishing question: what has to happen before there can be a human society with a history?

We are on Sulawesi, in the Indonesian archipelago, listening at a considerable distance from the language in which these stories were made. La Galigo, also called Sureq Galigo, is an immense cycle of Bugis poetry. It is not one book that survives complete from the past. Its episodes have been sung, written, revised, circulated and kept in different manuscripts. Our narrative follows the opening of a particularly substantial witness: twelve volumes now preserved in Leiden, copied under the direction of Colliq Pujie in the nineteenth century. Those volumes are themselves only a large surviving portion of a much larger work. I will tell its connected story without pretending the whole tradition has a single fixed text.

The first movement begins with deliberation above the world. In the divine realm called Boting Langiq, a decision is taken that the Middle World should be populated. Powers from the Upper and Lower Worlds have a part in that decision. What follows is not simply a god waving a hand and making a finished kingdom. It is a process of sending, receiving, providing, negotiating. Batara Guru is sent down from the realm above. The place where he arrives will become Luwuq, a name that matters enormously to the epic and to later Bugis traditions about political beginnings.

For a listener who has never heard these names, let us hold onto just three places. The Upper World is where the divine order can be consulted. The Lower World is a separate source of beings and power. The Middle World is where the consequences become households, palaces, relationships, and eventually descendants. Movement between the worlds is real inside the epic. Their borders can be crossed. And a human kingdom will begin not by forgetting those borders but by joining what comes from opposite sides.

Batara Guru does not settle the Middle World alone. From below comes Wé Nyiliq Timoq. Their marriage brings the realms into a relationship, and it begins a royal genealogy that will occupy the poem for generations. The act is politically charged as well as cosmological: neither the upper nor the lower origin by itself supplies everything a kingdom needs. In the epic's imagination, settlement is an alliance among powers already in existence.

It can be tempting to hurry from this divine meeting to the first famous seafaring hero. But La Galigo does not hurry. It lingers over the difficulty of establishing a family. Batara Guru's household is complicated; the birth of an heir is not automatic. Wé Nyiliq Timoq has not yet had a child. The narrative places requests for descendants and the ceremony surrounding pregnancy within the making of the royal line. When Batara Lattuq is finally born, his arrival is not merely a genealogist's note, a name followed by a name. There are rites for the newborn, celebrations, a ceremony in which a child is placed upon a swing, and another marking his first contact with the ground.

What kind of epic gives this much space to a child's rites? One for which the beginning of a kingdom is also the beginning of procedures by which a community recognizes a person. The cradle, the swing, and the ground are not minor domestic ornaments interrupting real heroic action. They are part of the action. Before Batara Lattuq can become someone's father, a line of people has to receive him properly into the world.

Elsewhere another kingdom begins: Tompoq Tikkaq. Beings descend from above and rise from below there as well. La Urung Mpessi and I Pada Uleng are joined, and two daughters, Wé Adiluwuq and Wé Opu Sengngeng, belong to that family. Their fortunes, however, are not secured simply by exalted ancestry. The parents die; struggle breaks out around the young women; possessions are taken and the daughters are forced away. Even close to the beginning of the world's political order, the poem knows the experience of a household disrupted after the death of its protectors.

Here a new pattern appears. If the first story was about the right people being brought together, the second is about people who belong somewhere being driven out of it. La Galigo's genealogies do not erase bad luck. Descent from gods does not make a family immune to loss, and an heir's place is not always honored. The daughters' disappearance from the center of one court creates the possibility of change in another. The events at Tompoq Tikkaq, which initially seem like a separate beginning, will connect to Luwuq through marriage.

Batara Lattuq grows into the generation that can make that connection. Consultation with the Upper World is again involved in arranging a marriage. Batara Lattuq goes to Tompoq Tikkaq and marries Wé Opu Sengngeng. Her sister, Wé Adiluwuq, is married into the wider kin network. The family lines of two new kingdoms begin to cross. Where the poem's geography looked at first like separate spots on a map, it becomes a map of visiting, marrying and remembering.

A marriage in this world is never only a private scene between two lovers. A journey has to be made, the relatives must be brought into agreement, ceremonies confirm the alliance, and news travels between households. The bride's return to Luwuq with Batara Lattuq becomes part of the history of that realm. One has to imagine the repeated movement of guests and messengers, not inventing details that the surviving summaries do not give, but noticing that the epic repeatedly places the social labor of alliance in the foreground.

Then the expectation of a child returns. Wé Opu Sengngeng becomes pregnant. Her desires during pregnancy are difficult to satisfy, and the poem allows the court's efforts to provide for them to grow into an episode of their own. Birds are sent to fetch the foods she asks for. The image is wonderfully characteristic of this universe. What looks to us like the boundary between a palace and the natural world can become an active network of errands. The need of a pregnant woman changes the movement of beings across the landscape.

The pregnancy brings the birth of twins. The boy is Sawérigading. The girl is Wé Tenriabéng. Their names will become crucial to the larger cycle. They are introduced together, sharing the same family history and bound by a closeness that later gives the story some of its most difficult conflict. The boy will become a traveler and warrior, while the sister will act as a decisive counselor and, in episodes beyond these first volumes, a figure with access to extraordinary powers. For now, they are children whose arrival depends on every earlier marriage, exile and recovery.

Pause for a moment at the shape of the opening. It may be very different from what we expect an epic to be. We have no single desperate siege, no first duel that instantly supplies a hero's fame. The poem has asked who is entitled to found a kingdom, what powers must join for it to last, who has a claim when parents die, what marriage makes possible, and what ceremonies properly mark the arrival of a child. There is conflict, but much of the conflict is over the organization of continuity itself. The world is becoming capable of remembering a line of descendants.

One might even say that the story's abundance is its first argument. A brief myth could tell us, in a paragraph, that gods gave birth to ancestors. La Galigo wants the relationships to take time. If there is a marriage, there may be a journey and negotiations. If a child is born, there may be ceremonies that must be performed. If a family is established, something may go wrong in the next generation. The episode grows because the world that it depicts grows.

Sawérigading soon begins the voyages that make him so widely known. The Leiden volumes include a journey into the Lower World and an attraction to a young woman there who is dead; a proposed marriage cannot simply overcome that condition. They include further family journeys and excursions beyond the court. Already the young man discovers that the world outside Luwuq has its own boundaries, temptations and obligations. We should not join all those episodes into an invented single quest. They are successive adventures in a body of poetry whose scale greatly exceeds this introduction.

The most important pressure still lies ahead. In a later part of the story, Sawérigading will return to Luwuq and fall in love with his own twin sister. This is not presented as a straightforward romantic ideal. It is a crisis about the limits of kinship and what becomes of desire when the person desired is forbidden to marry. Wé Tenriabéng will play a decisive part in sending her brother away to seek another bride. The hero's great sea journeys will grow out of a family problem the founding genealogy has made possible.

That is the remarkable transition in this epic. The first divine generations make the Middle World inhabitable; their descendants make it complicated. A sailor can travel because there are realms to visit and families with whom his family is connected. A hero can become famous because there are obligations he may honor or violate. A courtship can nearly become a war because marriages already carry the weight of political boundaries. The divine decision to populate the earth has become the difficult daily work of living in it.

When we leave this first movement, the world that began empty is crowded with kin. We know the names of more places than when we began. We have watched a couple from opposite worlds establish a home, children marked by public rites, a dispossessed pair of sisters, a marriage joining courts, and the birth of a brother and sister. All of those events are important in their own right. Together they prepare us for the seafaring epic that follows. The hero will step onto his ship carrying an entire network of inherited relationships, whether he understands their costs or not.

## Text and evidence companion (not spoken)

### Textual boundary

Original English synthesis of the opening episodes of the Bugis *Sureq Galigo*. Narrative witness is the Leiden NBG-Boeg 188 manuscripts, volumes 1–3, described in Leiden's digital collection, UNESCO's 2010 nomination, and published Bugis–Indonesian volume 1 and manuscript abstracts. **Not a translation of the Bugis original.** Only part of the immense cycle survives in this Leiden compilation. The work's formation is debated; surviving nineteenth-century manuscripts do not prove a specific medieval original wording. No invented speeches or ritual choreography are asserted as source text.

### Source locators

- NBG-Boeg 188, volume 1: assembly of gods, arrival of Batara Guru from Boting Langiq, Wé Nyiliq Timoq from below, Luwuq, childlessness, birth and rites for Batara Lattuq, Tompoq Tikkaq and the orphaned sisters.
- Volume 2: Batara Lattuq's marriage to Wé Opu Sengngeng, joining Luwuq and Tompoq Tikkaq.
- Volume 3: pregnancy requests, birds fetching delicacies, birth of Sawérigading and Wé Tenriabéng, early excursions into other worlds.
- Later volumes 7–8: love for his twin sister, and her decisive refusal and advice; presented here as a foreshadowing, not as an event in the volume 1 opening.
