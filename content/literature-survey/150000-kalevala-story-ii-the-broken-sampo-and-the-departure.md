# The Kalevala — Story II: The Broken Sampo and the Departure

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

A wedding has taken place in Pohjola, the country of the North. A magnificent feast has ended, and Ilmarinen the smith has taken his bride home. One man is furious about the festivities. Lemminkäinen, the hero who has already traveled to the edge of death and been brought back by his mother, was not invited. He decides that an invitation is not necessary. He will go to the North and demand the hospitality he thinks he deserves. His mother warns him about what happens to guests who turn a slight into a challenge. As before, he rides away.

The mistress of Pohjola is Louhi, and she knows better than most what such a visit might bring. Her husband, the master of the house, becomes Lemminkäinen's adversary. The encounter passes through a series of verbal and magical challenges into violence. Lemminkäinen kills the host. A hall that recently rang with wedding songs becomes a place where the visitor must flee for his life. He escapes to an island, finds temporary pleasures there, and soon makes trouble enough that he cannot stay. By the time he returns to his own country, even his home has been devastated. His mother survives to meet him, but the heroic pattern of departure and return has changed. A man may win a duel and still find nothing secure waiting for him.

He tries another expedition with a companion named Tiera. Northern forces answer with bitter cold, stopping the adventure before it can become a glorious conquest. The comic and painful aspects of Lemminkäinen belong together. He is brave, sometimes resourceful, and sometimes ridiculous. He acts as though a sufficiently forceful desire should carry him through any obstacle, while the world keeps answering with consequences he cannot sing or fight away. The poem lets us enjoy his daring without pretending that daring makes him wise.

Then the epic moves to a darker household. Two brothers, Kalervo and Untamo, are enemies. Their feud destroys Kalervo's home, and a child named Kullervo grows up in the power of the hostile family. Even as a boy he is difficult to control. His captors try to kill him and fail; his anger survives where ordinary children might have been broken. They put him to work. Every task becomes another opportunity for ruin. Asked to care for a child, he causes disaster; sent to labor, he spoils the work. There is something grotesquely comic about the repeated failures, until we recognize what the comedy is protecting us from: an enslaved young man has grown inside a system of violence that has offered him no safe place to belong.

Kullervo is eventually sold into the household of Ilmarinen. The smith's wife sends him out to tend cattle and prepares a loaf of bread with a stone hidden inside it. Kullervo has one treasured possession, a knife inherited from his father. When he tries to cut the bread, the blade breaks against the stone. A deliberate insult has destroyed the object by which he remembers his family. He does not answer with a complaint. He turns the herd into a pack of predators and sends the animals home to attack the woman who mocked him. Ilmarinen's wife is killed. The smith, who helped bring the Sampo into the world and once returned from his northern wedding with such hope, is left without his bride.

Kullervo discovers that members of his birth family are still alive. He returns to them, but home does not repair him. He cannot work successfully there. He is sent to carry a payment or perform an errand, and on the road he meets a young woman. The encounter becomes a sexual violation entangled with gifts and persuasion. Only afterward do they discover that they are brother and sister, separated since childhood. The sister runs to her death. Kullervo returns home with the knowledge that the violence surrounding him has followed him into the relation he might most have wished to protect.

He sets off against Untamo, the enemy he associates with his family's ruin, and destroys the hostile settlement. There is no sense of a clean moral victory. Revenge has come too late to restore his sister, and when he reaches home again his family is gone. He comes to the place where the terrible meeting occurred and addresses his sword. Will it accept the life of its owner? In the poem, the sword answers that it has tasted innocent and guilty blood alike. Kullervo turns the weapon against himself. The world of the Kalevala has many powerful words, but none that can restore a human being to the life he might have had if the first wrong had not been done.

Ilmarinen seeks a replacement for the wife he has lost. A smith who can produce the Sampo tries to forge a woman of gold and silver. The figure is beautiful and cold. It offers neither warmth nor reply. The great skill of the craftsman meets its limit: a likeness is not a companion. Even the old singer Väinämöinen can see that a golden bride cannot solve the problem of human affection. Ilmarinen goes back toward Pohjola and tries to claim another young woman from Louhi's household. His attempt fails in violence and humiliation, and he turns the woman into a seabird. Again the poem reveals the damage caused when someone assumes a bride can be obtained by superior strength or cleverness.

The old wound concerning the Sampo now opens. Ilmarinen remembers what he forged and where it has been kept. Pohjola has prospered, while those in Kalevala speak of the treasure as something that ought to be shared or recovered. Väinämöinen joins with the smith and Lemminkäinen in a voyage north. Three very different kinds of power travel in the same boat: the old singer's knowledge, the smith's making, and the reckless hero's appetite for adventure. Their goal is Louhi's Sampo. They will not win it through another polite bargain.

The voyage itself produces one of the epic's most memorable objects. A great pike is caught, and Väinämöinen makes a kantele, a stringed instrument, from its bones. When he plays, the inhabitants of the natural world gather to listen. Animals pause, birds draw near, and even beings ordinarily untouched by human concerns are moved. The music is not decoration between battles. It demonstrates another way of exercising power: instead of forcing the world to obey, it can gather the world into a shared moment of attention. For a work assembled from singers' poems, that scene is also an image of what the epic itself hopes to do.

In Pohjola, Väinämöinen's music puts the defenders to sleep. The voyagers find the Sampo and wrest it from its protected place. They carry it to their boat and attempt to escape. But an object with the power to produce wealth is not something Louhi will surrender because a visiting singer has performed beautifully. She wakes, discovers the theft, and gives chase. The pursuit becomes a clash of storms and transformations. Louhi takes the form of a great bird, powerful enough to threaten the ship, and the people in the boat fight to keep hold of the mill. The marvelous object breaks.

It is an epic victory that looks nothing like an intact prize being carried home. Pieces of the Sampo fall into the sea; fragments reach the shores of Kalevala and are associated with future abundance. No one recovers the whole machine. The dispute that began when a singer promised a smith's labor in exchange for help and a bride has ended by destroying the thing everyone wanted. It is possible to hear a lesson about sharing wealth, or about the cost of treating another people's prosperity as a possession to seize. But the text does not permit a tidy commercial moral. The Sampo is both a treasure and an unstable concentration of good fortune. Its breaking changes the world.

Väinämöinen loses his first kantele in the struggle. He makes another instrument, this time from birch wood, and plays again. The replacement is not identical with what was lost, yet it carries the song onward. When the most productive machine in the world has been broken, the singer still has the ability to create a new sound. The epic is full of acts of making, and they do not all carry the same consequences. Ilmarinen's manufactured bride cannot love. The Sampo generates prosperity and incites war. Väinämöinen's instrument, made out of bone or wood, gathers listeners who are otherwise divided by fear.

Louhi has not finished opposing him. Diseases descend on Kalevala. A dangerous bear appears. The sun and moon are hidden, and the country sinks into darkness. The old singer and smith respond not simply with combat but with healing formulas, searches, and the recovery of light. In one story celestial fire falls into the water and must be pursued; in another the hidden luminaries must be freed from Pohjola. The world seems to return to the threatening state of the opening, when a great oak prevented light from reaching the earth. Again the work of the culture-hero is to permit ordinary life to continue: the return of day, food, animals, and people going about their lives.

You might expect the epic to end there. The Sampo has been fought over, the old adversaries have tested one another, and light is restored. Instead a new figure enters, Marjatta. She is a young woman who becomes pregnant through a berry. Her family rejects her, and she seeks a place to give birth. The child belongs to a different kind of story from the old singer's battles and voyages. Väinämöinen is asked to judge him and proposes that the infant be destroyed or disposed of. Then the child speaks, rebuking the elder for his judgment and recalling his own failures. The boy is baptized and recognized as a ruler of Karelia. The old singer is no longer the voice that decides the future.

Väinämöinen departs in a boat, leaving the kantele and his songs behind, with a promise or expectation that his knowledge may be needed again. The ending can be heard as an encounter between older cosmological songs and the coming of Christianity; the poem does not simply erase the earlier world. Its singer leaves, but the songs remain. The new child receives honor, but his appearance does not make the tragedies of Aino and Kullervo retrospectively harmless. The Sampo is broken, yet its fragments persist along the shore. Lönnrot's epic ends with succession rather than the perfect settlement of every argument.

Seen together, the two halves of the Kalevala have asked what human beings can make and what making cannot fix. A singer can create peace or trap an opponent in a marsh. A smith can forge abundance or a lifeless imitation of a wife. A mother can bring her son back from death while another family's vengeance leads to a sword turned inward. The world is never simply a competition between humans and monsters. It is also a struggle over who has the right to bargain with another person's life, whose labor creates wealth, and what is left of a community after its bravest people have tried to prove themselves right.

And there is one final reason to hear the last boat's departure differently. The Kalevala was made into the literary epic we know when Lönnrot arranged, adapted, and joined poetry he and others had encountered among living singers, especially in Karelia. We have heard the 1849 book's story, not a direct recording of one ancient narrator. The singers who preserved these verses did not vanish just because a national book acquired a famous title. Väinämöinen's instrument may be left behind within the fiction. Outside it, the work of singing and remembering has many voices, and the story belongs to that history too.

## Text and evidence companion (not spoken)

### Narrative witness and boundary

Original English telling of the second movement of Elias Lönnrot's 1849 *New Kalevala*, runos 26–50. Plot checked against the Finnish Kalevala Society's expert runo synopsis and the named public-domain 1888 Crawford translation. Themes of enslavement, sexual coercion, violence, and suicide are conveyed without embellishing contested actions into consensual romance; they are part of the received book's literary narrative, not independent historical testimony. Distinguishes Lönnrot's epic arrangement from a single premodern oral performance.

### Cycle locators

- Lemminkäinen's exclusion, killing, flight and winter expedition: runos 26–30.
- Kullervo's captivity, stone in bread, retaliation, incest, revenge and death: runos 31–36.
- Ilmarinen's golden bride and disastrous second courtship: runos 37–38.
- Sampo expedition, creation of first kantele, sleeping defenders, theft and breaking: runos 39–43.
- New kantele, Louhi's diseases and darkening of the sky, restoration of light: runos 44–49.
- Marjatta's child, new kingship and Väinämöinen's departure: runo 50.
