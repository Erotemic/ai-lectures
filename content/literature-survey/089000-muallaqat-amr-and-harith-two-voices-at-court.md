# Mu'allaqat IV: Amr ibn Kulthum and al-Harith ibn Hilliza — Two Voices in a Dispute

A paired reading of the last two odes in the conventional seven-poem anthology, often understood against rival tribal claims and courtly arbitration. The poems are kept separate even where their remembered background overlaps.

Imagine two public performances before a powerful ruler. In one, a poet refuses to concede an inch of dignity to anyone who thinks his people can be pushed aside. In the other, a poet lays out a case about agreements, accusations, responsibilities, and the record of previous conflicts. The first voice is associated with Amr ibn Kulthum and the tribe of Taghlib; the second with al-Harith ibn Hilliza and Bakr. Later tradition places the two odes within a rivalry that required judgment at the court of the Lakhmid king Amr ibn Hind. The poetry is transmitted with stories about that rivalry, but the stories do not give us a complete independent court transcript. The odes themselves are our strongest witnesses to how poetry could make a political argument in the form of communal self-presentation.

Amr ibn Kulthum's poem begins with an invitation to drink, with a sound of exuberant sociability before its declarations become confrontational. This is an important opening. A poem that later sounds like a rallying speech does not begin as a plain list of claims. It arrives through familiar pleasures and the company of people who will hear it. The voice then grows. It speaks as if the poet's tribe is fully present behind him. The repeated sense of a collective we is central: the ode is not only about an individual's bravery, but about a community's memory of what it has done, what it possesses, and what it refuses to surrender.

The language of honor becomes forceful. The poem remembers battles, defiance of kings, inherited horses and weapons, and the duty to protect dependents and guests. It boasts that its people do not submit meekly to threats. Like other early Arabic odes it can use an extravagant image to make status palpable. This is not an administrative proposal written in measured prose. Its power comes from the assurance that every person listening already knows the danger of being treated as weak. The poet magnifies the group's past acts until they become a claim to its future independence. To challenge the tribe is, in the poem's performance, to misunderstand the world in which the speaker lives.

We should be careful about what this voice does to people outside its circle. Communal solidarity can protect members against intimidation, but it can also treat honor as something proved through violence and rivalry. The poem claims its own people as generous, brave, and formidable; other groups appear largely insofar as they have encountered that power. That is a property of its stance, not an independent verdict that one tribal history was wholly virtuous. The art makes a collective voice strong enough to occupy the space of a ruler's court without asking permission to be heard as an equal.

Al-Harith ibn Hilliza begins differently, with the memory of departure and the movement of a woman's party, but his argument becomes more exacting as it proceeds. Accusations have been made, and he will not simply answer them with a louder boast. He recalls treaties, promises, and shared responsibilities. He names remembered episodes in which outsiders, neighbors, and rival groups may have acted, and he questions whether his own people can justly be held liable for everything alleged against them. He presents the audience with a history of agreements that must matter if judgment is to be something other than a royal whim. The ode is proud, certainly, but its pride takes a legal and diplomatic shape.

As the argument develops, al-Harith speaks of allies and overlords, obligations accepted in common, and the danger of listening to reports shaped by malice. He praises the ruler as a potential just arbiter while also insisting that the claims against his group must be tested. The later story of the court performance makes much of the speaker's ability to win attention and esteem. Whether or not all the traditional details of that performance are historically recoverable, the poem's posture is clear. A poet can defend a community by mobilizing memory and by asking a superior power to honor its own standards of fairness.

Read together, these odes are not simply two poems about fighting. Amr makes a voice of unyielding presence: we are here, we have fought, and no one may dishonor us. Al-Harith makes a voice of reasoned defense: remember the agreements, distinguish accusation from proof, and judge us in light of what has actually been undertaken. Both invoke the past as a resource. Both rely on the authority of a public audience. But one aims to make submission unthinkable, while the other aims to make injustice embarrassing before the ruler who has power to decide. They are two different uses of the same high poetic culture.

Each ode uses a fixed meter and sustained rhyme to organize an extended poetic argument. Its length creates room for openings that seem personal, travel and recollection, confrontation, praise, and warnings. The words have been preserved through generations of collectors, teachers, grammarians, and commentators who made decisions about which verses belonged and how they should be understood. The conventional seven-poem set has a complicated textual history, and some traditions add other poets to it. The story that all seven poems were written in gold and displayed on the Kaaba is famous but historically insecure. Calling them the Hanging Odes should not require us to repeat that story as fact.

This distinction between poem and later explanation matters especially when poems are said to have been performed at one particular court. An anecdote may help later readers understand why people remember an ode as an argument, but it cannot substitute for the words and structure of the poem. In Amr's voice, the social body is presented as an indivisible power. In al-Harith's, disputes are organized around accusations, witnesses, and covenants. Both are examples of how a poetic form could do the work of political language before the emergence of modern state archives or newspapers. The audience had to remember what the words made memorable.

We have now heard seven distinct voices: Imru' al-Qays lingering at ruins and riding through a storm; Tarafa insisting on the value of a brief life; Zuhayr praising peace after war; Labid tracing the endurance of a natural world; Antarah carrying love into battle; Amr ibn Kulthum defending communal pride; and al-Harith ibn Hilliza answering accusation with argument. The collection's literary significance does not lie in a single story those men all took part in. It lies in the range of ways the Arabic *qasida* could organize memory, desire, reputation, environment, and public obligation. Later readers preserved these poems not because their speakers agreed, but because the poems gave speech extraordinary force.

The last question is what it means to listen rather than merely admire. These odes arise from societies that placed enormous weight on group honor and on the ability of verse to memorialize or wound. Their ideals are not automatically ours. We can recognize the beauty of a ruined camp, the magnificence of a swift animal, the exhaustion of war, and the dignity of a person refusing humiliation while remaining alert to the violence and hierarchy these performances sometimes normalize. The poems are old, but their argument about who gets to tell a community's story is not over. That is why the *Mu'allaqat* deserve a place not simply as relics of an earlier Arabic language, but as a living challenge to the idea that literature is separate from power.
