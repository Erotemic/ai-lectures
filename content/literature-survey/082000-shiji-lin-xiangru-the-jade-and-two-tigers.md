# Shiji IV: Lin Xiangru, Lian Po, and the Jade That Came Home

A standalone narrative from *Shiji* chapter 81, the linked biographies of Lian Po and Lin Xiangru, about language, dignity, and the perils of competing egos in a state threatened by Qin.

A precious piece of jade is passed through the hands of a powerful king. He turns it over, shows it to those near him, and delights in its beauty. The envoy who brought it watches the king's pleasure and realizes that the promise made in return is not going to be honored. He is deep in the court of Qin, a kingdom far stronger than his own state of Zhao. There is no friendly army waiting in the next room. What he possesses is a fine stone, a ceremonial mission, and the ability to make the ruler's actions look shameful in front of his own attendants. Sima Qian's *Records of the Grand Historian* tells this as the great entrance of Lin Xiangru, a figure whose strength is not that he can defeat ten thousand soldiers but that he can control a negotiation when almost everything is against him.

The object is known as the He clan jade, a treasure with a history of its own. Qin's ruler offers fifteen cities in exchange for it. The people advising the Zhao king fear a trap. If they surrender the jade, Qin may keep it without ceding anything; if they refuse, Qin has a pretext for hostility. Lin Xiangru, not yet a prominent officer, proposes a way through the dilemma. Send him with the stone. If Qin acts honorably, he will complete the exchange. If not, he will preserve Zhao's dignity and recover the jade if he can. The promise is breathtaking in part because it does not depend on the envoy controlling his own situation. He must judge the king in front of him, quickly, and then improvise.

When the Qin king handles the jewel without arranging the promised transfer, Lin Xiangru steps forward and says there is a defect in the stone he must show. The king returns it. Lin holds the jade near a pillar and declares that he will smash it along with his own head if Qin tries to seize it. The threat redefines the scene. The stronger party can take a life, but cannot obtain the coveted object without risking its destruction. Qin's king retreats into ceremony. Lin requires a period of ritual preparation before the proper public exchange can occur. While the court performs its rites, he sends the jade home secretly in the clothes of a subordinate. When the audience resumes he admits what he has done and offers to accept punishment. Qin has neither received the treasure nor demonstrated the magnanimity that would have made it worthy of the treasure. The king releases him.

The episode is commonly associated with the phrase about returning the jade intact to Zhao. Its interest is deeper than a clever trick. Lin Xiangru takes an agreement between unequal powers and makes the strong party's reputation part of the bargaining field. He cannot enforce the promised cities. He can expose the king's bad faith, increase the cost of taking what Qin wants, and accept danger himself. Sima Qian uses the jewel as an object in which diplomacy becomes visible. The map in the Jing Ke story concealed a weapon. This stone works differently: it is valuable precisely because everyone can see it, and the envoy uses the threat of its loss to insist that public words matter.

Lin's next famous test comes when the rulers of Qin and Zhao meet at Mianchi. Qin's king wants Zhao's king to perform a humiliating musical act and orders the meeting recorded as a sign of submission. Lin Xiangru responds by pressing the Qin ruler to perform in turn, ensuring that Zhao's attendants record the exchange as well. He is prepared to place his own life in danger rather than allow the document of the meeting to become a unilateral humiliation. Here the weapon is neither dagger nor precious stone but the wording of a record. The situation is especially revealing in a book composed by a historian. The event that gets written down, and the way it gets written down, may become a political fact carried beyond the room.

Lin's service wins him a rank above Lian Po, a successful general who has defended Zhao with soldiers and sieges. Lian Po cannot bear the precedence awarded to a man whose accomplishments seem to him to be only words. He announces that when he meets Lin, he will shame him. Lin begins avoiding his colleague. He feigns illness rather than compete for a place in court and turns his carriage away when he sees Lian coming. His own attendants are offended. They followed a man known for defying Qin's king; now he appears afraid of a general of his own country. They threaten to leave him unless he can explain himself.

Lin asks them a question. Is Lian Po stronger and more frightening than Qin's ruler? Certainly not, they answer. Lin points out that he had stood up to Qin. He does not avoid Lian because he is frightened. Qin has not attacked Zhao while two men, one a general and one a diplomat, stand between it and easy conquest. If those men fight each other, they will make the state vulnerable. Lin compares them to two tigers whose struggle would destroy both. He will not place the satisfaction of his pride above the safety of Zhao. His attendants now see why the man who had threatened a king can choose to avoid a quarrel in the street.

The account's resolution is another carefully staged public action. Lian Po hears what Lin has said. Realizing the pettiness of his threat, he comes to apologize bearing a switch or thorny branch against his exposed back, an expression of shame and readiness to accept punishment. Lin receives him. They become intimate allies, joined by a relationship later described as friendship that can endure life and death. The moment is not the victory of language over force. It is the recognition that a state needs the soldier's force and the envoy's language together, and that rivalry between them could undo both. Sima Qian has led us from a precious object to the invisible value of cooperation.

The wider chapter continues with other careers, military campaigns, failures, and judgments; it is not a short moral fable in its entirety. We have selected the linked Lin-and-Lian sequence because it shows an aspect of the *Shiji* that a series about emperors and assassins alone would miss. Political history is made by people who cannot always settle questions with force. Their work occurs in confrontations over honor, documents, gifts, and rank. A word spoken in the wrong tone can furnish a pretext for war. A gesture of self-restraint can protect an entire state. The story of the jade would be only a clever adventure if Lin's later refusal to fight his colleague did not show what his cleverness was for.

A reader need not accept that every exchange took place in precisely the wording Sima Qian gives it. We can admire the construction of the chapter and ask about its ethical ideal at the same time. The historian places a soldier and a diplomat beside one another. He asks what courage looks like when it is not an attack; what power looks like when it is not a sword; and what kind of person can accept an injury to pride for the sake of something beyond the self. The jade is eventually returned. That is the visible success. The more difficult success is that two proud men learn not to destroy the country they both claim to serve.
