# The Book of Disquiet — Guided Reading: The Office and the Dream

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

Imagine beginning a book and discovering that the person who wrote it never decided which page should come first. Some of its pages appear to belong to a solitary aesthete whose surroundings dissolve into extravagant dreams. Others are the private reflections of a quiet assistant bookkeeper in Lisbon. Some are carefully shaped miniature essays; some break off; others resemble diary entries without providing the continuity of a diary. One edition puts a passage near the beginning, another elsewhere, and yet another omits it. The differences are not simply publisher's mistakes. They arise from the nature of the material Fernando Pessoa left when he died in 1935. What we call *The Book of Disquiet* was an unfinished literary project written across decades and assembled into books by editors only after his death. To encounter it seriously, we need to give up the expectation of an authorized chapter one followed by a chapter two. The book offers something else: a record of how consciousness can create worlds while a person's external life remains almost unchanged.

Our route is a guided reading in themes and identifiable textual situations, not a claim to reproduce any particular edition's order. We will begin with the early phase associated with Vicente Guedes and move into the later writing associated with Bernardo Soares. We will return to a handful of scenes and circumstances: an office, a street, a dream of departure, an afternoon of changing light, a sense of time passing without achievement. These are not episodes stitched into a fictional day by the original author. The sequence is ours, intended to help a new listener recognize the kinds of movement the fragments make.

Pessoa was a Portuguese poet, writer, and maker of literary identities. His famous heteronyms were not merely pen names. A pen name usually conceals the name of an author whose basic personality remains continuous. Pessoa imagined distinct authors with biographies, temperaments, intellectual commitments, and styles. Alberto Caeiro, Ricardo Reis, and Álvaro de Campos are prominent examples outside this book. Within *The Book of Disquiet*, the attribution changed as the project developed. Earlier fragments were connected with Vicente Guedes; later ones were placed under the name Bernardo Soares, whom Pessoa called a semi-heteronym, a figure closer to himself and less completely separate than some of the others. A new reader may be tempted to ask which one is really Pessoa. The better initial question is what each persona permits the prose to do.

The early phase, composed principally before the long interruption of the 1920s, often feels as though ordinary reality is only the thinnest visible surface of another country. Prose expands into landscapes of inward perception. Rooms, forests, light, and imagined distances become environments in which sensation takes on a life of its own. One early extended work associated with the project is *The Forest of Estrangement*. Its mode is dreamlike and symbolist: instead of a character undertaking an ordinary quest, a consciousness seems to enter a terrain where external and internal scenery cannot be pulled apart. It is possible to find the atmosphere excessive, to feel that beautiful words have almost displaced the objects they describe. That reaction is part of the challenge the writing sets. The passage asks us to experience a world made by sensation before asking whether that world has a conventional message.

We might think that a dream is something one escapes from upon waking. The Guedes material unsettles that distinction. What if waking life is merely the set of habits everyone agrees to call real, and the dream is not less real to the person feeling it? The question is not a claim that furniture vanishes when nobody observes it. It is about the different scales of importance available to human consciousness. A memory of rain, a moment of estrangement, or the atmosphere of an imagined landscape can govern an afternoon more completely than the events officially recorded on a calendar. Pessoa lets these states take up more verbal space than the transactions of everyday life.

The later persona brings us to a particular city and occupation. Bernardo Soares is an assistant bookkeeper in a commercial office on the Rua dos Douradores, in Lisbon's lower town. There are accounts to keep, papers to arrange, colleagues to encounter, a superior to obey. The workplace includes the boss Vasques, the head bookkeeper Moreira, other employees, and errand workers. Soares is not a revolutionary reorganizing the office. Nor is he portrayed as a great artist triumphantly hiding among clerks. He goes to work. He earns a living. He notices things other people may pass without recording. In the margins of a routine that seems to contain little adventure, he writes an elaborate inner account of existence.

There is something important about choosing a bookkeeper for this role. Soares handles figures that are meant to describe other people's business accurately. His professional work depends on balance, classification, and the disciplined transfer of information. But when he tries to keep accounts of his own perceptions, the totals will not close. A glance becomes a dream; a thought generates its opposite; a moment that seemed a revelation dissolves when he attempts to state it plainly. The office is not merely a symbol of oppression, because he depends on it and knows its customs. Nor is it the place where his true life is entirely absent. He observes the people there with feelings more varied than the simple desire to get away.

One of the most revealing fragments begins when Soares imagines leaving the Rua dos Douradores forever. In his daydream there would be no more office, no boss Vasques, no daily colleagues, no demands on his time. He imagines a release into art and private possibility. Then, while the fantasy is still taking shape, he recognizes that he would miss the world he has dismissed. The ordinary people and objects have become part of him. The imagined freedom brings not only pleasure but grief. We have not progressed through a novelistic crisis or reached a decisive choice to stay. The fragment changes direction inside a single thought. It is an example of how *The Book of Disquiet* makes reflection rather than action into its dramatic event.

Listen to the structure of that turn, even without hearing Pessoa's exact sentences. A desire seems obvious: leave the burdensome workplace. Then its opposite appears: if the workplace disappeared, something of the self would disappear with it. The second perception does not prove the first false. Both remain. This is an important method of the book. Soares's moods do not necessarily correct one another on the way to a final, stable conclusion. They coexist in the reader's memory. The same morning may produce disdain for the routine, gratitude for its shelter, a longing for elsewhere, and an awareness that elsewhere would become another routine once inhabited.

From the office we can walk into Lisbon. The city is not a backdrop in the vague sense that all stories need somewhere to occur. Its streets, river views, commercial spaces, weather, windows, and changing light are the raw material of many fragments. In one, the ordinary prospect of a journey becomes an occasion for the mind to wander so completely that the anticipated landscape scarcely registers. What remains afterward is less a travel report than a record of having failed to experience what one expected to experience. The mind's attempt to capture the journey has intervened between seeing and remembering. The result can be comic. A man goes somewhere and returns with pages about his inability to pay attention to where he went.

But these fragments are not simply jokes about absent-mindedness. They expose a paradox in literary observation. We imagine the perceptive writer as someone who pays perfect attention to the visible world. Soares often records how his attempts to attend turn into memories, analogies, inward stories, or doubts. He is an observer who notices the act of observing. This second layer makes the city unstable. A street is a physical place where a person could draw a map, but it is also a set of impressions that no map can capture. The same buildings seem different in rain, in sunlight, after a sleepless night, or under the burden of a new expectation.

At times the writing looks at an ordinary person and turns that stranger into an emblem of a whole imagined existence. At times the speaker experiences a momentary estrangement from the person who is supposed to be writing. He seems to stand beside his own thoughts, assessing them from a distance, and then beside that assessment too. This is not a neat medical account of dissociation, and we should not diagnose the fictional persona through fragments. It is a literary technique: the self becomes something to be watched rather than a firm platform from which the world can be judged. The reader may discover that the sentence doing the watching is itself part of the same uncertainty.

Dreaming is everywhere in the book, but it is not always comforting. The mind invents distant cities, possible lives, or forms of success that the working day cannot deliver. For an instant the imaginary world seems to enlarge existence. Then comes the realization that an imagined life is also one the dreamer has not lived. Rehearsing possibilities can become a way to avoid committing to any of them. Soares does not consistently celebrate imagination as freedom. He notices that it can make him passive, that it can place a screen between him and other people, that the pleasure of imagining a departure may reduce the likelihood of departing. The book's beauty is inseparable from this problem. The same capacity that gives the prose its richness may also keep its speaker within his room.

There is a familiar kind of plot in which a bored clerk dreams of a different life, quits his job, travels, and becomes the person he was meant to be. *The Book of Disquiet* does not supply that satisfaction. Sometimes the dream itself is the event, followed by the discovery that the office still exists and the salary still matters. More often no decisive plot event is needed. An ordinary afternoon is sufficient because sensation can change the apparent shape of a whole life. The book does not ask us to admire inactivity as a superior moral condition. It asks us to recognize how much of human experience consists of alternatives considered, abandoned, revived, or never spoken aloud.

Soares's attitude to other people is no less divided. He can speak as if society's expectations are intolerable and ordinary conversation a waste. Yet he also registers affection for colleagues whose limitations he knows. The office is a place of hierarchy and habits, but its inhabitants are familiar. The boss Vasques can represent the heavy factual demands of life, while still being a particular man with ordinary strengths and weaknesses. Such moments rescue the speaker from becoming merely a portrait of contempt for the practical world. He cannot always sustain the fantasy that everything outside his private consciousness is worthless. There are people there, and they have entered his life whether he planned it or not.

The early Guedes passages tend to stretch toward a secluded, highly wrought sensibility; the later Soares passages more insistently bring the dream into contact with rooms and streets. That contrast is a useful entrance, though it must not be exaggerated. Editors disagree over which passages belong where, some texts have uncertain dates, and both phases can contain the movements of beauty, weariness, estrangement, and thought. The work did not acquire a definitive structure from its author. We cannot pretend that every later fragment is more mature, or that the whole sequence moves toward a final revelation. That developmental story would be an editorial interpretation, not a recoverable plot.

The title's word *desassossego* is often rendered as disquiet: a state of unrest, a lack of settled ease. This does not mean that the speaker is constantly suffering in the same way. Some pages are intensely alert to beauty. Others are funny in their disproportion between an immense philosophical thought and a trivial physical setting. Some feel tender toward a moment that cannot last. Others are severe about the habits of society or the limits of human ambition. The disquiet is partly the refusal of any of these states to remain final. The moment one tries to possess an experience, it changes under the pressure of being described.

Think now about the act of writing itself. If the speaker's public life is small, one might expect the book to compensate by making him famous within its own world. It does not. The fragments are often preoccupied with anonymity, futility, incompletion, and the awkward distance between imagining a literary achievement and actually bringing one into existence. The irony is profound: Pessoa left unfinished the very book in which a fictive writer reflects on the difficulty of making art from a life dominated by interruption. The posthumous editors have had to create structures that the author did not complete. The book's physical history becomes an extension of its theme, although that coincidence should not persuade us that Pessoa planned the unfinished condition as a finished work of art.

Imagine a reader moving among different editions. In one, a certain passage may seem to prepare for a confession several pages later; in another, it may sit among fragments about the city; in a chronological arrangement, it may appear near texts by an earlier persona. The words do not lose all meaning when moved, but their relations shift. The reader becomes more conscious of the construction of continuity. This is why an overview cannot honestly announce that the book begins with one mental state, passes through a crisis, and ends with recovery. It might explain how one edition shapes that impression, but it cannot attribute the order to an author who did not leave it settled.

What, then, have we actually read together? We have visited a symbolist interior landscape; met a clerk working in Lisbon; followed a fantasy of escape that turns into fear of loss; watched the city transform under attention; considered the pleasures and traps of dreaming; and witnessed a self that becomes doubtful as soon as it tries to describe itself. These are recognizable strands within the project, not chapters in a single invented journey. Their coherence lies in a recurring problem: a person can lead a small outward life and an immense inward one, and neither life will fully answer the demands of the other.

The book leaves us in a place that will not resolve itself. A street remains a street. Accounts still need to be balanced. A passerby goes on toward a destination the writer may never discover. At a window or desk, another perception arrives, seems momentarily complete, and becomes a fragment. The temptation is to ask when Soares will finally begin living. But that question assumes a distinction the prose continually tests. The writing is part of his life. It reveals the cost of retreating from the world and the intensity with which the world continues to reach him. To finish this guided reading is not to reach the book's final, authorized page. It is to recognize why a work built from the desire to understand experience might remain open even when one closes the volume.

## Text and evidence companion (not spoken)

### Form and scope

Original thematic guided reading of Pessoa's unfinished *Livro do Desassossego*, composed in phases primarily c. 1913–20 and 1929–34, first assembled as a Portuguese book in **1982**. This is **not** a definitive fragment order, not a simulated transcript from one edition, and not a full read-through. The commentary is original prose and quotes no modern English translation. Some named examples come from direct Portuguese fragments in the scholarly LdoD Archive; other contextual statements come from the Casa Fernando Pessoa and textual scholarship.

### Identified fragment families / listening route

- **Early Guedes phase:** extended symbolist/decadent prose such as *Na Floresta do Alheamento* (*The Forest of Estrangement*); textual attribution and dating are editorial questions.
- **Later Soares phase:** assistant bookkeeper on the Rua dos Douradores; boss Vasques and Moreira; small everyday commercial life rendered through introspection.
- **Directly located late fragment:** the fantasy of leaving the office, Vasques, the errand workers, and the cat, then feeling grief at imagined liberation. [LdoD Archive, Fr071.a, Pizarro witness](https://ldod.uc.pt/reading/fragment/Fr071.a/inter/Fr071_a_A_WIT_ED_CRIT_P).
- **Recurring themes:** Lisbon under weather and changing light, dreams versus living, observer watching himself, affection and estrangement, imagination and passivity. Not a claim that these fragments appear in that order in any published edition.
