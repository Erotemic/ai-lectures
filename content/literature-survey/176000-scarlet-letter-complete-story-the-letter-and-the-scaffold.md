# The Scarlet Letter — Complete Story: The Letter and the Scaffold

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

A woman stands on a platform in seventeenth-century Boston with a baby in her arms and a brilliant red letter sewn to her dress. Around her is a crowd. Some spectators want to punish her more severely. Others study the needlework and judge it indecently beautiful for the purpose it serves. Ministers and magistrates demand that she disclose the man who fathered her child, but she will not. The public thinks it is witnessing a story whose meaning has already been settled. A woman committed adultery, the law has discovered it, and the scarlet letter is supposed to teach everyone what she is. Nathaniel Hawthorne's *The Scarlet Letter* begins by making that public certainty feel strangely incomplete. The crowd sees the letter. It cannot see the whole history inside the woman wearing it. And standing among the spectators are two men whose hidden knowledge will reshape the meaning of the symbol they have placed upon her.

The woman is Hester Prynne. Her husband sent her from Europe to the Massachusetts colony and failed to follow for a long time. Believed by some to be dead, he has been absent while Hester lived without him. Now she has given birth to a daughter, Pearl. The magistrates can punish the visible evidence of her relationship, but the father's identity remains unknown. Hester's dress carries an embroidered A, the public mark of adultery. Yet she makes the letter with such art that the punishment becomes impossible to separate from an expression of her will. The city has compelled her to wear a sign. It has not determined exactly what that sign will look like, or what she will become while wearing it.

A stranger appears in the crowd and learns why the woman is on the scaffold. His face changes. He is the long-absent husband, but he conceals his name and adopts a new one, Roger Chillingworth. He signals Hester to keep silent. Later, when he is allowed to visit her in prison as a physician, they speak privately. He admits that marrying a younger woman and leaving her alone had been a mistake. But he also reveals a dangerous intention. He will find the man who shared her secret. Hester's punishment is already public. Chillingworth wants to discover the hidden partner and extract a punishment of his own. He makes Hester promise that she will not reveal who he is.

This promise begins another captivity. Hester is released from prison, but she must live on the margin of the settlement. She chooses a small cottage away from the crowded center, close enough to earn a living through fine needlework and far enough to feel the exclusion. Her skill is in demand. The people who reject her as a moral example are willing to wear the objects she creates. She stitches ceremonial garments and clothing for the wealthy while being denied a share in their esteem. Only wedding veils are withheld from her work, as though her hands have become unacceptable precisely where respectable marriage is publicly celebrated.

Pearl grows, and the novel invites us to see her through several different eyes. To Hester she is a beloved child, lively and unpredictable, difficult to govern because of the force of her imagination. To the settlement she may be evidence of a sinful origin. To her mother she sometimes seems a living reflection of the scarlet letter, a beautiful but troubling reminder of a choice that cannot be undone. The child responds to the bright mark almost before she understands language. Hester cannot simply take off the letter and live as though the crowd never saw it. Pearl will ask questions the city has no right to answer for her.

Meanwhile, the young minister Arthur Dimmesdale has become one of Boston's most admired spiritual leaders. His sermons seem to carry an intensity that moves the listeners to tears. But his health declines. He is pale, exhausted, and often places a hand over his heart. The congregation imagines exceptional spiritual sensitivity. Readers gradually see that the public minister has a private wound. Dimmesdale is Pearl's father. The person who should be standing beside Hester has watched her punished alone. His parishioners admire him most when he is most oppressed by what he has concealed.

Chillingworth, trained in medicine, establishes himself as the minister's physician. At first the arrangement looks benevolent. The doctor becomes a close companion to the suffering clergyman, and both live in the same house, where Chillingworth can watch him almost continuously. But the more the physician investigates Dimmesdale's illness, the less he acts like a healer. He is fascinated by the possibility that secret guilt causes the symptoms. He presses into the minister's private thoughts, listens for confession, and interprets signs of inward turmoil. He has come to believe the person he seeks is within reach.

One day, while Dimmesdale sleeps, Chillingworth examines the place over his heart. Hawthorne does not tell us directly, at that instant, precisely what the doctor sees. He gives us Chillingworth's terrible satisfaction. From then on the physician's closeness becomes a form of deliberate torment. He has discovered enough to pursue his revenge. There is a second scarlet letter in the story now, not publicly embroidered but perhaps written in guilt upon the body. The ambiguity matters. The mystery is not just about the marks that can be found, but about how people choose to read them.

Hester continues to live quietly and labor for people in need. She helps those who despise her, brings food to the poor, and gives what time she can to the sick. Gradually, the citizens begin to associate the letter with her work rather than only with her offense. Some take its A to mean ability rather than adultery. The visible sign stays, but its public meaning changes. Hester does not receive a formal declaration of innocence. Her place is precarious, and the humiliation has done lasting damage. Yet the people of Boston cannot make her entire life fit inside the original verdict.

The authorities become concerned about Pearl. They wonder whether a woman marked by sin is fit to raise a child, and they consider removing the girl from Hester. The dispute brings Hester to the governor's house, where dignitaries examine the child's religious education. Pearl answers some questions mischievously, deepening the adults' suspicion. Hester makes an impassioned defense of her right to keep her daughter. Dimmesdale intervenes, arguing that the bond between mother and child may serve a providential purpose. He helps preserve Hester's custody without acknowledging publicly why the matter concerns him so much. The scene offers one of his few acts of courage before the end, and even here his truth is disguised within the language of his profession.

Pearl's childhood becomes a series of small confrontations with the letter and the faces around her. In the forest and at the shoreline she finds signs and invents games. Among other children she is both excluded and capable of frightening them. She senses that her mother's mark and the minister's habit of clutching his heart belong to the same mystery. But she cannot yet explain it. Her questions have the persistence of questions that adults keep refusing to answer. The child is not a realistic detective solving a case. She is a pressure on the world of concealment that the adults have built.

Dimmesdale's private suffering intensifies. He tries to discipline himself through fasting, vigils, and self-punishment, hoping that torment can replace an honest confession. He imagines speaking the truth to his congregation. Instead, his sermons deepen the people's reverence. When he accuses himself in religious terms, they understand him as a saint too humble to acknowledge his goodness. He has become trapped in an irony he helped create: his attempts to communicate guilt strengthen the public image that protects him from the consequences of admitting it. The more he is adored, the harder it becomes to step out of the role.

One night Dimmesdale climbs the scaffold where Hester once stood before the crowd. He tries to enact a confession in darkness, when almost no one can see. Hester and Pearl pass by, returning from a deathbed visit, and he calls them up beside him. For a brief moment the three form the family that has never existed openly. Pearl asks whether he will stand with them there in daylight. He cannot promise that. A meteor passes overhead. Dimmesdale imagines the letter A written in the sky; other people may read the sign differently, and the narrator refuses to settle its supernatural meaning. Chillingworth appears nearby, watching. The scene repeats the book's opening stage but denies the release that a true public acknowledgment might bring.

Years have changed Hester's understanding of Chillingworth. She agreed to keep his identity secret, but she can see what he has become. His desire for revenge has consumed his capacity for sympathy. She confronts him, asks that he stop pursuing Dimmesdale, and eventually decides she must reveal to the minister that the physician is her husband. The promise she made at the beginning has become a weapon used against another person. Hester realizes that silence can carry obligations, but not every obligation remains morally defensible when the situation changes.

She arranges to meet Dimmesdale in the forest. The scene differs from the public town: tree trunks, uneven light, a brook, and a place where they can speak without the eyes of magistrates. Hester tells him the truth about Chillingworth. Dimmesdale is horrified that the man closest to him has been his enemy. For a moment he blames Hester, then recognizes the physician's revenge as the greater cruelty. They speak of their past affection and of the life they might still attempt together. Hester proposes that they escape by ship to Europe and begin again.

In the forest, Hester removes the scarlet letter and lets down her hair. The gesture seems to change the atmosphere. Sunlight comes through the trees, as if nature briefly grants the freedom the colony has refused. Dimmesdale feels a possibility of life beyond constant confession and fear. But Pearl, playing across the brook, refuses to come to her mother while the familiar letter is missing. She wants Hester to put it back. The moment can be read as a child's attachment to what she knows, or as a symbolic refusal to pretend the past can be erased by a quick gesture. Hester replaces the A, and Pearl returns. They will have to carry their history with them, wherever they go.

The planned departure is soon complicated. A ship is preparing to sail, but Chillingworth finds a way to arrange his passage as well. Escape will not be as simple as leaving Boston. Dimmesdale, meanwhile, has been chosen to deliver the important Election Sermon. The town is gathering to celebrate its civic and religious order. The minister approaches that day with sudden energy and a terrible inward decision. He must choose between the future Hester has offered and the truth that has been deferred since Pearl's birth.

On Election Day the market square is full of movement, music, soldiers, officials, and townspeople. Hester stands amid the festivities, wearing the letter the crowd knows. Pearl moves restlessly among them. When the minister emerges from the church, listeners speak of a sermon of extraordinary power. He appears changed, almost carried by an intensity that cannot last. Passing Hester, he seems outwardly distant. Chillingworth follows, anxious that the secret might slip beyond his control. The pressure of public ceremony and private truth has reached its last possible encounter.

Dimmesdale stops at the scaffold. Before the assembled crowd he calls Hester and Pearl to join him. Chillingworth tries to prevent it, because a confession would free the man from the intimacy of secret torment. Hester helps the minister climb. Dimmesdale acknowledges his connection to the woman and child, names the wrongdoing concealed beneath his public sanctity, and confronts the community with its error in believing the man they admired was untouched by moral failure. He draws aside his clothing. The narrative refuses to give a single, final account of what, if anything, is marked on his chest. Pearl kisses him, and he dies.

For Hester the confession is not an uncomplicated victory. The man she loved has finally named her, but he has not joined her in a shared future. Their escape will never happen. The public hears the truth and immediately begins making new versions of it. Some spectators insist they saw a visible mark upon Dimmesdale. Others claim to have seen no such thing. Still others interpret his words as a general lesson in the sinfulness of even the best of men, not as an admission of his own specific act. The community that confidently assigned meaning to Hester's letter at the beginning remains determined to govern meaning at the end.

Chillingworth soon declines and dies, as though the purpose that had sustained him disappeared with his victim. He leaves property to Pearl. Hester and Pearl depart from the town for a time. Reports suggest that Pearl eventually finds a life abroad, perhaps with marriage and children, but Hawthorne does not follow her in detail. His account leaves room for distance between a mother's punishment and the future open to her daughter. Pearl need not be condemned to live forever as the town's emblem of transgression.

Years later Hester returns alone to the small cottage near Boston. No official orders her to resume the scarlet letter. She places it on her dress of her own accord. People come to her for counsel, particularly women troubled by love, dependence, injustice, and the narrow lives society has offered them. She listens and helps. When she dies she is buried near Dimmesdale, but not in the same grave. They share a memorial bearing the symbol that gave the book its name. The red letter survives them both, impossible to reduce to one meaning.

Hawthorne frames the entire story with a narrator who claims to have discovered evidence of the old case while working in a nineteenth-century customhouse. Even that supposed documentary foundation is part of the fiction's play with history and invention. We are not asked simply to choose whether Hester was guilty. We are asked to observe what happens when a community uses a visible sign to make a complicated human life seem completely known. The first scene looked like the end of an investigation. By the final grave, we have seen the limits of the judgment, the cruelty of revenge, the danger of hidden guilt, and the endurance of a woman whose meaning could not be fixed by the letter they forced her to wear.

## Text and evidence companion (not spoken)

### Narrative basis

Original complete major-arc telling of Nathaniel Hawthorne's 1850 romance, including its fictional “Custom-House” frame and all twenty-four chapters. Controlling witness is the University of Virginia Library's electronic transcription from the 1850 Boston first edition; Project Gutenberg #33 is a free alternative text. The characters and Puritan Boston setting are literary fiction, not archival proof of an actual Hester Prynne case.

### Scene anchors

- “The Custom-House” introduction: fictive discovery of the letter and the old tale.
- Chapters 1–4: prison, scaffold, Pearl, returning husband under the name Chillingworth.
- Chapters 5–8: cottage and needlework, Pearl's isolation, governor's hearing over custody.
- Chapters 9–12: the physician and minister, suspected secret, midnight scaffold, meteor.
- Chapters 13–15: changed public reading of the letter, Hester's questions about revenge, Pearl's demand for truth.
- Chapters 16–20: forest meeting, Chillingworth disclosed, prospective escape, removal and replacement of the letter.
- Chapters 21–24: procession, sermon, daylight scaffold and death, disputed sign, Hester's return and shared memorial.

### Editorial limits

The novel deliberately withholds certainty about the mark on Dimmesdale's chest and the meteor's meaning; this retelling does not resolve those ambiguities. The concluding reports about Pearl's later life are presented as suggestions. Historical discussion of Puritan Boston belongs primarily to the companion analysis, not to the novel's narrated events.
