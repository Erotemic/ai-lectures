# The Kalevala — Story I: The Song and the Forge

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

Before there is a road through the forest, before there is a village to follow it, before any person can point to the north and name a distant country, there is water. A woman of the air descends into the sea. She is Ilmatar, who floats for years, lifted and tossed by waves. A bird seeks a place to nest and settles on her knee. The eggs grow warm. When the knee moves, they break, and their pieces become the sky, the earth, the sun, and the moon. Out of the same immense interval of time comes Väinämöinen, already old when he is born, a singer whose knowledge will repeatedly prove more powerful than a sword.

This is the world of the Kalevala as Elias Lönnrot arranged it in the fifty-poem edition published in 1849, drawing on Finnish and especially Karelian oral poetry. It does not behave like a modern novel with one hero moving through a single plot. Its singers know the origins of things. A tree's beginning may matter as much as a battle. An ancient word can mend a wound. An object can make a household flourish and then become the cause of a war. We will follow its main connected cycles in two tellings, beginning with the making of the world and ending this first part at a wedding in the North.

Väinämöinen brings growth into the new country. Trees sprout. One oak rises too high and spreads so wide that it shuts out the light. Even this first abundance contains its danger: the world cannot flourish under a tree that permits nothing else to grow. A little man emerges from the water, impossibly small until he grows into the one who can fell the oak. The enormous trunk crashes down. Light returns. Väinämöinen clears ground for grain but leaves a birch standing for birds. In a poem of epic strength, that detail matters. He is not the master of a landscape built solely for human use. A tree still belongs to the cuckoo.

A young man named Joukahainen hears of the old singer and resents being told that wisdom belongs to someone else. He arrives with the confidence of youth, ready to compete in knowledge. At first he offers lists: the names and habits of living things, facts about the world, things he has learned. Väinämöinen does not need to outdo him with a longer list. He sings. The song changes the ground beneath Joukahainen. The young challenger's possessions and body sink into the marsh. It is a terrifying contest because song has ceased to be a metaphor for power. Words make the world obey.

To save himself Joukahainen promises his sister Aino as a wife for the old singer. He has no right to make a gift of her future, but the promise goes home with him. Aino is not delighted. Her family sees advantage in a marriage to a famous and wealthy man; she sees a life she has not chosen. The story follows her grief with a patience that the men who arrange her future do not show. She goes to the water. In the received epic she is lost there, and the poem surrounds her death with sorrow and transformation. To say simply that a promised bride refused would miss the price of the bargain; to say she died merely to advance a hero's adventures would miss the voice the poem gives her suffering.

Väinämöinen mourns, too late. He later catches a strange fish and does not recognize that Aino has returned to him in another form. The fish slips away and speaks of the opportunity he has lost. The wise singer, who can put another man into the earth with a verse, has been foolish in the one relation where knowing facts is no help. No spell can make Aino agree to a life she does not want. He seeks counsel about finding a bride and sets out toward Pohjola, the northern country, where other dangers and bargains await.

Joukahainen has not forgotten his defeat. He shoots at Väinämöinen on the journey. The old singer falls into the sea and drifts helplessly until an eagle bears him toward land. At last he reaches Pohjola. Its mistress, Louhi, is powerful, practical, and a formidable negotiator. She shelters the stranger but does not give away a future. Väinämöinen wants to return home. Louhi wants a wonder made for her land: the Sampo, a device that can produce prosperity. She proposes a bargain involving her daughter. The old singer knows a craftsman, Ilmarinen, who can forge what Louhi demands. He promises the smith's work although the smith is not there to consent.

Väinämöinen eventually brings Ilmarinen north by trickery and magical means. The smith faces a task different from an ordinary day at the forge. He is asked to produce something that does not belong to the known inventory of things. Heat rises, iron changes color, and creations emerge that are not yet the Sampo. Only after the labor of repeated forging does the extraordinary object appear, a source of grain, wealth, and salt in the epic's description. Its precise mechanism is never explained as though this were an engineering diagram. Its meaning is clear enough: whoever possesses it can hope to thrive without the customary limits of labor and bad harvests.

Louhi takes the Sampo into the North and locks it away behind strong protections. Ilmarinen has accomplished the impossible. But the bride he had been offered does not automatically follow. The daughter of Pohjola is not a tool to be delivered with the smith's payment. The forging wins prestige; love and marriage remain uncertain. The Sampo will keep working in Louhi's country while the people in Väinämöinen's country remember who made it and who now benefits from it. The story plants a claim that will grow later into a violent attempt to recover the marvelous object.

Another singer-hero enters the epic: Lemminkäinen, rash, attractive, quick to desire, and unable to heed warnings for long. He wins or takes Kyllikki, a woman from an island community, and a promise is made between them about his fighting and her social life. The marriage does not give either of them security. When the agreement fails, Lemminkäinen decides to seek a new bride in Pohjola. His mother warns that the road is dangerous. He sets out anyway, confident that daring will mend what patience could not.

Louhi demands tasks from him. He must pursue an animal no ordinary hunter can catch and undertake other perilous deeds. Finally he must face the swan of Tuonela, the realm of the dead. On that road a hostile figure attacks him. Lemminkäinen dies and his body is broken apart in the river of death. Far away, his mother reads the sign of disaster in a household object left behind. She travels to the dark water and works to recover him. Piece by piece she gathers what remains of her son. With immense labor, charms, and divine assistance, she restores his body and life.

It is one of the most moving passages of the whole epic because the reckless hero does not rescue himself. The mother becomes the one who undertakes the impossible quest. She has no magical mill and no marriage prize to gain. She wants her son back. Lemminkäinen may return to boasting and misadventure, but the poem has allowed his survival to depend on a form of devotion quite unlike the swagger that led him to Tuonela.

Väinämöinen, meanwhile, needs to build a boat. Even the act of making a vessel demands the right words of origin. He seeks the missing formula in the realm of the dead and finally inside the ancient giant Antero Vipunen, who has knowledge buried with him. The strange journey turns the familiar idea of wisdom upside down. Knowledge is not simply on a tablet or in a teacher's school; it is guarded inside bodies, landscapes, and dangerous histories. To acquire words one must sometimes enter the place where ordinary life ends. When Väinämöinen returns, his boat can be completed, and the road toward Pohjola opens again.

The old singer and the smith both hope for the daughter of Pohjola. Väinämöinen's fame is no guarantee. Ilmarinen arrives with his skill and persistence, and this time Louhi sets tests for the bridegroom: dangerous fields and creatures that cannot be mastered by force alone. The smith must work through each peril with help and counsel. The bride makes her choice. She goes with Ilmarinen, and the poem prepares for a wedding so elaborate that the preparations become a story in their own right.

At the feast the host's wealth and the guests' hunger are both treated seriously. Beer must be brewed and food prepared. Songs of welcome fill the hall. Guests are instructed in the obligations of kinship. The bride receives advice, much of it stern, about leaving her childhood home and living in another household. She weeps for what she is abandoning. The marriage is a victory in one arc, but the poem does not pretend it is an uncomplicated prize handed to the smith. The bride's life will have a before and an after. In the long farewell we hear how much a grand public celebration can demand from one person.

Ilmarinen travels home with his wife. Väinämöinen remains the great singer who knows how to praise the occasion, yet he does not receive the young woman he desired. Lemminkäinen, still capable of turning every exclusion into an insult, has not been invited. Louhi possesses the Sampo. These facts sit beside the happy feast like objects waiting to be noticed after the music stops. The old stories have not run out. They have acquired debts.

The first half of the Kalevala can feel like several epics sharing a landscape: a creation song, the tragedy of Aino, the bargain for a miraculous mill, a mother's journey to the dead, a wise man's search for words, a wedding. Lönnrot's arrangement makes their connections audible, but each cycle retains something of the oral song tradition from which it was drawn. The great singer may know the birth of the earth and still fail to understand a young woman's grief. The great smith may forge an impossible machine and still find his home exposed to disaster. For now the new bride has crossed the threshold and the northern feast has ended. We will find out what becomes of these people when the Sampo, and the promises made around it, come due.

## Text and evidence companion (not spoken)

### Narrative witness and boundary

Original English telling of the first large movement of Elias Lönnrot's *New Kalevala* (1849), runos 1–25, not a translation of a single oral performance. Sequence and details checked against the Finnish Kalevala Society's runo-by-runo synopsis and J. M. Crawford's public-domain 1888 complete English rendering. Crawford's antiquated names and interpolated notes are not silently treated as modern Finnish/Karelian pronunciation or a substitute for the 1849 Finnish text. The work is a published editorial composition assembled from multiple traditions; its opening is not an unmediated ancient eyewitness account.

### Cycle locators

- Creation, oak, grain: runos 1–2.
- Joukahainen, Aino, fish, attempted killing of Väinämöinen: runos 3–7.
- Louhi, Sampo bargain, forge of Ilmarinen: runos 7–10.
- Lemminkäinen and his mother: runos 11–15.
- Väinämöinen's search for missing words, Tuonela and Vipunen: runos 16–17.
- Suitors, tasks, wedding, women's farewells: runos 18–25.
