# Kumulipo — Guided Reading II: The Genealogy of Day and the Living Crown

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

A genealogical chant can make the past sound like a landscape. Each name is a place one passes, another relation one must acknowledge, another path leading toward the present. The *Kumulipo* has already carried us through the deep night, the first living beings, the paired creatures of sea and land, and the emergence of day. Now the chant turns increasingly toward named ancestors, and its ancient breadth begins to acquire a focused political meaning. It is moving toward people for whom this genealogy was sung and kept.

If that sounds like a narrow conclusion after a cosmic opening, we should examine our expectation. To a listener who hears genealogy as merely a list of distant relatives, the movement from coral to chiefs seems like a contraction. To a listener for whom genealogy establishes relation and responsibility, it can be the opposite. The world that began with the birth of living beings is now shown to be continuous with a particular human line. A person who receives the chant is not placed outside the great origin; that person belongs inside it.

The later wā give us relationships among figures of divine and human ancestry. Lailai appears at the transition to daylight, and the lines that follow do not present one simple, uniform family chronology comparable to a modern birth registry. Named people and gods are connected; stories of movement and generation enter; the list can widen into brief narrative flashes. The pace differs from that of a novel, because its chief unit is not a dramatic scene but a relation that can lead to another relation. An episode may be introduced by a marriage or a birth, then make way for its descendants.

In Liliʻuokalani's nineteenth-century English translation, some periods are short and others extend across many pages of names. This unevenness is one of the things that a good listening guide must preserve. We cannot make each wā a single equal step in a simplified staircase, as if the chant were written to illustrate a modern classroom chart. We can instead recognize its rhythm: life appears; relationship creates continuity; a name calls up another name; the line bends toward the ancestors of a chiefly house.

Imagine hearing a pair of names and knowing that the marriage links two remembered houses. The words are not arbitrary labels. A skilled chanter and an informed audience can hear associations that a newcomer may miss entirely: rank, place, relationship, the remembered consequences of a union. A genealogy is a form of information, but it also places the information in public speech. It tells the audience which connections matter and gives them a form that can be performed and retained.

This is why the birth of a chief can be a cosmic occasion. The verse does not have to say that the chief personally created the animals or the islands. It connects the line in which that chief stands to a story of the world's coming into being. The connection can support political legitimacy, but it is not merely a forged administrative certificate. It is an inherited and elaborated account of what a person's rank and obligations mean in a world where ancestry reaches beyond the human.

As the chant proceeds, the names of great figures and ancestors associated with Hawaiian tradition appear. The sixteenth wā in the 1897 translation includes a succession of named ancestors, marriages, births, and chiefs. Its English page is visually different from the early list of sea beings, yet the form is recognizably the same kind of work. Relationships make the next generation intelligible. Names are placed in sequence and in pairs. The work still asks how beings can be connected across seemingly impossible distance, now through genealogical time rather than the gulf between sea and forest.

The final lines of that portion lead toward Lonoikamakahiki and Kaʻīʻimamao, the high-ranking ancestral figure to whom the chant is associated. The Queen's published edition also includes a genealogical table relating that line to her brother, King Kalākaua, and to herself. It is important to hear the distinction. The table is a helpful explicit supplement in the printed English book; it should not be silently treated as if it were a stanza of the traditional sung text. The poem and its publication history belong together in our study, but they are not identical layers.

According to the Queen's own printed presentation, the chant honored Kaʻīʻimamao. Its composition has traditionally been attributed to Keaulumoku, sometimes dated around the beginning of the eighteenth century. We should not assign a precise year to an oral composition merely because one nineteenth-century title page gives a rounded date. What matters securely is the association of the chant with a high-ranking chiefly genealogy, its life in Hawaiian language and ceremonial memory, and the importance of the printed versions for the Hawaiian monarchy of the nineteenth century.

Kalākaua had the Hawaiian-language text printed in 1889. His sister Liliʻuokalani prepared an English translation that was published in 1897. These are not irrelevant bibliographic afterthoughts. They mean that the chant was available in two different political and linguistic circumstances. One publication made a major Hawaiian-language genealogy more widely visible. The other carried it into English during a period when Hawaiʻi's sovereignty had been violently contested and its queen had been removed from power.

Liliʻuokalani's history is not a minor footnote to the poem's reception. She became queen in 1891. In 1893, a group backed by the presence of United States forces overthrew the Hawaiian constitutional monarchy. She was later imprisoned in ʻIolani Palace in 1895. She began her work on the translation during that period and completed it afterward. The 1897 English edition therefore belongs to the history of a dispossessed sovereign presenting a Hawaiian intellectual and genealogical inheritance to readers, including people who had been taught to see that inheritance as unimportant.

We should be careful not to turn every line of the older chant into a secret coded protest against the overthrow. The chant did not originate in 1895. Its earlier ceremonial function and genealogy have their own histories. But the decision to publish and translate a work can give it new force. A text that had already linked living chiefs to a cosmic genealogy could also serve, in a period of contested rule, as testimony to the depth and sophistication of Hawaiian accounts of legitimacy and belonging.

There is also a danger here for listeners outside Hawaiʻi. We might hear the *Kumulipo* only as a story about monarchs and mistake the nonhuman beings of its opening for an elaborate introduction to somebody else's family pride. Yet in the poem, the earliest lives are not disposable scenery. The genealogy can be understood as placing ancestors in relation to the land and the sea, not merely above them. A chiefly claim may connect to a cosmic order whose existence exceeds any one ruler. The beginning makes the ending possible, and the ending asks us to remember the beginning.

The same caution applies to a popular summary: first there is darkness, then coral, then plants, animals and humans, then royalty. That synopsis can be a useful map but a poor substitute for listening. The chant's movement is not simply a linear parade toward something better and better. Some relationships are paired across environments; the poetic language loops, echoes and returns. The sequence can sustain different readings of time. Scholars have noted that different Hawaiian interpreters understood its temporal and spatial dimensions in different ways. Genealogical time is not identical to a modern scientific timeline.

By the final period, the names themselves may be unfamiliar to a new English-language listener. Instead of trying to memorize every one, attend to the repeated act. A person is named along with a partner; a child is named; an ancestral link becomes possible; the link is joined to another. The same movement that produced a natural world through births now produces a socially and politically remembered world. That continuity is the form's deepest drama.

A work of literature need not contain a single final confrontation to have an ending with force. The *Kumulipo* travels from an almost unimaginable darkness to the named ancestors of an identifiable Hawaiian chiefly line. A listener who began with the coral organism now knows why its naming matters to someone who might live many generations afterward. The birth of a creature in the sea and the honor due a remembered ancestor have been placed within one account of relation.

The Queen's translation ends its own printed presentation with a genealogy that reaches the modern names of Kalākaua and Liliʻuokalani. In that movement, an inherited chant also becomes a document with a new public life. What began in deep darkness is made audible in a moment of political daylight and danger, when the right to speak for a kingdom was being fought over. The listener leaves not with a king claiming to stand outside nature and history, but with a lineage that insists it has been inside them all along.

## Text and evidence companion (not spoken)

### Textual and political boundary

Original English guided listening to the genealogical movement of the *Kumulipo*, wā 8–16, with the 1897 printed genealogy distinguished from the older sung content. Primary English witness: Liliʻuokalani's 1897 translation; later interpretations in Oliveira and scholarship on genealogical understandings of time are labeled as interpretation. This is **not** a full performed chant or proof of every ancient lineage as documentary historiography. Textual chronology and exact original date are contested; the Queen's original title-page attribution to Keaulumoku and c. 1700 is an attribution rather than a verified timestamp.

### Source locators

- Liliʻuokalani 1897, Eighth/Ninth Era: Lailai, Kii, Kāne, Kanaloa, beginning of day and named descendants.
- 1897, Tenth–Fifteenth Era: increasing emphasis on gods, people, marriages, ancestors and places.
- Sixteenth Era: chiefly pairings and lines to Lonoikamakahiki and Kaʻīʻimamao.
- 1897 edition after the Sixteenth Era: printed Kalākaua and Liliʻuokalani genealogical chart, an editorial appendix distinct from the chant's stanzaic body.
- University of Hawaiʻi at Mānoa Liliʻuokalani resources and Oliveira, *Ancestral Places*: publication dates, translation during imprisonment, genealogy and sovereignty interpretation.
