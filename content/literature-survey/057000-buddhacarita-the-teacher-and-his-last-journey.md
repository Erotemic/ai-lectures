# Buddhacarita, Part Three: The Teacher and His Last Journey

The continuation of *Buddhacarita* in the **Chinese-language witness**, chapters XV–XXVIII, as represented by Samuel Beal's 1883 English rendering of the Chinese text. These chapters are not asserted to survive in their original Sanskrit wording. The Chinese text is a transmission with interpretive features of its own.

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

A man has spent his youth asking what can free living beings from suffering. He has left his father, abandoned royal power, rejected the search for pleasure and the pride of bodily torment, and sat through a night of vision and inquiry. Now he has reached an answer. In the continuation of the *Buddhacarita*, the difficult part is no longer how to find the truth. It is what to do with it. A discovery made in silence must somehow be spoken to other people who have not made the same journey.

For this part of the story, our surviving witness changes. The original Sanskrit verses are no longer available for comparison line by line. A Chinese translation, along with a Tibetan transmission, preserves a continuation of the longer work to twenty-eight chapters. I will follow the Chinese narrative as a named witness. It is not legitimate simply to read its wording backward into a lost Sanskrit manuscript and pretend we have recovered the same poetic lines. But the Chinese version offers a substantial story of the Buddha's teaching, return, and final journey.

The Buddha, as Siddhārtha is now called, hesitates. The truth he has understood is subtle and difficult, while ordinary people are busy with wishes and fears. If a listener will not understand, teaching might appear useless. Yet the world is not made of identical minds. Some people are ready to recognize a path. The Buddha chooses to teach. His first journey as a teacher brings him to the Deer Park near Vārāṇasī, where five ascetics who once knew him are living. They remember that he abandoned extreme austerity. They do not expect him to have accomplished what all their practices could not.

When he approaches, however, the encounter changes. He speaks of a path that avoids the extremes of indulgence and self-punishment. He explains suffering, its arising, its cessation, and a way of training and understanding that can lead to that cessation. The five listen. One begins to see what the teacher is showing rather than merely repeat an old argument. A small community forms around the teaching, and the man who had left home alone is no longer alone. The poem's attention shifts from one person's awakening to the unpredictable question of how a teaching moves between people.

There are conversions and arguments. People approach from different lives: seekers with established practices, wealthy patrons, members of ruling families, and people without royal status. Some have learned to think that sacrifices and inherited ritual power can settle the question of a good life. The Buddha asks them to attend to conduct and understanding. In the Chinese continuation, stories of teachers who change their minds are not simple displays of humiliation. They show that even a respected person's position need not be final if another account better explains suffering and release.

King Bimbisāra, who once offered the renunciant political power, hears him again as a teacher. The meeting is different now. A worldly ruler and a religious teacher are not competitors for the same throne. One must govern a kingdom; the other speaks about a discipline that kings and servants can hear. The Buddha's instruction is not a proclamation that ordinary social problems disappear. It invites the ruler to consider how desire, action, and consequence shape human conduct, including the conduct of people who possess authority.

A supporter known for his generosity to the needy, Anāthapiṇḍika, becomes important to the community. In the Chinese story he is associated with the purchase and provision of the Jetavana park, which will serve as a place where monks can stay and teaching can be heard. An itinerant teaching cannot become a durable community through a famous founder alone. It needs places where people can gather, listeners who will offer food, and practices for living together. The beautiful site is not simply a stage for an isolated miracle. It is part of the ordinary infrastructure through which words can become a tradition.

One of the hardest returns is to the prince's own family. The Buddha goes back to the region of Kapilavastu. King Śuddhodana has imagined this encounter for years, but the son he sees is not the royal heir he expected. The Chinese narrative gives the father a piercing image: it is like finding a longed-for spring and discovering that the familiar comfort of water is no longer there. The son's face is recognizable, but his relation to rank and family has changed. He holds a begging bowl where a ruler would carry emblems of sovereignty.

Father and son meet in a space filled with memory. The king grieves for what might have been. The Buddha teaches that even royal power cannot overcome aging and death. The encounter does not simply reverse their positions, as though the prince has become a king of a higher empire. He is offering a kind of gift different from an inheritance: an account of action, attachment, and the possibility of release. The father begins to understand that the son's departure was not only a refusal of his own house. It was the condition of a vocation whose effects now reach that house too.

The community grows among members of the Śākya family and beyond it. The people who once watched the young prince's ceremonial outings now watch a mendicant pass through town. There are mixed reactions: admiration, confusion, pain, recognition. A child called Rāhula belongs to the family the Buddha left. The episode recalls the human cost already heard in the earlier cantos. A text about renunciation must keep facing those who did not choose to renounce. The teacher's calm cannot be used as proof that the household's loss never mattered.

The narrative moves through further public encounters. There are rivalries and threats. In the Chinese continuation, Devadatta is associated with attempts to injure the Buddha, including a dangerous elephant sent against him. The creature charges toward a man who does not answer its violence with violence. It is stopped and pacified. This is a dramatic image of the path the poem has been praising since Māra's defeat: the strongest response to aggression is not always the greater blow. The narrative does not require us to treat the miracle as verifiable zoological reportage in order to understand what it is doing with the image.

A woman known as Āmrapālī approaches the Buddha. She is a celebrated figure associated with Vaiśālī. Her encounter brings a different presence into a narrative often dominated by kings and male renunciants. The Chinese text also describes conflicts among the Licchavis, showing how the teaching crosses a landscape of powerful families and civic rivalries. People are not asked to leave their identities at a city's gate before hearing the Buddha. They bring their wealth, pride, hopes and disagreements with them.

The Buddha grows old. That is a striking development in a poem that began with a prince startled to discover what age does to bodies. The teacher who explained impermanence is not exempt from it. His last journeys do not erase illness or the exhaustion of travel. He prepares his disciples for a future without his physical presence. The community must not remain dependent on imagining that its founder will always be on the next road, available to answer the next question. A teaching that concerns change must survive the teacher's own passing.

Near the end he lies between trees and prepares to die. The followers around him confront precisely the grief against which the prince once hoped to find a cure. He does not offer them the permanence of a preserved body. He returns to the importance of attention and practice. The scene is an ending to the great royal dilemma of the beginning. The son who might have become a powerful king has become the head of a community, but even that role is temporary. His work must continue in other people's understanding.

The narrative distinguishes the moment of death from the community's response. Lamentation fills the gathering. The body is honored; different people want a share in what remains. The relics become objects of dispute and distribution. The continuation ends not by pretending that enlightenment has removed history, but by showing how religious memory takes material form. Places, monuments, institutions, and the division of relics become part of the story of a teaching after its founder is gone.

The final chapters are emphatically literary and devotional. The Chinese witness celebrates a teacher whose power is moral and spiritual, and it records marvels and conversions in that light. We can listen to the story's shape without using it as an unexamined biography. It begins with a birth read as destiny, moves through a son's departure and a lonely search, then turns toward a community that will carry the consequences of his answer. The original Sanskrit poem is incomplete in our hands. Its Chinese continuation is neither disposable nor identical to the lost Sanskrit. It is an important surviving account with a history of its own.

Consider the old king one last time. He hoped his son would protect a country. At their reunion he discovers that the son has been working on the problem of how anyone might live in a country where every person eventually dies. At the final funeral, the question becomes public: how will a community protect a teaching after the person who spoke it can no longer defend it? The story gives no guarantee that followers will agree. Indeed, the division of relics shows them negotiating possession already. What survives is not a king's perfect succession. It is a living argument about suffering, compassion, and how a person should act when nothing they love can be held forever.
