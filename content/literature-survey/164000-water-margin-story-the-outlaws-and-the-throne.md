# Water Margin — Complete Story: The Outlaws and the Throne

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

At the end of a story about one hundred and eight outlaws, the emperor has given them what their leader always said he wanted: recognition. They have fought the empire, accepted its pardon, fought its enemies, and brought back victories. Yet the very government that has welcomed them home is preparing to dispose of them. What would it mean for men who called themselves brothers to win an official title and lose almost everything else?

That is the question to carry into *Water Margin*, also called *Outlaws of the Marsh* or *Shuihu zhuan*. It is one of the major novels of Chinese vernacular literature. Our telling follows the large arc of the received one-hundred-and-twenty-chapter version: the gathering of the outlaws at Liangshan, their imperial pardon, their military campaigns, and the destruction of their fellowship. Other famous versions end earlier. We will not smuggle those endings into this story as though they were the same book.

Long before the band takes shape, the novel opens with a sealed hall. An imperial official visits a religious precinct, ignores the warnings attached to a forbidden place, and has it opened. From it escape one hundred and eight spirits. In the world of the story those spirits will find human lives. The premise casts a shadow over every later attempt to classify the outlaws: are they criminals, heroes, destined beings, or all three? We are not asked to settle that question before meeting them.

The political world into which they emerge is a Northern Song court where office can be purchased by talent of the wrong sort. Gao Qiu begins as a gifted player of the kicking-ball game, a man who can amuse powerful people. Patronage carries him upward until he commands military affairs. From there the novel gives his power a human consequence. His adopted son desires the wife of Lin Chong, a military instructor. Lin Chong's skill and discipline cannot protect his household against a courtier's appetite. A trap arranges for him to enter a forbidden hall carrying a weapon, and he is condemned. Even exile is not enough for those who want him out of the way.

On the road to his place of punishment, Lin Chong nearly dies. The person who intervenes is another man who has already learned how quickly official power can become violence: Lu Zhishen. Before he became a monk, Lu was a soldier and an impulsive defender of people being abused. He heard that a butcher was exploiting a young woman and her father, confronted him, and beat him to death. His flight carried him to a monastery. Monastic discipline and Lu's temperament proved a bad fit. He drank, fought, tore through expectations, and became famous for uprooting a willow tree through sheer strength. He is enormous, terrifying, often funny, and not always easy to praise. Yet when he finds Lin Chong helpless, he acts. He protects the prisoner against men hired to kill him.

The protection can take Lin Chong only so far. At the depot where he is assigned to watch military fodder, a fire is set to murder him under cover of accident. One snowy night he shelters in a temple and overhears the plot. The trap finally becomes undeniable. Lin kills those responsible and flees. What had begun as the attempt to live honorably inside a military institution ends with that institution expelling him. The way to Liangshan Marsh is opening, though the man arriving there does not yet wish to be a revolutionary.

Liangshan is difficult to reach. Water, reeds, channels and guarded approaches make it a refuge from ordinary police pursuit. But refuge does not guarantee justice. Wang Lun, its first chief, is cautious, jealous of stronger men and protective of his own position. Lin Chong reaches the marsh yet encounters another hierarchy whose leader fears talent. The rebels have escaped the court but brought human rivalries with them.

Elsewhere another strand begins with a convoy. Yang Zhi, a fierce warrior struggling to restore his career, must escort costly birthday gifts. The gifts represent the accumulation of wealth and favor among powerful officials. Chao Gai, an able local figure, joins with a clever scholar named Wu Yong and several companions to seize the convoy. They do not win by meeting the escort in a fair fight. Their scheme uses travel fatigue, the heat, a seller's wine and the appearance of harmless strangers. Yang Zhi's careful orders cannot prevent a deception that turns his entire assignment against him.

The robbery has consequences. Officials pursue the thieves. A county clerk named Song Jiang secretly warns Chao Gai in time to escape. That warning is an early glimpse of the novel's most consequential man. Song Jiang is no giant warrior. He is a modest official known for generosity and personal networks. People call him Timely Rain because help seems to come from him when they need it. He understands the bureaucracy well enough to serve it, and the system's corruption well enough to help those who evade it. He lives between two loyalties long before he becomes the chief of the outlaws.

Chao Gai and the robbers reach Liangshan. Wang Lun does not wish to receive them. Wu Yong recognizes that Lin Chong has his own grievances against their narrow-minded host. The old chief is killed in a violent change of leadership, and Chao Gai takes command. The marsh is no longer merely a hideout for a few fugitives; it is a growing alternative power. But the means of founding it are not clean. The novel makes an alliance of people injured by authority through conspiracy and murder, then lets that alliance call itself righteous.

Meanwhile we meet Wu Song, whose name becomes inseparable from a tiger. A strong and headstrong traveler, he drinks too much at an inn and ignores warnings about a beast on the road. On a mountain path the tiger attacks. Wu Song kills it with his bare hands after his staff fails him, and fame follows. A smaller story might end with the feat. *Water Margin* uses it as the entrance to a much darker life.

Wu Song visits his brother, Wu Dalang, who is physically unimpressive and married to Pan Jinlian. Pan Jinlian begins an affair with Ximen Qing. The lovers poison Wu Dalang and attempt to cover the death. Wu Song discovers the crime and takes revenge. He kills the people responsible and gives himself up to the authorities. There are readings of this episode that celebrate brotherly loyalty and punishment of a murderer. Yet the same narrative also turns the woman at its center into an object of accusation and spectacle. We should not mistake the book's excitement about vengeance for a settled moral judgment that every modern reader must share.

Punishment again turns into further violence. Wu Song is sent away, finds a patron whose interests he serves in a violent contest for a tavern, and is betrayed by another set of powerful men. He survives an ambush at Flying Cloud Pool. Then comes the killing at Mandarin Duck Tower, where the revenge expands beyond those most directly responsible. A man who once seemed to defeat a monster on a mountain has become capable of monstrous excess himself. His path to outlawry is not simply the fall of an innocent man. It is also the story of what his anger learns to permit.

Song Jiang's fall is quieter at first and no less irreversible. The woman Yan Poxi discovers a letter linking him to the wanted Chao Gai. When she threatens to expose him and demands concessions, their quarrel ends in her death at his hands. He too becomes a fugitive. His network of friends repeatedly saves him from capture or punishment, but each rescue ties him more tightly to people outside the law. In exile and then in imprisonment at Jiangzhou, he keeps trying to imagine a place for himself in official society. One night, drinking at a riverside tower, he writes a poem that officials interpret as seditious. The man of letters is sentenced to die.

The execution ground brings out the band. The rescue is noisy, fast and bloody. Men rush in, disguise is broken, weapons flash, and Song Jiang is carried away from a public death. Among the rescuers is Li Kui, the Black Whirlwind, a fighter of astonishing strength whose response to obstruction is often to strike harder. He is loyal to Song Jiang with an intensity that can be moving. He is also capable of killing bystanders while consumed by the energy of a fight. In him the outlaw fellowship's promise and danger appear together without much disguise.

Song Jiang reaches Liangshan. Chao Gai remains its chief, but the newcomer draws men by reputation as well as friendship. The novel now starts to grow outward in many directions. We meet strategists, river fighters, archers, professional soldiers, hunters, monks, thieves, wealthy landowners and servants. Each arrives with his own grievance or adventure; some join voluntarily, others because the group has cornered them. The effect is cumulative. One hundred and eight is not just a number of names to memorize. It is a series of demonstrations that the state's frontier between honored soldier and hunted criminal can move beneath a person's feet.

The story's women also complicate the fellowship. Sun Erniang and her husband operate an inn whose grotesque hospitality includes violence against travelers. Gu Dasao takes decisive action to rescue relatives imprisoned by officials; she plans rather than waiting for men to act for her. Hu Sanniang is an accomplished warrior captured in the fighting around Zhu Family Village and married to the short, predatory Wang Ying by arrangement of the outlaw leadership. The title of brotherhood is not an assurance that the organization respects women as equals. The novel can grant a woman tactical brilliance in one scene and make her fate a transaction among men in another.

At Zhu Family Village, the growing Liangshan force encounters a fortified local power. Its defenses are designed to frustrate an open assault. One attack fails; another brings unexpected reversals. Wu Yong must use infiltration and an understanding of local divisions to overcome what strength alone cannot do. The third attack succeeds only after alliances and disguises change the balance. The story becomes, for a time, a lesson in collective warfare. It is no longer a band of famous individuals doing famous things. They are learning how to coordinate reconnaissance, military force, local intelligence and deception.

The growth that makes them stronger also makes their future more difficult. Liangshan has enough fighters to defeat government commanders, and it repeatedly recruits the very men sent to suppress it. Huyan Zhuo arrives with formidable cavalry and a system of linked armored horses; later he joins the marsh. Guan Sheng, associated with the heroic legacy of Guan Yu, begins as an imperial commander and ends in the outlaw ranks. The band defeats the state by acquiring the state's skills. But once the organization resembles an army, the question becomes sharper: does it wish to overthrow the dynasty, replace the men at its head, or win permission to serve it?

Chao Gai's death brings a change of leadership. During an expedition against Zeng Family Village he is struck by an arrow and later dies. His death is tied to the enemy commander Shi Wengong, whom the band now seeks to defeat. Song Jiang assumes command in practice, but a condition attached to the old chief's death complicates succession: whoever captures the enemy deserves the leading place. Wu Yong draws into their world a wealthy martial gentleman named Lu Junyi, the Jade Unicorn. A sequence of manipulations and betrayals destroys Lu's secure standing at home. After imprisonment and danger, he becomes another great recruit. He eventually captures Shi Wengong. The fellowship has gained a heroic deputy and settled the matter of vengeance, but it has also shown how its own schemes can ruin a man in order to recruit him.

The last members are gathered. A stone inscription reveals the names and places of the one hundred and eight stars. There are thirty-six Heavenly Spirits and seventy-two Earthly Fiends. Song Jiang becomes chief, Lu Junyi his deputy, with Wu Yong among those who organize the ranks. The men swear loyalty to each other. Their hall is the Hall of Loyalty and Righteousness. The language matters. They do not present themselves merely as enemies of the court; they claim to preserve virtues that the officials have failed to honor.

Yet Li Kui can hardly bear the talk of pardon. To him, the palace is full of men who deserve overthrow, not supplication. He has a practical argument as well as an emotional one. When the court sends negotiators, some of its representatives are contemptuous and corrupt. Offers collapse amid insults, mischief and distrust. Liangshan defeats imperial armies, including forces under Gao Qiu, the same official whose patronage helped drive Lin Chong from lawful life. A reader could reasonably expect the band to turn those victories into a new political order. Song Jiang thinks differently. He believes the emperor and the wicked men around him can be separated. If the sovereign sees the outlaws' true devotion, perhaps they can come home.

That difference is not a minor disagreement between timid and bold personalities. It shapes the meaning of everything the band has done. To Li Kui, brotherhood means refusing a humiliating return to the men who hunted them. To Song Jiang, the ultimate confirmation of brotherhood is to turn their strength into loyal service recognized by the throne. He wishes to remove the stain of outlawry. And to remove it, he must accept the authority of the state that assigned the stain in the first place.

The pardon finally comes. The outlaws surrender their separate standing and are enrolled to fight on behalf of the court. For a moment the campaign seems to vindicate Song Jiang. They take the field against the Liao, defeat formidable opponents, and return with a reputation as useful soldiers. In the extended one-hundred-and-twenty-chapter version, they also wage campaigns against the rebel leaders Tian Hu and Wang Qing. Those chapters supply more stratagems, new marvels and long marches. But the larger motion stays the same: men gathered because official injustice expelled them now prove their worth by suppressing other armed groups whom the dynasty calls rebels. The story has turned around on itself.

The final major campaign, against Fang La in the south, is a catastrophe for the fellowship. The band has survived court armies and local strongholds, but it cannot survive indefinitely as an imperial expeditionary force. Enemy defenses, ambushes and dangerous passes take men whom earlier chapters taught us to admire. Some die through extraordinary acts of courage. Others are lost in confusion, disease, water or accidents of siege warfare. There is no single duel whose victory can restore the original number. The list of the living grows shorter with every march.

A man who was once almost indestructible, Wu Song, loses an arm and does not return to the same life. He later remains at a Buddhist monastery. Lu Zhishen, who once tore up a willow tree, comes to the end of his journey near the Qiantang River. Hearing the river's tidal sound, he recognizes the fulfillment of an earlier religious saying and dies seated in meditation. A warrior's death has become a spiritual departure. Yan Qing, the resourceful attendant and companion of Lu Junyi, sees that official reward may carry danger and slips away before the final honors. The novel presents several kinds of exit: death on campaign, withdrawal into religious life, and the deliberate choice to disappear.

At last Song Jiang returns with the survivors. The state distributes ranks and rewards. What remains of Liangshan is smaller than the titles make it appear. Worse, the men who hate Song Jiang have survived too. Court officials arrange poisonings. Lu Junyi is destroyed. Song Jiang receives poisoned wine. He recognizes too late that loyalty has not protected him from the very intrigues to which his companions were vulnerable at the beginning.

Before his own death, Song Jiang sends for Li Kui. He fears that when Li Kui hears he has been murdered, his loyal friend will raise a violent rebellion and disgrace the name they have worked to cleanse. So Song Jiang gives him poisoned wine as well. The act is one of the novel's most difficult endings to interpret. The chief who once led men out of execution grounds kills the follower who loves him, not because the follower has betrayed their bond, but because he will be too faithful to it. Li Kui accepts the coming death. Brotherhood survives in a form that is almost impossible to separate from obedience.

The tragedy continues after their bodies are gone. Companions such as Wu Yong and Hua Rong die at Song Jiang's grave. The emperor is later made to confront a dream of wronged loyal men and orders posthumous recognition. The book's machinery of imperial justice acts when there is little left on earth to save. It supplies monuments, honors and a memory of loyalty. It cannot reassemble a fellowship of one hundred and eight living people.

We have traveled from a sealed chamber of released spirits to a hall of ranked heroes, then from that hall into the service of an emperor, and finally into the grave. There is no simple lesson that all rebels are good or all rulers wicked. Some outlaws murder the defenseless. Some officials try to help. The novel gives friendship real power while showing how easily collective force becomes predatory. It makes official honor a cherished hope and a lethal trap.

Consider the snowy temple where Lin Chong discovers his officers intend to burn him. At that point, justice seems to require leaving the government behind. Consider the moment when Song Jiang insists that the band must return and receive the emperor's grace. There, justice seems to require entering the government again. The movement between those two convictions is the central tragedy of *Water Margin*. The men who come to the marsh cannot find a safe place in the system that made them outlaws. Their leader cannot imagine a final legitimacy outside that system. The result is a magnificent assembly of lives and a question still open when the last honor has been proclaimed: what was their loyalty for?

## Text and evidence companion (not spoken)

### Edition and narrative boundary

Original whole-arc retelling of the **120-chapter Yuan Wuya tradition**, including Tian Hu and Wang Qing as part of the expanded pardoned-outlaw campaigns. The story does not claim that every modern printing or translator preserves this ending. The **100-chapter** version omits Tian Hu and Wang Qing; **Jin Shengtan's 70/71-chapter recension** ends before the pardon and replaces the long aftermath with an alternate dream. Chinese text witness is the chapter-indexed 120-hui text on Chinese Wikisource; its textual variants were not independently collated with a critical print edition. The romance is fiction based on a historical Song Jiang tradition, not a factual account of 108 documented biographies. The narration is an original prose overview, not a translation.

### Plot locators

- 120-chapter prologue and chapters 2–11: release of spirits; Gao Qiu; Lu Zhishen, Lin Chong and the snowy temple.
- Chapters 14–21: birthday-gift convoy, Wu Yong, Chao Gai, Liangshan coup, Song Jiang and Yan Poxi.
- Chapters 23–31: tiger, Wu Song, Wu Dalang and Pan Jinlian, Flying Cloud Pool and Mandarin Duck Tower.
- Chapters 36–43: Song Jiang at Jiangzhou and rescue, Li Kui, Gongsun Sheng and the religious register.
- Chapters 47–50 and 60–71: Zhu Family Village, Chao Gai, Lu Junyi, and the stone rankings.
- Chapters 75–82: failed negotiation, imperial campaigns against Liangshan, pardon.
- Chapters 83–110: Liao, Tian Hu and Wang Qing in the extended 120-chapter edition.
- Chapters 111–120: Fang La, departures, deaths, official rewards and poisoning.
