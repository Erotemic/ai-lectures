# The Book of Disquiet — Analysis: Personas, Fragments, and the City

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

Suppose you found several hundred pages by a dead writer. Some were typed, others handwritten. A number bore initials suggesting a planned book; others seemed related but were not clearly marked. Many had no reliable date. A few existed in more than one version. The writer had considered different people as the book's imagined author and had left instructions about revising older work without carrying them out. You could arrange the pages by the dates of composition, by subject, by the personalities to whom they were assigned, or by an impression of literary rhythm. Every arrangement would make a book, but not the same book. This is not an invented puzzle. It is close to the editorial problem posed by Fernando Pessoa's *Livro do Desassossego*, now known in English as *The Book of Disquiet*. Its history forces us to ask not only what a literary work means but where, exactly, the work begins and ends.

The most basic bibliographical facts already resist simplification. Pessoa developed the project principally in two separated periods, an earlier phase around the 1910s and a later phase from the late 1920s into the 1930s. He died in 1935 without publishing the book as a whole. An official Portuguese edition appeared only in 1982, organized by Jacinto do Prado Coelho, with collection and transcription by Maria Aliete Galhoz and Teresa Sobral Cunha. Later editors made different decisions about which pieces to include and how to order them. Teresa Sobral Cunha produced an edition distinguishing the early Vicente Guedes material from the later Bernardo Soares work. Richard Zenith assembled a different arrangement, and Jerónimo Pizarro took a more documentary and chronological approach. These are not interchangeable formats of an already fixed novel. Their changes in selection, chronology, and attribution can alter the impression of the speaker's development and the book's apparent form.

Pessoa's own idea of an author makes this uncertainty productive as well as troublesome. His heteronyms were invented writers with their own distinct commitments and literary habits. They should not be collapsed into pseudonyms that merely protect Pessoa's legal name. The early *Book of Disquiet* material is associated with Vicente Guedes, an aesthete shaped by symbolist and decadent prose. The later material is attributed largely to Bernardo Soares, an assistant bookkeeper in Lisbon, whom Pessoa described as a semi-heteronym. The term indicates a figure less thoroughly separated from Pessoa than the fully developed heteronyms, but it does not give us permission to read each fragment as an entry from Pessoa's own diary. The difference between the person who wrote the pages, the author-figure named on the page, and the voice speaking from a given fragment is central to the work.

An ordinary novel invites us to reconstruct a character from action, dialogue, and changing circumstance. Soares presents almost the inverse difficulty. We are given an extraordinarily dense record of attitudes, impressions, self-corrections, and states of feeling, but very few decisive events. He goes to work in an office, passes through Lisbon, sits at a café, observes colleagues, reflects upon dreams, and returns to the same physical surroundings. If we describe him only as a bored clerk, we miss nearly everything the writing accomplishes. If we describe him only as a profound philosopher, we miss his evasions, vanity, self-doubt, and the material arrangements that make his introspection possible. The art lies in the friction between an ordinary outward life and an inward activity that seems almost impossible to exhaust.

Consider his famous fantasy of leaving the Rua dos Douradores. The speaker imagines freedom from work and colleagues, then discovers that freedom would involve loss. His dependence on the office has made even its inconveniences part of his identity. What makes the fragment interesting is not a final decision about whether to resign. No such plot resolution is required. The speaker's mind revises its account while speaking. A reader encounters desire and its objection in the same space. That movement is a general principle of Pessoa's prose: insight is often followed by recognition of the insight's limits. The effect is not the steady accumulation of wisdom. It is an expanding awareness of how difficult it is to possess a settled feeling about anything.

The fragment is also a social scene. Vasques the boss is not an abstract demon of capitalism, nor a secret genius misunderstood by artists. He represents the routines of an ordinary commercial enterprise and is described as a person with whom the narrator has a complicated relation. The other office workers acquire a familiarity that does not disappear when Soares criticizes their unreflective lives. The clerk can claim that work prevents him from living, then understand that the people at work constitute a large portion of what he knows as life. The office offers wages, stability, repetition, hierarchy, and limited social ties. It constrains and sustains him. To appreciate that tension is to prevent the book from hardening into a simple fantasy that creative people are superior to everyone who keeps accounts.

Lisbon provides the second kind of structure. The city is particularly important in the later Soares phase, where the Rua dos Douradores becomes a repeated point of departure. The reader sees streets, windows, the Tagus, changing weather, working people, and the movements of urban daylight through a consciousness that will not stop turning those observations into imagined landscapes. The city is not merely described and then interpreted. Its physical appearances are constantly altered by the speaker's state. Light makes the same view into another view. A cloud changes the moral atmosphere of an afternoon. A familiar street may seem unreal because the person observing it cannot bring himself to inhabit it without mediation. The effect is urban but not documentary. We should not mistake it for a reliable guide to all Lisbon's inhabitants or its political economy.

There is a critical limitation to this perspective. Soares's external world is narrow, and the narrowness is shaped by his social position as well as by literary choice. He has employment and enough material security to return repeatedly to private reflection. The lives of people who clean, carry, trade, and struggle around him often appear as fleeting objects of perception. The book can make the anonymity of the city movingly visible without thereby providing a social history of everyone in it. When the narrator transforms strangers into emblems of his own moods, he risks depriving them of their independent purposes. The reader can value the sensitivity of his observation and still ask whose inward lives are left largely unheard.

There is also a danger in treating the text as an autobiography. Pessoa lived in Lisbon, worked in commercial correspondence and translation, and had an extensive literary life concealed from much of the public. Some details overlap with Soares. But the book's own phrase about an autobiography without facts is a formal proposition, not a guarantee that the writer's real biography can be directly recovered through these fragments. One could read a passage as a description of Pessoa's personal emotions, then build a psychological portrait from it. That would miss the intermediate act of creating a fictional author who is himself fascinated by self-description. The book invites identification and interrupts it, making the boundary between testimony and invention a question rather than a settled answer.

The difference between Guedes and Soares complicates another common claim: that this is the one sustained record of Pessoa's melancholy. In the earlier phase the prose often looks outward toward elaborate symbolist images and inward toward states of aesthetic estrangement. The later phase has a more specific working environment and a stronger diary-like quality, though the diary is missing the continuous days and finished retrospective design that such a form often implies. Neither phase is emotionally uniform. The writing can be ironic, exact, contemplative, contemptuous, affectionate, visually exuberant, and weary. Reducing it to depression would flatten both its craft and its intellectual conflicts. A literary narrator's account of sadness is not a medical examination, and sadness alone cannot explain the work's fascination with form.

Dreaming is frequently treated as the opposite of real life, but here it is simultaneously a method of attention and a means of escape. A fantasy can reveal what the speaker desires; it can also make desire self-sufficient and therefore prevent action. The clerk's imagined journeys sometimes deliver a richness that actual travel cannot, because the actual world has weather, money, other people, and disappointing detail. Yet a writer who believes that imagination is superior to experience must still confront the dependence of every imagined world on the sensations that prompted it. Soares tries to leave reality by transforming it into prose, but he returns to the desk and window. His art reveals an ambivalence, not an effortless triumph of inward life over outward life.

The prose style is essential to this argument. Pessoa can begin with a commonplace observation and extend it into an abstract paradox, or allow a sentence to change its apparent direction under the pressure of qualification. He can turn an ordinary sight into an elaborate sequence of comparisons, then undercut the grandeur by remembering the physical circumstances of the observer. What feels like a great philosophical discovery may be made by a man in a rented room who still must go to work tomorrow. This alternation of the sublime and the everyday provides a distinct kind of humor. It prevents the writing from becoming an uninterrupted display of solemn profundity, though some readers will find the rhetoric highly wrought. The book remains readable partly because the speaker can be skeptical of his own performances.

How should one compare it with other great modernist writing? Proust's *In Search of Lost Time* explores how memory transforms a lifetime, but its large retrospective architecture has a destination in the narrator's artistic discovery. Joyce's *Ulysses* compresses a city into one day, with an elaborate system of changing styles and characters. Kafka's fiction makes bureaucratic and existential pressures unfold through increasingly impossible situations. *The Book of Disquiet* shares an interest in unstable perception, routine, consciousness, and language, but it lacks a fixed single-day structure, a completed lifetime arc, or an agreed plot. Its basic unit is the fragment, and its cumulative effect depends on repetitions and contradictions that resist closure. A comparative reading should not try to rank these works by how neatly they meet the same definition of a novel.

One consequence of the fragment form is that readings can change dramatically with editorial placement. If a gloomy passage is placed beside a luminous description of Lisbon, the reader may perceive a deliberate contrast. If it is placed with several similarly gloomy passages, a mood begins to look like an entire philosophy. A chronological edition may cause us to notice Pessoa experimenting across decades; a thematic edition can strengthen the illusion of a consistent persona speaking from an established world. Neither effect is wholly invented, but neither can be treated as an unmediated authorial command. Editing here becomes interpretation with material consequences. The scholar must read handwriting, decide what belongs, identify versions, and make unavoidable choices about sequence.

That does not mean all possible editions are equally persuasive. The manuscripts and typescripts provide evidence: markings, paper, variant drafts, dates where available, and attribution notes. The LdoD Archive at the University of Coimbra makes this visible by offering facsimiles and transcriptions alongside multiple scholarly arrangements. A reader can compare how the same piece has been assigned and placed. This is a profound opportunity for literary study because it turns the printed book from a natural object into a historical construction. A responsible edition does not conceal its decisions. It gives a reader a way to understand how the text was constituted and what the editor could not finally know.

Translation adds a further layer. English readers often encounter the work through a translation shaped by a particular edition's selection and order. The intensity of Pessoa's prose depends on rhythm, register, word choice, and the distance between lyrical language and everyday commercial vocabulary. A translator must make literary decisions even before considering the fragments' sequence. We should be cautious about attributing a striking English phrase to the author as though its exact wording were available to the reader independent of mediation. In an audio overview, long translated extracts are especially tempting because they appear to carry their own emotional authority. But a responsible lecture can describe the form, situations, and questions in newly written prose while referring interested listeners to the identified editions and the Portuguese witnesses.

What is left of personal identity in such a book? Soares repeatedly watches himself thinking, then doubts the position from which he watches. A person who can be both the observer and the observed becomes harder to describe as a single stable object. But the book is not only a philosophical treatise about the self. The difficulty of stable identity is also enacted materially by Pessoa's shifting author figures and the posthumous history of the pages. The fictive author watches his own consciousness; the historical author invents a figure who can do the watching; editors later determine which pages belong to that figure; readers assemble another temporary portrait as they move among the fragments. The instability is present at every level, from sentence to archive.

One could approach this as a failure of completion. Pessoa did not produce a definitive manuscript ready for print; subsequent generations had to do work the author never finished. That is historically true. But the resulting reading experience has a distinct artistic force. The book seems able to start again at nearly any point. A fragment that has no narrative sequel can still change the meaning of fragments read before it. It is a work in which incompletion is both an editorial fact and an aesthetic condition, though we should not confuse the two by claiming the author deliberately left every uncertainty to create a perfect masterpiece. Admiring the finished effect does not erase the practical contingency of its survival.

We can end with the modest scene of a person walking from an office into a city. Nothing in the external description requires a grand novel: a salary, a superior, a rented room, a street, the light over buildings. Yet Pessoa's fragments disclose the magnitude of what can happen between noticing something and explaining why it matters. They also ask what is lost when a person remains forever an observer of a life he cannot bring himself to enter. The book's refusal to resolve that tension is not an instruction to live as Soares does. It is an invitation to inspect the habits by which we turn the world into stories about ourselves. *The Book of Disquiet* is neither a guide to happiness nor merely a record of despair. It is a sustained experiment in what prose can reveal when experience refuses to settle into a single life story.

## Text and evidence companion (not spoken)

### Scope

Original analysis of the unfinished *Livro do Desassossego*: Pessoa's fictive authors, Guedes and Soares phases, work and observation in Lisbon, dream, self-description, editorial construction, fragment form, and translation mediation. The argument does not claim a single authorized text or ordering and does not quote a protected modern English translation.

### Identified textual/documentary anchors

- The early **Vicente Guedes** author figure and symbolist/decadent *Grandes Trechos*; later **Bernardo Soares**, a semi-heteronym associated with the assistant bookkeeper of Rua dos Douradores. See Casa Fernando Pessoa's period and authorship records.
- Direct **Soares office/departure fragment**, witnessing simultaneous longing for freedom and regret for colleagues: University of Coimbra LdoD Archive [Fr071.a](https://ldod.uc.pt/reading/fragment/Fr071.a/inter/Fr071_a_A_WIT_ED_CRIT_P), with editorial crosswalk.
- **1982 edition:** Jacinto do Prado Coelho (organization/preface), Maria Aliete Galhoz and Teresa Sobral Cunha (collection/transcription). Subsequent editions differ in scope/order: Sobral Cunha, Zenith, Pizarro; see scholarly archive.
- **Aesthetic topics:** first-person voice, portrait of Lisbon, office as material condition, dreams, stylistic self-qualification, fragments without authorial final order, and an edition's effect on literary interpretation.

### Limitations

No definitive authorial ordering exists, and scholarly editions disagree about fragment inclusion, attribution, and chronology. The analysis is synthetic critical prose grounded in public academic/institutional scholarship and a located primary Portuguese fragment; it is **not** a complete independent collation of every fragment in each edition. A fictional persona's claims are treated as literary discourse, not direct biographical testimony or clinical material. All historical and textual dating claims are sourced to the actual manuscript/edition scholarship, not to the imaginary experiences of Soares.
