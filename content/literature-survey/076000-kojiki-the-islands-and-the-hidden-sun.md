# Kojiki, Part I: The Islands and the Hidden Sun

Selected connected narratives from the first volume of the *Kojiki*: divine emergence, Izanagi and Izanami, Yomi, the birth of Amaterasu, the heavenly cave, and Susanoo's serpent battle. The text was compiled in 712 CE, so this is an early-medieval Japanese text about mythic time.

At the beginning there is no island on which a king could stand. Heaven and earth are first becoming separate, and deities appear before the familiar features of the world have taken shape. Some emerge and withdraw from view. Their names, relationships, and successive generations fill the opening of a book that does not begin like a modern novel. It is building a lineage for the world itself. This is the *Kojiki*, the Record of Ancient Matters, compiled in Japan in 712 of the common era. Its earliest pages move in mythic time. They do not describe eyewitness accounts of the formation of the islands. They ask how the visible land, the gods who inhabit it, and the ruling families who later claim authority can be placed within one remembered order.

Among the divine figures are Izanagi and Izanami, often described as the male and female who invite. They are given a jeweled spear and stand on the floating bridge of heaven. When they stir the water below, drops fall from the point and make the first island. They descend and establish a dwelling around a great pillar. Their union is told through a ritual of circling and greeting, but an initial attempt goes wrong. They consult higher deities, repeat the procedure in a different order, and begin to produce the islands of Japan. The story attaches ceremony and relationship to geography. The shape of a coastline is not an empty object here; it belongs to a sequence of births.

The two then produce further deities associated with the living world's materials and forces. The birth of the fire deity proves fatal to Izanami. She dies from the burns, and Izanagi mourns with anger. His grief produces more violence: he kills the fire deity, from whose blood and body still more beings arise. The tale does not present creation as a single peaceful act after which everything is settled. Every change can release another force. Birth leads to death, death to vengeance, vengeance to further births. Izanagi cannot accept the loss and sets out to retrieve Izanami from the land of the dead.

He finds her in Yomi, the underworld, and pleads with her to return. She tells him that she has eaten the food of that place and must seek permission from its powers; above all, he must not look at her. Izanagi waits, then breaks the prohibition. When he produces light, he sees the terrible state of her body. He flees in horror. She sends pursuers after him, and the chase becomes a contest over the boundary between the living and the dead. At last he blocks the entrance with a great stone. Across the barrier they exchange bitter words. Izanami threatens to take many human lives each day; Izanagi answers with the birth of more. The separation of life from death has become permanent, but not without an ongoing contest between them.

Izanagi returns to purify himself after contact with Yomi. The cleansing produces deities, culminating in three figures of exceptional significance. From his left eye comes Amaterasu, the radiant sun goddess. From his right eye comes Tsukuyomi, associated with the moon. From his nose comes Susanoo, a stormy and difficult figure. The tale links cosmic illumination to purification after death. Amaterasu is given rule over the high plain of heaven; her brother Susanoo, instead of settling into the domain assigned him, weeps and cries for his lost mother until the land is disturbed. He becomes a problem the world has not yet solved.

Susanoo seeks to visit his sister before his departure. Amaterasu suspects he intends to seize her realm. To test his claim that he comes without treachery, they undertake an oath-making contest that produces further divine children. The result does not end the conflict. Susanoo's conduct grows increasingly destructive. He ruins fields and sacred places; in one episode a flayed horse is hurled into a weaving hall, with fatal consequences for someone at work there. The violence is shocking partly because it attacks labor, ceremony, and the protected space of women. Amaterasu withdraws into a rocky cave and shuts the entrance. The sun disappears. Heaven and earth become dark.

The other deities assemble at the cave to devise a response. Their answer is not an army. They set up a mirror and other objects, prepare a ceremony, and have a goddess perform a dance so exuberant that the surrounding deities burst into laughter. The laughter reaches Amaterasu inside the cave. Curious about what could cause celebration while the sun is absent, she opens the entrance slightly. She sees her reflection in the mirror and is drawn farther out. A strong deity pulls her free, and a barrier is placed behind her so she cannot retreat again. Light returns. There is something striking about the means of rescue. The beings whose world depends on the sun cannot force sunlight into existence by command; they draw it out through ritual, image, movement, and the unexpected sound of joy.

Susanoo is punished and expelled from heaven. He descends to the land of Izumo, where he meets an old couple in tears with their daughter. They explain that a vast serpent with eight heads and eight tails has taken their daughters one by one; only this daughter remains. Susanoo offers to help, but he asks for her hand in marriage. He orders strong rice wine prepared and placed where the serpent will come. When the creature arrives, it drinks from the vessels until it falls into a stupor. Susanoo draws his sword and kills it. Inside the serpent's tail he finds a remarkable sword, which he offers to Amaterasu. Later tradition associates this weapon with the imperial regalia, but the story first gives it to us as a prize extracted from a monster's body.

Susanoo's marriage and settlement mark a change in his role. The figure who disrupted heaven now participates in establishing life on earth, though the consequences of his earlier acts cannot simply be forgotten. The *Kojiki* is filled with such transformations: a parent who creates and destroys, a lost spouse who cannot be reclaimed, a sun goddess lured from darkness, a dangerous brother who becomes a serpent-slayer. Its gods are not interchangeable symbols. Their rivalries and attachments create a landscape in which subsequent figures will contend for rule.

The text's later political purpose also matters. Its preface presents the compilation as an act of preserving and ordering inherited accounts under an imperial court. A narrative that explains the births of deities and islands becomes, over its subsequent volumes, a genealogy of rule. Modern scholarship asks how court interests, oral traditions, writing practices, and earlier sources shaped this received book. Those questions cannot be solved by treating its opening as a neutral historical report. Nor should a modern reader dismiss the myths merely because they do not describe geological events. Their literary work lies elsewhere: they give a world its remembered relationships, conflicts, and sacred locations.

The story is not finished when the sun emerges or the serpent falls. The world has been formed, and its divine households have quarreled. Next the narratives will turn to a different kind of question: who holds a land already inhabited by other powers, and how does one kind of authority pass into another's hands? The cave has opened. The islands exist. The struggle over who may speak for them is only beginning.

## Text and evidence companion (not spoken)

- Controlling witness: *Kojiki* book I, selected episodes from opening generations through the Susanoo serpent story, using Basil Hall Chamberlain's 1882 translation as a historic English access witness. [Wikisource, Chamberlain translation and section index](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Kojiki_%28Chamberlain%2C_1882%29); [Wikipedia, Kojiki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kojiki) for orientation only.
- Specific episodes: the beginning of heaven and earth; seven generations; island of Onogoro; divine marriage and birth of islands; Izanami's death and the Yomi pursuit; purification and birth of the three deities; Susanoo and Amaterasu's contest; the heavenly rock cave; the eight-headed serpent and the sword. The source's romanization and euphemisms are nineteenth-century choices and should not be treated as linguistically authoritative.
- Historical context: 712 CE compilation under court sponsorship is early medieval, not the date of mythical events. [Columbia University reading syllabus on Kojiki and its formation](https://www.columbia.edu/~dbl11/Lurie-Kojiki-seminar-2017-syll.pdf).
