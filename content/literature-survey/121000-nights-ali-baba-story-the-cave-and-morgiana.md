# Ali Baba and the Forty Thieves — The Story: The Cave and Morgiana

A complete story-first retelling of the received Galland–Diyāb tale, from the woodcutting to Morgiana's final rescue and Ali Baba's family's inheritance.

Ali Baba is cutting wood in the hills when he hears a company of riders approaching. There are too many of them to be harmless travelers. Their horses are swift, their weapons visible, and they move with the certainty of men who know the road belongs to them. Ali Baba hides in a tree, hoping only to escape notice. The riders stop near a huge rock. Their captain dismounts and speaks two words that make the mountain change. A doorway opens where stone had seemed seamless. Forty thieves enter a cave, leaving their horses outside; afterward their captain shuts the entrance with another command. Ali Baba has seen enough to make himself rich, or to get himself killed. He stays hidden and waits until the last rider has gone.

The words are easy to remember: open sesame. Ali Baba climbs down and repeats them. The rock opens for him. Inside are stores of silk, rich merchandise, gold and silver, the accumulated wealth of people robbed on roads far beyond the forest. Ali Baba is a woodcutter. He does not know the victims or the robberies, but the gold itself is before him, no longer protected by its criminal owners. He fills sacks with coins, takes no more than his beasts can carry beneath bundles of firewood, commands the rock to close, and returns home. A secret that should have belonged to forty armed men now belongs to one anxious man with donkeys.

He and his wife have lived without enough money to make counting it a familiar activity. Looking at their new gold, she suggests measuring it. A scale can serve when a mountain of coins makes counting ridiculous. She borrows one from the household of Cassim, Ali Baba's brother. Cassim has married into wealth and lives very differently. His wife, suspicious about why a poor relation needs measuring equipment, puts sticky grease beneath one of the scale's pans. When the scale comes back, a gold coin is stuck to it. Her guess has been rewarded. She confronts her husband with the discovery, and Cassim, consumed by the thought that Ali Baba has kept a fortune from him, goes to demand his share of the secret.

Ali Baba would have preferred silence. But once Cassim knows enough to threaten him, the cave is no longer his private discovery. He explains the location and the command words. Cassim gathers pack animals and great sacks, dreaming of carrying away riches enough to make even his former wealth seem small. He reaches the rock, speaks the words, and enters. The treasure intoxicates him. He fills his containers with gold and paces among the hoards imagining what he will buy. Then he turns toward the door and cannot remember how to open it. He knows the command has the name of a grain, but he tries other names. In the sealed cave, each wrong word makes the treasure feel more like a burial chamber.

The thieves return. Cassim panics, rushes toward the opening as it appears, and is caught. They kill him, divide his body, and display the remains inside the entrance to warn anyone who has learned their secret. The gold has not defended him. He has acquired, for a few hours, everything he wanted, and he has lost the one thing that would have enabled him to carry it home: his memory of two ordinary words. When he fails to return, his wife goes to Ali Baba. Her envy has turned into fear. Ali Baba travels to the cave and finds his brother's body. He carries the remains out under bundles of wood and brings them home in secret. If the town learns how Cassim died, the thieves may learn who else has found the place.

Now another person comes to the center of the story. Morgiana is a capable woman enslaved in Cassim's household, accustomed to managing its practical affairs. Ali Baba entrusts her with the problem of burying Cassim without inviting questions. She does not simply mourn; she devises the necessary story. She buys medicine and speaks of her master as gravely ill, so the neighborhood will accept that he has died of sickness. She finds a cobbler who can sew, persuades him to let his eyes be covered, and leads him by a winding route to the place where the body lies. He joins the pieces so that the family can conduct the funeral without public discovery of the violence. In the bustle of mourning, Ali Baba takes charge of his brother's household, and Cassim's widow is brought into his family. The terrible marks of the cave have been made invisible to the people who live around them.

The robbers discover that Cassim's body has vanished. That tells them something far worse than a stranger's theft. There is another person who can enter the cave, and that person may reveal the secret. One thief goes into town to search. He visits the cobbler, who boasts of a remarkable piece of work he has done: sewing a dead man back together, though blindfolded. The thief offers money for a demonstration of the route. The cobbler can be led, counting steps and recalling turns, close enough to recognize the house. The thief marks the door with chalk so the gang can find it later. Their leader is pleased with this simple preparation. Morgiana sees the mark.

She does not know precisely who made it, but she knows that a secret sign on the door is a danger. She copies it onto neighboring doors until the street offers no unique target. When the thieves arrive at night, they find many houses bearing the same mark. Their mission fails. The captain punishes the scout who made the mistake. A second thief tries again using a different mark. Morgiana sees that mark too and repeats her trick. At last the captain comes himself, learns the house by careful observation, and decides he will trust his memory rather than paint. Ali Baba, who believes he has hidden his new fortune, has no idea how close the danger has approached. Morgiana understands something that his gold cannot teach him: a mark need not be a message to the person whose house bears it. It can be a message about him to someone else.

One evening a merchant arrives, asking lodging for himself and his mules. He carries many large oil jars. He is polite, apparently weary, and Ali Baba offers hospitality. In fact he is the robber captain in disguise. Most of the jars hold armed men curled inside, waiting for the captain's signal; one contains the oil that makes the cargo plausible. After the household retires, the robbers will emerge and attack. It is a simple plan, if the people within the house remain unaware of what has been brought into their courtyard.

Morgiana has work to finish in the kitchen. Her lamp goes out and she needs more oil. Rather than wake the household, she goes into the courtyard with a vessel. As she approaches the first great jar, a voice inside asks whether the time has come. The voice is low, but Morgiana does not mistake it for the sound of liquid. She instantly understands that to cry out would give the hidden men warning. She answers as though she were their captain: not yet. She moves from jar to jar, receiving the same question and giving the same quiet reply, until she finds the jar with real oil. Her next decision is dreadful and decisive. She heats that oil in the kitchen and pours it into the jars containing the armed men. When the captain comes to signal his followers, none answers. He examines the courtyard, discovers the disaster, and flees.

Ali Baba hears about the invasion only after Morgiana has defeated it. She shows him the jars and explains the hidden enemy. The man who opened the mountain could never have been more surprised by the sight of treasure than he is by the recognition that his own house had become an enemy camp. Morgiana has preserved the family through a form of attention he lacked. In gratitude Ali Baba grants her freedom. Yet the robber captain survives, and a man who has lost all his companions may be even more dangerous than one who still commands them.

After an interval the captain returns under another name, establishing himself as a merchant and cultivating the friendship of Ali Baba's son. He is eventually invited to supper. The invited guest behaves courteously but refuses food with salt. Within the customs of hospitality represented by the tale, to eat another person's salt is to accept a bond that cannot readily be reconciled with treacherous murder. The guest wishes to keep that bond unmade. Ali Baba accepts the explanation as a peculiarity of diet. Morgiana looks at the stranger's face, recognizes the old captain, and sees that he carries a hidden dagger.

A guest has entered the house, and Ali Baba is about to be killed at his own table. Morgiana does not shout that the man is an assassin, for the danger is too immediate. She dresses as a dancer and asks another servant to accompany her with a drum. She dances before the company, moving from figure to figure until she draws a dagger of her own. The weapon seems part of her performance. She turns it toward herself, then toward a spectator, withdrawing each time in the manner of a practiced dance. At the end she passes among the company to receive coins. When she comes to the robber captain, she strikes him with the blade and kills him. The household is horrified until she opens his clothing and reveals the weapon hidden there. Her recognition, not Ali Baba's generosity or his son's friendship, has prevented the last murder.

Ali Baba rewards Morgiana by arranging her marriage to his son. The tale presents this as a culmination of gratitude and advancement, following her emancipation. A modern listener may wish to know more about her wishes than the narrative allows us to hear. She has been the most observant and effective person in the household, yet her future is decided in the language of kinship and reward. That tension remains with the happy ending; it should not be erased by pretending either that she had no agency or that the institution in which she began was free.

After the captain's death, the secret of the cave is safer. Ali Baba revisits it, takes wealth carefully, and later admits his son to the knowledge of the command words. His family continues prosperous. But when we recall what made that prosperity possible, the simple phrase 'open sesame' seems almost a trick played on the listener. Ali Baba found a cave; Cassim could not find his way out; the robbers found the family; Morgiana found the signs, the concealed men, and the returning enemy. The true secret that saved the household was not the opening of a rock. It was the ability to notice what other people believed no one would see.

## Text and evidence companion (not spoken)

- Named story witness: the **Galland–Diyāb** Ali Baba tale as represented in the public-domain [*Supplemental Nights*, volume III, "Story of Ali Baba and the Forty Thieves"](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/60889/60889-h/60889-h.htm), and the [1909 Galland-family English version](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/The_Arabian_Nights_(1909)/The_Story_of_Ali_Baba_and_the_Forty_Thieves). This is an original full-arc retelling, not a claim to translate a pre-Galland Arabic text.
- Sequence checks: timber trade; Cassim's wife and the marked scale; Cassim's forgotten command and death; Morgiana's funeral fiction and blindfolded cobbler; repeated chalk marks; oil jars and Morgiana's pre-emptive action; captain's salt refusal; dagger dance; family resolution.
- Historical context: [BnF on Galland's compilation and Hannā Diyāb](https://heritage.bnf.fr/bibliothequesorient/en/thousand-and-one-nights-0); [Horta on the tellers](https://wordswithoutborders.org/read/article/2020-04/tales-of-aladdin-and-their-tellers-from-aleppo-to-paris-paulo-lemos-horta/). Cassim, Morgiana and Ali Baba are literary figures; the story does not document a particular robbery or medieval city.
