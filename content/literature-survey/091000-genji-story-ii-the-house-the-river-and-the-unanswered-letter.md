# The Tale of Genji — The Story II: The House, the River, and the Unanswered Letter

The conclusion of the complete-plot orientation. Genji's late marriage and loss give way to a generation in which the outward splendor of his court survives but the cost of desire becomes harder to disguise. The ending is told, including its refusal of a tidy resolution.

The Shining Genji has what a nobleman in the capital could spend a lifetime pursuing. A beautiful house. Family connections to the throne. Children whose fortunes rise. Attendants and visitors who give his smallest preferences consequence. He has become an expert in the politics of a world where poems, clothing, rooms, and marriages are never merely private. He has also learned how to manage several relationships as if the beauty of his arrangements might compensate for the pain of the people living within them.

Then a marriage is proposed that even he cannot dismiss. The retired emperor Suzaku wishes to secure the future of his treasured daughter, the Third Princess, and asks Genji to take her into his household. Genji is reluctant, yet her position makes the match a public honor. He marries her. She is very young and not the experienced companion that Murasaki has become. For Murasaki, this new wife is different from the other women she has endured. Murasaki has Genji's love and the standing of many years, but not a pedigree equal to an imperial princess. Formal rank enters the house and takes a place that affection cannot dislodge.

Murasaki tries to maintain her composure. She knows the practiced refinements of the court and has spent years making their life bearable. She also understands that Genji's admiration never quite frees her. She wishes to become a Buddhist nun, to withdraw from the network of desire and obligation that has governed her from girlhood. Genji will not consent. He cannot imagine life without her and treats that need as sufficient reason to withhold the choice. He loves her deeply. That fact has become a reason she cannot leave.

The new princess's own position is no refuge. Kashiwagi, a young nobleman, becomes obsessed with her after catching a forbidden glimpse. The barrier of blinds that should protect her privacy becomes a scene of exposure. He presses his desire upon her and a sexual encounter takes place under circumstances of profound inequality and coercion. She bears a boy, Kaoru. The world is told that he is Genji's son; Genji learns that he is Kashiwagi's. The pattern of the concealed child from Genji's affair with Fujitsubo returns in a painful reversal. Once Genji was the man who put another husband's household at risk. Now he must acknowledge a child whose parentage threatens his own.

Kashiwagi is consumed by dread and illness and dies. Genji can be cruel in the knowledge he now holds, even while others praise his dignity. The Third Princess wishes to renounce her worldly position, and she takes vows. Genji, who resisted Murasaki's desire to enter religious life, cannot keep this woman in the same way. He is surrounded by people whose lives have been altered by his public honors and private choices. Kaoru grows as a child of his household, bearing the name and apparent status of a son. He will later develop a religious seriousness that makes his inner life appear unlike that of the man who has accepted him.

Yugiri, Genji's son by Aoi, has his own story of desire and the complicated aftermath of Kashiwagi's death. He grows close to Kashiwagi's widow, Ochiba, despite his existing marriage. The arrangement is not a simple repetition of his father's youthful exploits; grief, responsibility, loyalty, and desire are bound together in ways he cannot control. The lives of the generation Genji raised are not clean solutions to the problems he left them.

For Murasaki, the crisis is more bodily and final. Her health declines. Genji attends to her, prays, organizes ritual, and tries to secure her survival with the attention and resources that have so often worked in the palace. She knows the beauty of the seasons and the delicacy of the ceremonies that mark them, but she also knows that beauty is a passing condition. A religious ceremony offers no guarantee against death. She dies. Genji loses the person whose company had made his extraordinary success feel like a life rather than a performance.

The following year is given to his mourning. He is still among the living, still addressed with ceremony, but nearly every sight at home returns him to someone who is not there. The dresses, the poems, and the seasonal changes that once held the world's fullness now measure absence. He destroys or puts away belongings that make memory unbearable. He thinks of religious withdrawal at last, as if the path Murasaki wanted might be one he could follow only after refusing it to her. Then the narration takes an extraordinary step. It does not stage a grand death for the Shining Genji. A chapter title meaning roughly 'Vanished into the Clouds' stands where a story of his death might have stood. The subsequent narrative knows that he is gone. The man who seemed to command the attention of a whole court has departed the page without the long public farewell one might have expected.

Time moves onward. The palace remains, people inherit positions, and a name can carry splendor after the person who gave it meaning is dead. We meet Kaoru, known publicly as Genji's son, though the reader knows the hidden fact of his birth. We meet Niou, an imperial grandson of Genji, glamorous, impulsive, and skilled at attracting attention. Kaoru has a fragrance that seems to rise naturally from his body. Niou surrounds himself with carefully chosen scents. The detail is amusing, but also precise. These young men inherit the old court's tendency to make even personal qualities into a competition. One appears thoughtful and withdrawn, the other brilliant and pursuing. Neither is free from the temptation to regard another person's life as the answer to his own longings.

The final major movement of the tale begins away from the capital, beside the Uji River. A prince who has withdrawn from court lives there in religious reflection with his daughters. His household is not rich, and its remoteness feels almost like a different moral world from the mansions where Genji made arrangements. Kaoru visits because he is attracted to the prince's seriousness. He seeks instruction, and he is drawn to the elder daughter, Oigimi. Her younger sister is called Nakanokimi. The sisters have grown up seeing how precarious their family's position is. Marriage to a brilliant young courtier might seem a rescue, but rescue can be another name for surrendering the security one already has.

Oigimi is reluctant to become Kaoru's wife. Her father has taught her to be cautious of worldly attachments, and she does not share his confidence that a marriage would make her happy. Kaoru continues to press his wish. When the old prince dies, the sisters' position becomes even more fragile. Kaoru may think he is offering protection, but his insistence becomes an additional demand on a woman who has little power to satisfy him on her own terms. He tries to redirect his attention and imagines that Niou might marry Nakanokimi. The sisters fear being separated; one proposal changes the other woman's possibilities. The emotional plot is made of acts that can be described as kindness from the point of view of their author and experienced as pressure by the person receiving them.

Nakanokimi does become involved with Niou and enters his household. His affection is real, but the court has other claims on him, including a politically appropriate marriage. She has reason to worry about becoming an attractive but subordinate attachment. Oigimi remains at Uji and grows weaker. Kaoru has not stopped wanting her. She dies. The loss does not transform him into someone without demands. He mourns sincerely, and then begins to look for ways to recover an image of what he has lost.

He learns of another daughter of the prince, a young woman born outside the recognized household and raised far from the prestige of Uji. She is called Ukifune, a name associated with a boat adrift. Her uncertain social position has already made her vulnerable to disappointing arrangements. Her resemblance to Oigimi captures Kaoru's attention. He wants her near him, perhaps even to care for her, but he also wants her to stand in for the dead woman. A person can become an object of devotion and still be scarcely seen as herself. Ukifune is frightened by the great difference between their status. Kaoru installs her privately at Uji, where his protection also makes her dependent on him.

Niou learns of her. Where Kaoru approaches with melancholy calculation, Niou moves with quick desire and a willingness to deceive. At a critical early encounter Niou gains access by presenting himself as Kaoru. Ukifune does not have the circumstances in which to choose freely what is happening to her. Yet the novel refuses to simplify her response to either man. She is drawn toward Niou's ardor and depends upon Kaoru's steadier promises. She knows that discovery would threaten her reputation, her future, and those near her. To the two men she is a beloved woman and a rival claim. To herself she has become a person trapped by choices she never set in motion.

One night Niou takes Ukifune across the Uji River in a boat. It is one of the tale's most memorable scenes: water, dark banks, a man intoxicated by the romance of secrecy, and a woman whose feelings cannot be made as certain as his declarations. The river is beautiful, but a boat is not a palace chamber. It can drift. The lover who says his love is sure has not asked what it means for the woman to be carried somewhere she cannot safely remain. Ukifune sees how little direction she can exert over her own life.

The pressure grows. Messages arrive. The two men's servants, plans, suspicions, and visits make concealment untenable. Ukifune thinks of drowning. Then she disappears. The attendants at Uji believe she has taken her own life, and their fear of scandal shapes what they tell others. A funeral is arranged without a recovered body. Kaoru and Niou grieve, each in his own way. The story does not ask the listener to treat the attendants' assumption as established fact; in fact, it is about to reveal another course of events.

Ukifune is found alive by a Buddhist cleric's party, in a state of profound disturbance. She is cared for among religious women. The memory of what has happened to her remains difficult and sometimes fragmentary, and those around her debate what she needs. Slowly she asks to leave the old life behind through Buddhist vows. Becoming a nun will not turn the past into something harmless; it may allow her to live somewhere that the two men are not entitled to enter. The narrative that once seemed to belong to Genji and then to his heirs now turns toward the possibility of a woman's refusal.

Kaoru discovers that Ukifune is alive. He wants to reopen the connection. He sends a letter with her young brother, a messenger whose presence might break through the distance she has created. In a world where an elegant letter is nearly irresistible as a claim to be answered, Ukifune refuses to return to him. She will not allow the signs of the old court to pull her back. She is distressed by the encounter, but she does not give him the reply he expects. The boy departs without the reunion the reader might have been trained to hope for.

Kaoru interprets the refusal through the habits he inherited. Perhaps, he suspects, another man is keeping her from him. It is the final misunderstanding of a tale filled with men who believe they can explain women's absences by discovering the presence of another rival. The possibility that she might have chosen absence for herself remains hard for him to accept. No narrator arrives to settle everyone's future. There is no court ceremony that heals the separation, no marriage in which the competing claims are reconciled, and no promise that a life of renunciation abolishes suffering.

The Tale of Genji began with the death of an imperial favorite who could not be protected by the love of a powerful man. It ends with a woman who survives by going where those powerful men's love may no longer govern her. Between those scenes lie estates, poems, marriages, children, fragrant robes, political triumphs, acts of tenderness, coercion, grief, and faith. The book's great achievement is not that it tells us which of these people got the life they deserved. It lets us hear their wishes pressing against each other, then declines to make the wish of its most powerful man the final word.

## Text and evidence companion (not spoken)

**Scope.** Final portion of the received *Genji monogatari*: chapters 34–41 (Genji's late life); 42–44 (transition); 45–54 (Uji narrative). This is a continuous original retelling, not a claim to reproduce individual chapters or translate the Japanese text. The blank/untold space associated with “Vanished into the Clouds” is not presented as a scene of Genji's death. Ukifune's survival and refusal at the end are indispensable, not an optional sequel.

**Primary/secondary orientation.** [UVA Japanese Text Initiative, 54 received parts](https://jti.lib.virginia.edu/japanese/texts/MurGenr/index.html); the Metropolitan Museum of Art, [*The Tale of Genji: A Japanese Classic Illuminated*](https://resources.metmuseum.org/resources/metpublications/pdf/The_Tale_of_Genji_A_Japanese_Classic_Illuminated.pdf), including plot summaries and literary reception; [*The Tale of Genji: A Visual Companion*](https://www.jstor.org/stable/j.ctvc7754r), chapter images and explanatory summaries; [Taleofgenji.org summary](https://www.taleofgenji.org/summary). All are records of text/transmission, not verification of fictional biographies.

**Narrative checkpoints.** The Third Princess marriage, Murasaki's wish for vows, and Kashiwagi (34–36); Kaoru's birth and the Third Princess's withdrawal (36); Yugiri/Ochiba (37–39); Murasaki's death and Genji's mourning (40–41); the successors (42–44); the Eighth Prince, Oigimi and Nakanokimi (45–49); Ukifune, Niou and the river (50–51); disappearance, rescue, vows, refusal and unresolved ending (52–54). English chapter titles vary considerably by translator.

**Textual caution.** Critical interpretations of consent, spirit possession, and Ukifune's disappearance are identified as readings of narrated events, not historical diagnoses or character testimony invented here. The ending is open, but the refusal is in the received text; there is no legitimate “happy reunion” ending to the story as transmitted.
