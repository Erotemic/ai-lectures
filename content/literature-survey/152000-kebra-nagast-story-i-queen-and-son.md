# Kebra Nagast - Story I: The Queen, the Water, and the Son

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

A merchant returns to Ethiopia with an account of a king who is very difficult to impress. His name is Solomon. He rules in Jerusalem, his court is splendid, and he understands matters of trade, government, and worship so thoroughly that people travel great distances to ask him questions. The merchant, Tamrin, has seen the king's building projects and his methods of administration. He has also watched how Solomon talks to workers and strangers. When Tamrin reports all this to his own ruler, Queen Makeda, he is not merely bringing news of a foreign palace. He is offering her a new idea of what a monarch might learn.

This is the first movement in the Ethiopian Kebra Nagast, or Glory of Kings, a book whose main story reaches across the Red Sea and whose argument reaches across the Bible. The Queen of Sheba appears briefly in the biblical First Book of Kings, but that text does not name her Makeda, give her a son, or describe an Ethiopian royal succession. The Kebra Nagast does all three and more. We are going to follow its narrative, as the book tells it, without treating the book's claims about Solomon's age as independently established history. It is a medieval Ethiopian Christian work about an ancient past, and it knows the difference between a kingdom's wealth and a kingdom's right to call itself blessed.

Before the journey, the book spends considerable time establishing its frame. It invokes earlier biblical generations, the covenants given to Noah and Abraham, the chosen people, and the sacred object called Zion. In its language, Zion often means the Ark of the Covenant, the tabernacle or dwelling of divine law, and sometimes a heavenly pattern to which that earthly object corresponds. An assembly of learned Christian fathers speaks in the opening pages. Readers hoping to plunge immediately into an adventure may find the beginning indirect. But its purpose is clear: when the Ark eventually moves, we are not to see the movement as an accidental theft of expensive furnishings. We are to understand that the book believes the world's sacred order can move with it.

Makeda, meanwhile, has a perfectly practical reason to travel. Tamrin's account has convinced her that Solomon possesses the kind of wisdom her kingdom could profit from. She gathers attendants, offerings, and costly goods and sets out to meet him. Her journey is not presented as a helpless woman's attempt to find a powerful man. She is a ruler who has made a choice, commanded preparations, and left her own land because she wants knowledge. Solomon receives her with ceremony and begins to explain the arrangements of his court. He shows her how he decides matters, and she listens closely. The scenes are a meeting of royal minds before they become anything else.

Solomon also speaks about the God he serves. Makeda has inherited forms of worship that the book judges inadequate. In Jerusalem she hears a new account of the maker of heaven and earth and of a law older and higher than any ruler. The book makes her change of allegiance a central event: she is drawn toward the God of Israel through the teaching of the king. For a medieval Christian author, this is the foundation beneath the later ancestry story. The queen's journey is intellectual and religious as well as geographic. Knowledge leads to worship; worship will later be made to lead toward the ownership of Zion itself.

Yet Solomon is no abstract teacher. He has desires of his own. He admires the queen and wants her to remain with him. The narrative shows him calculating how to bring her into his bed. At a feast he has her given food prepared to make her thirsty. When she grows ready to retire, she insists on a condition: Solomon must swear not to take her by force. He agrees, but demands a condition from her in return. She must swear not to take anything from his house by force. She laughs at the idea. Her own realm has riches, and she did not cross the sea in order to steal his property. She agrees to the bargain.

They sleep in the same chamber on separate beds. Solomon has left water where the queen can see it, and he pretends to sleep. The feast has done its work. She wakes with a dry mouth, rises quietly, and reaches toward the water. Solomon seizes her hand. He declares that she has broken her oath by taking something of his. She asks whether drinking water can really count as robbery. He insists that water is a possession of the highest value. To obtain it, she releases him from his own promise. They sleep together, and the story's dynastic future begins.

It is important to tell that sequence accurately. The book calls Solomon wise, and its argument depends on his extraordinary authority. But the scene also describes deception and coercion. Makeda had explicitly tried to secure freedom from force. Solomon contrives the circumstances in which she must surrender that protection in order to drink. Calling the encounter a romance would leave out the mechanism the narrative itself supplies. We can follow what the work says while still hearing the imbalance of power in the bargain. The text's reverence for Solomon should not silence Makeda's earlier words.

During the same night Solomon dreams of a brilliant sun that illuminates Israel and then moves away toward Ethiopia, where it remains. He waits for its return, but it does not return. The dream is an image of the transfer the book will eventually narrate. The king who thinks he can direct every circumstance has been given a sign that he may not control where glory dwells. The imagery will deepen later when the Ark leaves Jerusalem. For now, Solomon has no knowledge of how this could occur. He has the dream and the approaching departure of the queen.

Before Makeda goes, Solomon gives her a sign by which a child of their union might be recognized: a ring. She leaves for her own country and gives birth to a son. The child's name is rendered in different forms in different translations; Bayna-Lehkem is one form associated with the figure later called Menelik. In the story he is brought up in Ethiopia, where the people notice his resemblance to Solomon. The queen does not simply send him to Jerusalem as a gift to his father. He grows into a young man of his own will. The question of whom he resembles becomes a question of where he belongs.

He asks about his father and seeks permission to travel. Makeda has reason to hesitate. If Solomon recognizes this son, will the rich and powerful court draw him away for good? The queen has made her own country his home and her own throne his likely inheritance. She sends him with the sign that can identify him and with instructions meant to ensure his return. There is another story taking shape inside the original one. Makeda once traveled to acquire wisdom; now her son travels to discover the father whose wisdom made his own birth possible. But the second journey threatens to repeat the first at a cost to the mother.

The young man arrives in the region of Jerusalem and is met by people who cannot ignore his likeness to the king. Word reaches the court that a visitor looks as though Solomon himself has returned to youth. Recognition grows before any formal proof is produced. When father and son meet, the king's joy is intense. The ring confirms what the face has already suggested. Solomon embraces the young man and treats him as his own. The book gives this moment genuine warmth. Yet that welcome leads directly into a conflict. Solomon wants his son to remain in Jerusalem, close to the father's power and the sacred inheritance of Israel.

The son refuses. His home is Ethiopia, and his mother is waiting. He is not a wandering prince in search of a kingdom; he already has one to return to. Solomon can make promises about honor and wealth, but he cannot make the young man forget the queen who raised him. The disagreement has a sharp human shape. Each parent has a legitimate claim of attachment, but the son cannot live in both courts. A journey begun to verify a relationship has become an argument about what the relationship should require. The narrative sides with the return, but not without making departure painful.

Solomon then adopts a strategy of his own. He orders that the firstborn sons of the nobles of Israel, and representatives of the priestly families, go with his son to Ethiopia. If the young king will not stay in Jerusalem, Jerusalem will send a court with him. The youths are distressed. Many have parents, places, and lives they do not wish to leave. Their fathers grieve too. This part of the book is easy to overlook if one remembers only the spectacular voyage of the Ark, but the relocation begins with living people. The new kingdom is to be supplied with counselors and clergy, and the cost of founding it falls on families whose consent is not the story's chief concern.

Among those sent is Azariah, son of the priest Zadok. The young companions receive instructions concerning the law, worship, royal dignity, and their duties to the ruler they will serve. Solomon has the son consecrated as a king and gives him another royal name, David. Names and relationships overlap as they often do in dynastic literature: the person called Menelik in later tradition is also the Ethiopian David of this narrative. The ceremony is meant to give an Ethiopian ruler a public place within the inherited story of Israel. But what the young travelers carry is not yet the thing that will change the world.

At night, Azariah has a dream. An angel commands him to prepare sacrifices and remove Zion, the Ark, from its dwelling. The instruction is presented not as an invitation to enrich Ethiopia by stealing a valuable object, but as the will of God, who has judged Israel and chosen another place for the sacred presence. Azariah wakes and tells trusted companions. Their preparations must be secret because the departure cannot be openly negotiated with Solomon or the priests. They are to ask permission for worship at the sanctuary, then follow the angel's direction. The reader hears an astonishing claim: a sacred object protected by priests and king is going to leave by a plan that those guardians did not authorize.

And there is a second irony. Solomon's own policy has made this possible. By insisting that the best-born sons of Jerusalem go to Ethiopia, he has supplied the future travelers and the priests capable of carrying out the plan. His efforts to honor the son also remove the human custodians of the object he treasures. The Kebra Nagast does not make Solomon foolish. It makes him wise enough to act effectively, yet unable to see the meaning of his actions. The sun in his dream has already gone east and south. The king can arrange ceremonies, oaths, and departures, but he cannot bring back a light that the story says God has chosen to move.

For Makeda, far away, these movements are still invisible. She knows the child has gone to meet his father. She does not yet know what he will bring back or how his return will change her court. For Solomon, the city prepares to say farewell to a son he wishes would stay. For Azariah and his companions, the journey has become dangerous before it begins. And for the reader, the central question is no longer only whose son this young king is. It is where the center of a sacred history can be located. The Ark's departure from Jerusalem will become the book's central claim about the location of sacred authority.

## Text and evidence companion (not spoken)

### Scope and source boundary

Original English narrative overview of the received *Kebra Nagast*, principally chapters 1-48. The 1922 public-domain English translation by E. A. Wallis Budge is the named textual witness. The narration does not purport to translate Ge'ez or verify its antique dynastic claims. The encounter in the water scene is accurately identified as manipulation and coercion; this is not silently recast as a mutually chosen courtship. The name Menelik and the forms Bayna-Lehkem/David are kept textually distinct.

### Episode locators

- Biblical-theological frame, Zion and ancestral covenants: chapters 1-20.
- Tamrin's report and Queen Makeda's departure: chapters 21-25.
- Wisdom, worship, the feast and oath: chapters 26-30.
- Water, dream, parting, sign and child's birth: chapters 30-34.
- Recognition in Jerusalem and decision to return: chapters 35-38.
- Consecration, law and sons of the nobles: chapters 38-45.
- Azariah's dream and removal plan: chapters 45-48.
