# The Song of Ullikummi: The Stone Raised Against the Storm

An original narration of the damaged Hittite-language witness to a Hurrian Kumarbi myth (*CTH 345*), following the extant tablet sequence without presenting a reconstructed closing victory as fully preserved text.

The enemy of the storm god is growing out of sight. He has no sword and no army, and he does not need to learn the names of the gods he will threaten. He is made of stone. Each day the stone grows taller. It stands in the sea and rises toward the sky, rooted on the shoulder of a being older than the struggle for divine kingship. What the gods are about to face is not a warrior who can be persuaded, frightened, or drawn into a contest of honor. It is a monument that advances by growing.

The story is known as the *Song of Ullikummi*. It is preserved in Hittite on clay tablets from the world of Hattusa, the capital of the Hittite kingdom in Anatolia. Yet the divine family and much of the plot belong to a Hurrian tradition carried across languages. The text survives in pieces; a modern telling has to distinguish its visible course from the places where broken tablets require caution. The names may be unfamiliar, but the rivalry is immediately recognizable. A displaced father wants to undo the reign of the son who replaced him.

The father is Kumarbi, a god associated with an earlier generation of divine rule. His rival is the storm god Teshub, also written in transliteration as Teššub, lord of the city Kummiya. Earlier songs in the cycle describe struggles over kingship among the gods. Ullikummi's story opens in that atmosphere of old defeat and new plotting. Kumarbi will not accept that the throne belongs securely to Teshub. If a divine army could not restore his position, perhaps he can create a different sort of challenger.

Kumarbi leaves his ordinary place and goes toward a vast rock beside the water. The myth describes an act of generation with the rock, making this origin as unsettling as the child it will produce. There is no human mother waiting in a chamber, no nurse measuring the infant. A rock gives rise to a stone being made for conquest. Kumarbi calls the child Ullikummi, a name associated within the story with crushing Kummiya, the city whose god he intends to overthrow.

He imagines the future before the new being can speak. Ullikummi will climb into the territory of the gods, subdue Teshub, break his support, and bring down the divine order that Kumarbi resents. These are wishes of the father, not proof of what will happen. The song allows us to hear the entire purpose of the creation before the other gods even know the danger is there. A child comes into being already carrying someone else's revenge.

To keep his weapon hidden while it grows, Kumarbi calls the Irshirra deities, divine attendants who can carry the stone child away. They take Ullikummi toward the dark region beneath the familiar world. There they set him upon the shoulder of Upelluri, or Ubelluri in another spelling, the vast primordial bearer of heaven and earth. This ancient support is not joining Kumarbi's conspiracy. He does not initially notice the new burden being placed upon him. He is older than the quarrels that now unfold above him.

The image has two scales at once. To the Irshirra attendants, they are placing one body on a giant's shoulder. To those living in the world above, that body will soon become an enormous feature of the horizon. The child grows at an extravagant rate, rising through the sea until water that once might have concealed him surrounds only the lower part of his body. The stone keeps rising. Above the sea, it becomes a presence against the sky.

The sun god sees the danger and brings warning. Teshub and his brother Tashmishu must take it seriously. They cannot dismiss the news as an ordinary rivalry among powerful deities, for an object is appearing that seems beyond their accustomed weapons. With the goddess Shaushga, associated with Ishtar in Mesopotamian terms, the brothers climb Mount Hazzi to look out toward the sea. From the mountain they see Ullikummi.

Teshub's first encounter with the stone is the encounter of a king with something his kingship may not be able to contain. It is immense. It does not salute him. It has no message he can answer. Shaushga's response follows one familiar path of divine action: if force is dangerous, perhaps allure and music will work. Her effort cannot succeed in the usual way. The monster is stone. He does not respond as a person would respond to singing, beauty, or invitation. The song gives us the failure of another kind of power before the storm god tries his own.

Teshub calls upon the forces attached to his rule. Storm, wind, thunder, and the armed strength of his companions belong in this contest. Yet the assault does not accomplish what he requires. Ullikummi withstands the attack and continues to threaten the gods. In this portion the surviving witnesses are damaged, and the precise order of every movement cannot be narrated as though the tablets were complete. What is clear is the failure of straightforward confrontation. The storm god has met an enemy that storm cannot bring down.

The consequences reach into his household. The goddess Hebat, Teshub's consort, is cut off and threatened as the monster's massive presence crowds the divine places. Messages must carry news between separated gods. Tashmishu becomes more than a companion in battle: he is the brother who can speak and seek help when Teshub's own power has proved inadequate. The song's action shifts from the mountain where Ullikummi was seen to the search for a different kind of answer.

The answer comes by way of Ea, a god of wisdom and technical resource whose name and role also connect this story to the broader ancient Near East. Ea's insight is not that the stone giant needs a larger thunderbolt. It is that the gods have misunderstood why Ullikummi is so difficult to defeat. The stone is not simply standing in the sea by its own strength. It has been placed on Upelluri's shoulder, attached to a support older than the present world order. If that connection remains, ordinary combat may never reach the real source of the problem.

Ea goes to the ancient bearer. Upelluri's position is astonishing: heaven and earth rest upon him, and yet he did not perceive all the quarrels taking place among their inhabitants. Now the presence of Ullikummi has become something he can notice. The strain or alteration to his burden reveals what the younger gods did not understand. There is a point of contact under the stone, and the location of that contact matters more than the visible height of the monster.

The problem calls for a tool from the earliest time. Ea asks that an implement be brought out from its old keeping place: the cutting instrument by which heaven and earth were once separated. That primal act of division has made an ordered world possible. Now the same implement will be used to divide a newly created threat from its ancient foundation. The solution is striking because it is not a stronger version of the storm god's power. It belongs to another order of knowledge, the knowledge of how the world itself was put together.

Ea and the gods seek to cut Ullikummi away from Upelluri's shoulder. The surviving text describes the ancient cutter and the intended severing; damage interrupts parts of the ensuing action. Later fragments indicate that the stone's power has been undermined and that Teshub can challenge the monster again. They do not give us a fully continuous, undamaged account of the final blow in every modern reconstruction. The listener deserves to know where the confidence of the story comes from and where it ends.

Even with those gaps, the shape of the myth is powerful. Kumarbi thought to defeat his rival by making a being that cannot be negotiated with. He concealed the being beneath the world so that it could grow before anyone was ready. He chose a support whose strength did not belong to either side of the current divine quarrel. Yet that same dependence becomes a weakness. The monster is terrifying because of its foundation, and vulnerable once the foundation is understood.

The story also changes what victory means. If we read only the opening threat, the future seems to belong to whoever can produce the strongest child. If we read the surviving response, the gods who remain in power survive by discovering a hidden relation. Teshub cannot solve this with a storm. Shaushga cannot solve it with song. Ea brings out an object that belongs to the creation of the world and finds the join that must be cut. The decisive action is not merely striking. It is knowing where to strike.

This myth is often discussed alongside Greek stories about succession among the gods. There are resemblances between the conflict of Kumarbi and Teshub and later accounts of older gods displaced by younger ones; there are also comparisons between the stone challenger and monstrous opponents of Zeus. But resemblance does not let us draw an exact line of borrowing for every scene. We know the Hittite written version and can study its Hurrian background. We do not possess an uninterrupted recording of every performance or every step of transmission into other traditions.

What survives is enough to make Ullikummi a character unlike many human epic enemies. He does not boast that the gods should fear him. He does not speak a persuasive claim for the throne. His will is largely his father's design, built into him before he ever appears on the horizon. The stone's silence is part of the terror. No appeal can change a program of conquest that does not listen.

Think of the gods on Mount Hazzi seeing the shape in the water. The storm god looks toward an enemy that is getting taller while he watches. A war between divine generations has produced a geological kind of fear: something enormous and impersonal, something that changes the world around it simply by continuing to exist. When the story sends Ea down to Upelluri, it takes us below that surface. Power above depends on structures below. Before the king can regain his security, someone must understand the support that made the threat possible.

The broken tablets leave some gestures and speeches beyond our recovery. They do not erase the central imaginative achievement: a son of stone raised to overthrow the sky, a storm god unable to bring it down, and an older tool sought for a cut made at the root of the monster's strength. *The Song of Ullikummi* asks what happens when a regime tries to defeat the future by manufacturing an unbeatable thing. Its surviving answer is that no force is self-explanatory. Even a stone giant has a place where it stands.

## Textual basis and source notes (not spoken)

- **Primary textual boundary:** The Hittite-language *Song of Ullikummi*, CTH 345, in the Hurrian Kumarbi cycle, preserved on damaged tablets from Hattusa. CTH numbers and tablet/column organization used as locators; the original Hurrian antecedent is not preserved as this complete Hittite composition.
- **Scholarly witnesses:** Hans Gustav Güterbock, “The Hittite Version of the Hurrian Kumarbi Myths: Oriental Forerunners of Hesiod” (1948), reprinted in *Perspectives on Hittite Civilization*, pp. 39–48: https://oi.uchicago.edu/sites/default/files/uploads/shared/docs/as26.pdf ; Gary Beckman, “Hittite Literature,” in *From an Antique Land* (2009), https://deepblue.lib.umich.edu/bitstream/handle/2027.42/83244/Hittite%20Literature.pdf?sequence=1 ; Harry A. Hoffner, *Hittite Myths*, 2nd ed. (1998), especially *Song of Ullikummi* (CTH 345).
- **Story locators:** Tablet I, Kumarbi's revenge, conception and Irshirra attendants; tablet II, the growing stone, Sun god's warning, Mount Hazzi, Shaushga and storm god; tablet III, Ea's consultation with Upelluri and the primordial cutting implement; fragmentary concluding confrontation.
- **Editorial boundary:** Whether the surviving fragments permit a fully detailed final victory is disputed. No missing speech, final coup, or event inside a lacuna is supplied as text. Details are independently paraphrased, not transcribed from Hoffner's or Beckman's copyrighted translation.
- **Orientation:** https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ullikummi ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kumarbi .
