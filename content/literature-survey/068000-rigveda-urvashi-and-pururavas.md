# Ṛgveda: Urvaśī and Purūravas at the Parting

A verse-bounded literary narration of the alternating voices of Ṛgveda 10.95, without importing the full later romantic legend into the older hymn.

A man speaks to someone who is already leaving. He asks her to stop, to listen, to let their words meet while they still can. The woman refuses. She has gone like the first light of morning; she is as difficult to hold as the wind. There is no long prologue introducing their childhoods or how they first encountered one another. We have entered a conversation after the decisive event. Two voices remember an intimacy that has ended, and neither can make the other speak the words the other needs.

The speakers are Purūravas and Urvaśī, figures of a story that later Sanskrit tradition will develop into a much fuller romance. In *Ṛgveda* 10.95, however, we have eighteen verses of a dialogue hymn. Urvaśī is a celestial woman associated with the *apsarases*, and Purūravas is a mortal king or hero. Readers of later plays and ritual prose may know the conditions of their union, stolen sheep or lambs, a flash of lightning, and a separation caused by the sight of the man naked. Those details belong to other recorded tellings and should not be smuggled back as if this hymn stated the complete later plot. We will listen first to the voices the early poem actually preserves.

Purūravas begins by asking for speech. The request is both modest and desperate. He cannot require her to return, so he tries to keep her in conversation. His words suggest that things left unspoken have brought them no joy. The first movement of the hymn therefore makes speaking itself into an action. Nothing has been resolved; there are no witnesses who can impose a verdict. One person asks the other to remain in a shared space made of words. The answer is separation. Urvaśī tells him to return home. She will not stand still for his wishes.

The poem moves between voices in a way that can be difficult for a modern listener. Images of arrows, speed, cattle, lightning, and lost companions flash past. Some words have generated different readings among translators, and the speakers are not always identified by an explicit name at the opening of each verse. The received tradition assigns them in alternation. The effect is a quarrel interrupted by memories, not a tidy narrated account. Each voice remembers the same relationship from a position the other cannot occupy. Purūravas is stranded in the wish to repeat what has ended. Urvaśī speaks as someone whose leaving is a fact rather than a question.

Their former life was not without pleasure. The poem recalls closeness and sexual union. Urvaśī speaks of yielding to Purūravas's desire, but the terms of their recollection do not create a simple idyll in which every moment was mutually understood. Her voice can sound reproachful. She says that she had tried to teach or warn him and he had not listened. He experiences abandonment; she remembers limits he did not recognize. No amount of poetic beauty should make that asymmetry disappear. This is one of the reasons the hymn holds listeners: the two speakers are not simply a couple waiting for the author to reunite them.

Purūravas recalls the women who accompanied Urvaśī, names that move through the verse like a vanished company. Their appearance reminds us that she was never merely one human bride to be enclosed in his household. She belongs to a divine world with companions, movements, and relationships of its own. The language of the hymn can associate these women with bright cattle and dawn-like motion. Its metaphors are not instructions to treat every image as a separate event in a realistic landscape. They convey that the realm toward which Urvaśī goes cannot be held back by the claims of one mortal man.

The dialogue turns toward the child born of the union. A son is mentioned, and the language of paternal care and future separation enters the quarrel. The existence of a child does not immediately reunite the parents. Instead it sharpens the cost of the break. The man who has lost his companion also confronts the shape of his responsibility as a father, while the woman is not simply reduced to the task of providing continuity for his royal line. The text's allusions are compressed. Later literature will supply names, circumstances, and a more orderly account of their son. The hymn gives us the emotional problem before it gives us that narrative completion.

Purūravas speaks wildly of what may happen if he is abandoned. He imagines destruction and the loss of a body, and the poem approaches a death wish. Urvaśī responds with stern restraint. He should not die from this grief. A mortal must continue living even when a divine companion has left. This refusal to join his despair is easy to misread as lack of feeling. It can also be heard as the hardest boundary she has set: she will not allow his threatened self-destruction to become a means of drawing her back. The hymn does not tell us to approve every claim either speaker makes. It asks us to hear how longing and freedom collide.

The end does not simply repeat the first rejection. Urvaśī offers words about divine rites and a possible relation to the world of the gods. The concluding verse points Purūravas toward sacrifice and a heavenly future. Translators differ about some of the compressed language, but the motion is clear enough: the conversation closes without a straightforward restoration of their former household life. A different kind of possibility is named, one that belongs to ritual order and life beyond the present quarrel. The mortal cannot force the celestial woman to return by insisting that their earlier happiness ought to bind her forever.

For listeners who know Kālidāsa's later drama *Vikramorvaśīyam*, or the version in the *Śatapatha Brāhmaṇa*, this older poem may feel like a set of fragments. Later authors tell how Urvaśī and Purūravas came together and why they parted. The stories are worth telling in their own right. But calling the Vedic hymn an incomplete draft of the later romance puts the centuries in the wrong order. A dialogue poem can assume that an audience knows a myth and use only the charged moment it needs. It can also leave us with questions whose answers were never part of its artistic purpose.

The name Urvaśī stays with the poem's opening image: a presence impossible to hold like the wind. Purūravas responds as a person who hopes the right words can reverse time. When she speaks of his failure to listen, the argument turns inward. Perhaps the problem is not merely that he does not know how to persuade a departing lover. He has not grasped what she has already told him about the difference between their kinds of life. The hymn's emotional reach comes from a simple human situation, made more severe by a divide between mortal and celestial existence. One voice asks to be heard. Another answers and goes. The conversation is the surviving scene, and it is enough.

## Source companion (not narrated)

- Primary text: *Ṛgveda* mandala 10, hymn 95, verses 1–18. [University of Texas, Karen Thomson and Jonathan Slocum's metrically restored Vedic text](https://lrc.la.utexas.edu/books/rigveda/RV10); [Sanskrit, speaker assignments and public-domain Griffith translation](https://vmlt.in/rigveda/10/095).
- Important boundaries: opening request and refusal (10.95.1–2); recollections and companions (3–11); child and separation (12–14); death anxiety and counsel (15–17); sacrificial/heavenly possibility (18). Exact translations and some voice attributions are interpretive; the sequence is not a continuous prose romance.
- Scholarly context: Stephanie W. Jamison and Joel P. Brereton, *The Rigveda: The Earliest Religious Poetry of India* (Oxford University Press, 2014), hymn 10.95. Later *Śatapatha Brāhmaṇa* and Kālidāsa versions have distinct episodes and should be compared separately.
- Orientation: [Wikipedia, Pururavas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pururavas) and [Urvaśī](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Urvashi). Later narrative details are not silently backdated to the surviving Vedic hymn.
