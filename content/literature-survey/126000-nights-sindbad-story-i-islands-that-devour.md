# Sindbad the Sailor — Story I: Islands That Devour

The complete frame and first four voyages in two-part story-first narration, following the named Laurence Housman 1907 literary retelling. The fifth through seventh voyages and return to the frame are in the next lecture.

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

A poor porter walks through Baghdad under a load that seems to grow heavier with every street. His name is Sindbad. He stops outside a great house where servants hurry past and a feast is being served. The smells reach him through the doorway: roasted meat, spices, sweet things, all the good food he has struggled to buy. He thinks of the man who owns this house, another Sindbad, a merchant whose wealth has become almost proverbial. The porter speaks of the unfair division of hardship in the world. Why should one Sindbad carry burdens for a living and the other possess a mansion full of comforts?

The wealthy man hears him, or hears of his complaint, and invites him inside. He does not rebuke the porter for speaking plainly. He places him among guests and announces that the money on display did not arrive without suffering. He offers to tell the story of his seven voyages, one on each of seven days, so his namesake may decide whether the rich man's fortune was acquired cheaply. The sailor speaks to a man who has every reason to doubt him, and the first voyage begins not on the open sea but in the pleasures of the same city where they both live.

Sindbad the Sailor's father had left him a fortune. Young Sindbad spent it with little thought for what would remain. When the money was almost gone, he sold what he could, purchased goods and left Basra with merchants whose ships traveled beyond familiar ports. They stopped on many islands, buying and selling. One day they found an island so green and welcoming that the sailors went ashore to cook. They lit fires on its grass and began preparing a meal. Then the land shook. The captain shouted for everyone to return to the ship. The island was not land. It was an immense sleeping creature, a whale or fish whose back had collected earth and plants. The heat of their fires had awakened it.

As the creature submerged, the shore vanished. Sindbad flung himself into the water while the ship escaped. He seized a wooden object that had fallen into the sea and clung to it through the night. When he finally reached a real island, he was exhausted, alone and stripped of the property he had intended to sell. Strangers cared for him. He learned of the king who ruled the place and gained a position at court, showing the politeness and practical sense he had scarcely exercised in Baghdad. Then a ship arrived carrying goods labeled with a merchant's name: Sindbad. The captain thought the owner drowned at the false island. Sindbad recognized his own cargo, identified himself and recovered it. He returned to Baghdad richer than when he departed, having survived a world that can disguise a living creature as a safe place to stand.

He might have stopped. He enjoyed his home, gave gifts and entertained friends. But luxury soon lost its power to satisfy him. So he set out again. On the second voyage the ship called at another island, and Sindbad lay down beneath the shade of trees. The sailors sailed without him. He awoke to an empty horizon. There was food and water, but no human companion and no evident way to leave. While searching he found an immense white dome with no entrance. The sky darkened. A gigantic bird came down upon the dome and settled there to brood. Sindbad realized he was looking at the egg of the legendary roc, a bird said to carry elephants in its talons.

Using the cloth of his turban, he fastened himself to the bird's leg. At morning the roc rose, lifting him high enough that the landscape became an indistinct pattern beneath him. It landed on a mountain plateau and flew away with an enormous serpent. Sindbad was left beside a deep valley glittering with diamonds. He could have stepped on precious stones without searching for them, but the valley was also inhabited by giant snakes. Riches lay where no ordinary trader could safely go. Sindbad remembered a story about merchants who threw fresh meat into inaccessible diamond valleys. Stones adhered to the flesh, birds carried the pieces up to their nests, and the merchants drove away the birds to gather the jewels.

He stuffed diamonds into his clothes, fastened himself to a piece of meat, and waited. A huge bird carried the meat, and Sindbad with it, out of the valley. The merchants who came to recover their treasure were astonished to find a living man in the nest. He shared his strange adventure with them, traded among their company and returned home with wealth that seemed almost as implausible as the roc's wings. But the most important possession he had taken from the valley was not a diamond. It was the knowledge that the merchants' method, devised for extracting stones, might also carry a person out of death.

He set out a third time. A strong wind brought his vessel to an island where hostile creatures swarmed aboard, drove away the sailors and left the travelers stranded. Seeking shelter, the men entered a huge building. Its owner was a monstrous giant with a single terrifying eye. The creature inspected the terrified merchants as though choosing food, seized their captain, roasted him and ate him. The survivors had found a lodging place that was a slaughterhouse. Sindbad urged them to prepare rafts before they tried resistance. They heated long iron spits in a fire and drove them into the sleeping giant's eye. Blinded and roaring, he rushed from the building.

The men reached the sea and launched the rafts. The giant came to the shore led by another of his kind. Stones smashed the boats and killed most of the fugitives. Sindbad and two companions escaped to another island. There they found a new terror: a serpent large enough to swallow a person whole. One man disappeared into its jaws as the others watched. The next night another climbed a tree with Sindbad, but the serpent followed and took him as well. Alone, Sindbad prepared an absurd-looking defense by lashing planks around his body so the snake could not open its mouth wide enough to swallow him. Through the night it lunged and twisted against the wooden barrier, unable to reach the flesh inside. At dawn he was still alive. A passing ship took him aboard, and he found himself again among merchants, goods, and the familiar miracle of a return to Baghdad.

The fourth voyage began with a storm that ruined his ship, followed by a danger less easy to recognize than a giant. Sindbad and his companions reached an island whose people fed the survivors strange food. The hungry sailors ate it eagerly. Soon their senses failed and their hosts began fattening them, intending to eat them. Sindbad refused enough of the food to keep his reason and escaped when the opportunity came. After traveling through the countryside he found men gathering pepper, who helped him reach an ordered city where he could speak to a king and make himself useful.

The people of this city rode their horses without saddles. Sindbad proposed making a saddle and showed local craftsmen how to construct one. The king was delighted, rewarded him, and asked him to teach the craft to others. Sindbad acquired wealth and married a woman of high standing. He had once entered the land barely escaping a cookpot; now he had a household and the favor of a ruler. Yet danger returned through the institution that had most firmly rooted him there. A neighbor's wife died, and the man began weeping as though he too were condemned. In that city, a living spouse was buried in the tomb with the dead partner. Sindbad protested against such a practice but could not change it. Soon his own wife fell ill and died.

He was carried with her to the place of burial. He descended into a deep cavern with the body and a small provision of bread and water. The opening was sealed above him. What the king had presented as a marriage blessing had become a death sentence. Other bodies lay in the darkness. When another funeral lowered a living person beside a dead spouse, Sindbad did a terrible thing to survive: he killed the newcomer and took the provisions that would have kept that person alive. He repeated the act when need drove him, accumulating both supplies and ornaments from those buried there. The tale does not make his conduct easy to admire. It forces us to see what the demand to live can do in conditions where society itself has arranged the trap.

At last he heard a sound in the cavern, followed it through the darkness, and found that an animal had been entering and leaving through a passage toward the sea. He crawled after it and emerged into daylight. A passing ship carried him away, along with jewels he had collected among the dead. Returned to Baghdad, he distributed gifts and resumed his pleasant life. He spoke as though he had learned never to leave home again. Yet his listeners knew he had promised seven voyages, and he had told only four. The sailor would still have to explain why a man who had survived a moving island, a flying bird, a giant's table and a living grave would ever step aboard another ship.

## Text and evidence companion (not spoken)

- **Named narrative witness:** [Laurence Housman, *Sindbad the Sailor & Other Stories from the Arabian Nights* (Hodder & Stoughton, 1907), illustrated by Edmund Dulac](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/47285/47285-h/47285-h.htm), First through Fourth Voyages. Housman is a modern literary retelling, not a direct 9th-century or medieval Arabic manuscript or the sole canonical text.
- **Related earlier translation:** [Richard Burton, *Book of the Thousand Nights and a Night*, volume VI](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/58722/58722-h/58722-h.htm). Plot details vary; this story maintains Housman's broad sequence and important end beats rather than splicing incompatible passages into an invented original.
- **Narrative and ethics:** The whale/fish island, roc/diamond valley, giant and serpent, island of forced feeding, saddle invention and live burial are literary adventures, not empirical evidence for foreign societies. A listener's ethical unease at Sindbad's violence in the burial cavern is addressed again in 128000.
