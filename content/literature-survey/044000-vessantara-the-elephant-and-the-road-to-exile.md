# Vessantara: The Elephant and the Road to Exile

The first of two original narrations of Jātaka no. 547, the *Vessantara Jātaka*, using the identified Pāli commentary narrative represented by the Cowell/Rouse English translation, volume VI. The work is a Buddhist account of the perfection of giving; this part follows the frame, miraculous birth, contested gift, banishment, and forest home.

A prince gives away a white elephant, and an entire kingdom turns against him. The animal is magnificent and valuable, but its value is not just the beauty of its tusks or the wealth attached to a royal stable. It is believed to carry the blessing of rain. People have watched their crops grow under the sign of its presence. Then visitors from a drought-stricken country come and ask for it, and Prince Vessantara gives it to them. From the visitors' point of view, he has made a gift capable of saving their fields. From his own people's point of view, he has carried away a public good that was not his to dispose of without consequence.

The story of Vessantara is Jātaka number 547, one of the longest and most celebrated birth stories of the Buddha. Its frame is set during the Buddha's teaching life, when a marvelous rain prompts the telling of an earlier existence. The former birth is not offered as an ordinary biography recoverable through palace archives. It is the story of generosity pressed toward its farthest limit, with consequences for the people whom the giver loves.

Long before the elephant is led away, the tale surrounds Vessantara's arrival with signs and vows. His mother Phusatī is associated with acts of giving in an earlier existence, and with boons granted before she is born as a queen. Her son, the future Vessantara, shows a desire to give from childhood. This is not a late conversion after he sees misery for the first time. The poem makes the impulse part of his identity and then asks what will happen when his identity collides with his obligations.

Vessantara marries Maddī. They have two children: a son, Jāli, and a daughter, Kaṇhājinā. Their home is a royal household, with all the comforts and dangers that entails. The prince can open stores and distribute goods; he can protect petitioners who arrive at his gates. But he also lives under the expectations of a city that counts upon its ruler to maintain the resources entrusted to the realm.

When the request for the auspicious white elephant comes, Vessantara does not bargain. The elephant has been linked to rain and prosperity, and the petitioners have come from a place that desperately needs both. He gives the animal, along with its equipment and ornaments, in the manner of a lord who makes gifts ceremonially and without hesitation. It is a triumphant act for the suppliants. They have obtained something no ordinary transaction could buy.

News spreads. The people of Vessantara's own kingdom are outraged. The elephant was not a spare ornament that could be removed from the treasury without loss. The prince has given away the sign of a people's welfare. The citizens demand that his father, King Sañjaya, punish or expel him. The king is torn between affection for his son and the demands of the realm. The tale gives the public a political voice. Their anger is not merely a comic misunderstanding invented to make Vessantara look more generous.

Sañjaya announces that Vessantara must leave. The sentence is exile, a removal from the city and its privileges. Vessantara accepts the punishment but asks for one last day in which to give. He proposes a vast distribution of wealth: animals, vehicles, precious things, and attendants, assembled in numbered groups. The famous gift of the seven hundreds is both spectacular and troubling. It measures a generosity so sweeping that even people and labor can appear within the list of things a ruler transfers.

Maddī hears that her husband is to depart. She does not agree to remain behind in safety. When told that a forest life will be hard, she insists that hardship shared with her husband is preferable to a comfortable separation. The children will go as well. This choice matters because she is not merely carried off by the plot as the name of the prince's wife. Her resolve gives the family its first shape in exile. At the same time, the years of risk ahead will not be distributed evenly among them.

A city that once received Vessantara's gifts watches the family depart. The scene is ceremonial and sorrowful. The king's parents grieve, the streets are full, and people see a family leaving a house built for them. Vessantara turns to look back at the palace. The earth trembles in the Buddhist tale, making the violence of separation visible on a cosmic scale. The road ahead is real road, but also the path of an ethical experiment that will strip away every remaining possession.

The first petitioners meet them soon after departure. They ask for the horses drawing the chariot. Vessantara gives the horses away. Supernatural deer take their places for a time, but this relief does not last. Another visitor asks for the chariot itself, and Vessantara relinquishes it too. The family continues on foot, carrying the children. In the received narrative even trees bend down to offer fruit to the little ones. Marvels surround their journey, yet the everyday hardship of walking with tired children does not disappear.

The family comes to a mountain wilderness, often called Vaṅka. The text describes a path through villages and forests, encounters with people who ask where they are going, and the eventual approach to a remote hermitage. By divine assistance a dwelling has been prepared for them, but it is not a palace in disguise. Their life is one of gathering fruit and roots, making simple provisions, and keeping watch over the children among animals and trees.

Maddī takes responsibility for much of the daily work. At dawn she goes to gather what the family needs. Jāli and Kaṇhājinā wait and play near the dwelling. Vessantara lives in the forest as an ascetic, no longer distributing royal treasure to queues of petitioners. For several months the new life has a fragile rhythm. The children know where their mother will return each evening, and the family makes a home in a place that is supposed to test its attachment to home itself.

That rhythm is what makes the next request so difficult. Far away, an old brahmin named Jūjaka lives with a young wife, Amittatāpanā. The story gives their household its own awkward chain of circumstances. Jūjaka has lost money entrusted to another family, and the daughter given to him in settlement now demands help with domestic labor. Her treatment by other women and her humiliation at the river become pressures she passes on to him. She wants servants. Jūjaka has no means to purchase them, and hears of a prince who refuses no petitioner.

He sets out to find Vessantara. The route is long; men and beasts, forest dwellers and guardians, all stand between him and the hermitage. Yet he persists, not because he seeks the salvation of the realm or the feeding of the hungry, but because he wants children to work for his household. His request will bring an entirely different meaning to the prince's cherished word, gift.

The text has spent many pages making Vessantara's generosity admirable. It has also placed other people's lives in the path of that generosity. The elephant belonged in some sense to a kingdom. The chariot carried a family. Maddī has chosen hardship beside her husband, but the children have not chosen what they are about to face. When Jūjaka approaches, the story's greatest moral difficulty no longer lies in whether the prince can bear to lose another treasure. It lies in whether a father's commitment to giving can make his children into possessions he is entitled to give away.

That question is not one to be resolved before the scene occurs. We leave the family in the forest, with Maddī gathering food and the children close to the dwelling, while the old man comes nearer. The future Buddha's perfection of generosity will be tested not by wealth but by the deepest attachments of a household. The narrative has already shown why listeners might admire Vessantara. It now prepares us to see why others might be horrified.
