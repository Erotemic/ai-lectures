# Adventures of Huckleberry Finn — Complete Story: The River and the Choice

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

There is a boy who has been told that a respectable life will make him good. It will require clean clothes, school, regular meals, prayers, and the ability to sit still in a parlor. He is not persuaded. His name is Huckleberry Finn, and his favorite things are usually things respectable adults disapprove of. Yet the freedom he wants for himself is small beside the freedom another traveler will seek on the Mississippi River. That man is Jim, enslaved by a woman who has taught Huck religious lessons. Their journey becomes one of the most unsettling adventures in American fiction, because Huck will be asked to choose between what he has been taught is right and what he has come to know of another human being.

Mark Twain's *Adventures of Huckleberry Finn* begins where *The Adventures of Tom Sawyer* left off. Huck and Tom have found a fortune. Huck's share is held for him by Judge Thatcher. The Widow Douglas has taken him into her house and is trying to make him respectable. Her sister, Miss Watson, corrects his speech and manners and tells him about heaven and hell. Huck understands neither the attraction of sitting in heaven with a harp nor the reason that a grown person's account of goodness should be trusted. But he does understand kindness, at least when he meets it. He likes the widow better than the rules surrounding her. And he likes the excitement Tom Sawyer promises when the boys organize a gang of robbers, although Tom's splendid criminal plans mostly consist of making everyone play at being figures from adventure books.

Huck's own father is a darker presence. Pap Finn is a violent drunk, furious that his son can read and has money. He sees education as an insult and the boy's property as his to seize. Huck tries to protect the fortune by putting it in the judge's hands, but no legal arrangement can make Pap safe. Pap takes him to a cabin across the river and keeps him shut inside. At first Huck likes living outdoors again, fishing and avoiding lessons. Then the beatings and drunken rages grow worse. The boy recognizes that if he stays, his father may kill him. Neither blood relation nor official authority offers him the safety it claims to provide.

He devises his own escape. He finds a canoe, stocks it with supplies, and makes the cabin appear to have been the scene of a murder. A slaughtered pig supplies blood; marks on the floor and other misleading traces suggest a terrible struggle. Huck slips away to Jackson's Island, which lies in the river near the town of St. Petersburg. Soon boats are searching for his body. He hears cannon fired over the water in the hope that a corpse will rise. He has made himself dead in the eyes of the town, and for a little while he enjoys the strange protection of not existing.

But another fugitive has chosen the island. Huck finds the remains of a fire and then Jim, the man enslaved by Miss Watson. Jim is frightened to see a supposedly murdered boy standing in front of him, until he understands the trick. He has fled because he overheard Miss Watson discussing the possibility of selling him downriver, away from everything and everyone he loves. The words make the danger real. Huck's flight from his father and Jim's flight from enslavement meet in the same patch of woods, but they are not equivalent. Huck can hope eventually to return as a mischievous boy. Jim knows he may be hunted, captured, punished, and separated permanently from his family.

They make a camp and learn how to live together. They fish, build fires, watch the weather, and move their belongings when the river rises. Jim understands some things Huck does not, including the habits of the water, while Huck is quicker at certain small tricks. Both are superstitious, sometimes comic, sometimes fearful. Huck's carelessness with a dead rattlesnake leads to a living snake striking Jim; the boy has to confront how an apparently harmless joke can injure the person sharing his shelter. They discover a floating house carried by the flood. Inside is a dead man. Jim sees his face and stops Huck from looking. Only near the end of the novel will Huck learn why.

The island cannot protect them indefinitely. Huck goes ashore disguised as a girl to learn what people are saying. The woman he visits quickly sees through his disguise, but he obtains the news he needs. Townspeople suspect Jim of Huck's murder. They are coming to search the island. Huck runs back to warn him. The two leave in haste, taking to the raft that will become their moving home. At night they drift with the current; by day they try to hide. They will need provisions, luck, and a way to avoid people who see an escaped enslaved man not as a person in danger but as property to be recovered.

The raft is rough and exposed, but on it they establish a companionship unavailable to them on shore. They can talk through the night, argue, sleep, share meals, and watch the stars. Huck has always taken pride in knowing when grown people are talking nonsense, yet he is no more reliable than they are. He repeats conventional opinions without examining them. He teases Jim over stories of kings and the biblical Solomon, then mistakes Jim's skepticism for ignorance. Jim has not had Huck's schooling, but schooling has not taught Huck to recognize how foolish he can sound.

The river offers moments of temptation that reveal their differences. A wrecked steamboat, the *Walter Scott*, sits stranded in the current. Huck, trained by Tom's extravagant adventure tales, wants to explore it. Jim understands that a wreck is dangerous and that being discovered by the wrong person could be disastrous. Huck climbs aboard anyway and overhears criminals arguing about what to do with a companion they have turned against. The boys' make-believe robbers have suddenly become real ones. Huck is appalled when the men contemplate leaving a victim to die. Even as he escapes, he tries to arrange a warning that might bring help. The episode matters because the impulse that makes Huck reckless also gives him flashes of imaginative sympathy. He can picture the fate of a man he does not like.

Life on the raft is not a sustained conversation about noble principles. It includes cold meals, wet blankets, cramped shelters, and arguments that go nowhere. Huck has read about French kings and asks why different peoples speak different languages; Jim points out difficulties in the explanation Huck offers. Huck treats his own bafflement as proof Jim is wrong. Later they will argue over whether a particular king's proposed solution to a dispute could possibly be wise. Their talk is funny because each thinks the other is missing something obvious. But it is also the sound of two people making a life together, in circumstances where much of the country believes one of them should have no right to choose his own conversation.

At one point the river swallows them in fog. Huck paddles ahead in the canoe to secure a line; the current tears the raft loose. He and Jim call to each other through the white darkness and lose their bearings. Huck spends a terrified night trying to find his friend. When he finally reaches the battered raft, Jim is asleep from exhaustion. Huck sees an opportunity for a prank and claims there was no separation. Jim first wonders whether he dreamed the whole disaster. Then he sees the broken oar and leaves caught on the raft. He tells Huck how frightened he was, how overjoyed he felt when the boy came back, and how cruel it is to turn such fear into entertainment. Huck is ashamed. He apologizes, something he has been trained to think degrading when addressed to a Black man. He remembers that apology long afterward. It is one of the first moments when the book asks us to notice not merely that Huck likes Jim, but that Jim can hold Huck accountable.

Their destination is Cairo, Illinois, where the Ohio River joins the Mississippi. If they reach that point, they hope to travel upriver into free states. Jim speaks of finding work, buying his wife's freedom, and bringing his children out of slavery too. The plan frightens Huck, who has learned to understand the freedom of an enslaved person as a loss inflicted on an owner. Even the thought of Jim's children being freed sounds to Huck like a threat to someone else's property. He feels as though he is becoming a thief. He does not yet have words for the fact that his moral education has reversed the meaning of right and wrong.

He nearly turns Jim in. When men searching the river ask what sort of person is on the raft, Huck has his chance. But he improvises a lie that the man aboard is his sick father and suggests a contagious disease. The searchers keep their distance. Huck returns to Jim, uncertain why he has done the thing he will be told is sinful. He feels bad when he considers betraying Jim, and bad again when he considers not betraying him. That confusion is the novel's central pressure. Huck is capable of an act of loyalty before he is capable of explaining it.

The river frustrates their plan. In the darkness and confusion they pass the place where they should turn toward freedom, and afterward a steamboat strikes their raft. Huck is swept away. He reaches shore alone and finds himself among the Grangerfords, a wealthy family who receive him with generous hospitality. He gives a false name, George Jackson, and is treated like a guest. The Grangerfords have handsome rooms, fine clothing, and a dead daughter's sentimental paintings and poems. Huck admires them. Their domestic elegance seems almost unreal beside the crude cabin of his father.

But they are locked in a deadly feud with the Shepherdsons. Both families carry guns to church; both speak of honor. Huck befriends Buck Grangerford, a boy near his own age. Buck cannot tell him what began the conflict. It has lasted so long that the original cause scarcely matters. Buck's sister Sophia secretly plans to run away with Harney Shepherdson, a young man from the rival family. The lovers' departure sets off another burst of violence. Huck sees men murdered and then sees Buck, his friend, killed. The beautiful house has not produced a better civilization than the people Huck fled. The family may speak graciously over breakfast and shoot another family by the afternoon.

Jim has survived the wreck and, with help, recovered their damaged raft. When Huck reaches him, the reunion feels like rescue in more than one sense. On the river again, the two are relieved to escape the land's ceremonies and cruelty. They travel in daylight when they safely can, but the river is not a magical border outside society. Boats, towns, laws, and strangers continually press upon them. It is a road on which freedom can be imagined and pursued, not a country in which slavery has disappeared.

Two new passengers arrive: elderly and middle-aged confidence men running from people they have cheated. One claims to be a dispossessed English duke; the other says he is the rightful king of France. Huck understands they are frauds but does not expose them to Jim. The impostors assume the raft and its occupants can be put to work. They plan to sell performances, stir religious enthusiasm, and obtain money by whatever story a town is willing to believe. Their titles are absurd, but people on shore have shown that real titles and social respectability are no proof of wisdom.

The impostors have a keen sense of what their audiences want to hear. One congregation is ready to believe a story about a pirate suddenly redeemed by religion. Huck sees the older fraud weep and speak movingly of his supposed conversion, then collect the money offered in response. In other towns they present themselves as actors or preachers, deciding which role will give them access to a crowd's purse. A religious gathering and a theater appear very different, but the frauds see them both as places where a person can command attention by seeming to be someone else. They do not invent people's hunger for authority. They exploit it.

The duke and the king improvise theatrical performances with ridiculous incompetence. They rehearse scraps of Shakespeare, misremember speeches, and advertise attractions designed to extract payment. Their most successful show offers an obscene spectacle to an all-male audience, runs only a few moments, and takes the crowd's money. The men who have been tricked return with new audiences, less eager to expose their own foolishness than to share the humiliation. The swindle works because the victims help it work. Huck watches a society in which pride can be a more powerful accomplice to fraud than stupidity.

In another town a man named Boggs is shot after publicly insulting the respectable Colonel Sherburn. A mob gathers to lynch the shooter, but Sherburn faces it down with contempt, accusing its members of bravery only when they are numerous enough to feel safe. The scene is not an endorsement of Sherburn's killing. It is an uncomfortable portrait of two forms of violence: the self-assured man who shoots an obnoxious drunk, and the crowd that hopes to turn the killing into a festive act of vengeance. Huck sees people who call themselves honorable behave without restraint.

At night, away from the performances, another side of Jim becomes visible. He thinks about the wife and children he has left. Once he tells Huck about his little daughter Elizabeth. He had spoken to her sharply after she failed to obey him, not realizing that illness had left her unable to hear. The memory of his mistaken anger and the child's fear still hurts him. Huck has grown accustomed to treating Jim as a good companion. He is now being shown a father who carries grief, guilt, and love as fully as anyone else. Jim's family is not merely the goal written at the end of a journey; they are already present in his mind each night on the raft.

The confidence men become more dangerous when they meet an opportunity to steal from three bereaved sisters. A man named Peter Wilks has died and left money to relatives who are expected from England. The frauds impersonate the dead man's brothers. The town welcomes them, praises their feelings, and helps them enter the family's affairs. Huck has lied often to save himself, but watching these men exploit grief sickens him. He particularly likes Mary Jane, the eldest sister, who trusts people easily and shows kindness to others without considering how it will benefit her.

The impostors intend to take the inheritance and disappear. They are also willing to sell the family's enslaved people, separating those people from one another, and to let the sisters bear the grief their supposed uncles have made. Huck steals the bag of gold and tries to find a way to give it back. Surprised while carrying it, he hides it in the dead man's coffin. The funeral proceeds with the money lying where no one would willingly look. There is comedy in the grotesque hiding place, but there is desperation too: Huck has to use deceit against deceivers because the ordinary protections of a trusting household have failed.

The true English relatives arrive. Handwriting is tested and stories are compared. At last the men decide to open the grave, and the money is discovered in the coffin. In the confusion Huck escapes to the raft. For a moment he thinks he has shaken off the frauds. Then the king and duke return and board it again. Jim and Huck have become attached to people they cannot safely defy, an exaggerated version of the wider problem confronting Jim: the powerful may dictate where he goes and what becomes of him.

The final betrayal comes when the king turns Jim in and takes forty dollars for his chance at the reward. Huck learns that Jim is being held at a place called the Phelps farm. The raft, the long journey, and the companionship that made it bearable have led not to freedom but to a cage. Huck is alone. His training offers what sounds like a respectable course: send word to Miss Watson, tell her where her property is, repent for helping an escaped man, and accept forgiveness. He begins to write that letter. He even feels the relief of having made the choice grown people would approve.

Then he remembers Jim. Not a category from a sermon, but the man who kept watch so Huck could sleep, who talked with him on the river, who called to him in the fog, who shared his food, who loved his children. These remembered acts refuse to fit the rule he has been taught. He tears up the letter. In one of the novel's starkest decisions, Huck accepts the prospect of damnation as he understands it rather than betray his friend. He is not suddenly a modern political philosopher. He still believes his culture's terrible claim that helping Jim is wrong. His moral achievement is that, at the decisive moment, he follows the evidence of a relationship rather than the authority of that claim.

He goes to the Phelps farm and finds a new maze of mistaken identities. Aunt Sally Phelps is expecting her nephew, Tom Sawyer. She assumes Huck is Tom, and Huck plays along. Then the real Tom arrives, and the two boys arrange for Tom to pass as his brother Sid. The house is full of anxious hospitality, ordinary chores, and a man imprisoned in an outbuilding. Huck's goal is simple: get Jim out. Tom eagerly agrees to help. That willingness should seem generous until we see how he intends to do it.

Aunt Sally is delighted by the boy she assumes is her nephew. She is not a cartoon villain. She fusses over meals, worries about family, and embraces Huck with uncomplicated affection. When she asks about the boat that supposedly delayed him, Huck invents a steamboat accident. She wants to know whether anyone was hurt. Huck claims only a Black person was killed. The answer reassures her. In the logic of the household Jim has been brought into, a Black death does not count as a human casualty. The exchange flashes by amid everyday domestic chatter, which makes it more disturbing, not less. Huck is about to try to free a man from people who may treat him kindly one minute and deny that his life has the same value the next.

Tom has read adventure romances and cannot bear the thought of a simple rescue. Jim could be freed by practical means, but Tom insists that an escape worthy of literature requires tunnels, secret messages, dangerous animals, a rope ladder baked into food, inscriptions, and endless ritual obstacles. He wants Jim to keep a journal and raise creatures in confinement. He invents difficulties solely to overcome them. Huck objects that some of these things are unnecessary or absurd. Tom knows they are necessary to the sort of story he wants to live.

Jim must endure the delay. The novel's earlier comedy about Tom's imaginary robber gang now returns with harsher consequences. A boy who has a book in his head regards another man's imprisonment as raw material for entertainment. Tom's schemes require stolen objects, frightened household members, more digging than anyone needs, and elaborate anonymous warnings that bring armed men to the farm. Huck participates, partly out of friendship and partly because Tom's confidence has always intimidated him. He has made his great moral choice, but he has not finished learning how to exercise judgment when someone else seems to know the rules.

Finally Jim escapes with the boys. Armed men pursue them, and Tom is shot in the leg. They reach safety for a moment, but Tom needs a doctor. Jim could run and use the opportunity to disappear. Instead he remains with the wounded boy, helps the physician care for him, and sacrifices his immediate chance of escape. He is captured again. The doctor reports that Jim's care saved Tom from greater danger. The people who have been willing to treat him as property can recognize his goodness when it benefits a white child, yet they have not been willing to begin with the principle that he possesses a life of his own.

The household's confusion finally breaks. Aunt Polly arrives and reveals the boys' identities. Tom now announces a fact he has known from the beginning: Miss Watson died and, in her will, freed Jim. Tom has orchestrated the entire elaborate rescue knowing that the man in the shed was already legally free. For Huck, the revelation explains why Tom, with all his attachment to respectability, was willing to assist. For Jim, it means that the suffering and danger of the supposed rescue were never necessary. The joy of freedom arrives burdened with the knowledge that another person's game prolonged his captivity.

Tom gives Jim forty dollars for his patience. Huck learns that the fortune Judge Thatcher holds for him is safe after all. Then Jim tells him the truth about the dead man in the floating house: it was Pap Finn. Jim had shielded Huck from seeing his father's body. The news closes a circle opened at the cabin. Huck will never again need to fear Pap's return, and Jim's quiet act of protection has been part of the story all along, unnoticed by the person he protected.

There is another proposed return to civilized life. Aunt Sally wants to adopt Huck. He can imagine the lessons and manners starting over, the same constraints that have always followed him. So he decides to leave for the Territory before anyone can make him respectable. The novel does not tell us what becomes of Jim's reunited family, or promise that the laws and racial violence of America have been dissolved. It ends with the boy looking toward another frontier and another escape, while the reader is left to reckon with the different meanings of freedom the journey has exposed.

That ending can make a listener laugh and then feel the laughter catch. Huck has escaped a murderous father, a family's blood feud, confidence men, and a plan to return Jim to an enslaver. Jim has risked his life for freedom, for his family, and repeatedly for Huck. The two have floated together under a sky that seems to offer room enough for everyone. On land, people with manners and religion and respectable names have defended things that ought to be indefensible. The river has not redeemed them. But it has given Huck the experience by which their claims may be judged. He began believing that goodness came from being civilized. He ends without a settled theory of goodness, having made at least one choice that mattered more than any theory he was taught.

## Text and evidence companion (not spoken)

### Narrative scope and witness

An original, spoiler-complete **continuous whole-novel retelling**, following the 1885 first American edition of Mark Twain's *Adventures of Huckleberry Finn* (43 chapters, with the final chapter labeled “Chapter the Last”). It is not a performance of Twain's vernacular, a chapter-by-chapter synopsis, or a substitute for his prose. Particular emphasis: Huck's captivity and staged death; Jackson's Island; Jim's family and the Cairo plan; fog and moral apology; Grangerfords; duke and king; Wilks fraud; Huck's torn letter; the Phelps escape; legal freedom, and the unresolved last departure.

The novel is set before the American Civil War, and was first issued in England in 1884 and in the United States in 1885. This manuscript uses the 1885 American wording as its controlling narrative witness, not a claim to collate Mark Twain Project manuscript variants independently.

### Primary-text anchors

- Chs. 1–7: Widow Douglas and Miss Watson, Tom's gang, Pap Finn, confinement, the staged murder and departure.
- Chs. 8–11: Jackson's Island; Jim's account of flight; floating house; rattlesnake; disguise and warning.
- Chs. 12–16: wreck exploration, raft and fog, Huck's apology, the Cairo plan and the searchers.
- Chs. 17–18: Grangerford household, Buck's death, Shepherdson feud, reunion with Jim.
- Chs. 19–23: the two frauds, their shows and bogus titles; Boggs and Sherburn; Jim's memory of Elizabeth.
- Chs. 24–30: the Wilks impersonation, the gold in the coffin, claimants, exposure and Huck's unsuccessful attempt to shake the impostors.
- Ch. 31: Jim sold to the Phelps farm, Huck's contemplated betrayal and the destroyed letter.
- Chs. 32–39: mistaken Tom/Sid identities; the elaborate and deliberately unnecessary escape preparations.
- Chs. 40–43: escape, Tom's gunshot wound, Jim nursing him, identities exposed, Jim's legal freedom, Pap's death and Huck's departure.

### Limits

Dialogue here is original paraphrase, not an attempt to reproduce regional Black speech. Twain's racial epithets are neither silently removed from the historical novel nor repeated gratuitously in a modern overview. The analysis recording examines their function and reception. The first American edition is our main textual witness; the authoritative Mark Twain Project edition is cited for its bibliographic and editorial apparatus, **not claimed to have been fully collated**.
