# The Hunchback Cycle — Story I: Four Confessions and Three Tales

First of two continuous narrations following the Hunchback cycle in John Payne's 1882 English witness. This part follows the frame, three embedded accounts, and the beginning of the tailor's account; the barber, his six brothers, and the frame's conclusion follow in Part II.

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

In Basra, as the version we are following names the city, a tailor and his wife meet a cheerful court entertainer whose back is bent. They invite him home for a meal. It is meant to be an evening of amusement. The tailor buys fish; the guests sit together, talking and eating. Then the wife presses a large bite of fish into their guest's mouth and urges him to swallow it all at once. He chokes. A fishbone lodges in his throat. The entertainment stops, and the man lies still.

The couple believe they have killed him. Before they can begin to mourn properly, a second thought overwhelms the first: if the body is found in their house, who will believe that the death was an accident? The tailor's wife devises an escape. Her husband must carry the man as though he were their sick child. They will seek a doctor, speaking loudly of the child's illness for the benefit of anyone who passes. Their performance succeeds. They reach the house of a Jewish physician, give a servant money for a consultation, prop the entertainer on the stairway, and disappear before the doctor comes down.

The physician, pleased to receive a paying patient, hurries down in the dark. His foot strikes the motionless body. It tumbles down the stairs. When the lamp arrives and he sees the man lying below, he believes his own carelessness has killed a patient before the examination could begin. He tells his wife; she proposes carrying the body onto their roof and lowering it through a neighboring ventilation opening. The neighboring home belongs to the Muslim official who manages the ruler's kitchen. That official has been missing food, and he is convinced animals have been stealing it. Coming home, he sees a stranger standing below the opening. He strikes the apparent thief with a cudgel. The man falls. Once more, the living person feels for a pulse, sees no movement, and concludes that a killing has just occurred.

The kitchen official carries his burden to the market and stands it against a wall. There a Christian broker comes past after drinking too much. Someone had earlier taken his turban, and in his unsteady state he takes the man near the wall for another robber. He attacks. A watchman finds him over the body and arrests him. The broker sobers up in a prison cell and discovers that the single mistake he believes he made may cost his life. By morning a gallows has been prepared.

The rope is around the broker's neck when the kitchen official comes forward. He cannot bear to see a man executed for what he thinks was his own deed. He confesses. The rope changes necks. Then the physician comes forward and insists that he had killed the man by sending him tumbling down the stairs. The rope changes necks again. Finally the tailor pushes through the crowd and explains the fishbone, the meal, the false sick child, the doctor. He too declares himself guilty. The executioner is tired of moving a rope from one condemned person to another, but the law is prepared to hang someone at last.

What saves the four, for the moment, is that the supposedly dead entertainer belongs to the ruler's own household. The sultan hears that his favorite performer has been found dead and that four strangers each claim responsibility. Instead of allowing the hanging, he orders the condemned people and the body brought before him. He finds the chain of confessions astonishing. The broker seizes on a possibility. He offers to tell a story even more remarkable than the one the court has just heard. If the king will listen, perhaps his pleasure will overcome his demand for punishment.

The broker tells of a merchant who came to Cairo with expensive fabrics and found a shrewd intermediary to sell them. The merchant dealt generously, delaying payment even when his broker was prepared to settle accounts. One day, persuaded to stay for a meal, he ate with his left hand. His host could not resist asking why. He showed that his right hand was gone, and the ordinary dinner became the entrance to another life.

He had come from Baghdad with goods to sell. At a Cairo market he met a wealthy woman and fell in love. She bought cloth from him. Soon they arranged secret meetings at her magnificent house. Every visit cost him a little more than he could comfortably spend. He brought gifts, sent meals, left money under her bedding, and returned again, unwilling to recognize that the fortune funding his happiness was nearly exhausted. At last he could no longer pay for the pleasures he had come to expect. He passed a soldier in a crowd, took a purse, was caught, and confessed. The punishment was the loss of his right hand.

Ashamed and wounded, he concealed the injury from the woman he loved. She understood more than he wished to tell. Seeing the severed hand and the purse, she grieved and confessed that she had kept the money he left behind rather than spending it. She married him, returned the stored fortune to him, and cared for him, but she did not survive long afterward. He inherited her property and resumed trade. His generosity toward the broker had been made possible by a life in which love, generosity, theft, punishment, and inheritance had become terribly entangled. The broker ends his account as though its surprises ought to outweigh a dead entertainer. The ruler is not convinced.

Next comes the official in charge of the kitchen. He remembers a feast at which a guest refused to eat a splendid dish of meat flavored with cumin. Everyone pressed him to try it. The guest insisted on washing his hands with extraordinary care first. He had neither thumbs nor great toes. The food and the missing digits had a connection, he said, and if his hosts wanted his company they must hear why.

The guest had once been a prosperous young merchant in Baghdad. He had fallen in love with a woman attached to the household of Zubayda, the caliph's wife. To meet her he accepted a dangerous plan: attendants smuggled him into the palace in a chest. Guards began opening the chests one by one. He thought he would be discovered and killed, but the woman who had arranged the meeting helped him pass through the ordeal. Inside, their courtship progressed toward marriage. They might have seemed to have survived the dangerous part when the woman learned that he had arrived for her with traces of the pungent cumin dish still upon his hands. The ceremony of washing, and his failure to perform it to her exacting standard, became a disaster in their marriage. She demanded a punishment that left him without thumbs and great toes. Ever afterward the smell of the dish sent him back to the experience. What a dinner host considered a harmless delicacy had become, for him, an unbearable reminder.

The official explains that he had heard all this from a man who would not eat dinner. The ruler listens, admits the tale has its interest, and still threatens to execute the four. The Jewish physician begins his own account. He once treated a young man who ate with the left hand because the right had been removed. When asked how this happened, the patient spoke of a house in Damascus, a concealed death, and a necklace that betrayed him long afterward.

As a young traveler he rented a house where a mysterious woman began visiting him. The meetings brought him delight, then a second young woman came with the first. In the morning he woke beside a body. The second woman had been murdered while he slept, and the first had vanished. The young man feared that no one would believe his innocence. He buried the dead woman beneath the courtyard, covered the ground and fled. Years later he returned, opened the old hiding place, and found jewelry among the remains. When he tried to sell a necklace, he was suspected of theft. His explanations were not believed. His hand was cut off before the terrible connection between the necklace and the buried woman could be understood. The necklace eventually reaches the governor, who recognizes it as belonging to a daughter missing for years. The youth is brought before him and at last tells the whole affair. The governor reveals that the woman who first visited him was his elder daughter and the murdered woman her younger sister. The elder sister has since died in grief and guilt. The governor acknowledges that the youth has been punished unjustly, orders compensation for the lost hand, and offers marriage to another daughter. The tale ends with a new household formed after the old one was destroyed. Its horror is not merely the first murder. It is the way concealment, fear, and official haste prolong the consequences across years and cities.

The physician concludes his story. Three men have now offered the king embedded histories of other people's suffering. The king does not declare any of them sufficiently wonderful to pardon the defendants. His judgment is part of the joke, but it is also real danger: stories have earned attention, not yet safety. All that remains is the tailor, whose wife first served the fish. The tailor begins to speak of an encounter with an extraordinarily talkative barber, and the court hears that the end of this affair may require listening not only to a fourth story, but to a man's account of all six of his brothers.

- **Scope:** The frame and three subordinate stories are paraphrased without reproducing Payne's language. The fourth witness and barber's six-brother inset resume in 124000. The status of the entertainer as apparently dead is a premise that the tale later revises; do not substitute modern medical certainty for the fiction.
- **Variant caution:** Payne's locale is Basra. Other English witnesses place the story elsewhere. Individual motives and episode order depend on named translations; this narration follows Payne for continuity.
- **Interpretive boundary:** Criticism of harsh bodily punishment and the use of religious identities for comic construction is modern interpretation, not an assertion about the attitudes of all earlier audiences.
