# The Song of Roland — Analysis: Honor, Poetry, and Invented Enemies

A literary and historical reading of the Oxford-text epic: its distance from the 778 campaign, the tragic argument over the horn, its way of making a nation of listeners, and the responsibility to distinguish its religious polemic from history.

Why does Roland not blow the horn? It is the simplest question in a poem whose politics and religion may at first seem very far from us. Oliver asks him to call Charlemagne back while help might still matter. Roland refuses because honor demands that he face overwhelming odds. Later, when the men are dying, he decides to call. The sequence is not a mere puzzle about an arrogant soldier. It forces two understandings of duty into conflict. One protects a reputation that will endure in song. The other protects the people whose lives are in the commander's hands. The poem praises Roland, but it has allowed Oliver to state the cost of Roland's ideal with devastating clarity.

A reader who thinks this is simply the account of a battle in 778 has already lost the poem's historical distance. Charlemagne did campaign in Spain that year, and his retreating forces suffered an attack at a Pyrenean pass. Early Frankish records associate the loss with Basque attackers, not the immense Muslim armies that confront Roland in the chanson. Einhard's later biography of Charlemagne names a Roland, a high-ranking officer, among those killed. These are slender historical anchors for a poem that invents speeches, councils, enormous armies, kings, angels, and the machinery of an empire-wide confrontation. The surviving Old French text belongs to a much later medieval world, customarily associated with the period around the late eleventh and early twelfth centuries. A battle has become material for thinking about loyalty, Christian power, and the meaning of collective memory centuries later.

The earliest and most celebrated witness is preserved in the Oxford manuscript, Bodleian Library Digby 23, in an Anglo-Norman variety of Old French. The poem belongs to the genre called the chanson de geste, a song of deeds. It is made in units called laisses, usually held together by a repeated vowel sound rather than the end-rhyme patterns familiar from many later European poems. Actions and words can return in new formulations: a council states a proposition, another speaker reshapes it, then the army gives it physical consequence. A sword stroke is described with forceful clarity, and the next stroke comes like a reply. Such repetition is not necessarily evidence that an editor forgot to remove a draft. It is one of the work's techniques for making public experience memorable, and it has a grandeur that a single fast-moving synopsis cannot reproduce.

Those techniques create both intimacy and scale. The council in which Roland nominates Ganelon is an exchange among recognizably proud men. The betrayal grows through journeys and private conversations. At Roncevaux the description expands into an enormous series of individual encounters. The poem names leaders and lets them become visible in action. Yet every individual battle occurs within a hopeless arithmetic: the rear guard cannot continue fighting indefinitely against an enemy who keeps arriving. The death of Oliver is not just another line in a tally because he has spent the poem speaking to Roland as someone who loves him well enough to disagree. The epic needs that friendship for its catastrophe to be more than military spectacle.

Ganelon matters for the same reason. If he were a simple embodiment of evil who betrayed the army for money, his trial would be uncomplicated. Instead he believes he has a right to vengeance for personal dishonor. In his own defense he acknowledges his hostility toward Roland but denies that retaliation against Roland amounts to treason against Charlemagne. That distinction has a social logic in a world of feuding lineages and powerful vassals. Thierry's challenge denies that the commander of a royal rear guard can be separated from the king's public trust. The trial asks whether private honor may override the obligation that holds the political community together. The judicial combat is strange to a modern court, but the conceptual problem is recognizable: can someone authorize deadly harm and then say that the public consequences were not his responsibility?

Oliver's disagreement offers a second test of honor. In a song that gives magnificent language to courage, Oliver is not made contemptible for advocating caution. He is a brave fighter and Roland's companion. His judgment is concerned with living men, not merely with the account others will later give of them. It is possible to read the poem as wholly endorsing Roland's refusal, yet it deliberately preserves Oliver's repeated appeal and the terrible lateness of Roland's reversal. The moment when the horn finally sounds changes the same action from a possible rescue to a summons for burial and vengeance. A signal can travel just as far while its moral value changes completely with time.

Religion shapes every level of the epic, and its claims require more than a sympathetic paraphrase. The Christian warriors speak of duty to God. Turpin fights as an archbishop. Angels minister to Roland, and miracles strengthen the victorious emperor. The enemy, by contrast, is represented as worshiping idols with names including Muhammad and Apollo. That is not a faithful report of Muslim belief. Islam rejects idolatry; the poem's invented paganized 'Saracens' reflect the ignorance, fantasy, and polemical aims of the Christian tradition that produced this telling. The line between the story's imagined enemy and historical Muslims cannot be allowed to disappear simply because the poem has achieved canonical status.

Nor should we skip over what follows the defeat. The destruction of the enemy's places of worship, mass baptism, and the conversion of Bramimond are presented within the poem as signs of righteous victory. A modern reader can describe this internal viewpoint without endorsing coercion or treating it as a neutral chronicle. We know much more about medieval Muslim societies than the poem chooses to admit. Its Saracens are built as an opposing image in which the Frankish heroes can see their own unity reflected. The women and civilians of the defeated society receive little independent voice. The poem's grandeur and its ideological violence coexist.

There is another difficulty in the language of national memory. 'France' in Roland's speeches is an object of love and political imagination, but it is not a modern nation-state dropped into the eleventh century. The poem helps later audiences imagine a community through shared grief, service to a ruler, geographical names, lineages, and a common sacred cause. To say it contributed to a French literary tradition is different from saying its characters held today's national identity. The oldest famous copy is Anglo-Norman, a reminder that medieval linguistic and political geography crossed boundaries our maps now make look fixed.

The figure of Charlemagne helps the poem hold together what might otherwise be two separate epics. The first centers on a betrayed rear guard and the deaths of beloved companions. The second brings armies of gigantic scope and the victory over Baligant. It is easy to think the second battle weakens the first by supplying a compensatory triumph. But the emperor's grief cannot be compensated in that way. He must bury Roland, judge Ganelon, and accept another summons to battle. The poet makes his ruler a great avenger and a man worn down by the endless demand for vengeance. An empire can keep winning and still fail to give its people peace.

What survives most strongly is an argument about the cost of making greatness visible. Roland has all the qualities a warrior culture admires, and those qualities help lead his companions to their deaths. Ganelon invokes his own injured honor in defense of an act that destroys the rear guard. Charlemagne exercises authority over enormous armies but must rely on messengers, oaths, and men whose private hatreds he cannot detect in time. The poem does not abolish heroism. It makes heroism inseparable from institutions and obligations that sometimes contradict it.

So when the horn sounds, hear two songs at once. One proclaims that Roland and his companions are so brave that their names deserve to be preserved forever. The other asks what courage would have meant an hour earlier, when there were still people to save. We can take the poem seriously as medieval art without accepting every judgment it makes about its opponents, and we can admire a hero while refusing to pretend that his most famous decision was without victims. That tension is not an obstacle to reading the Song of Roland. It is why a medieval war song can remain worth arguing about.

## Evidence and interpretation (not spoken)

- **Primary poem:** [*The Song of Roland*, C. K. Scott-Moncrieff translation, Project Gutenberg #391](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/391), read as an Oxford-text tradition rather than a verified record of the 778 campaign.
- **Textual scholarship:** David Raybin, [“The Song of Roland,” *Oxford Bibliographies in Medieval Studies*](https://doi.org/10.1093/obo/9780195396584-0207) (2016): Oxford MS Digby 23, Anglo-Norman witness, *chanson de geste* form and literary importance.
- **Historical cross-check:** [Wikipedia: Battle of Roncevaux Pass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Roncevaux_Pass), with pointers to Einhard's *Vita Karoli Magni* and Royal Frankish Annals; the historical attack was attributed to Basques rather than a Muslim host.
- **Textual orientation:** [Wikipedia: The Song of Roland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Song_of_Roland).
- **Criticism versus historical fact:** The reading of Roland's refusal and the distinction between a courtly-feud claim and treason are interpretive judgments from the poem. Christian claims about Islam within the epic do not become evidence about Islamic religious practice. Descriptions of forced conversion are not endorsements.
