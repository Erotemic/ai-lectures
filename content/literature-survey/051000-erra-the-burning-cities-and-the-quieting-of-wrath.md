# Erra and Ishum: The Burning Cities and the Quieting of Wrath

A continuation and conclusion of the original five-tablet Akkadian poem, with tablets III–V treated as dramatic argument and a litany of civic devastation rather than as an invented sequence of individually witnessed military campaigns. The composition's protective epilogue is included.

Picture a city in which people no longer know whom to trust. The market remains a place where traders should meet, but the road to it has become dangerous. A family closes its door and listens to sounds that might mean a neighbor needs help or that a stranger has come to kill them. Officials speak, but the promises attached to their offices have lost force. Violence begins to feed on its own expectation. One blow is answered by another because everyone assumes another blow is coming.

This is the world the *Epic of Erra* imagines when the god of devastation gains his opening. Erra has been urged into action by the Seven, and Ishum has tried to restrain him. Marduk's accustomed place in Babylon has been disturbed through a proposal to repair his divine image. The exact transition in the surviving tablets is damaged, but the poem has carried us into a condition of catastrophe. This is not a story that counts military victories and arranges them into a triumphal procession. Its great subject is what happens to the inhabited world when violence becomes its guiding principle.

Erra speaks of power as if the ability to ruin a place were a sufficient reason to ruin it. His declarations range across cities and people. The world has been complacent; the human population seems too numerous or too secure; acts of punishment must be made visible. In the poem, the divine speaker can announce a terrible intention and imagine it accomplished. That rhetorical movement is part of the terror. The people who are named or implied in his speech are given no equal opportunity to answer.

The destruction reaches beyond a battlefield. Families are separated. Civic bonds fail. The conduct that would normally be condemned can become ordinary when institutions fall apart. The poem gives us unsettling inversions: places meant to shelter are unsafe; people whose roles require restraint behave as predators; victims and attackers can be difficult to distinguish in the disorder. Catastrophe is not only a pile of ruined bricks. It is a change in the expectations on which daily life depends.

At times Erra seems to revel in these reversals. A person who ought to be at prayer is exposed to violence. A city famous for its protection cannot guarantee safety. A ruler can no longer make his command good. Every feature of ordinary life that might have prevented desperation becomes an occasion for further fear. The text is forceful because it thinks about devastation from inside the city rather than keeping its gaze on banners and chariots.

Ishum hears all of this. His speech is a different sort of force. He is no less divine than the violent companion he addresses, and no less able to grasp the scale of power. But he keeps showing Erra the meaning of that scale. If the god can devastate the world, it does not follow that the world ought to be devastated. If terror can make a name famous, fame won by terror is not the same thing as wise rule. Ishum continually asks the question that Erra's boasting tries to avoid: what will remain when the work is finished?

Erra answers with arguments that sound like inevitability. People have failed to honor him. Divine status has been disregarded. An old order must be shaken before a proper order can return. The defense is circular. Violence is supposed to make the world recognize the god; a world ruined by violence can then be offered as evidence of the god's greatness. Ishum presses against the circle, because a god who measures honor by the size of his destruction can never be satisfied for long.

The poem's middle tablets are notoriously difficult to reconstruct, both because of broken passages and because divine speeches sometimes shift in ways that translators and editors must carefully assign. We should hear this as a long argument shaped by the language of destruction, not as a verbatim battle report from which every city and every death can be independently located on a map. The text's imagery is not less serious for being literary. Its force depends precisely on turning the ruin of a human community into the proof that a god's appetite has escaped control.

Ishum does not simply repeat that destruction is bad. He searches for a way to bring Erra back to a condition in which his power is not constantly being spent. He acknowledges that the terror associated with Erra is already real. There is no need to demonstrate it endlessly. A ruler or god can have strength without making every neighbor suffer for it. The speaker's task is not to deny what Erra can do but to change what Erra thinks it means to be powerful.

The argument gradually turns. Erra begins to hear the cost of his own campaign, and the possibility arises that the same will that started the catastrophe could stop it. In one account of the poem's movement, Ishum's intervention and declarations of Erra's already established might help make further destruction unnecessary. Later passages direct violent energy elsewhere and envision the restoration of order. The text's handling of this transition is complicated, not the simple moment when an evil person becomes good.

In the final tablet, Erra's own stance toward his anger changes. He acknowledges the dangerous course of wrath and praises Ishum's work in bringing the crisis under control. This is not a conventional military surrender to an opponent. Erra remains a god with the capacity to destroy. His companion has won a different sort of victory, one achieved by interrupting a god's devotion to the power of his own anger.

The poem promises recovery. The land can be populated again, cities can regain their life, and the conditions of worship and daily exchange can be restored. It would be false to tell this ending as though it undid everything that had been suffered. Rebuilding does not resurrect every person lost or make fear never have existed. The story's hope is more limited and more credible: the violence can stop; the structures people need may be established again; a god capable of devastation can also be brought to recognize limits.

Then the epic turns from narrated events to its own life in the world. It says that the poem itself can carry protective force. The name of Erra, once associated with the capacity to bring disaster, becomes part of an appeal to keep disaster away. A tablet bearing the work may be placed in a house. Where its words are honored, the destructive beings are asked not to strike. The text becomes not just a story about the dangers of an unleashed god but a thing people can possess in the hope that his anger will pass them by.

Some surviving tablets shaped for display and some protective extracts show that this last idea was not simply a line left inside a literary ending. People did copy the poem into objects associated with protection. One should not claim that every such tablet had precisely the same ritual use. But the material record gives the story an afterlife beyond literary recital. A household could keep an inscribed text and understand that words about destruction might stand as a guard against destruction.

Near the close comes the colophon that names Kabti-ilani-Marduk, son of Dabibi. It associates the poem with a vision received at night, a complete work delivered to the author in a way the text presents as extraordinary. This deserves careful attribution. The colophon is an ancient testimony to how the work presents its origin, not a means of verifying that a supernatural event occurred. It also means the epic bears a more specific attribution than many anonymously transmitted narratives of the ancient Near East.

Consider the reversal that has taken place since the opening. At first the Seven urge Erra to leave his rest because their lord ought to be feared. Ishum knows the danger of that invitation and tries to prevent it. The poem then leads us through the horror that the first speeches made possible. By the close, the same concern with divine reputation and power can be put to another use. Erra is not diminished by ceasing to destroy. The words that once summoned terror can be recited to ask that terror remain outside the door.

There is also a difficult political insight in the work. People experiencing violence may be told that their suffering serves a greater restoration, that it is necessary to prove a power, or that it must continue until everyone has recognized who rules. The poem does not let that logic rest comfortably. It shows how destruction can be its own justification and how the language of cosmic necessity can conceal a will to dominate. Ishum's resistance matters because he addresses the source of the harm, not merely its symptoms.

The experience of the poem is an experience of listening to dangerous speech. We hear a god being stirred from rest. We hear another god object. We hear an attempt to make the withdrawal of a protector sound like a minor administrative matter. We hear cities described as objects on which a ruler can demonstrate power. We hear the objection repeated until rage finally begins to lose its hold. And at the very end we learn that these words are to be spoken again, or kept in writing, in homes whose inhabitants want to be safe.

A guardian tablet on a wall cannot restore a city that has already burned. Yet it can tell us what sort of world the people who owned it feared. They feared violence that arrived without proportion, the collapse of ordinary institutions, the sudden nearness of plague and warfare, and the withdrawal of protection they had assumed would last. They hoped that a story naming those forces could help keep them at a distance. *Erra and Ishum* makes fear itself part of the history of literature: a poem about calamity that also tries to become an instrument against it.
