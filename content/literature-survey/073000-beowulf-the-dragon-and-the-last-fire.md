# Beowulf, Part II: The Dragon and the Last Fire

The latter portion of *Beowulf*, from the return to Geatland through the dragon fight, Wiglaf's reproach, and the funeral, approximately lines 1888–3182.

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

Beowulf has killed two monsters for a foreign king. He has received rings, horses, weapons, and praise. None of these gifts is an ending. He must carry them home, give an account of what he did, and resume his life among the people to whom he belongs. The poem follows him back across the sea to Geatland, where King Hygelac and Queen Hygd receive him. Before the court he tells his story again. The retelling is not an unnecessary second version of adventures we already know. It shows how a deed becomes a political event once it is narrated at home, weighed by a ruler, and placed among relations of loyalty and power.

Beowulf brings treasures that Hrothgar gave him and offers them to Hygelac. Gift-giving here is not a private exchange of expensive souvenirs. A great lord's reward becomes evidence that a follower has served honorably; a follower's return gift acknowledges the authority of his own king. Queen Hygd is praised for generosity and good judgment, unlike an infamous queen from an older story who punished men for approaching her with the wrong respect. The poet places small judgments of conduct beside scenes of immense violence. To hear only the monster fights is to miss the world in which the hero's strength has meaning.

The years that follow pass more quickly in the telling. Hygelac dies in a disastrous expedition. Beowulf survives, and when the dead king's widow offers him the throne he does not seize it. He supports the young heir Heardred instead. Heardred is later killed amid the struggles of Swedish claimants, and Beowulf finally becomes king. He rules the Geats for fifty years. The poem does not give us fifty years of domestic chronicle. The long interval is compressed into the fact of a reign, so the next crisis can be judged against decades of relative stability. Beowulf has become the kind of elder Hrothgar once was: a man whose hall and people seem safe because he is there.

In a mound near his kingdom lies an ancient hoard. It had been hidden there by the last survivor of a vanished people, someone who placed gold and weapons underground because no one remained to inherit or use them. The lament over that treasure is among the poem's most desolate moments. There are no companions left to receive the cups, no harp at the feast, no horse carrying a lord into the courtyard. The precious objects endure after the social world that gave them value has disappeared. At some point a dragon takes possession of the barrow and guards what it cannot spend. Its age and immense strength make it frightening, but the treasure makes it an image of possession without fellowship.

A fugitive enters the mound. He does not seek heroic glory. He is trying to escape punishment and steals a decorated cup, hoping it may help him negotiate his safety. A small human act awakens a disaster. The dragon discovers that something is missing and searches the land with fire. Buildings burn, homes vanish, and even the king's hall is consumed. Beowulf is enraged and troubled. The hero who once volunteered to help another nation now sees his own people threatened. It would be tempting to describe the encounter as an inevitable duel between two proud beings. Yet the poem makes us watch houses catch fire before it lets us stand with the king. The dragon is an enemy because people suffer.

Beowulf decides to fight. He has met dangers before and survived, but this time he is old. He orders a special iron shield, large enough to withstand flame better than wood would. He recalls his earlier exploits, his loyalty to Hygelac, the fate of other rulers, and the feud that has drawn Geatland into the affairs of its neighbors. The backward glances are not distractions from the dragon. They allow us to hear an old person's accounting of a life just when he is preparing to risk its end. Beowulf still believes in his strength. He also knows that no one can indefinitely bargain with death.

He takes eleven companions to the barrow, among them the young warrior Wiglaf. The fugitive who took the cup shows them the way. Beowulf speaks a final public boast and vows to face the dragon, as he once faced Grendel. But the resemblance between the two fights is deceptive. The earlier hero could tear an enemy's arm free with his hands. Now he carries armor and weapons into a place built around the weight of what has been hoarded for centuries. He calls out. The dragon emerges, roaring, and the battle begins in fire.

The flame strikes Beowulf's shield. He advances, but his sword does not do what he needs it to do. The dragon recoils and returns, burning hotter. The companions who came with the king flee to the woods. This abandonment is one of the poem's decisive events. The defeat that approaches is not simply the product of aging muscles or a stronger monster. It is also a failure of reciprocal obligation. The king has given these men arms, rewards, protection, and a place in his retinue. In the moment when he needs their service, they leave him alone.

Wiglaf stays. He rebukes the fleeing warriors and reminds them of the promises made while accepting gifts from their lord. The words may sound severe to modern ears, but within the poem's social world they identify a bond that is supposed to survive fear. He enters the fight, holding his own shield as Beowulf's fails under the heat. The king strikes at the dragon; his sword shatters. The dragon bites him in the neck, and its venom begins to take hold. Wiglaf wounds the creature, and Beowulf finds the strength for a final blow with a knife. The dragon dies. So does the possibility that Beowulf can return to the life he protected.

The king knows the wound is fatal. He asks Wiglaf to enter the mound and bring him some of the treasure so he can see what he has won for the Geats. The request is strange and moving. A dying ruler wants to inspect gold that may help his people, but the objects themselves cannot prolong his life. Wiglaf returns with cups, ornaments, and the splendid standard from the barrow. Beowulf gives thanks that he has obtained wealth for his nation. He asks Wiglaf to care for the people, to build a memorial on the headland, and to let travelers know whose bones lie there. He gives the young man the precious collar and armor he wears. The gifts cross from the dying hand to the living one. Beowulf's body grows still.

Wiglaf turns on the men who fled. He tells them their failure has destroyed the protection they enjoyed. He predicts that neighboring enemies will hear of Beowulf's death and move against a kingdom deprived of its strongest defender. The poem's world has never been a clean contest between peaceful heroes and monsters. Swedish and Geatish feuds, the deaths of kings, and the betrayals of alliances have run beneath the monster stories from the beginning. Now the public consequences of one leader's death become impossible to conceal. The dragon is dead, but the people may be less safe than they were before the fight.

A messenger carries the news. He does not merely say that the great king has fallen. He remembers the disastrous raid in which Hygelac died and the long feuds with Swedish rulers. The listeners must understand what Beowulf's strength had been holding back. Warriors bring the body from the barrow. The stolen treasure is gathered and the dragon's corpse is pushed from the cliff into the sea. The objects that occasioned the calamity are carried toward a new burial, not distributed through a living court. Gold has returned to the condition in which it began: wealth removed from use among the living.

The Geats build an immense funeral pyre. Smoke rises from the headland and is seen far away. A woman sings a lament that looks beyond one man's death to the terror of invasion, captivity, and humiliation. Her fear refuses to let the elegy become merely private. Then the people raise a barrow that sailors can see from the water and bury the treasure within it. Twelve warriors ride around the mound speaking the name of their king, remembering his generosity and courage. The poem's final praise calls him gentle toward his people, eager for fame, and kind. The terms do not make all his choices beyond criticism. They show what his followers most wanted to remember.

A listener may have expected a hero to defeat the third monster and return crowned with glory. Instead, *Beowulf* ends with a victory that costs the hero his life and exposes the vulnerability of his nation. The hall at the beginning and the barrow at the end are two kinds of monument. The hall gathers people into a living exchange of speech, food, honor, and gift. The barrow keeps an image of that life after it has ceased. Beowulf's greatest strength could save Heorot for a time and Geatland from a dragon, but not abolish mortality, fear, or the political consequences of losing a king. The poem leaves us with a question no sword can answer: how can a community survive the passing of the person who seemed to hold it together?
