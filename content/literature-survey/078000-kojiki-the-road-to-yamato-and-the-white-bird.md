# Kojiki, Part III: The Road to Yamato and the White Bird

A selective journey through books II and III: the eastward expedition of the figure later called Jimmu, the stories of Yamato Takeru, and the account of Nintoku's tax remission. These are legendary and exemplary narratives from an early-medieval compilation, not a modern chronological history of Japan.

A company sets out toward the east, hoping to establish rule in a land that is neither vacant nor simply waiting for them. The leader belongs to a lineage that the earlier stories of the *Kojiki* connect to the descent of heavenly powers. But ancestral descent does not open every road. The travelers encounter resisting people, unfamiliar terrain, illness, and the stubborn fact that they are strangers. The expedition is traditionally associated with the ruler later called Emperor Jimmu. The received book writes his story with the confidence of a royal genealogy. A modern listener must hear its mythic and political purpose without treating the named dates of early reigns as independently established chronology.

The travelers initially approach from the west. In a violent confrontation one of the leader's brothers is wounded and later dies. The party changes its course, attempting to approach the region from a direction better aligned, within the story's symbolic geography, with the power of the sun. The journey around the coast becomes a sequence of losses and divine interventions. When the company reaches the region of Kumano, a dangerous influence incapacitates its members. Help comes through a sacred sword, made available by divine command. The sword is not merely a better military tool; it signals that the expedition's claim to authority is being narrated as part of a larger sacred order.

The leader's progress continues with guidance from a remarkable bird sent from the heavenly realm. In Japanese tradition the crow is often called Yatagarasu. It does not erase the need for difficult travel or negotiation, but it marks the route through a country that cannot be mastered by force alone. There are encounters with local chiefs and beings whom the narrative classifies according to their relation to the approaching power. Some submit; others resist; some are overcome in ambush or open combat. One can hear the texture of a conquest legend in which geography, political allegiance, and supernatural approval are woven together. The people encountered do not have the same control over how their stories are told as the conquering lineage does.

The expedition culminates in an establishment of rule associated with Yamato, and the account continues with marriages, descendants, and the struggle to secure succession. We have crossed a threshold within the *Kojiki*. A divine genealogy has become the foundation for a story about rulers who occupy earthly places, marry into local families, and claim authority over other communities. Much of the text's subsequent material is genealogical or annalistic, though individual episodes can suddenly become vivid dramatic tales. The compilation does not adopt a modern distinction between myth, court memory, and verifiable state archives, so readers must make that distinction in the way they discuss it.

One of the most compelling later figures is the prince commonly called Yamato Takeru. His story begins with disturbing violence within his own family. The ruler who is his father fears his son's dangerous strength and sends him on campaigns against distant opponents. The young man goes not merely as an obedient warrior but as someone whose relationship to his father has been badly damaged. He kills enemies by stealth as well as in combat. In a famous western episode, he disguises himself to gain entry to a gathering and kills the Kumaso leaders. His cunning wins him a name associated with Yamato and heroic ferocity. Yet the success that gives the prince fame does not bring him peace with his family.

A further episode in Izumo turns on friendship and betrayal: Yamato Takeru contrives to replace an opponent's real sword with a harmless imitation and then defeats him when they exchange weapons. The scene makes no attempt to provide a modern sportsman's code of honorable combat. Its hero survives through deception, and its admiration for cleverness coexists with unease about the brutality of his methods. His father sends him on another expedition, this time toward the east. Before departing, he visits his aunt at a sacred place and receives a sword that will later be known as Kusanagi, the grass-cutting sword. The gift creates a bridge from the divine world of the opening myths to the dangers of a traveling human warrior.

Enemies attempt to trap him in a field of burning grass. He uses the sword to clear a path and turns the flames against his attackers. The weapon's name and story are bound to this encounter. The prince wins, but travel continues to demand sacrifices that combat cannot settle. At a dangerous stretch of sea, the waves rise against his party. His companion Ototachibana offers herself to the water so that the others may pass. The storm quiets. Yamato Takeru reaches safety with the loss of someone who had chosen to share his dangers. The poem and songs associated with his grief give a different sound to his journey: the man who could defeat ambushers cannot recover the person who stepped into the sea.

The end comes through another challenge. At Mount Ibuki, the prince ventures against a mountain deity, leaving behind the protecting sword. He treats the encounter with insufficient caution and is struck by illness. Though he struggles onward, his body fails. He longs for his home and remembers the land he may never see again. His final songs and death are among the *Kojiki*'s most affecting legendary passages. Those who come to mourn him see a white bird rise from the place of his burial. They follow its flight, and the story turns his absence into movement through the landscape. The hero of violent campaigns has become a figure who cannot be held in one grave.

The political story continues beyond him. In the book's third volume, the account of a ruler conventionally called Nintoku includes an episode in which he observes the condition of his people. Looking from a height, he notices that little smoke rises from their cooking fires. He understands the absence as a sign of hardship and remits taxes for several years, accepting the deterioration of his own residence while the people recover. Later he sees smoke rising again and takes it as evidence that households have been restored. The tale is a model of kingship in compact narrative form: a ruler reads the ordinary lives of subjects not from courtly praise but from whether people can cook a meal. It is an exemplary story from the received text, not proof that every historical detail occurred as described.

The third volume contains other episodes of marriage, rivalry, succession, and song. We are selecting scenes, not pretending that its many genealogies and brief notices are a single omitted adventure. What connects the selections is the change in scale. First the gods gave birth to islands. Then heavenly authority was claimed over a land with older powers. Now rulers travel through contested territories, people die on expeditions, and an emperor's virtue can be measured by the smoke rising from poor houses. The *Kojiki* uses literary devices to place everyday political acts within a story that began before people existed.

Its influence lies not in offering us a transparent history of every early ruler, but in the way it gathers songs, names, mythic locations, local tales, and imperial lines into one consequential work. It has shaped understandings of Japanese myth, courtly legitimacy, ritual, and the relation of place to memory, even as historians and readers continue to debate its construction. The first volume contains a sun hidden in a cave; the later books contain a ruler searching the horizon for signs of cooking fires. Those two images do not mean the same thing, but the distance between them measures what this remarkable early-medieval compilation is attempting: to carry a remembered world from the emergence of light to the obligations of human power.
