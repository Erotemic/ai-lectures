# Shiji I: Jing Ke and the Dagger Hidden in a Map

A narrative reading of Sima Qian's *Records of the Grand Historian*, chapter 86, the biography of the assassin-retainers, centered on Jing Ke. The episode is presented as Sima Qian's account, not as a stenographic record of everything said at the Qin court.

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

A man approaches a king carrying a map. In the room, the guards stand at a distance, the ministers know the rules of court, and a large kingdom is receiving what appears to be an offer of submission. Only the emissary and a few people far away know that the map conceals a dagger. For one suspended moment, a political decision depends on how far a scroll can be unrolled before anyone understands what is happening. The story comes from the *Shiji*, or *Records of the Grand Historian*, a work assembled in Han China by Sima Qian. In its eighty-sixth chapter Sima Qian brings together several men who attempted killings on behalf of patrons, states, or personal loyalties. The most famous is Jing Ke. Before we enter the royal hall with him, we need to understand why he has been sent there.

In the late Warring States world, the kingdom of Qin is swallowing its neighbors. Qin commands enormous armies, uses organized administration to sustain its campaigns, and steadily removes the options available to smaller states. Yan, far to the northeast, faces a danger it cannot answer in an ordinary contest of strength. Its crown prince, Dan, has a personal history with the ruler of Qin and fears what will happen if Yan waits. The plan he entertains is a desperate one: remove the man directing the conquest, or perhaps seize him and force a settlement. The biography lets us see the plan moving through a chain of introductions, obligations, and sacrifices. It does not ask us to assume that assassinating a ruler would have stopped the machinery of Qin's expansion.

Jing Ke is not introduced as a man who announces himself in a shower of victories. He has wandered, read, practiced swordsmanship, and encountered people who judge him differently. He spends time in the markets of Yan with a dog butcher and Gao Jianli, a musician who plays the zhu. Their drinking, singing, and sudden weeping are remembered as if a public marketplace could briefly become a private room. A respected older man, Tian Guang, sees more in Jing Ke than the casual companions of the market might suspect. The prince has been searching for someone able to take on his project; Tian Guang recommends him. But the recommendation carries a terrible consequence. After the prince cautions Tian Guang to keep their meeting secret, the older man takes this as a sign of distrust and kills himself, intending to demonstrate that he will not betray the confidence. The narrative belongs to a world in which a person's reputation could be made or destroyed by how others interpreted an act of loyalty. We can recognize the force of its values without treating suicide as a duty.

Jing Ke comes to Dan and hears the problem. Simply asking for an audience with Qin's ruler will not work. He must arrive with an offering so substantial that suspicion yields to anticipation. Two things are chosen. One is a map of valuable territory in Yan, a sign that the state is prepared to surrender land. The other is the head of Fan Yuqi, a general who has fled Qin and on whom the Qin ruler has placed a bounty. The prince initially refuses to betray the man who sought refuge with him. Jing Ke approaches Fan himself, explaining the injuries Qin has done to him and proposing that his death might create an opportunity for revenge. Fan accepts, according to Sima Qian, and kills himself. Jing Ke's mission is now built on the deaths of two men before he has even crossed the border. The details of their final speeches are part of the historian's shaped narrative; the political fact is the author's insistence that heroism here has a human cost.

A specially obtained dagger is treated with poison and hidden inside the rolled map. The prince appoints a young warrior, Qin Wuyang, to accompany Jing Ke. Jing Ke delays because he is waiting for another companion, but Dan suspects that he has lost his nerve. In anger Jing Ke sets out without the person he wanted. At the Yi River, those who know the plan wear white garments in farewell, and Gao Jianli plays while Jing Ke sings. The poem of departure makes the cold river and the likelihood of no return part of one unforgettable scene. This is not a triumphal procession. Every person standing on the bank is sending a friend into a place where success and survival are both improbable. Sima Qian describes the music changing from lamentation to fierce resolve; the mood changes, but the journey remains one-way.

The emissaries reach Qin with gifts and bribes for a court intermediary. The story tightens around manners. The Qin ruler puts on ceremonial robes and receives them with elaborate protocol. Jing Ke carries the box containing Fan Yuqi's head; Qin Wuyang bears the map. But when the two approach, the younger man visibly loses his composure. The courtiers notice. Jing Ke speaks quickly, attributing his companion's shaking to the fear of a provincial stranger who has never stood before such a ruler. Qin accepts the explanation and requests the map. What follows is narrated with almost theatrical attention to small movements. Jing Ke takes the scroll. The king opens it. As the last section unrolls, the dagger becomes visible. Jing Ke grips the king's sleeve and tries to strike. The king pulls away, tearing the fabric, and runs.

It should have been a brief attack, yet the room's rules extend it. Armed attendants have been stationed outside the royal hall, and those immediately present are not permitted to wear swords. The king's own sword is long and difficult to draw in panic. The ruler runs around a pillar while Jing Ke pursues him. Ministers are too astonished to act coherently. A court physician, Xia Wuqie, throws his medicine bag at Jing Ke. The attendants finally shout instructions about how the king should draw the sword behind his back. Qin's ruler succeeds, strikes his attacker, and incapacitates him. Jing Ke throws the dagger but misses, hitting the pillar. Wounded, he mocks the failed attempt and claims he had wished to take the ruler alive and compel an agreement. The king's men kill him. A map that seemed to offer submission has turned into an instrument of revolt, but the revolt has failed.

Why tell the chase so carefully? Because Sima Qian can show a powerful ruler reduced to the physical problem of extracting a weapon, and a would-be assassin reduced to the narrow geography of a room. The account is neither an unambiguous celebration of Jing Ke nor a simple denunciation. The man acts with steadfast purpose; the plan depends on betrayal, suicide, and violence; and the intended liberation does not come. Qin will conquer Yan. The doomed action is remembered, but not because it changes the military result. It acquires its afterlife through the historian's attention to the choice, its preparation, the failure, and the names of the people drawn into it.

Sima Qian ends the chapter by questioning sensational versions of the tale. He rejects claims that the king was wounded and objects to extravagant stories about the prince's mission. He says he heard an account that went back to people associated with the court physician who threw the bag. That does not make every line of dialogue a verifiable transcript. It shows a writer thinking about reports and trying to distinguish plausible testimony from embellishment. His concluding judgment considers five assassin-retainers whose plans succeeded or failed, and asks why their names endured. They pursued their purposes without disguising what they wanted, he suggests. What kind of fidelity deserves remembrance when its means and its results are so costly? The chapter leaves that question open to us.

The *Shiji* does more than preserve a popular adventure. It gives Jing Ke a literary form: the market, the patron, the river, the map, the run around the pillar, and the historian's correction of legend. Its story is still exciting because a large empire is briefly brought into focus through a single sheet of territory unrolling in an audience chamber. Its deeper importance is that it remembers the people who could not stop that empire, and asks what history should do with honorable intentions when history itself records their defeat.

## Text and evidence companion (not spoken)

- Controlling text: Sima Qian, *Shiji* 86, **刺客列傳** (*Biographies of Assassin-Retainers*), Jing Ke section, at [Chinese Text Project](https://ctext.org/shiji/ci-ke-lie-zhuan). [Chapter 86 with annotated bilingual display](https://warringstates.day/library/shiji/86-ci-ke-liezhuan) provides a useful second alignment, though its English wording is not reproduced.
- Source sections: Jing Ke in Yan; Tian Guang; Fan Yuqi's head; preparations and the Yi River; scroll and dagger in the Qin audience; the Grand Historian's concluding correction of reported legends.
- Context: [*Records of the Grand Historian*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Records_of_the_Grand_Historian) and [Jing Ke](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jing_Ke). These pages are for orientation, not independent evidence for individual speeches.
- Textual boundary: Sima Qian writes centuries after many of the events he narrates and stages speeches as part of historical-literary writing. The biography is a shaped source, not a verbatim court record. The lecture does not repeat the claim that Jing Ke wounded the king, which Sima Qian explicitly disputes.
