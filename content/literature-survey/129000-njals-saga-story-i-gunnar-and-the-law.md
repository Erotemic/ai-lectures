# Njáls Saga — Story I: Gunnar, Hallgerðr, and the Price of Peace

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

A man can be the finest fighter in Iceland and still spend most of his life trying not to fight. A man can know the law better than anyone around him and still be unable to keep his friends alive. The two men are Gunnar of Hlíðarendi and Njáll of Bergþórshvoll. Their friendship stands at the center of one of medieval Iceland's great stories, but the story begins before the friendship, among marriages, debts, and insults that can follow a person for years.

Iceland in this narrative has farms, assemblies, chieftains, marriages arranged with care, and disputes whose settlement may take weeks of speech and bargaining. It has no king to stand above every household and settle every quarrel. People appeal to law, kin, reputation, and the threat of force. A death can be answered in silver, if the parties can bear to accept silver. A humiliating sentence can prove more dangerous than a wound.

The saga first draws us into the affairs of Hrútr and a woman named Unnr. Hrútr travels to Norway, becomes entangled with the powerful Gunnhild, and returns to Iceland with a curse hanging over his marriage. He and Unnr separate. The financial terms of that separation become a legal contest, and the law does not necessarily favor the person with the better claim. It favors those who can formulate the claim, summon the proper witnesses, and make a challenge credible. These opening episodes may seem remote from the later tragedy, but they establish the world. What people say at an assembly has force. What people promise in marriage has consequences. And an injury poorly settled can remain alive for another generation.

A girl named Hallgerðr grows up in this world. She is tall, beautiful, proud, and difficult to govern; her uncle Hrútr notices something troubling about her temperament. Her foster-father Þjóstólfr is devoted to her and quick to violence. When Hallgerðr marries Þorvaldr, the marriage becomes bitter. Her husband strikes her, and Þjóstólfr kills him. Later she marries Glúmr, a kinder man whom she loves, but Þjóstólfr kills him too after conflict breaks out. The men who supposedly defend Hallgerðr's honor leave her twice widowed. She is not the killer, yet she learns that another man's violence can change her life before she has finished speaking.

Now we meet Gunnar, son of Hámundr, at Hlíðarendi. He is strong and exceptionally skilled with weapons, notably his bow. He is also courteous, well loved, and reluctant to kill. His brother Kolskeggr is loyal to him. Across the landscape lives Njáll, a beardless man whose strength is judgment. He can foresee consequences that others do not yet see. His wife Bergþóra has a much sharper temper. Their sons, especially Skarphéðinn, will eventually test the limits of their father's patience.

Before Gunnar and Hallgerðr marry, Gunnar helps the divorced Unnr recover the property owed by Hrútr. Njáll supplies the legal strategy. Gunnar approaches the matter with a display of confidence and skill sufficient to make Hrútr take the challenge seriously. What seemed unreachable becomes negotiable. The episode begins the friendship between a fighter who knows when he needs advice and a lawyer who understands that even excellent advice may need a formidable person to deliver it.

Gunnar journeys abroad. He acquires fame and wealth through dangerous expeditions and comes home with the prestige of a successful traveler. At a gathering he encounters Hallgerðr. She is striking; their interest is immediate. Njáll warns him that the match will bring difficulty. Gunnar knows something of Hallgerðr's earlier marriages, but attraction and pride are persuasive. He marries her. For a while the farms of Gunnar and Njáll remain close in friendship. Their wives do not.

Bergþóra and Hallgerðr are women of strong will, accustomed to exercising authority in their households. A seating dispute and exchanges of contempt begin a feud between them. The quarrel does not stay in the hall. Servants and dependents are sent to kill one another. After each killing, Gunnar and Njáll bargain over compensation. They can still sit together and speak as friends; they can still settle a price. But the dead men are not the wives who ordered the deeds, nor the masters who paid the silver. Again and again, poorer people bear the fatal cost of a conflict conducted by households richer and more secure than they are. Njáll and Gunnar preserve their own friendship while the circle around it becomes scarred.

One of the killings is accompanied by mocking verse about Njáll's inability to grow a beard. His sons cannot bear the insult. They kill men who carry the mockery, and a family matter becomes an obligation of kinship and reputation. Njáll continues to counsel peace, but we can see the dangerous difference between his wishes and the habits his sons are learning. They love him and defend his name. They are also learning that killing can make a humiliation stop.

Then hunger and bad harvests put Gunnar's farm in trouble. He seeks food from a neighboring farmer, Otkell, and is refused the sale he wants. Hallgerðr arranges for a servant to steal food and other goods. When the theft becomes plain, Gunnar is appalled. He strikes her in the face. Hallgerðr does not fight back on the spot. She tells him she will remember the blow. The saga has taught us to listen carefully when someone says such a thing. In these stories memory may sit quietly for years before it acts.

Otkell's neighbors and advisers stir the quarrel. False stories are passed along. Gunnar is repeatedly encouraged to answer provocation, and each answer makes the next provocation easier. Otkell and his companions ride carelessly or insultingly near him, and fighting follows. Gunnar kills men while defending himself or responding to attacks, but the distinction between deserved and undeserved retaliation grows more difficult to preserve in a lawsuit. Njáll can still arrange compensation. He cannot erase the dead from the memories of their families.

More enemies gather. At horse fights and assemblies, hostile men push Gunnar to act. He fights brilliantly, sometimes against many attackers, and afterward grieves at how often blood has been required of him. His skill makes him famous; it also convinces adversaries that they must attack him in greater numbers, or through legal traps rather than open combat. Njáll warns him against certain patterns of killing. Gunnar listens. Then circumstances, anger, and pride carry him past the warning. He is condemned to a lesser outlawry: he must leave Iceland for three years. Kolskeggr is to go as well.

This is not death, at least not yet. The sentence gives Gunnar a route back to honorable life if he will endure absence. His household prepares for departure. He rides away, looks toward the slope of his home and the fields he knows, and cannot make himself continue. The farm seems to him too beautiful to leave. He turns back. Kolskeggr proceeds abroad, but Gunnar will stay on land he has chosen over the terms of his sentence. The choice is moving precisely because it is disastrous. We may admire his love of place while knowing how many people he is making vulnerable by refusing the safer road.

The outlaws' enemies have been waiting. They gather to attack Gunnar at Hlíðarendi. The assault comes to his home, not to an agreed battlefield. Gunnar shoots from the house, and men fall. The defenders are few; the attackers must find a way past a warrior who can hold them off with a bow. Eventually his bowstring is cut. Gunnar needs hair to twist another string and asks Hallgerðr for two locks of hers. She reminds him of the blow he once gave her. She refuses. It is one of the most terrible returns of memory in the saga: a private humiliation answers a public emergency, years after the man who caused it may have believed the matter over. Gunnar continues to defend himself as he can, but the attackers kill him.

The saga does not simply close the earth over Gunnar. His son Högni and Njáll's family must consider what vengeance means, and settlements are made under strained conditions. Gunnar is said to be heard speaking or singing from his burial mound, unbroken in spirit. Kolskeggr, who left Iceland as ordered, makes a very different life abroad. The contrast is hard to miss. The brother who obeyed the exile survives; the brother who could not leave his land becomes a figure of heroic memory and a dead man at his own farm.

Gunnar's story is often told as the tragedy of a great warrior betrayed by his wife. That explanation is too small for the events we have heard. Hallgerðr remembers a blow, but the killing has been prepared by many earlier choices: the theft, the provocations, the repeated settlements that settled only part of the quarrel, Gunnar's own responses, his enemies' plots, and his refusal to depart. Njáll saw the dangers and helped as best he could. Seeing is not the same as preventing.

For a time the friendship of the two men offered an alternative to feud. A fighter could accept a lawyer's advice, and a lawyer could work with someone strong enough to make his advice matter. When Gunnar dies, that possibility is wounded. But Njáll's own sons are grown, their father has taken young men into his care, and the disputes that seemed settled have begun to cross from one generation to the next. The story will turn from a warrior besieged inside his house to an entire household facing fire. It will ask whether the law can do more than count the dead after they are gone.

## Text and evidence companion (not spoken)

**Primary literary witness:** Anonymous, *Brennu-Njáls saga*, in George Webbe Dasent's public-domain English translation *The Story of Burnt Njal* (1861; one-volume edition 1900). [Online 1861 text](https://original.sacred-texts.com/neu/ice/njal/njal000.htm); [online complete chapter index](https://www.fulltextarchive.com/book/njal-s-saga/); [Project Gutenberg #17919](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/17919). The narration is original explanatory prose, not Dasent's wording. Icelandic names are simplified for audio accessibility.

**Episode locators:** Hrútr, Unnr and Hallgerðr's earlier marriages (ch. 1–18); Gunnar's introduction and the recovery of Unnr's claim (19–25); foreign journey and marriage (29–35); wives' dispute and killings (35–45); theft from Otkell and slap (47–50); horses, fights and lawsuits (53–74); Gunnar's exile and final battle (75–81). Chapter numeration follows Dasent, and editions may divide sections differently.

**Historical boundaries:** The action is set chiefly in the late tenth and early eleventh centuries; the preserved saga was composed much later, probably in the late thirteenth century. No invented dialogue here should be mistaken for a verbatim historical deposition. For manuscript context see Svanhildur Óskarsdóttir and Emily Lethbridge, eds., [*New Studies in the Manuscript Tradition of Njáls saga*](https://scholarworks.wmich.edu/mip_nmw/1/) (2018). [Wikipedia: Njáls saga](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nj%C3%A1ls_saga); [Icelandic Commonwealth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Icelandic_Commonwealth). The ethical framing around servants' lives and Hallgerðr's agency is the lecturer's interpretive analysis of the received story, not an asserted judgment of the saga's unknown author.
