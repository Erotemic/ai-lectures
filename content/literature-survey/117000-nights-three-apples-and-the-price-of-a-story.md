# One Thousand and One Nights — The Three Apples and the Price of a Story

A complete account of **The Three Apples** as an independent murder-and-detection cycle, through its pardon; the larger nested narrative of Nur al-Din Ali and Badr al-Din Hasan is identified as the tale that wins the pardon, but its own lengthy plot is reserved for a later overview. Based on John Payne, Volume I, with cross-reference to the existing Burton-witness openings.

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

A fisherman has spent an entire day on the Tigris without bringing home a meal. He has a net, a basket, and a family that will need to eat regardless of whether the river has been generous. As he walks through the streets of Baghdad, he sings a complaint about what it means to be poor: knowledge and wit do not buy bread when no one wishes to pay for them.

Three men hear him. They are not ordinary pedestrians. One is Harun al-Rashid, caliph of the great city, moving in disguise through the streets with his vizier Ja'far and the strong man Masrur. The caliph had wanted to learn how his officials were treating the population; now the fisherman reminds him that the city's prosperity does not reach everyone who lives beside its river. He asks the old man to cast his net once more, promising a handsome payment for whatever comes up.

The fisherman returns to the river and casts his net. This time it is heavy. For a moment the weight looks like luck. What he pulls ashore is a locked chest, not a haul of fish. The caliph honors the bargain and pays him. Back at the palace, the chest is broken open. Beneath a succession of wrappings and a basket is the body of a murdered young woman, dismembered and concealed for disposal in the water.

The caliph is appalled. How can such a murder have occurred in his city without anyone having answered for it? His indignation immediately takes the form of another danger. He commands Ja'far to find the killer within three days, threatening to hang the vizier and forty of his kin if he fails. One woman is dead, and now dozens more lives hang upon a deadline that may have nothing to do with the difficulty of solving a crime. The ruler who claims to demand justice is also making it more likely that innocent people will die.

Ja'far does not possess a detective's miraculous insight. He cannot interview the deceased woman, and no witness has come forward. For three days he remains unable to identify a murderer. On the appointed morning, he is brought out beneath the gallows, his relatives gathered near him for the same punishment. A crowd fills the street to see what is about to happen. Then a young man pushes forward and declares himself the killer. Before the officials can lead him away, an older man comes out of the crowd and insists that he himself committed the murder. Each wants to bear the blame.

The caliph considers hanging them both. Ja'far protests: if only one killed the woman, executing the other would be unjust. The younger man gives details that only the killer would know, and his account is accepted. The older man turns out to be the dead woman's father and the young man's uncle. The young man was married to his cousin, and they had three children. This is not a contest between a criminal and a detective who has triumphed through reasoning. A father has tried to save the man who killed his daughter. The murderer has volunteered because he cannot endure what he has done.

The caliph asks why he killed his wife. The young man begins with the ordinary details of their marriage. She was affectionate and attentive; they were happy; the children were young. Then she became ill. During her recovery she developed a longing for an apple, something that would please her senses after weeks of sickness. Her husband searched the markets of Baghdad and found none. Rather than disappoint her, he traveled south to Basra, where he was able to obtain three apples from the caliph's gardens. The journey was long and expensive for such a small gift, but he was glad to do it.

He brought them home. His wife was exhausted and hardly able to enjoy the fruit. Some days later he was walking through the streets when he saw a black slave carrying an apple. It appeared to be just like the ones he had purchased. The husband asked where the man had obtained it. The slave laughed and answered that his mistress had given it to him after her foolish husband had traveled all the way to Basra to buy it. The details seemed too precise to be invented. The husband heard them as proof that his wife had given a gift meant for her pleasure to a secret lover.

He went home and asked how many apples remained. There were only two. She had no explanation. In the swift interval between seeing a missing apple and asking his wife a question, he accepted the stranger's boast as evidence. The jealous certainty became rage. He killed his wife, cut up her body, and concealed it in the chest that the fisherman would later draw from the river. Then he returned to the house where their children lived.

One child was weeping. His son had taken an apple from his mother's room to play with it outside. A passerby had seized the fruit, beaten the boy when he tried to reclaim it, and gone away. The child had protested that his mother was ill and that his father had traveled to Basra to buy the apples. This is how the stranger knew the details. He had repeated the child's story and turned it into a boast about an imaginary affair. The wife had not betrayed her husband. The husband had murdered her because he trusted the taunt of a thief more readily than the woman with whom he lived.

The confession is a narrative of recognition that comes too late. The young man can retrace every step from the journey to Basra to the sight of the apple, the question he never truly asked, the violence, and the child's tears. Knowing the truth cannot restore his wife to life. He begs for punishment. His wife's father, standing beside him, has also lost her; his attempt to take the blame shows how tragedy can make competing loyalties impossible to reconcile.

The caliph is moved and chooses to spare the young husband. One might wonder whether this response meets the demands of the murdered woman, who cannot speak for herself. The tale's ruler is merciful to the person who confesses, but its picture of justice is more troubling than a tidy court judgment. A man killed his wife without proof, and it was easier for the court to understand the intensity of his regret than to imagine her lost future. The story leaves that discomfort in the reader's hands.

But the caliph's anger needs a new target. He orders Ja'far to find the slave whose lie set everything in motion, again allowing three days and again making his own minister's life the security for success. The vizier has escaped one impossible assignment only to receive another. The murderer appeared voluntarily the first time. The slave has every reason to disappear. Ja'far cannot solve the puzzle by the deadline and prepares for execution once more.

On the last day he takes leave of his children. As he embraces a little daughter whom he dearly loves, he feels a rounded object hidden in her clothes. He asks what it is. An apple, she answers, and one bearing the caliph's name. The household's own slave, Rayhan, had given it to her after she paid him. Ja'far now recognizes the object for what it is: the missing third apple. He summons the slave, who confesses that he snatched the fruit from a boy playing in the street and ignored the child's pleas. The court has been waiting for a clever investigator to trace a criminal through the city. The solution arrives because a father hugs his daughter.

Ja'far brings the slave before the caliph. The chain of events is complete: the fisherman, the chest, the murdered woman, the man who killed her, the child who lost the apple, and the slave who stole it have all become visible. The caliph marvels at the coincidences and the foolishness that have carried so much sorrow into one family. Yet the slave is now under threat of death. Ja'far needs a way to save him.

He offers a bargain that belongs to the *Nights* more deeply than any legal argument. If he can tell a story even more astonishing than the story of the three apples, will the caliph pardon the slave? Harun agrees. Ja'far begins the long story of Nur al-Din Ali and his son Badr al-Din Hasan, with its family quarrels, separated lovers, journeys, apparent impossibilities, and extraordinary reunion. It is a different tale and deserves to be heard whole in another sitting. For this story's purpose, we need to know the result: the caliph is delighted, the bargain is honored, and the slave lives. The dead woman's husband too is supported rather than condemned, in this received ending.

We have come a long way from the original wish for an apple. The poor fisherman wanted food, the sick woman wanted a small comfort, the husband wanted to please her, a boy wanted to play, a slave wanted to seize a thing he liked, a ruler wanted justice, and a minister wanted to survive. Almost every intention is overwhelmed by what follows. The apple is a lovely, ordinary fruit. It has no enchantment. All the power that destroys and then rescues lives belongs to human speech, imagination, haste, authority, and misrecognition.

Consider one last symmetry. The husband destroyed his wife because he believed an invented story too quickly. Ja'far saves the slave by offering a story deliberately shaped to hold the ruler's attention. In one case, listening without judgment leads to murder. In the other, listening becomes an opening for mercy. Shahrazad, telling all this to a king who has turned jealousy into a program of killing women, is not giving an innocent lesson about how every story should be believed. She is showing that people in power must learn how dangerous their own certainty can become. When the sun rises, the caliph has heard another tale. In the room beyond the tale, Shahryar has let Shahrazad live another night.

## Text and evidence companion (not spoken)

- **Controlling text:** John Payne, [*The Three Apples*](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/The_Book_of_the_Thousand_Nights_and_One_Night/The_Three_Apples), in *The Book of the Thousand Nights and One Night*, vol. I (1882–84). The larger Burton version is indexed in [Gutenberg, *The Book of the Thousand Nights and a Night*, vol. I](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/3435). The two translations are separately identified witnesses.
- **Ending boundary:** *The Three Apples* is complete as a court/murder cycle only after Ja'far trades the separate **Nur al-Din Ali and Badr al-Din Hasan** tale for the slave's pardon. This overview describes that bargain but does not claim to retell all the events of the much longer intervening family story. A future entry should cover it independently.
- **Plot checkpoints:** the poor fisherman's net, the chest and murdered wife, Ja'far's three-day ultimatum, the young man's confession and father's attempted substitution, the three apples from Basra, the child's stolen apple, the second three-day ultimatum, Ja'far's daughter and Rayhan, the pardon-for-story bargain.
- **Critical context:** [One Thousand and One Nights](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/One_Thousand_and_One_Nights), orientation only. The murders and threats are events **within the fictional narrative**; the story is not evidence of how the historical caliph Harun al-Rashid administered criminal justice.
