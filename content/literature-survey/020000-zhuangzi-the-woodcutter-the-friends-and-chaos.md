# Zhuangzi, Part Two: The Tree, the Friends, and Chaos

Original linked-episode narration. Its main text is the received *Zhuangzi* Inner Chapters 4–7. Later vignettes come separately from Outer Chapters 13, 17, and 18, which should not be treated as unequivocally authored by the historical Zhuang Zhou. Primary chapter text and Legge translation on Chinese Text Project, with received-text history checked against the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy.

A young man wants to save a country from a ruler's cruelty. He has the courage, the training, and the confidence of a student who has listened to the right teachers. His plan is to go to the ruler, speak the truth, and persuade him to change. Before he goes, he asks his teacher for advice. The teacher is Confucius, or rather a Confucius who appears as a character inside the *Zhuangzi*. That qualification matters. These conversations are literary inventions, not stenographic records of conversations between the historical Confucius and his disciples.

In the fourth chapter, Yan Hui explains that the ruler of Wei acts recklessly and his people suffer. He intends to bring wise counsel. The fictional Confucius warns that this confidence may get him killed. A ruler intoxicated with power will not necessarily receive moral instruction as a gift. He may hear it as an insult. Yan Hui tries several plans: he will be firm but modest; he will respect the court's ceremonies; he will invoke the judgments of antiquity. Each plan rests on an image of himself as the person who already knows the truth and needs only to present it correctly. The teacher rejects them.

At last Yan Hui admits he does not know what to do. The teacher proposes what the chapter calls the fasting of the mind. This is not merely going without food, and it is not a trick for becoming agreeable. The listener must relinquish the eagerness to force everything encountered into the ready-made answers with which he arrived. Listen beyond what the ears report, beyond the mind's rehearsed categories; make room for what is actually there. Only then can you enter a dangerous situation without bringing an unnecessary war of egos into it.

The counsel is unsettling. A desperate country needs help, and the student is told to begin by loosening his hold on the savior role. That can sound like an excuse for quietism. But the immediate issue is practical: how does one act under power that does not want correction? Courage without an understanding of the situation may be only another way to feed the ruler's cruelty. The chapter will not let noble intentions stand in for consequences.

A second story returns to wood. A carpenter passes a gigantic oak beside a village shrine. Its shade covers so much space that crowds could gather beneath it. His apprentices see magnificent timber. He sees nothing worth cutting. The wood is unsuitable for tools, beams, boats, coffins. The tree is declared useless. That night it appears to him in a dream and challenges the idea that a tree's value should be measured by whether someone can turn it into furniture. Fruit-bearing trees are damaged for their fruit; useful timber trees are cut while young. By becoming useless to the carpenter, the old oak has remained alive long enough to become immense.

This is not exactly the same parable as the twisted tree of the first chapter. That one challenged Huizi's inability to imagine any activity beyond building. This great tree dreams, as if its own life could answer the human assessment. It offers a point of view that the buyer of timber was not seeking. The question becomes not 'What else could we do with this thing?' but 'Why must the thing be ours to do something with?'

In the fifth chapter, people whom ordinary society might marginalize because of injuries or bodily differences have startling moral authority. A man with a punished and mutilated foot draws followers who see more deeply in him than those who treat appearance as a summary of worth. Confucius appears again, sometimes as a critic, sometimes as a student within the story's changing perspectives. The chapter's title is commonly translated as the signs, or seal, of complete virtue. We should not translate the text's ancient, often harsh descriptions of bodies into a modern lesson that physical difference magically makes someone wise. Its sharper observation is that courts and crowds have been taught to rank people by bodies and titles, and that this ranking can miss the very capacities it claims to measure.

One anecdote speaks of a man so strikingly unattractive by customary judgments that his presence ought to repel others, yet people will not leave him. A mother's children prefer to be near him; rulers seek his counsel. His influence does not rest on a beautiful face, persuasive rhetoric, or an official post. The narrator struggles to describe the steadiness of a person whose character is not exhausted by what people see. This too is a story about how a lens chosen in advance can prevent recognition.

Then death enters. In the sixth chapter, the great teacher, friends speak together about the workings of change. One becomes ill; another visits him and sees the body's form altered. Rather than protest that his body should remain as it was, the sick man imagines the vast process of transformation as a skilled maker casting material into new shapes. If an arm became a rooster, perhaps it could announce the dawn; if another part became a wheel, perhaps it could carry him. The point is not that suffering is pleasant. It is that a person who knows transformation to be the condition of existence may resist the demand that the universe freeze into a preferred version of himself.

These are not all stories of serenity. The work can be comic, severe, and metaphysically extravagant. One speaker imagines life and death as phases of the same movement, but we still hear friends at sickbeds. They do not vanish into a slogan about detachment. The friendships matter precisely because their bodies are mortal. To accept transformation does not require pretending that no one is lost.

In the seventh Inner Chapter, the subject turns again to governing. The highest ruler, says one encounter, need not display mastery by imposing an elaborate scheme on every life. A mind like a mirror receives what comes before it without dragging all things into its own private concerns. Those who wish to govern well are tempted to add features, classifications, and interventions until every difference has been standardized.

The chapter ends with a short fable that can be told in the time it takes to count to seven. The ruler of the southern sea is named Shu; the ruler of the northern sea is Hu. They often meet in the land of Hundun, the ruler of the center. Hundun entertains them kindly. Wishing to repay their host, they notice that ordinary people have seven openings in their faces by which they see, hear, eat, and breathe. Hundun has none. This seems a deficiency that benevolent visitors ought to correct. Each day they bore one opening into their host. On the seventh day, Hundun dies.

The story does not leave room for an elaborate defense of the guests. Their gratitude is real; their project is fatal. They cannot imagine that the person who welcomed them might exist in a way they do not understand. They turn kindness into surgical normalization. At the beginning of our journey a huge bird was mocked by small fliers who could not imagine its wind. Here a host is destroyed by friends who cannot imagine his body. The difference between misunderstanding and destruction is the power to carry a mistaken judgment into action.

Outside these seven chapters, the transmitted collection contains other stories now inseparable from its fame. Their literary value is substantial, but their authorship is not secure in the same way traditionally proposed for the Inner Chapters. We should enjoy them without silently making every speaker the historical Zhuang Zhou.

In the Outer Chapter called Heaven's Way, a wheelwright named Pian is working below a hall where a lord reads the writings of ancient sages. The wheelwright asks why a living man should rely on words left by the dead. His own craft contains something he can neither make precise in a formula nor pass automatically to his son. When he works too slowly, the wheel is loose; too forcefully, it is tight. The fitting rhythm lives in his hand. The lord is angry at first. What can a carpenter know about the authority of books? But the example is difficult to dismiss. Words can guide and preserve; they cannot perform every gesture they name. The book delivers this warning through words, which is part of its teasing honesty.

In the Outer Chapter called Autumn Floods, Zhuangzi and his friend Huizi walk beside a river. Zhuangzi remarks on the free movement of the fish, which seem to him happy. Huizi objects: he is not a fish, so how could he know a fish's happiness? Zhuangzi responds by turning the question. Huizi is not Zhuangzi; how does he know that Zhuangzi does not know? The exchange twists once more over where the question was asked and what counts as knowing. It is often read as a philosophy of other minds. It also works as a scene about two friends who know exactly how to irritate and delight one another with a question.

In the Outer Chapter called Perfect Happiness, Zhuangzi is found after his wife's death beating a vessel and singing. A friend is horrified. Was he incapable of grief? Zhuangzi replies that he mourned at first. Then he considered the changes by which what had no particular shape became a life, and how life becomes death. She now rests in a vast transformation that no protest can reverse. A long lament would not restore her. The scene is strange, even painful, because it lets a philosophical conviction meet the ordinary expectation that love must look a certain way. We are not required to conclude that bereaved people ought to drum. We are invited to ask what grief is for and how different lives give it form.

There is no universal verdict at the end of this collection. Some characters suffer from learning too little about a situation; others from trying to know it so completely that they destroy it. A butcher survives by understanding where not to force the blade. A wheelwright knows that a page cannot hold the whole of a motion. An old tree survives the carpenter. A generous host dies at the hands of improvers. A friend can refuse one form of mourning because he has considered what change entails.

The recurring lesson may be less a doctrine than a method of attention: when a distinction appears self-evident, ask whose distinction it is; when a plan appears unquestionably helpful, ask whom it has failed to understand; when a story seems to hand us an answer, listen for the joke still hidden inside it. These passages have lived for centuries because they leave their listeners something more durable than the comfort of being right.

## Textual boundaries and episodes

- **Inner Chapters, received 33-chapter text:** [Chapter 4, *Human World*](https://ctext.org/zhuangzi/man-in-the-world-associated-with); [5, *Signs of Full Virtue*](https://ctext.org/zhuangzi/seal-of-virtue-complete); [6, *The Great Teacher*](https://ctext.org/zhuangzi/great-and-most-honoured-master); [7, *Responding to Emperors and Kings*](https://ctext.org/zhuangzi/normal-course-for-rulers-and-kings). The ruler of the center is Hundun, not a general term for modern chaos theory.
- **Separately labeled Outer Chapters:** [13, *Heaven's Way*](https://ctext.org/text.pl?if=en&node=42060) (Wheelwright Pian); [17, *Autumn Floods*](https://ctext.org/zhuangzi/floods-of-autumn) (fish conversation); [18, *Perfect Happiness*](https://ctext.org/zhuangzi/perfect-enjoyment) (wife's death and drumming).
- [Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, textual history](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/zhuangzi/); [Wikipedia: Zhuangzi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zhuangzi_(book)).
- This is a **curated sequence**; not a verbatim translation, not a continuous chronicle of the historical Zhuang Zhou, and not a claim that all thirty-three received chapters have identical composition or authorship.
