# The Tale of the Bamboo Cutter — The Story: The Child from the Moon

A full, continuous telling of the anonymous early Japanese prose work *Taketori monogatari*, from the bamboo cutter's discovery to the smoke of Mount Fuji. The narrative follows the received Japanese text as printed in *Kokubun taikan* (1903) and presented by Koten Library. No supposed original chapter divisions are imposed on the spoken story. Original English narration rather than translation of prose or waka poetry.

An old man goes into the hills to cut bamboo. He has done it so often that the path and the tools have become part of his life. He cuts stalks to make things people need, and at the end of the day he carries the material home. Nothing in such a day promises a kingdom, a miracle, or a grief beyond ordinary understanding.

Then he sees a stalk shining from within. The light comes from the hollow part between two joints. He approaches, cuts it open, and finds a child so small she could sit in the palm of his hand. She is beautiful, and her presence appears so clearly a gift that he cannot leave her in the grove. He carries her home to his wife. Because she is tiny, they can keep her safely in a basket. They begin raising her as their own daughter, without knowing who sent her or what price a miraculous child might carry.

The old man's name is Sanuki no Miyatsuko, though the tale is better remembered by what he does than by that name. After the discovery, more astonishing things happen in the bamboo grove. Gold appears in the hollows of the stalks he cuts. A poor craftsman's work becomes a path to wealth. The couple's household grows rich, and the child grows even faster. In three months the infant has become a young woman. Her beauty seems to fill the rooms with light. The old man, however weary or troubled he feels, can be consoled simply by seeing her.

The couple have the ceremonies proper to a daughter's coming of age performed. They give her the name Nayotake no Kaguya-hime, the Shining Princess of the Supple Bamboo. Celebration fills the house. The miracle that entered in a stalk no wider than an arm has changed not only a family but the family's position in society. The bamboo cutter can now entertain people who once would have looked past him.

Kaguya-hime grows up inside a world of screens, attendants, and social expectations. The news of her beauty travels far beyond her parents' rooms. Men come to look and wait for a glimpse. Many depart after receiving nothing more. Five important suitors remain. They are accustomed to wealth, access, and the assumption that wanting something intensely is a reason to possess it. They write, plead, and keep vigil. Their titles are large; their understanding of the woman they hope to marry is very small.

The old man worries. He loves his daughter and does not want to force her, but his world teaches him that a young woman cannot indefinitely refuse the ordinary household future expected of her. Kaguya-hime is unmoved by the argument that such exalted men must be accepted because of their rank. She proposes a different test. Each suitor will be assigned an object from distant or impossible regions. Whoever can bring the genuine object may claim to have fulfilled her condition. The tasks are not ordinary tests of courage. They expose whether the men truly hear what she asks and how quickly the appearance of success matters more to them than the truth.

Prince Ishitsukuri is told to bring the stone begging bowl once used by the Buddha. It is supposed to be a treasure from India, far beyond the ordinary reach of a man eager for marriage. The prince announces a grand expedition. The years pass. At last a bowl arrives, carefully wrapped and presented with a poem about hardship on the road. It is blackened and old, and the prince expects age and his elaborate story to give it authority. But he has not been to India in the way he implies. He has taken a soot-covered bowl from a temple close to home.

Kaguya-hime examines the object. The true miraculous bowl should have a radiance that this one lacks. She returns it with a poem that exposes the distance between the desired light and the object before her. The prince tries to continue the courtship even after the fraud has been discovered. The tale makes a joke at his expense, explaining a phrase associated with throwing away shame. Behind the laughter is a sharp observation: the man who wanted to marry a woman he barely knew assumed he could deceive her with something she had no reason to accept.

Prince Kuramochi receives the task of bringing back a jeweled branch from Mount Hōrai, a marvelous island of distant immortals. This prince is more inventive. He understands that a plausible counterfeit will require skill, time, and secrecy. He arranges for craftsmen to make an exquisite branch with precious stones and precious metals and conceals the work. Then he returns with a story of perilous seas and a wondrous realm, so full of persuasive detail that the bamboo cutter begins to hope the problem of his daughter's marriage has finally been solved.

For a moment the branch appears to satisfy the demand. The workmanship is magnificent. Yet the people who made it have not been paid. The craftsmen arrive at the house looking for the wages the prince owes them. In asking to be paid, they reveal that the wondrous branch grew not on Mount Hōrai but in a workshop. Kaguya-hime does not have to discover some hidden flaw with supernatural eyes. Ordinary laborers explain the truth. The prince, exposed before the household, lashes out at the craftsmen who have spoiled his scheme. The beautiful counterfeit leaves behind unpaid work, anger, and humiliation. The object was false, but the harm done in making it was real.

A third suitor, the wealthy minister Abe no Miushi, must bring a robe made from the fur of a fire-rat, a fabulous creature whose covering is supposed to survive flames. Money is his answer to impossibility. Rather than undertake a heroic voyage, he commissions merchants to find the treasure abroad. At length a garment arrives, strikingly beautiful and costly. The minister has paid so much that he seems to have purchased certainty along with it. Kaguya-hime asks for the obvious proof: place it in the fire. If it is truly a fire-rat's robe, flame cannot destroy it.

The robe burns. The costly evidence disappears in smoke. No speech about the prestige of the seller can repair it. The story turns the minister's disappointment into another verbal joke, but its principal comedy is already complete. Money can command a remarkable object. It cannot make the object what the buyer wishes it to be.

The fourth man, the grand counselor Ōtomo no Miyuki, is sent for a jewel in the neck of a dragon. He imagines himself a commander of a magnificent hunt. He organizes men and ships to seek the creature, demanding that others risk the ocean for his courtship. His servants have little appetite for an expedition against a dragon. Men disappear into delay and excuses; the expected treasure never arrives. At last the counselor takes ship himself and finds that commanding a storm is nothing like commanding retainers.

The wind rises. Thunder shakes the ship. The sea hurls it toward destruction. The sailors know how helpless the expedition has become, and the counselor begins to beg the dragon's forgiveness. He comes ashore battered, ill, and so altered in appearance that his pursuit of glory has produced a caricature of it. Instead of examining his arrogance, he blames Kaguya-hime for sending him on the impossible errand. The suitor who imagined he could seize a dragon's jewel has learned only that danger is terrifying when it finally reaches his own body.

The fifth suitor, the middle counselor Isonokami no Maro, must find a special cowrie shell associated with a swallow's nest. It is a strange, delicate treasure, not a king's weapon or a piece of costly jewelry. To find it, the counselor orders elaborate contrivances built beneath the eaves where swallows nest. Servants search; the arrangements fail. Eventually he goes up himself, suspended in a basket. He reaches into a nest, grasps something, and believes he has succeeded. Then the apparatus gives way. He falls onto a large vessel below and suffers terrible injuries. The prize in his hand is not a shell at all. It is a swallow's dropping.

The tale has pressed its jokes very far. In the first four attempts, deception, vanity, money, and arrogance have produced public embarrassment. In this fifth, the man's desire leads to a mortal injury. Kaguya-hime sends him a poem of concern; he answers, and dies. The sequence does not allow a reader to laugh quite as freely at the end as at the beginning. It has shown us five men who accepted an impossible bargain rather than hear a refusal. Their actions belong to them, but their failures have costs beyond wounded pride.

The suitors are gone. It might seem that Kaguya-hime has won the right to live quietly with her parents. Then the emperor hears of her. A ruler does not approach marriage as one of the five suitors did. He possesses messengers, rank, and the expectation that his wishes can become orders. He sends a lady of the court to inspect the famous woman, and when Kaguya-hime refuses to present herself, the emperor tries another route. He offers the bamboo cutter honors in exchange for his cooperation. The old man's love and desire for his daughter's security are now set against the possibilities of imperial favor.

The emperor finally comes in person under the pretext of a hunt. He sees Kaguya-hime, and her beauty only strengthens his resolve. He attempts to take her away. But she fades into a shadow, vanishing from the grasp of royal authority. The miracle draws the same line that the five objects drew more comically. Rank is no reason to believe one can possess another person. The emperor, confronted with a power beyond his own, ceases trying to compel her. What remains is correspondence. Poems pass between them, words that acknowledge distance rather than abolish it. He has not married her, but they form a connection through language.

Time passes. The house that began in a shining bamboo stalk has become a place of wealth, attention, and a kind of precarious peace. Then Kaguya-hime begins to look at the moon with a sorrow that cannot be explained by the failed suitors or the emperor's letters. As the year turns, the sight of the bright disk makes her weep. Her parents try to comfort her. The old man asks what troubles her. She cannot continue hiding the truth.

She belongs to the Capital of the Moon. Her stay on earth was only for a time. On the fifteenth night of the eighth month, envoys from that other realm will come to take her back. The old couple have raised her as their daughter. To them, the prospect that she will leave is not made less terrible by discovering that her birth was marvelous. They have lived with her, cared for her, rejoiced in her growth, and become old while the girl from the bamboo was learning human affection. What she tells them sounds like a sentence passed upon the whole household.

The old man cannot accept it. He insists that he will protect her. The emperor, told of the approaching visitors, sends soldiers and archers to defend the house. Men guard the walls; others take positions on the roof. The preparations are as earnest as the pursuit of the dragon's jewel was foolish. This time the danger is real to people who are trying to keep someone they love. Yet the scene also contains a painful misunderstanding. Kaguya-hime is not being abducted by an ordinary enemy whose road can be blocked with a spear. Her return is part of an order that even she seems unable to reverse.

The appointed night comes. A light greater than daylight enters the house. The people from the moon arrive in a wondrous vehicle. The soldiers who have been carefully placed at their posts cannot bring themselves to resist. Weapons and walls lose their meaning. Doors open where they had been secured. The envoys announce that Kaguya-hime must leave, and they bring a robe of feathers that belongs to the world from which she came.

She does not rush gladly toward them. She grieves for the old man and woman, and she wishes to send word to the emperor. She leaves letters. She also leaves an elixir of immortality for the ruler who could not persuade her to live at his court. The envoys remind those assembled that earthly attachments are not the same as the order of the moon. A robe is put upon Kaguya-hime, and with it her feelings for the world she is leaving are altered. The garment does not merely make flight possible; it makes the parting complete. The grief that remains will be borne by the people who cannot put such a robe on.

The moon's visitors depart with her. The bamboo cutter and his wife are left in the house that gold had enriched and love had filled. All the wealth that followed the miraculous child cannot buy a further day with her. The emperor receives her letter and the elixir. He might have what countless powerful men have wanted: the means to live forever. But he asks what value such an extension of life could have if it cannot restore the person whose absence causes his sorrow.

He orders the letter and the medicine carried to the mountain said to be nearest heaven and burned there. The smoke rises from its summit. The tale connects the place's name, Fuji, with immortality and with the many soldiers who take part in the act, turning grief into an explanation of a mountain familiar to listeners. It is a literary play on names, not a scientific account of volcanoes or an etymology we must accept as historical fact.

The story began when light inside bamboo made an old man rich in ways he could not anticipate. It ends with smoke ascending into the sky, the sign of a gift the emperor refuses to use. Between the glow and the smoke lies a long experiment in possession. Five courtiers try to win Kaguya-hime through objects they cannot obtain honestly. An emperor tries to win her by power and discovers its limits. The parents ask only to keep the daughter they raised, and even their love cannot hold her.

The last separation changes the earlier comedy. Kaguya-hime's refusals were never merely the tricks of a proud woman testing unworthy men. She was a person whose place in this world was more fragile than anyone had understood. The people who wanted to own her could not see that. The old couple, who loved her without first asking what treasure she could bring them, knew something truer. Yet they could not keep her either. The tale leaves them with a grief that the moon can neither understand nor cure.

## Textual and bibliographic companion (not spoken)

- **Primary received text:** anonymous *Taketori monogatari* (竹取物語), based here on *Kokubun taikan: Monogatari bu 3*, Maruoka Katsura and Matsushita Daizaburō, eds. (1903), pp. 1–28. [Koten Library: Japanese text, English translation, paragraph loci and notes](https://kotenlibrary.jp/en/taketori/). The website's numbered 'chapters' are **modern editorial divisions**, not authorial original chapters. The prose above is entirely newly written and does not borrow the translation's phrasing or translate its waka.
- **Independent witness and contextual control:** [Japan Search / National Institutes for Cultural Heritage, *Taketori monogatari*](https://jpsearch.go.jp/en/gallery/ndl-rEG4P3boPx8): manuscripts, early Heian attribution and tripartite structure; [Library of Congress, seventeenth-century scroll](https://www.loc.gov/item/2021667427/). Historical translation for comparison: F. Victor Dickins, *The Old Bamboo-Hewer's Story* (1888), as reprinted in *Primitive and Mediaeval Japanese Texts* (1906), listed in [Premodern Japanese Texts and Translations](https://wwwres.meijigakuin.ac.jp/~pmjs/trans/index.html).
- **Orientation:** [Wikipedia: The Tale of the Bamboo Cutter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Tale_of_the_Bamboo_Cutter); [Wikipedia: Kaguya-hime](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kaguya-hime). These are not the textual authority for the detailed incidents.
- **Plot completeness:** Discovery / naming / gold; five individual suitors and their consequences; emperor's visit, disappearance and poetic exchange; the lunar revelation, celestial descent, feather robe and letter; the elixir burned on Fuji. The fifth suitor's fatal outcome and the craftsmen's exposure of the jeweled branch are retained; the narrative is not flattened into five identical failed errands.
- **Interpretive boundary:** Composition date is approximate and manuscript transmission is later. The text's own Fuji name-play and ideas about immortality are features of the literary work, not accepted history or geology. Neither the old translator Dickins's theological theory nor a single modern feminist interpretation is presented as the anonymous author's stated intention.
