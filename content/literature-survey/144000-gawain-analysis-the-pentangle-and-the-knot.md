# Sir Gawain and the Green Knight — Analysis: The Pentangle and the Knot

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

There is a small but revealing change at the end of Sir Gawain and the Green Knight. At the beginning, Gawain leaves Camelot carrying a perfect figure: a five-pointed knot of gold, its lines joined so that none has a beginning or end. At the finish he returns with another sort of knot, a strip of green silk tied around him because he wanted to escape death. The pentangle is what a knight ought to be. The girdle is the record of what this knight actually did. But there is a third meaning too, given by Arthur's court, which copies the girdle and makes it a sign of fellowship. One object, three meanings. The poem is not chiefly interested in whether Gawain can kill a monster. It wants to know how people live when their ethical ideals collide.

We should first put the poem in its proper historical setting. It survives in one medieval manuscript, now called Cotton Nero A X slash two, copied in the late fourteenth century. That manuscript contains three other major poems, Pearl, Cleanness, and Patience. The poems have long been associated with an anonymous writer whom scholars conventionally call the Gawain-poet or Pearl-poet. They share a striking command of alliterative verse and religiously charged storytelling, although an anonymous manuscript does not give us a name, a biography, or an incontrovertible authorial signature. The language belongs to the northwest Midlands of medieval England, rather than the London literary language familiar from Chaucer. We do not know who the poet was, whose household heard the poem first, or exactly when the work was composed.

The court depicted in the poem is older still, the legendary Arthurian court. Arthur's knights had circulated through French, Welsh, Latin, and English storytelling well before this poet wrote. It would be mistaken to imagine that the work is reporting medieval British history, or that it appeared in a single stable Arthurian universe. One romance may make Gawain the greatest champion in the land; another may portray him far less generously. Here the poet chooses him because his reputation for graciousness is almost perfect. To test an indifferent or wicked knight would prove very little. To test the model of courtesy is to discover where the model strains.

In form, this is one of the most carefully assembled narratives of its period. Four large movements, often called fitts, carry the beheading challenge, journey, castle tests, and Green Chapel reckoning. The long lines are bound together by repeated initial sounds: a kind of verbal patterning that works in the ear rather than by end-rhyme alone. Stanzas close with a short linking phrase called the bob, followed by a rhymed wheel. That alternating motion gives the poem room for expansive scenes and sudden pointed endings. A translator can convey the story while losing much of the original acoustic experience. If you listen to a reading of the Middle English, the consonants themselves seem to draw the words into dense knots.

The poet begins with the destruction of Troy and the founding of Britain, setting Arthur among kingdoms whose fame is inseparable from ruin. This is more than decorative ancestry. The first great act at Camelot is an apparent display of effortless superiority. The young court eats magnificently, dances, and exchanges gifts. Arthur refuses to dine without some extraordinary adventure. The demand for marvels becomes a liability. Into a household determined to display itself as the center of chivalry rides a challenger whose terms expose how quickly reputation can trap people. The Green Knight is dressed as a festive guest, carrying the winter-green holly of peace, yet also holds a weapon capable of severing a body. He is not simply an outside enemy. He speaks the court's own language of games and honor.

The arrangement of the two games is the poem's central engineering. A blow at Camelot creates a duty due a year later; an exchange of winnings at Hautdesert creates a duty due each evening. In the first, Gawain knows the danger but cannot know the outcome. In the second, he thinks the terms harmless until an unexpected gift makes them dangerous. The tests are interlocked. The lady's girdle matters not because it is seductive, expensive, or green in isolation, but because accepting it while pledging secrecy means that he cannot keep his word to both parties. If he rejects the gift, he believes he must face the axe unprotected. If he discloses it to Bertilak, he breaks the woman's confidence. The poem has made courage, courtesy, secrecy, and truthfulness difficult to reconcile.

The pentangle speech, often remembered as heraldic ornament, makes that conflict sharper. The five points are explained through sets of five: bodily capacities, Christian devotion, and social virtues. The image asks us to picture a life in which the various obligations reinforce one another without friction. Gawain's body and faith, for example, should support his steadfastness. Mary is represented inside his shield, facing the warrior who carries her image into danger. But the knight's trial takes place partly in a bedroom, not a battlefield. A shield cannot tell him how to respond to a host's wife who asks for a kiss while his host is away. A code designed to look flawless from a distance proves difficult to inhabit in circumstances the code has not anticipated.

This does not mean that the poem secretly despises knightly courtesy. It repeatedly shows Gawain acting with skill and restraint. The lady is clever, active, and willing to use his reputation against him. She praises his fame and then asks why a celebrated lover and gracious knight does not behave like one. If he rejects her sharply, he fails courtesy. If he indulges every suggestion, he fails loyalty. Gawain's answers are a high-wire performance, not simple prudish refusal. He meets the verbal pressure with delicacy. Through two days of exchanges he neither insults her nor appears to violate the household bargain. The concealment is therefore a test of the loyalty he has claimed to embody. The poem makes the failure small enough to be recognizably human.

Notice the contrasting hunt scenes. Bertilak's men pursue deer, then a boar, then a fox. The first day's chase is collective and controlled, though the animals suffer. The boar fights ferociously and confronts the lord. The fox survives through evasion until it is trapped. The three hunts are placed beside the lady's three visits so that the reader keeps comparing hunting and being hunted. Yet we should resist saying there is a precise one-to-one allegory in which Gawain becomes first a deer, then a boar, then a fox. The parallel works through changing tactics, heightened pressure, and bodily vulnerability. A hunted animal does whatever it can to stay alive. So, eventually, does the knight who is waiting for an axe.

The hunts add a violence that the polished exchanges in the castle might otherwise conceal. Readers sometimes remember only the bedroom conversation and the comic twist of the three kisses. The poem devotes sustained attention to the rituals of animal pursuit and cutting up game. It makes aristocratic hospitality possible through killing. The carcass offered to Gawain each evening has to be transformed by the next exchange into a kiss from the lady. The exchanges compare two economies of possession: creatures brought down in the forest and tokens secured in an intimate chamber. Even if the story's primary ethical question concerns Gawain's promise, its imagery keeps asking what it means to claim something as a prize.

Christian practice is more than a pious decoration pasted over an older folktale. The seasonal journey carries Gawain from Christmas to New Year. He seeks Mass, invokes Mary, and goes to confession before the final encounter. The Green Chapel sounds religious but looks like a hollow in the earth, and the green challenger cannot be classified as a saint or devil from his appearance. There is also the competing reassurance of the girdle, a material object that promises life by a magical power. The story does not give us an easy modern binary in which Gawain must choose science over superstition or Christianity over paganism. Instead it locates his spiritual unease in the fact that he fears death and has failed to disclose a gift. The deepest conflict is between a promise and the human wish to be spared.

The green color holds these tensions rather than resolving them. A holly branch stays green in winter. A living body appears impossibly green while surviving beheading. The silk girdle offers protection against death. Green suggests fertility and resilience, but the Green Knight's flesh also threatens Gawain's own flesh. The same color accompanies seasonal renewal and the anxiety of mortality. Scholars have debated whether the character reflects vegetation lore, the otherworld, Christian symbolism, or courtly romance traditions. The poem does not supply a single key that would decode every green object. In the story's own logic, green is the color of a question: what survives when the ordinary rules of bodies and honor are interrupted?

Morgan le Fay's appearance near the end further complicates the idea of a cleanly moral test. Bertilak says she engineered the enchantment, and says one object was to terrify Guinevere. The lady of the castle carried out her portion of the plot with her husband's knowledge. So who has been tested, and by whom? If Gawain learns to confess a limited failure, it is because two powerful people concealed the terms of a game and staged temptations around him. The revelation supplies a causal explanation, not a moral verdict from an impartial judge. We are entitled to notice its cruelty even while admiring the story's clever construction.

The woman at Hautdesert has often been reduced to an instrument for testing male chastity, but she is much more vivid than a passive lure. She understands the rules of courtly language and turns them back on their most celebrated practitioner. Yet the final explanation is given by her husband, not by her, and attributes the whole design to Morgan. The lady does not receive an extended final account of what she desired. The poem gives a woman verbal control in the center of the narrative, and then restores male explanation at its conclusion. That unevenness is worth hearing rather than smoothing away with a confident claim about what she really felt.

Gawain's own speech at the end also refuses a tidy modern hero portrait. He is rightly troubled by having hidden the girdle. But he then lists famous male figures supposedly led astray by women, from Adam onward. His anger moves from owning his choice to repeating an old rhetoric of female blame. An interpretation that takes this speech as the poem's uncontested wisdom misses the contradiction. A reading that simply excuses him because women played a part misses his own confession. The poet lets us hear a man in pain, ashamed and defensive, struggling to make his sense of himself match what happened. That is psychologically richer than a final moral pinned to the page.

And then the court laughs. We might expect a ceremony of shame, but Arthur and his knights put on green bands as a tribute. The difference between their response and Gawain's is one of the poem's hardest questions. They may be frivolous, unable to understand how seriously he feels his promise. Or they may recognize something Gawain does not: honor without forgiveness becomes intolerable. A perfect pentangle cannot admit a crack; a fellowship of human beings can make room for a member who returns with one. The green lace changes from evidence of solitary failure to a sign borne together. It is possible to judge the court's cheerfulness and still value its kindness.

The final allusion to the order of the Garter, including its motto, ties the romance to later chivalric ceremony. It also gives the reader one last small uncertainty. Are we supposed to admire an order's capacity to convert embarrassment into prestige, or to notice how quickly signs change their meaning? A strip of cloth does not possess a single interpretation by nature. Gawain calls it a reminder of fault, Bertilak a minor mark of survival, and Arthur a token of community. These meanings coexist. The poem's closing gestures keep the public story from swallowing the private one completely.

What makes Sir Gawain and the Green Knight lastingly important is not merely its memorable beheading trick, though the talking head is difficult to forget. It combines romance, seasonal writing, ethical reasoning, comedy, religious practice, and formal verbal artistry to ask what goodness looks like after a person discovers he is not entirely good. The best possible knight is brave, courteous, loyal, and truthful. The actual knight learns that fear can bend a truthful person, and that an admission of failure may be more meaningful than a performance of perfection. He survives the axe. Whether he survives his own judgment is the question the poem leaves alive.

## Text and evidence companion (not spoken)

### Evidentiary and interpretive boundaries

The poem is anonymous and is transmitted uniquely in Cotton Nero A X/2. Association with the *Pearl*, *Cleanness* and *Patience* poet is a strong conventional scholarly position, not documentary biographical certainty. The original is late medieval alliterative verse with bob-and-wheel endings; the supplied story is a modern independent paraphrase, not a rendering of that poetic texture. Analysis of Morgan, the lady, the hunts, the court's laughter and Gawain's antifeminist speech is marked as interpretation, not a claim that the poet gave readers one mandatory answer. Neither the Green Knight's supernatural feats nor Arthur's court is treated as independent historical record.

### Evidence guide

- Manuscript contents, foliation, c. 1375–1400 copying, anonymous author: British Library **Cotton MS Nero A X/2**.
- Four fitts, alliterative line, bob and wheel, nested tests, deer/boar/fox: Stanford's Arthurian literature outline and the original poem's structure.
- Heroic reputation of Gawain in varied traditions: Middle English Text Series introduction to Gawain romances.
- Lady's tests, Morgan's role, Gawain's tirade, green-girdle response: the received **fitts III–IV**; Weston is a complete but editorially selective public-domain prose witness.
- Symbolism, double standard, competing interpretations of laughter: the present lecture's critical synthesis; not an externally settled historical fact.
