# Enmerkar: Seven Mountains and a Clay Message

Original narrated traversal of the surviving Sumerian composition *Enmerkar and the Lord of Aratta*, Oxford ETCSL c.1.8.2.3, not the separate *Enmerkar and En-suhgir-ana*. Its final lines and several intervening passages are damaged.

The city of Uruk wants something it does not possess. Its temples rise above the plain, its ruler has a glorious divine ancestry, and its goddess Inana has given the city her favor. But far away, beyond a series of mountain passes, lies Aratta, a place associated with precious stone, metals, and the materials that would make a great shrine shine. Uruk has power; Aratta has what Uruk desires. Between them stand seven mountains and a messenger who is about to learn that words can be as hard to carry as a load of stone.

In the Sumerian poem the city is called Unug or Kulaba, names belonging to the city and its sacred district. Its ruler is Enmerkar, son of the sun god Utu in the language of the tale. He wants the people of Aratta to supply gold, silver, and lapis lazuli for the great sanctuary of Inana. He also wants submission. The poem makes no effort to conceal that the splendor of a holy building can be inseparable from a king's demand that another people acknowledge his supremacy.

Enmerkar brings his desire before Inana. She is the goddess who is honored in both places, which is one reason this rivalry is unstable. Both rulers imagine her favor to be theirs. She advises Enmerkar to select a messenger capable of endurance and speech. The route will not be easy. It climbs into the mountains, crosses hostile and unfamiliar country, and descends into the place where another sovereign has his own court and his own claims.

The messenger is sent with an ultimatum. Aratta must send materials and laborers to build and beautify the shrine. Otherwise its people will suffer devastation. The demand is spoken in a language of sacred authority, but it is also a threat. The valuable objects that will make Uruk magnificent are to be carried by the mountain people's own beasts and laid at the service of a distant king.

The speech includes an incantation about a time when dangerous animals will no longer threaten humanity and many lands will speak to Enlil with one voice. Its grammar and theological implications are debated, especially whether the appeal imagines the restoration of a common tongue or a change that produced linguistic division. We can hear its dream of a world unified by divine order without turning it into a simple, settled account of the historical origin of language.

By night the messenger goes under the stars. By day he moves under Utu's light. The poem repeats the ascent and descent, the movement across five mountains, six mountains, seven. Repetition makes the road visible to the ear. The king can compose a command in a moment. The envoy must spend his strength turning that command into a spoken event in a foreign court.

At Aratta, the lord asks why the messenger's master presumes to command him. The envoy announces Enmerkar's rank, his threats, and the demand for tribute. The lord of Aratta answers with an argument Enmerkar should understand: Inana, he says, is the protector of his own city. Why should a place defended by the goddess kneel to a place claiming the same patron? Aratta is not simply a warehouse from which Uruk may take what it likes.

The messenger replies that Inana has favored Enmerkar and his shrine. The lord of Aratta is shaken. His language becomes more elaborate as he searches for an answer. He compares his mountain country to a tree whose roots are traps, a barrier made strong by the goddess. He refuses easy surrender, but the confidence of the refusal is no longer secure. The tale is not giving one side a long heroic campaign and the other side silence. It gives each side a voice.

An exchange of conditions begins. The lord of Aratta proposes a test involving grain, a commodity of the river plain. His country is wealthy in stone but vulnerable to hunger. Enmerkar answers through the powers of Nisaba, goddess of grain and of scribal wisdom. He prepares and sends barley toward Aratta. The envoy who first carried only words now arrives accompanied by loads that make those words materially persuasive.

The grain transforms the courtyard. People who have endured hunger receive food. The Sumerian poem speaks of the stored barley, the pack animals, and the abundance suddenly present in Aratta. This is a strange moment if we expect a simple military epic. Enmerkar tries to compel Aratta, yet the contest makes him feed the very city he threatens. Tribute and generosity are caught together, and the rulers are forced to demonstrate the resources that sustain their claims.

The lord of Aratta still resists. Conditions become riddles, tests of objects and materials that cannot easily be named. One asks for a scepter that is made from no ordinary wood, metal, or precious stone. Enmerkar obtains wisdom from Enki and answers with an astonishing object, prepared through a procedure whose details the damaged text does not fully explain. The messenger carries it again through the mountains, and the other king is forced to look at what seemed impossible made present.

Neither ruler can simply throw down a weapon and declare the matter settled. They need the signs of legitimacy: a suitable scepter, a suitable champion, the patronage of gods, the favor of a city. To the modern ear the tests may sound fantastical, but their political logic is familiar. A ruler's authority must be recognized, displayed, and repeatedly defended. Prestige is never only a feeling one has about oneself. Other people must acknowledge it.

The lord of Aratta challenges Enmerkar again: send a garment of no ordinary color, and send a champion to compete with his own. Enmerkar's answer grows long. His messenger has crossed the seven mountains again and again, carrying threats, replies, evasions, demands, and riddle answers. The poem dwells upon the envoy's exhaustion. He is not a disembodied voice. He has a mouth that grows heavy and a memory that can be overfilled.

Finally the message becomes too long for the man to repeat. Enmerkar presses clay and writes upon it. The poem makes an extraordinary claim: a written message, inscribed on a tablet, replaces speech carried intact within a messenger's mind. It treats the act as a beginning, the establishment of a way of sending complex words across a distance that could overwhelm even an expert speaker.

This does not mean historians have discovered the literal invention of writing in an encounter between two identifiable kings. Sumerian writing has an archaeological history, which is not verified by a literary scene. The scene matters because of what its audience saw in writing. The tablet is an extension of royal authority. It can hold words that no single bearer can manage, carry a command across mountains, and confront a rival with an object that cannot be dismissed as the envoy's faulty memory.

The messenger makes his journey again and places the tablet before the lord of Aratta. The poem describes the written signs as little wedges or nails. He looks at the message and grows angry. The marks on the clay cannot themselves feed his people or yield his city to Uruk. Yet they are not merely marks; they insist that someone absent remains present in the court, making demands.

Then the goddess's favor appears to move again. The storm god brings thunder to the mountains. Grain springs up in Aratta as though fertility has answered the city in its own language. The lord sees an abundance no longer supplied only by Enmerkar's envoy. He declares that Inana has not abandoned Aratta. His refusal to submit may no longer rest on nothing but pride. The gods of the tale do not always behave like permanent political possessions.

The poem moves toward a contest involving champions and sacred display. A remarkable passage evokes the goddess's movement among cities and peoples, imagery of fertility, floods, and divine favor. The last portion grows damaged. There are glimpses of prosperity in Aratta, lists of flocks multiplying, and precious goods being brought for the shrine of E-ana. But too many lines have disappeared to narrate a neat scene in which a named general takes possession of the mountain city.

This story belongs with other Sumerian tales of Enmerkar and of Lugalbanda, a legendary world in which Uruk's relations with Aratta are told in more than one composition. Here the weapon that finally matters most is neither the slingshot nor the sword. It is the command sent through a reliable carrier and, at the critical moment, fixed on a clay surface. The poem does not deny physical force, but it understands that rival cities may struggle through demands, gifts, boasts, and writing long before an army arrives.

It also tells us something about the messenger. The king commands; the messenger travels. The king speaks; the messenger hears. When the king's words exceed what a human mouth can bear, the king invents within the story an object that can travel in place of memory. We remember the mighty Enmerkar because the poem names him. But the scene of writing becomes powerful because an ordinary functionary has reached the limit of his endurance.

The mountains still stand when the tablets end. The story has not become a proven historical record of Aratta's location, of a universal language, or of the invention of letters. It has become something more sharply literary: a struggle in which the difference between speaking and writing changes what rulers can demand of people they cannot see.
