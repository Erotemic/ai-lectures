# Recognize acute illness and deterioration

## Stroke, Heart Attack, and Severe Allergic Reactions

A person suddenly cannot lift one arm. Another has crushing discomfort in the chest and breaks into a cold sweat. A third develops throat swelling and difficulty breathing after eating a food to which they are severely allergic. All three situations can require immediate emergency medical care, yet they do not have the same cause or first-aid response. A useful responder recognizes the pattern, calls for professional help promptly, and takes only actions appropriate to the suspected emergency.

In the United States, call nine-one-one for suspected stroke, heart attack, or anaphylaxis. Elsewhere, use the local emergency number. Do not postpone that call to complete a home examination or to see whether symptoms settle. Emergency dispatchers can guide immediate steps and arrange appropriate transport. A bystander does not need to establish a definitive medical diagnosis before requesting help.

### Stroke: a sudden loss of function

A stroke occurs when blood supply to part of the brain is interrupted or a blood vessel bleeds into or around the brain. The specific treatment depends on which event has occurred, so it is important not to assume that a remedy for a blood clot is appropriate for every suspected stroke. The first-aid task is rapid recognition and activation, not selecting a hospital medication.

Think of the letters B, E, F, A, S, T. Balance: sudden loss of coordination or unexplained difficulty walking. Eyes: sudden vision changes. Face: one side droops or feels weak. Arms: one arm drifts or cannot be raised normally. Speech: words are slurred, confused, or difficult to produce. Time: call emergency services immediately. These are recognition prompts, not a complete list. Sudden severe headache, especially with other neurological symptoms, can also be important.

The onset time matters. If you know when the person was last well, tell the dispatcher and medical team. If you woke to discover symptoms, distinguish the time you discovered them from the last time you were known to be normal. This information can affect treatment decisions. Do not delay the call to search for a perfect timestamp.

A transient ischemic attack may produce stroke-like symptoms that go away. Resolution does not make the event safe to ignore. The person still needs urgent medical evaluation, because transient symptoms can signal a serious vascular problem. Do not offer aspirin as a generic response to suspected stroke: a bystander cannot determine whether the event involves bleeding. Do not give food or drink to someone who may have impaired swallowing.

### Heart attack: blood supply to heart muscle

A heart attack involves insufficient blood flow to part of the heart muscle, usually because of a coronary artery blockage. The person may experience pressure, squeezing, fullness, or pain in the chest. Discomfort can spread to an arm, back, neck, jaw, or stomach. Shortness of breath, sweating, nausea, unusual fatigue, or lightheadedness may appear, sometimes without the classic intense chest pain. Symptoms can start gradually, disappear and return, or be difficult to describe.

The first action is to activate emergency services and follow dispatch guidance. Have the person stop exertion and remain in a position that makes breathing easier. Note symptoms and timing, and be ready to report known medications or allergies. Do not have the person drive themselves to the hospital. Emergency medical personnel can assess and treat problems during transport.

Guidelines provide a narrow role for aspirin in some alert adults with nontraumatic chest pain while waiting for emergency help, with important exceptions for allergy or prior medical advice and a provision to defer when uncertain. This is not a universal rule for every collapse or chest complaint. The safest educational principle is to involve the dispatcher and follow current local protocols rather than experimenting with medicines. Medication decisions for suspected stroke are different from those for possible cardiac causes.

Heart attack and cardiac arrest must also be distinguished. During a heart attack, a person may remain awake and breathing. Cardiac arrest means the heart is no longer producing effective circulation; the person becomes unresponsive and is not breathing normally. In that situation, activate emergency services, begin CPR and use an available AED according to training and dispatcher instructions. Do not wait for a chest-pain story before recognizing arrest.

### Anaphylaxis: a systemic allergic emergency

Anaphylaxis is a rapidly developing, potentially life-threatening allergic reaction. It can involve breathing difficulty, swelling of the lips or tongue, a sensation of throat closing, hives or flushing, severe gastrointestinal symptoms, dizziness, or collapse. Not every person has a visible rash. The combination of symptoms and a possible trigger may be informative, but absence of an obvious food or insect exposure does not rule out anaphylaxis.

Epinephrine is the primary immediate treatment for anaphylaxis. A person at risk may carry a prescribed epinephrine autoinjector. The 2024 American Heart Association and American Red Cross first-aid guidelines recommend that the person use the available autoinjector, with a first-aid provider assisting if needed, and that emergency services be activated. Follow the instructions for that specific device and the person's emergency plan. An antihistamine is not an adequate substitute for epinephrine when anaphylaxis is occurring.

Symptoms may improve after an injection and then require further medical assessment or treatment. Do not interpret a temporary improvement as a reason to cancel emergency assistance. If breathing becomes absent or abnormal and the person is unresponsive, recognize possible cardiac arrest and follow the CPR and AED pathway. If the person is alert, avoid unnecessary walking or standing; positioning should account for breathing difficulty, injury, vomiting, and dispatcher directions.

### A responder's shared framework

The shared steps across these emergencies are simple but consequential. Recognize that something serious may be happening. Call for help early. Describe observable findings rather than claiming certainty about a diagnosis. Record when symptoms began, what changed, any relevant exposures or medicines, and the person's response to assistance. Monitor consciousness and breathing, and be ready to change course if the person's condition deteriorates.

You can learn pattern recognition without pretending that every symptom is specific. Shortness of breath can occur in heart attack, anaphylaxis, lung disease, or anxiety. Dizziness alone has many causes. The point is not to match every symptom to one disease; it is to identify combinations and sudden changes that require immediate evaluation.

Fast action is valuable because these conditions can worsen quickly, and effective treatments are often time-dependent. The bystander's role is to shorten the interval to professional care and provide appropriate immediate assistance, not to solve a complicated diagnosis on the living-room floor.

## Breathing Distress: Recognition and Escalation

Breathing distress is one of the observable changes that can make an uncertain illness urgent.

Someone at a family gathering suddenly stops joining the conversation. They lean forward, breathe faster than before, and appear frightened. Their breathing is noisy, or perhaps so quiet that it is difficult to hear. A bystander may say that the person is just anxious. Another may say it is asthma. At this stage, neither label is as useful as recognizing that the person may be struggling to move enough air and needs help.

### Look at the work of breathing

Breathing distress means breathing is difficult or insufficient, and it can arise from many causes. The visible signs may include rapid or unusually slow breathing, labored use of neck and chest muscles, inability to speak in complete sentences, unusual wheezing or high-pitched sounds, a weak cry in a child, or marked changes in skin color and alertness. A person may sit forward instinctively because that position makes breathing easier. A young child may show nostril flaring or skin pulling in between the ribs with each breath.

The absence of loud wheezing does not prove that a person is fine. Very little air movement can produce few audible sounds even when the situation is severe. Blue or gray skin discoloration can be a late or variable sign, and recognizing danger should not depend on waiting for it. Sudden confusion, exhaustion, collapsing, or inability to stay awake signal a serious problem regardless of what sounds can be heard.

First aid begins with observation and escalation, not a remote diagnosis. Check scene safety, responsiveness, breathing and whether the person can communicate. If there are severe signs, call emergency services immediately, or direct a specific bystander to call while you remain with the person. Provide the location, the observed problem, relevant history if known, and whether the person is conscious and breathing. Follow dispatcher instructions.

### Several mechanisms can look similar

An airway can narrow because of asthma or another respiratory condition. An allergic reaction can cause swelling and breathing difficulty, sometimes accompanied by hives, vomiting, dizziness, or low blood pressure. A foreign object can obstruct the airway suddenly, especially around food or small objects. Heart conditions can produce shortness of breath, sometimes without classic chest pain. An infection, lung problem, chemical exposure, or carbon monoxide exposure may also present with breathing difficulty. Panic can change breathing too, but serious causes should not be dismissed as anxiety on appearance alone.

The difference between **an obstructed airway** and **difficulty breathing through an open airway** is especially important. A person with effective coughing after a choking episode is in a different situation from a person who cannot speak, breathe, or cough effectively. Training in age-specific choking response provides the appropriate intervention. Blind finger sweeps or improvised techniques can make obstruction worse. This part of the discussion concerns recognizing distress and getting the right help, not replacing hands-on choking and resuscitation instruction.

The environment can create a second danger. If multiple people in a confined space have headaches, dizziness, or breathing symptoms near a fuel-burning appliance, suspect a hazardous atmosphere such as carbon monoxide and prioritize moving away from exposure without becoming another victim. A rescuer must not enter an unsafe area simply to start a familiar first-aid routine.

### Support the person while help is coming

For a responsive person in respiratory distress, encourage a position of comfort. Often that is sitting upright or leaning slightly forward, but do not force them into a posture that worsens symptoms. Reduce unnecessary exertion and remain calm and observant. Loosen restrictive clothing if appropriate, and help the person access their own prescribed emergency medication, such as an inhaler or epinephrine auto-injector, according to training, local rules, and the relevant emergency plan. Do not give unfamiliar medications or delay emergency help in order to search for a treatment.

Ask short, useful questions if the person can answer: What happened just before this began? Do they have a known condition and a prescribed rescue device? Was there a known allergen, bite, or new exposure? Are they able to speak? Is this getting worse? These questions should not become a long interview while they struggle for air. A person who cannot answer because of breathing difficulty requires prompt emergency response.

Observe changes over time. Can they still talk? Is their breathing becoming quieter because it is improving, or because they are tiring? Are they becoming less responsive? Has an appropriately administered prescribed rescue medication helped? Report these observations to responding professionals. Use a clock if possible, but do not wait for a time threshold when severe distress is present.

### What if the person becomes unresponsive?

If the person collapses and is unresponsive, assess breathing promptly using current first-aid guidance. If they are not breathing normally, activate emergency response, begin CPR according to your training, and use an AED as soon as it is available. Gasping is not normal breathing. If an unresponsive person is breathing normally, monitor them and position them appropriately while protecting their airway and following dispatcher instructions. Age and other circumstances can change the exact intervention, which is why supervised CPR/AED training matters.

Do not assume a person must have a dramatic collapse before needing help. Respiratory distress can progress while someone is still conscious. The crucial skill is to recognize severe work of breathing early enough that emergency care is already on its way if the situation worsens.

### An observation exercise

Imagine three scenes. In the first, a person walks up stairs, breathes quickly, then recovers while speaking comfortably. In the second, someone at rest cannot finish a sentence, is pulling hard at the neck with each breath, and seems confused. In the third, a child has a weak cry and visible retractions between ribs. These scenes do not establish diagnoses; they illustrate increasing concern about breathing effort and effectiveness. The second and third descriptions warrant immediate emergency assessment rather than a wait-and-see approach.

The conclusion is deliberately simple. You do not need to name the disease to help someone who is struggling to breathe. Notice the work of breathing, the person's ability to speak and remain alert, and the speed of deterioration. Call for help early when serious signs appear, support their position and prescribed care within your training, and be ready to follow resuscitation guidance if breathing stops. Recognition and timely escalation are practical skills in their own right.

## Seizures: Recognition and Scene Care

A person at the dinner table suddenly stops responding. A few seconds later they slump to the floor and begin jerking. People may panic, try to hold their arms still, or look for something to put between their teeth. Those impulses are understandable, but they can cause harm. First aid for a seizure is primarily about preventing injury, keeping track of time, recognizing when emergency help is needed, and supporting the person during recovery.

### Recognize that seizures have several forms

A seizure is a disturbance of electrical activity in the brain. It does not always look like the dramatic full-body shaking shown in films. Some episodes involve staring, brief lapses of awareness, repeated automatic movements, unusual sensations, or sudden confusion. Others involve loss of awareness, collapse, and rhythmic muscle movements. The observer's task is not to diagnose the neurological type. It is to recognize a change in awareness or movement, protect the person from the environment, and describe what actually happened.

If someone begins convulsing, note the start time as soon as you can. You do not need perfect timing to be useful, but duration is one of the most important facts for deciding whether to activate emergency services. Many seizures stop after a short interval. A prolonged seizure, or one that repeats without the person recovering between episodes, is a serious medical emergency. The established first-aid guidance uses approximately five minutes as a critical threshold for ongoing seizure activity; do not wait beyond it to seek help.

### Make the space safer

Move nearby hard, sharp, or hot objects out of the way. If the person is falling, help ease them to the ground when it is safe to do so. Place something soft and flat beneath the head if available. Remove eyeglasses and loosen restrictive clothing around the neck. Stay nearby and ask bystanders to give the person space. If practical, gently position them on their side, especially when they are not awake and aware, to help keep the airway clear. Do not force a position against violent movements or place yourself in danger.

Do not restrain their limbs or attempt to stop the seizure physically. The muscles are not moving because the person chooses to struggle, and holding them down can injure joints, muscles, or the person trying to help. Do not put a spoon, finger, wallet, or any other object into the mouth. A person cannot swallow their own tongue. Inserting an object can damage teeth or obstruct the airway. Do not offer water, food, or oral medication during a seizure or while awareness remains reduced.

Breathing may look irregular during a convulsive episode. Do not attempt mouth-to-mouth breathing during active convulsions. As soon as the seizure ends, reassess responsiveness and normal breathing. If the person is unresponsive and not breathing normally after seizure activity has stopped, follow emergency-dispatch instructions and begin the appropriate CPR and AED response. The distinction between activity still in progress and the condition after it stops matters.

### Know when emergency activation is indicated

Call emergency services for a first known seizure, an ongoing seizure that reaches about five minutes, repeated seizures without recovery between them, breathing trouble or choking, injury, a seizure in water, or failure to return to usual awareness. The 2024 American Heart Association and American Red Cross first-aid guidance also identifies seizures in pregnancy and in infants younger than six months among the situations warranting emergency medical services. For a known seizure disorder, an established individualized seizure action plan can provide additional instructions, but the presence of the plan does not negate emergency criteria.

If the event occurs near traffic, water, stairs, or machinery, the environment may create immediate danger even for a shorter seizure. Move hazards and secure the area first. For a person with a prescribed rescue medication, follow their documented action plan only if you have the relevant training and authority. Do not improvise drug dosing based on seeing a medicine in a bag.

An important detail is that 'seizure ended' does not necessarily mean 'person has recovered.' They may be sleepy, confused, frightened, or sore. The period after the seizure can involve reduced awareness. Stay with the person, explain calmly what happened as they become responsive, and help them regain a safe position. Do not rush them to stand or leave them alone simply because the shaking stopped. If they do not recover as expected, that itself is a reason for emergency assessment.

### Make a useful observation record

When it is safe, note what happened before the event. Did the person suddenly stop speaking? Were there repeated movements on one side? Did they fall? Did the whole body shake? Approximately how long did the activity last? Did another event begin before recovery? Was there an injury or difficulty breathing? These details help clinicians distinguish types of events. A careful written description is more useful than assigning a diagnosis such as 'grand mal' after a brief glance.

People who experience seizures may feel exposed or embarrassed afterward. Keep unnecessary onlookers away, respect privacy, and speak directly and calmly when they can understand. Explain the sequence without alarm or accusation. A person who has a known condition may have a plan for contacting family or a care team; ask what they need once they are alert, while following emergency guidance when indicated.

Seizure first aid is a striking example of effective restraint by the helper: we do less physically, but we pay closer attention. Stay, make the surroundings safe, time the event, support the airway by suitable positioning, and recognize the thresholds for emergency care. Those are learnable actions. They matter more than attempting to control movements that cannot be safely controlled by force.

## Diabetes Emergencies: Recognition and First Response

During a family gathering, someone who has diabetes becomes shaky, sweaty, and confused. They have trouble finishing a sentence. Another person suggests giving them insulin. A third reaches for a glass of juice. What should a first-aid provider actually do? The immediate task is to recognize a potentially serious change in condition, check whether the person can safely swallow, and get appropriate help. The difference between low blood sugar and dangerous high-blood-sugar illness matters enormously, but a bystander must not try to solve that uncertainty by improvising medication.

### Why glucose problems affect the whole body

Glucose is a major fuel used by the body, and the brain is particularly sensitive to inadequate supply. Insulin helps the body's tissues use and store glucose. Other hormones, including glucagon, help raise circulating glucose when needed. When blood glucose falls too low, a person may become hungry, shaky, sweaty, pale, dizzy, anxious, or unusually irritable. As it worsens, they may appear confused, slur words, have a seizure, or lose consciousness.

High blood glucose may develop more gradually. Thirst, frequent urination, fatigue, and dehydration can occur. In diabetic ketoacidosis, inadequate effective insulin leads the body to produce excess ketones, creating a dangerous acid-base disturbance. Warning signs can include nausea or vomiting, abdominal pain, deep or rapid breathing, fruity-smelling breath, extreme fatigue, and confusion. This is a medical emergency requiring professional treatment. Similar symptoms do not establish the diagnosis; other acute illnesses, including stroke, may produce confusion or altered behavior.

The observer should notice the person, not just the label 'diabetes.' Ask what changed, whether the person can respond normally, whether they have a glucose monitor or established care plan, and whether anyone knows their usual behavior. A continuous glucose monitor or home meter can provide useful context if it is available and the person or their designated helper can use it appropriately. But treatment of a clearly ill person must not be delayed by a search for equipment or a perfect number.

### Low glucose: an immediate action with a firm condition

The 2024 American Heart Association and American Red Cross first-aid guidance recommends oral glucose for suspected hypoglycemia when the person is **awake and able to swallow**. Glucose tablets are a preferred option when available; ordinary fast-acting dietary sugars can be used when they are not. The practical aim is to supply glucose promptly while the person can swallow safely, then observe for improvement according to the individual's plan and the guidance being followed.

The critical condition is awareness and safe swallowing. A confused person who can still clearly follow instructions and swallow may differ from someone who is barely responsive, choking, or unable to protect their airway. Never pour a drink or place food in the mouth of a person who is unresponsive, having a seizure, or cannot swallow. Oral sugar in that situation can enter the airway and cause harm.

Activate emergency medical services if a person with suspected low glucose cannot swallow, has a seizure, or does not improve within about ten minutes after oral glucose, as specified by the 2024 first-aid recommendations. Call sooner for worsening symptoms or other signs of emergency. In the United States, call 911 and follow the dispatcher. If the person becomes unresponsive and is not breathing normally, follow the standard emergency response for breathing assessment, CPR, and AED as directed by training and dispatch.

A person with a prescribed glucagon rescue product may have an individualized emergency plan. Family members or trained caregivers may be taught how to administer it. The form and instructions vary: injectable products and nasal products are not interchangeable in their use. Follow the actual prescribed product instructions and training; obtaining professional emergency care remains important after a severe episode. A first-aid lecture cannot substitute for a specific person's plan.

### High glucose: do not improvise insulin

A person with diabetes who is very thirsty and nauseated after several days of illness may have high glucose, dehydration, ketones, or something else. A bystander cannot safely choose an insulin dose from these symptoms. Insulin is a powerful medication with potentially serious consequences if given incorrectly. Professional care and the person's documented management plan govern medication decisions.

If there is vomiting, inability to keep down fluids, deep or difficult breathing, marked confusion, or other signs suggesting diabetic ketoacidosis, activate emergency care. The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention identifies diabetic ketoacidosis as a medical emergency. Someone who is extremely ill should not be left alone to 'sleep it off.' If they are alert and can swallow, their prescribed sick-day instructions may include glucose and ketone checks and fluids, but severe symptoms require urgent medical assessment rather than trial-and-error home treatment.

Not all dangerous high-glucose emergencies involve the same ketone pattern. Hyperosmolar hyperglycemic state and other illnesses can also cause serious dehydration and altered mental status. It is unnecessary for a first-aid provider to distinguish these mechanisms at the scene before requesting help.

### Support the person while help is coming

Approach calmly, introduce yourself, and obtain consent when possible. Keep the person seated or otherwise safely positioned, away from stairs, vehicles, or hot equipment. Ask about medical-alert identification and their emergency plan without delaying emergency activation. Record when symptoms began, what the person ate or took if known, any available glucose reading and timestamp, what was administered, and whether the condition changed. Do not invent a medical history from an alert bracelet; use it as a clue to share with responders.

If the person is recovering after oral glucose, the episode still deserves appropriate follow-up under their care plan. They may need longer-acting food, monitoring, or medical contact depending on the cause and whether symptoms recur. A brief improvement is not proof the underlying risk has ended, particularly when medication action may continue. Keep the person supported until they are reliably alert and an appropriate next step is arranged.

The decisive lesson is about recognizing limits. A bystander can observe, offer oral glucose to someone awake and able to swallow when low glucose is suspected, recognize severe illness, call for help, and communicate precise observations. They should not force food into an impaired person's mouth or invent an insulin treatment. These boundaries make first aid effective precisely because they are specific.

## Dehydration, Shock, and Signs of Deterioration

Across these different causes, the direction of change in the person’s condition remains important.

A person finishing a long walk feels dizzy, sits down, and asks for water. Another person becomes pale and confused after an injury. Both may look weak, but the situations are not interchangeable. One may have a relatively straightforward fluid deficit; the other may be experiencing shock, a failure to deliver adequate blood flow to vital tissues. Shock is an emergency that can have causes far beyond dehydration. First aid begins by recognizing the difference in urgency, checking responsiveness and breathing, and calling for help before the person deteriorates further.

### Fluid loss is not the whole story

The body maintains water and dissolved electrolytes across blood, cells, and other compartments. Fluid is lost through sweat, breathing, urine, vomiting, diarrhea, and other routes. In ordinary exertional dehydration, the person may be thirsty, tired, lightheaded, or have reduced urine output. These observations are useful but not diagnostic on their own. Similar symptoms can occur with heat illness, low blood sugar, bleeding, infection, medications, or heart problems.

Shock is a different physiological concept. It means tissue perfusion is inadequate for the body's needs. Major bleeding can reduce circulating blood volume. A severe allergic reaction can alter blood vessel behavior and airway function. Heart failure or a dangerous rhythm can limit forward blood flow. A severe infection can disrupt circulation. These causes require different definitive treatments. The first aider's job is not to make a final medical diagnosis from skin color; it is to recognize the warning signs and organize an appropriate response.

Possible signs of deterioration include new confusion, fainting, profound weakness, unusual sleepiness, cool or mottled skin, difficulty breathing, chest pain, repeated vomiting, or pulses that seem weak or unusually rapid. Some people show different patterns because of age, medication, temperature, or underlying conditions. A person can have serious internal bleeding without visible blood. A person can also become unwell without describing thirst.

### Use the first-aid sequence

Begin with scene safety. Look for traffic, power, extreme temperature, and other hazards. Approach, introduce yourself, and assess responsiveness and breathing. Activate emergency medical services promptly when the person is confused, breathing abnormally, severely injured, has suspected shock, or is otherwise critically ill. Follow dispatcher instructions. If the person becomes unresponsive and is not breathing normally, follow current CPR and AED guidance appropriate to the responder's training.

While help is coming, address an obvious life-threatening cause such as severe external bleeding using established first-aid methods. Avoid unnecessary movement, especially when injury is suspected. Positioning should account for breathing, consciousness, trauma, vomiting, and comfort. For an alert person with suspected shock, lying flat may be reasonable if tolerated. If the person is vomiting or at risk of losing airway protection, positioning may need to favor airway safety. Do not rigidly elevate legs in every case; pain, trauma, breathing difficulty, and other circumstances can make a different position safer. The American Heart Association and American Red Cross guidance is conditional for precisely this reason.

Help the person avoid additional heat or cold stress without delaying emergency response. Monitor how responsiveness, breathing, and skin findings change. Record relevant information: the time symptoms began, observed fluid losses, medications if known, injury mechanism, and any important changes. A concise, factual handoff to responders is more useful than an unsupported diagnosis.

### Oral rehydration has conditions

For an awake, oriented person with exertional dehydration who can swallow safely and has no signs of shock, confusion, or other emergency, offering potable water or a suitable rehydration drink can be appropriate. The 2024 AHA/American Red Cross first-aid guideline supports oral rehydration under these conditions. Carbohydrate-electrolyte drinks may be useful in some exertional settings, but the availability of an ideal product should not delay access to safe drinking water when oral rehydration is appropriate.

A person who is confused, repeatedly vomiting, struggling to breathe, or unable to swallow safely should not simply be urged to drink. Fluids given by mouth can be aspirated or delay necessary treatment. Severe dehydration from prolonged diarrhea or vomiting, especially in a young child or older person, deserves medical evaluation. Water is not a substitute for addressing bleeding, heatstroke, anaphylaxis, or a cardiac problem.

The first aider must also recognize that one measurement does not establish safety. A pulse oximeter or blood pressure reading may be informative in context, but readings can be inaccurate, and apparently reassuring values do not override obvious clinical deterioration. A person who is becoming less responsive is worse even if a single displayed number looks unchanged.

### Compare two stories

In the first story, an adult finishes strenuous exercise on a hot day. They are alert, breathing normally, have no chest pain or neurologic symptoms, and feel better after resting in a cooler place and taking oral fluid. They still need observation, and worsening symptoms change the response. But the initial pattern is consistent with an ordinary exertional fluid deficit.

In the second story, someone stumbles after an injury, grows pale, cannot clearly explain what happened, and becomes drowsy. The absence of visible bleeding does not make this a minor dehydration episode. Activate emergency help, monitor breathing and responsiveness, address immediate hazards, and do not try to walk the person off the problem or force oral fluids.

The goal is to notice a trajectory rather than argue about a label. Record what you see and how quickly it changes. When the signs point to compromised circulation or consciousness, first aid is a bridge to emergency care. Recognition, safe positioning, immediate help, and clear handoff are its most important contributions.

## Child and Infant Illness: Recognizing Deterioration and Calling for Help

The same attention to change needs an age-specific interpretation for infants and children.

An infant who is normally alert at feeding time suddenly seems difficult to wake. A preschool child who was playing an hour ago now has trouble breathing and can barely speak. In both cases, the urgent problem is not to identify the exact infection at home. It is to recognize that the child's condition has changed in a way that can require immediate medical attention.

Children are not simply smaller adults. Infants can show illness through changes in feeding, movement, breathing, color and responsiveness before they can describe pain. Older children may say only that they feel bad. First-aid skill here means observing what the child is doing now, comparing it to their usual behavior and knowing when the evidence calls for emergency help.

### Appearance, breathing and circulation

A practical initial assessment has three broad questions. How does the child appear and respond? How are they breathing? Do their color, movement and other signs suggest that circulation may be impaired? Pediatric emergency guidance often organizes these observations around appearance, breathing and circulation, though a lay responder is not expected to conduct a full clinical assessment.

Appearance includes alertness, eye contact, muscle tone, interaction with a caregiver and ability to be comforted. A newborn who has become unusually floppy or cannot sustain feeding may be showing a serious change. A child who is newly confused, extremely difficult to wake or unresponsive warrants urgent emergency response. The fact that a child has a fever does not tell you by itself how sick they are; behavior and physiological warning signs matter.

Breathing deserves direct observation. Look for visible struggle, rapid or labored breaths, unusual grunting, pulling in around the ribs or neck, pauses in breathing and a change in the color of lips or tongue. Some infants have variable breathing patterns during sleep, but new persistent distress is not something to dismiss as ordinary. Blue, gray or markedly abnormal color with breathing difficulty is an emergency sign. Do not delay calling emergency services while searching online for the right diagnostic label.

Circulation is harder for a layperson to assess accurately, but marked pallor, cold or mottled extremities accompanied by lethargy, collapse, new confusion or other concerning changes can be significant. A child who has persistent vomiting or diarrhea and is increasingly sleepy may be becoming dehydrated or suffering another serious illness. No single home measure of skin color, capillary refill or pulse proves or excludes shock.

### Infants need an especially low threshold for evaluation

Very young infants have different fever and illness thresholds from older children. A baby younger than about three months with a measured fever needs prompt medical assessment, even if the baby briefly looks comfortable. A poorly feeding newborn, a baby with a changed cry, green vomit, reduced movement or new weakness may need urgent evaluation without waiting for a high fever. The exact age, temperature measurement method and clinical instructions matter; follow current pediatric guidance and local emergency advice.

The important point is not to memorize a complicated list of diagnoses. Instead, track a change from the infant's normal pattern: feeding less or unable to finish, much harder to awaken, unusual breathing effort, abnormal color, persistent vomiting or fewer wet diapers in context. Those observations can be conveyed clearly to a clinician.

### What a parent or caregiver can do

Begin with scene safety, the child's responsiveness and breathing, and whether an immediately life-threatening condition is present. Call emergency services for unresponsiveness, absent or abnormal breathing, severe respiratory distress, rapidly decreasing alertness or other major red flags. Follow dispatcher instructions, including age-appropriate CPR/AED guidance when indicated. Pediatric CPR techniques differ by age and are best practiced in an accredited course.

If the child is awake and breathing normally but a concerning symptom remains, seek prompt pediatric guidance. For a very young infant with fever, use the urgent pathway recommended by the child's clinician and current pediatric guidance rather than treating it like an ordinary fever in an older child. Avoid giving food or drink to a child with impaired consciousness or inability to swallow safely. Do not force medication, induce vomiting or use adult medication assumptions to manage a child.

While help is arriving, a caregiver can observe and record the timeline. When did the change begin? Was there fever, cough, injury, possible ingestion, allergy exposure or a new medication? Has the child been drinking and urinating? Are symptoms progressing? What was their level of responsiveness ten minutes ago compared with now? Record measurements and dose times only if known. Speculation about the cause is less useful than a clear description of observed change.

### A scenario of progressive breathing difficulty

Imagine a child with a cold who begins breathing faster than usual. At first the child is still playing and speaking. Later they sit quietly, pull in at the ribs with each breath and struggle to finish a sentence. A wait-and-see judgment based only on the original mild cold misses the direction of change. Increasing effort and reduced ability to speak or interact are reasons to escalate. Even when a child has a known respiratory condition, follow their prescribed emergency plan and call for help when signs exceed that plan or worsen.

The same logic applies to dehydration. A child who vomits once and returns to normal behavior is different from a child with repeated losses, inability to hold fluids, decreasing urination and growing lethargy. The pattern of deterioration carries more information than a single symptom label. Again, first aid does not require guessing which pathogen is responsible.

### The handoff

Emergency communication should include the child's approximate age, location, current breathing and responsiveness, main observed changes, timing, known conditions, medications given and any possible exposure. If the situation changes while speaking to a dispatcher, update them. A concise report is better than a long explanation of every disease considered.

The strongest practical habit is to compare now with normal and watch the direction of change. For infants and children, marked changes in appearance, breathing or responsiveness require prompt action. Knowing that boundary does more good than memorizing a long catalog of diagnoses that cannot be confirmed in the living room.

#### Technical companion (non-spoken)

### Breathing Distress: Recognition and Escalation

### 015000 — Breathing Distress: Recognition and Escalation

#### Technical companion (non-spoken)

##### Recognize and report

- Signs requiring urgent emergency response include inability to speak because of breathing difficulty, serious chest/neck muscle effort, blue/gray skin changes, marked confusion or reduced responsiveness, stridor, or sudden worsening.
- Record onset, preceding activities or exposures, known diagnoses, prescribed rescue devices, whether the person is speaking, breathing changes, relevant medication administered under training, and response.
- Separate probable choking with **ineffective** cough or absent airflow from other distress; age-specific protocols and instructor-led practice are necessary.
- Unresponsive with absent or abnormal breathing is a resuscitation situation: emergency services, CPR, AED per current training/dispatcher guidance.

##### Case discussion

A noisy wheeze can accompany airway narrowing; a nearly silent person who is becoming exhausted may be even more concerning. Never wait for a specific oxygen saturation number or color change if severe distress is already evident.

**Clinical boundary:** This is an educational recognition lesson, not diagnosis or a substitute for CPR, choking-response, epinephrine or inhaler training. Local emergency protocols and dispatcher instructions take priority.

### Seizures: Recognition and Scene Care

### 016000 — Seizures: Recognition and Scene Care

#### Technical companion (non-spoken)

**Response mnemonic:** STAY (remain with person and time) → SAFE (clear hazards, cushion head) → SIDE (gently position on side when unaware if safe to do so) → REASSESS (normal breathing, responsiveness, injury). Do not restrain; do not put anything in mouth; no food, drink or oral medicine until fully alert.

**Activate emergency services:** first-time seizure, about five minutes of ongoing seizure, repeated episodes without return to baseline, trouble breathing/choking, injury, water exposure, pregnancy, infant younger than six months, or failure to regain usual responsiveness. Follow dispatcher instructions. If not breathing normally after convulsive activity ends, respond according to CPR/AED training.

**Observation note:** start/end time, type of movements, awareness, injuries, breathing, repeat episodes, recovery time, prescribed action plan when known. Respect privacy.

**Review status:** 2024 AHA/American Red Cross first-aid guideline and CDC seizure first-aid guidance inform the clinical thresholds. This educational manuscript does not replace current local training, emergency-dispatch instructions, or individualized seizure action plans.

### Diabetes Emergencies: Recognition and First Response

### 017000 — Diabetes Emergencies: Recognition and First Response

#### Technical companion (non-spoken)

**Hypoglycemia response:** suspected low glucose + awake and safely able to swallow → encourage oral glucose; tablets preferred if available, simple dietary sugars an alternative. Call EMS for inability to swallow, seizure, worsening symptoms, or failure to improve within roughly 10 minutes of glucose per 2024 AHA/American Red Cross. No oral intake when unable to swallow or not awake.

**Severe hyperglycemia/DKA clues:** vomiting, inability to retain fluids, deep/rapid breathing, fruity breath, confusion, severe fatigue, dehydration; requires emergency assessment. Do not administer insulin based on bystander assumptions or use extra glucose indiscriminately when high-glucose illness is suspected.

**Glucagon:** Some people have prescribed rescue medication and an action plan; product-specific instructions and caregiver training apply. Emergency medical care is still necessary for a severe episode. Do not assume one device's directions fit another.

**Handoff:** time of first symptoms, usual baseline if known, glucose reading and device/time, food/glucose administered, rescue medication if administered, vomiting/seizure/breathing changes, medical alerts, contact/plan. Follow the dispatcher and updated local first-aid training.

**Clinical status:** Grounded in AHA/Red Cross 2024 first-aid hypoglycemia recommendations and CDC diabetes emergency guidance. Independent clinical review pending; not a substitute for individualized medical instructions.

### Dehydration, Shock, and Signs of Deterioration

### 018000 — Dehydration, Shock, and Signs of Deterioration

#### Technical companion (non-spoken)

**Decision distinction.** Alert with uncomplicated exertional dehydration, normal swallowing and no shock signs: oral rehydration can be appropriate. Suspected shock, confusion, abnormal breathing, significant bleeding, altered consciousness, persistent vomiting, or severe pain: activate emergency response and prioritize airway and cause-directed first aid.

**Handoff record.** Time of onset, activity/environment, fluid losses, injury, responsiveness, breathing and changes, oral intake if any, medications or allergies if volunteered, and actions performed.

**Positioning limits.** AHA/Red Cross 2024 guidance supports supine positioning for alert suspected shock when tolerated, recovery positioning for airway risk under relevant conditions, and conditional leg elevation only without injury/contraindications. Do not delay calling EMS to find an 'ideal' position.

**Source attribution.** 2024 AHA and American Red Cross Guidelines for First Aid, especially oral rehydration for exertional dehydration and first-aid positioning of the person in shock; American Red Cross dehydration guidance. ### Child and Infant Illness: Recognizing Deterioration and Calling for Help

### 020000 — Child and Infant Illness: Recognizing Deterioration and Calling for Help

#### Non-spoken technical companion

**Emergency pattern (U.S. context).** Unresponsiveness, absent/abnormal breathing, marked respiratory distress, blue/gray lips or tongue, rapidly declining alertness or other life-threatening conditions -> call 911 immediately and follow dispatcher instructions. A very young infant with measured fever requires prompt clinician/emergency evaluation according to current pediatric guidance; it is not necessarily a 911 call when otherwise stable. Do not confuse this with routine older-child fever management.

**Structured handoff.** Age, baseline and present appearance, breathing and circulation observations, onset/time course, temperature measurement and method if known, feeding, urine, vomiting/diarrhea, trauma/ingestions/exposures, chronic conditions, medications or interventions and response. Report uncertainty rather than invent data.

**Original clinical sources.** American Academy of Pediatrics (HealthyChildren.org), *When to Call Emergency Medical Services (EMS) for Your Child* and newborn symptom guidance; American Red Cross, *Child & Baby First Aid*; AHA/American Red Cross, *2024 Guidelines for First Aid*. Clinical review is required. This educational text does not substitute for pediatric CPR training, local EMS instructions, emergency medicine assessment or current treatment protocols.
