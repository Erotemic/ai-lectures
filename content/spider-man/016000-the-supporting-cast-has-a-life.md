# The Supporting Cast Has a Life of Its Own: The People Who Refuse to Orbit Peter Parker

*Series:* Spider-Man: The Comics, the Character, and the Culture  
*Lecture ID:* 016000  
*Section:* characters-and-conflicts  
*Editorial form:* A character-study audio essay with selected comics scenes, contrasting genuine supporting-character agency with the storytelling habit of using loved ones as jeopardy.  
*Spoilers:* Discusses Gwen Stacy's death in *The Amazing Spider-Man* #121, the final scene of #122, major developments for Harry Osborn and Flash Thompson, and Aunt May discovering Peter's identity.

Imagine the first ten minutes of a Spider-Man movie in which Peter Parker does not appear. No glimpse of a mask, no distant siren, no wink at a camera. Just Aunt May trying to solve a problem at home. Mary Jane at an audition that goes badly. Harry Osborn dodging a question about his father. Robbie Robertson at the newspaper explaining to a younger reporter why a good headline and a true headline are not necessarily the same thing.

Would it still be a Spider-Man story?

I think it could be. In fact, the more interesting question is whether Spider-Man remains interesting when the people around Peter exist only to admire him, worry about him, or be kidnapped by someone who has figured out his address. A superhero can carry an action sequence alone. A long-running comic cannot build an inhabited life out of one person's reactions to himself.

The famous Spider-Man formula is often described as Peter having two jobs: he saves strangers while disappointing friends. That is a useful start. But it can smuggle in a bad assumption, as though his friends are simply stationary objects in an obstacle course. The real appeal of a strong supporting cast is that everyone is doing something Peter knows too little about. They are living in the minutes when he is off the page.

## A party girl who stays when the party ends

Consider Mary Jane Watson. Her early introduction comes with enormous comic-book confidence. For a long time Peter and the reader have heard about a young woman whom Aunt May wants him to meet. Peter expects the familiar indignity of an arranged date. Then John Romita Senior gives Mary Jane her full on-page reveal in *The Amazing Spider-Man* number forty-two, in 1966. She's dazzling, self-possessed, apparently pleased with the effect she has on him. The joke works because both Peter and the reader have underestimated someone before she enters the room.

It would have been easy to leave Mary Jane as an accessory: the spectacular alternative to the more reserved Gwen Stacy, a reliable source of flirting and occasional jealousy. But a memorable supporting character must be allowed to contradict the role the story seems to have assigned.

Look at the end of *The Amazing Spider-Man* number one hundred twenty-two. In the previous issue, the Green Goblin's attack has resulted in Gwen's death. Peter is shattered. In the aftermath, Mary Jane comes to see him. He lashes out. He accuses her, in effect, of being too frivolous to understand grief and tells her to leave. And Mary Jane, who could have defended herself with an equally cruel answer, starts for the door. Then she closes it again. She stays.

The great thing about that scene is not that Peter has secretly been correct about her and she must prove herself worthy. It is almost the opposite. He misreads her, hurtfully. The comic lets the person who looked like the party girl choose a response with more grace than the hero can manage at that moment. She has interior resources that Peter did not know about. And by keeping her response quiet, the page allows a decision, rather than a speech, to reveal them.

That is an important qualification to the idea that Spider-Man stories revolve around Peter's guilt. Mary Jane is not there simply to soothe his guilt. She has her own reasons to be wounded, to show up, to leave, or not to leave. A relationship becomes a story when the other person can surprise the protagonist by acting from a life that is hers.

Later comics give MJ more of that life: professional ambitions as an actress and model, family complications, her own anxieties about the people she loves, and a willingness to speak honestly to Peter when honesty is unwelcome. Different writing eras handle her independence with different degrees of care. The point is not that her relationship with Spider-Man is irrelevant. It is that she should still be interesting after we remove the web-shooters from the conversation.

## Gwen Stacy is more than the fall

Gwen presents a harder test for criticism. A great deal of discussion of Gwen Stacy begins at the bridge. Peter's girlfriend is killed in a landmark 1973 story; for decades afterward she is remembered through that loss. The event is enormously important to Spider-Man's publishing history. But remembering a character only as the moment she dies is a peculiar way to remember a person.

Before the Goblin's attack, Gwen had been a college student with relationships, ambitions, friendships, changes of opinion and conflicts with Peter. She was not simply a placeholder waiting to become a lesson about responsibility. The early college stories are full of social misunderstandings: Peter looks arrogant because he is preoccupied, the other students make judgments on incomplete evidence, and Gwen has to decide who this sharp, distracted newcomer really is. Those scenes do not gain their value only because we know what happens later.

A later comic can use Gwen's memory to deepen Peter's grief. It can also use that memory lazily, treating her as a perfect lost innocence whose only function is to make the present seem diminished. The distinction is worth listening for. Does the story remember that Gwen had opinions Peter disliked? That people disagreed with her? That she did not exist solely to arrange his character development? When the answer is no, the memorial has quietly erased the individual.

This is one reason the supporting cast matters even when a reader is primarily interested in Spider-Man's adventures. A story's ethical weight depends partly on whether the endangered people have lives rather than merely names.

## Aunt May opens the conversation

Aunt May is the easiest character to underestimate, in part because the comics deliberately made her frail for so long. She worries about Peter's health. He worries that telling her the truth might hurt her. Meanwhile he has spent years going out at night in a brightly colored suit to fight enormous mechanical tentacles. One might reasonably ask why May is always the person deemed too vulnerable to handle a difficult conversation.

In *The Amazing Spider-Man* volume two, number thirty-eight, cover dated February 2002, writer J. Michael Straczynski and artist John Romita Junior turn that question into the story. The issue is called *The Conversation*. May has discovered Peter's Spider-Man identity after finding his damaged costume. She confronts him. He can no longer protect her from the truth by lying about it.

Here is the twist. Peter tells her that Uncle Ben died because Peter once let a burglar escape. This is the guilt that has powered his heroism since his origin. But May carries her own version of responsibility. She tells him she had argued with Ben before he went out that fatal night. For years she too has imagined a way that she might have prevented what happened.

It is a profoundly Spider-Man idea: two people in the same house have been mourning the same person while privately taking more responsibility for his death than either can reasonably bear. But it is also an argument against Peter's habit of assuming that secrecy is always kindness. May's sorrow did not begin when he revealed his costume. She had a history of grief already, and withholding the truth had kept both of them from talking about it.

There is action in this comic, but not the kind a trailer would put first. May insists on being treated as an adult. Peter has to listen. The revelation changes the relationship, at least for this stretch of continuity, because she knows more than she did before. It is possible for a supporting character to change the story without throwing a punch.

And that change exposes a fundamental serial-comics problem. If May learns the secret, authors lose a familiar source of suspense. If she later forgets it, readers may feel that her growth has been taken away so the machinery can reset. This is not automatically proof that one version is better than every other. It is evidence that development has a cost, and that removing it has a cost too.

## Friends who are not required to remain friends

Harry Osborn begins as an uneasy college acquaintance and becomes one of Peter's closest friends. He is also the son of Norman Osborn, the Green Goblin. That is excellent soap-opera geometry. The person Peter wants to comfort may also be the person he cannot tell the truth about Norman. The friend has emotional loyalties that do not line up neatly with the hero's.

Harry's troubles with addiction, his desire for paternal approval, and his later decision to wear the Goblin costume make him more than a villain's son waiting for an inherited mask. He can be frightened of Norman, desperate to imitate him, angry with Peter, and genuinely attached to Peter in different stories. Not every version manages this complexity, but the best ones do not treat Harry's behavior as a switch labeled 'friend' or 'enemy.'

Flash Thompson provides a different kind of long-range experiment. In Peter's school days he is the athlete who bullies the apparently weak bookworm while admiring Spider-Man, without realizing that they are the same person. The double irony is funny: Flash is wrong about both men at once.

But if the character stayed a high-school bully forever, the joke would have an expiration date. Flash grows older. He serves in the military. Later comics make him the host of the symbiote in the Agent Venom stories, where questions of discipline, injury, identity and service reshape his relationship to the superhero he admired. The man who once mistook strength for the ability to intimidate someone smaller has to develop a more complicated idea of what it means to be brave.

You do not have to like every later Flash story to see the appeal. A secondary character is allowed to accrue time. The years in which Peter has been Spider-Man can count for someone else too.

## The newspaper has more than one opinion

At the Daily Bugle, J. Jonah Jameson offers a comic engine so loud that he can drown out every other employee. Peter sells photographs of Spider-Man to a man who publicly attacks Spider-Man. Jonah is a performance in himself. But the newsroom also needs people who do not think exactly as Jonah does.

Robbie Robertson is a crucial counterweight. He is an editor and journalist who can recognize Peter's decency without agreeing with all his excuses. More importantly, he can disagree with Jonah on professional and ethical grounds. That matters because a newsroom with only Jonah's voice is a joke about newspapers. A newsroom where Robbie can push back is a place where decisions about evidence, fairness, fear and profit actually have to be made.

There is a temptation to describe Robbie merely as 'the sensible one.' But that reduces him to another function of Jonah's personality. A stronger reading recognizes that professional judgment takes work: Robbie decides what he can defend, whom he trusts, and when to challenge a powerful colleague. Those decisions are not Spider-Man's. They are his.

The same principle operates in a more volatile key with Felicia Hardy, the Black Cat. Her early stories make her a thief, a daredevil and a romantic complication. The obvious version is a woman drawn into Spider-Man's moral orbit who must either accept his rules or abandon him. The more interesting version asks what Felicia wants before Peter arrives. She values freedom, danger, glamour, and the pleasures and risks of being someone whose name is already a performance. Romance becomes a conflict between two self-conceptions, not just a test of Peter's ability to resist temptation.

Her independence can make the relationship exciting. It can also make it untenable. Both results are better drama than treating her as a costume with a flirtatious voice.

## The city keeps moving off panel

There is no single definitive Spider-Man supporting cast across every adaptation. Films compress characters, games reorder relationships, alternate universes make entirely different choices. Gwen in an animated multiverse story is not simply Gwen from a 1973 Earth copied into a new suit. A reader should ask what a particular version is doing, rather than forcing everyone into one seamless biography.

But a useful test works almost everywhere. Ask three questions about a supporting character. What does this person want when Peter is not present? What can they refuse him? And what choice have they made that changes their own future, not merely his mood?

Mary Jane can close the door and stay. May can demand the truth. Harry can love his friend while being damaged by his father. Flash can outgrow a joke. Robbie can challenge his editor. Felicia can decline the role of an obedient partner. Gwen can be remembered for living, not only for falling.

None of these people needs to steal the title from Spider-Man. They need enough freedom that Peter cannot predict them, enough continuity that their experiences matter, and enough dignity that a comic remembers they are not props. Then the familiar scene works better: Spider-Man lands on a rooftop late for dinner, embarrassed and exhausted, and somewhere below him there are people whose evening has been complicated for reasons that have nothing to do with his suit.

Peter Parker might be the center of the comic. That does not mean everyone else should be standing still.
