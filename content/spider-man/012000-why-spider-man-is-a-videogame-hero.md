# Why Spider-Man Is a Videogame Hero: The City Is the Controller

*Series:* Spider-Man: The Comics, the Character, and the Culture  
*Lecture ID:* 012000  
*Section:* reinventions-and-adaptations  
*Editorial form:* Game-design criticism and development history, with necessary character and gameplay context.  
*Spoilers:* General mechanics and story premises for the 2004 *Spider-Man 2*, Insomniac's *Marvel's Spider-Man* (2018), *Miles Morales* (2020), and *Marvel's Spider-Man 2* (2023); no ending-dependent twists.

There is a peculiar way to play a Spider-Man videogame: ignore the plot. Ignore the purple mission marker. Ignore the bank robbery waiting three blocks away. Find a tall building, jump, and spend the next forty minutes getting across town by a method no insurance company would approve.

This is an extraordinary achievement in game design. In a Superman game, flight might be a button you hold to go somewhere. In a Batman game, traversal might be a set of tactical gadgets, each good for a particular obstacle. For Spider-Man, getting somewhere can be the attraction itself. The journey is a performance. A player can be late to an imaginary emergency and still be having the best possible time.

Why is Spider-Man so well suited to games? Not simply because he has powers. Plenty of superheroes have powers. It is because his most recognizable power creates decisions at a human scale: when to jump, where to attach a web, how much speed to keep, whether to go over or between the buildings, and whether to show off when nobody is watching.

A comic can draw a magnificent pose halfway through a swing. A film can choose its camera and edit the trajectory to perfection. A game hands you the possibility of ruining that pose with your own thumb. And then, ideally, it makes the ruined maneuver entertaining too.

## The problem of the invisible hook

The Spider-Man game problem sounds trivial until you try to solve it. He shoots a web. The web attaches to something. He swings. What exactly is that something? How does the game choose it? If there are three buildings in front of you, which edge should the web find? If you turn the camera, does the character change direction or keep his momentum? If the player makes a bad choice, should the game let him crash through a shop window, or quietly help?

Early three-dimensional Spider-Man games often solved part of the problem by treating the web as a means of forward travel rather than an actual rope attached to architecture. Neversoft's *Spider-Man* from 2000 remains a fondly remembered action game, with its own comic-book personality, villains and performances. But the web could appear to disappear into an accommodating sky. It got you from one challenge to another without asking a modern open world to make the geometry work.

The dramatic leap came with Treyarch's 2004 *Spider-Man 2*, a game associated with the second Sam Raimi film. It opened Manhattan as a place to cross, not simply as scenery behind a series of combat levels. The webs had to behave as though they connected to buildings, and the swinging had momentum. The result was a game in which players could enjoy the trip between missions more than some of the missions themselves.

Jamie Fristrom, who worked on Treyarch's swinging system, wrote a postmortem in the September 2004 issue of *Game Developer*. His account is a pleasure precisely because it does not pretend that someone woke up, invented perfect web physics, and went home. Treyarch had tried a more physical swinging prototype during work on an earlier game. It made the character stick to walls unintentionally. Players who practiced could improve, but the learning curve was steep. The team abandoned the experiment rather than risk the whole game.

When they revisited the idea, there were more prototypes, selected anchor points, revisions to the controls, changes to animation and camera behavior. Fristrom describes swinging as a collaborative effort: programmers, designers, animators and others kept testing it. The city itself mattered. Some street widths and building heights felt better than others. Even the question of whether a player should be able to use two web lines at once became a design discussion.

One of the finest details in that story is that physical accuracy was not the only goal. An attachment system that faithfully chose a nearby wall could still produce a miserable game if it kept pulling Spider-Man somewhere the player did not intend to go. The programmers had to make guesses about player intention. The sense of swinging is assembled from physics, anticipation, forgiveness and the deliberate arrangement of the playground.

When fans ask whether a swinging system is realistic, they may be asking three different questions: whether the web visibly attaches to a building; whether the motion respects some recognizable momentum; and whether the player feels responsible for the trajectory. Those are related, but they are not identical. A realistic fall can be a boring fall. A simulated web could be technically correct and spectacularly bad at guessing what your thumb meant.

## Insomniac's moving city

In 2018, Insomniac Games released *Marvel's Spider-Man*, a new continuity built around an older, more experienced Peter Parker. Its swinging immediately felt different: smooth, fast, cinematic, and remarkably easy to continue for long stretches. It did not throw away the 2004 problem. It revisited it with another set of priorities.

Game director Ryan Smith told *Game Developer* that the team began from two requirements: webs should attach to buildings, and the motion should have a physics-based swing. What took years was making those elements blend with wall-running, web releases and other traversal moves. The crucial work was often not the dramatic pendulum itself, but the transition between one satisfying action and the next. A swing that ends in a humiliating pause is not the same fantasy as a swing that flows into a run along a wall, a jump off a ledge, and another smooth arc.

In a PlayStation interview about the game's technology, Insomniac's Mike Fitzgerald made the point even more clearly. The studio did not want to reproduce the exact experience of hanging from a rope over Manhattan. Realistic rope dynamics would not automatically feel heroic. Architecture contained tagged potential attachment locations; the system selected useful points that helped preserve momentum and direction. Camera movement, character animation and changes in field of view supplied part of the feeling of speed.

The technical accomplishment reaches beyond the character. Fitzgerald described the city as hundreds of streaming tiles, loaded ahead of a player and discarded behind. Think about what that means for the fiction. Spider-Man is not walking through a small movie set and finding a painted backdrop at the end. He is moving fast enough that the engine has to prepare the next portion of New York before he arrives. The technological trick has to disappear so that the physical fantasy can remain uninterrupted.

A fan can criticize this system, reasonably, for being generous. It can feel as if the game is helping Spider-Man keep moving even when the player is imprecise. Someone who loves wrestling with the trickier control of the 2004 game may find that a loss of expressive challenge. But the generosity opens another possibility: a novice can look graceful, and an expert can learn to join moves into a satisfying rhythm. One design emphasizes hard-won mastery of the rope; another emphasizes the pleasure of inhabiting the hero continuously. Neither design decision is a simple referendum on whether the developers understood Spider-Man.

And the city must make the movement worthwhile. Wide avenues invite speed. Tall facades offer height. Dense streets make the trajectory feel close to danger. A river temporarily changes the problem. In an ordinary game, a map's buildings are obstacles. In a Spider-Man game, they are pieces of equipment.

## What changes when the body changes?

Insomniac's *Miles Morales* game arrived in 2020 and made a different argument without abandoning the basic movement. Miles is a younger Spider-Man, still learning how to inhabit the role. His friend Ganke knows his secret; his mother Rio is important to his neighborhood life. The playable hero retains the familiar swinging, but his animated body does not have to perform exactly like Peter's. Miles can move with a less polished, more improvisational energy. That difference is not simply cosmetic. It tells us how much confidence the character has found.

His electrical attacks and camouflage also change encounters. In the comics, Miles can emit a bioelectric shock called a venom blast; despite the name, it has nothing to do with Eddie Brock's alien symbiote. The game turns those abilities into a second vocabulary alongside webs and punches. It lets one Spider-Man feel related to another without being reduced to a costume swap.

Then *Marvel's Spider-Man 2* in 2023 makes two heroes playable in a larger New York, with Brooklyn and Queens joining Manhattan. It also gives both Spider-Men web wings: gliding surfaces that change how they travel across open spaces and rivers. Insomniac described the goal as complementing swinging, not replacing it. You can transition from a line-driven arc to a glide and back, altering your reading of the city. Near-instant switching between Peter and Miles changes the scale of the narrative too: New York contains more than one person who can answer a cry for help.

Here is the subtle risk. When a game gives the player a faster, cleaner, more convenient way to travel, the original swinging may become something used for atmosphere instead of necessity. Does adding a gliding mechanism dilute what made Spider-Man distinctive? Or does it give experienced players another set of decisions to choreograph? The answer depends on how well the two movement systems support each other. It is a design question worth arguing about, not a test of loyalty to one era.

## A hero who can dodge, but not solve everything

The traversal system is a successful way of expressing character through play. Combat is harder. Spider-Man should be stronger than an ordinary criminal, agile enough to avoid gunfire, and witty enough to turn panic into a joke. A game must also offer opponents who can threaten the player. Give every street criminal superhuman reflexes and New York begins to seem improbably dangerous. Make every fight trivial and a major portion of the game becomes repetitive. Games solve the problem with groups, gadgets, escalating attacks, environmental improvisation and enemies whose behavior must be read.

But the deeper Spider-Man problem is responsibility. In a comic, Peter can miss a date because he stopped to help someone. The writer has chosen the cost. In a game, the player often wants to swing off to collect another backpack, take photographs, or admire a sunset. A mission marker can insist the world is ending, but the open map says that you have time to do almost anything. The game offers an enormous range of choices while pretending that one choice is desperately urgent.

This is not merely a narrative flaw peculiar to Spider-Man. It is a tension built into open-world storytelling. The more freely a player can roam, the harder it becomes to make an appointment feel binding. Spider-Man magnifies the contradiction because his defining trait is an almost excessive sense of obligation. A player can be the most irresponsible possible Peter and still complete a story about his responsibility.

The solution is not necessarily to punish anyone who misses a mission. Imagine a game that literally deducted a loved one's health every time you stopped to appreciate the skyline. That could be faithful to Peter's anxiety and dreadful to play. Instead, strong Spider-Man games express moral pressure in smaller spaces: the dialogue after a rescue, a neighborhood that recognizes your help, the decision to stay with a friend, or a story sequence that makes a victory cost more than a health bar.

One of the cleverest things the 2018 game does is let you simply be present at street level. Swinging over New York is the fantasy of power. Pausing on a sidewalk, among people who go about their own days, reminds you what the power is for. The city is not only a route or a collection of crimes. It is a place full of people who do not exist solely to supply combat encounters.

We can now answer the original question. Spider-Man is a videogame hero because his fantasy lives in the space between intention and execution. You see a route, attempt it, discover that your body has momentum, improvise, and arrive somewhere you had not planned to go. That is what playing a game can offer that watching even a magnificent swing cannot.

And there is an extra joke for those of us who have spent half an hour joyfully ignoring the mission. Spider-Man is the superhero most likely to feel guilty about wasting time. The videogame player may be the person most pleased to waste it. The city somehow makes room for both.
