# Why the Spider-Verse Films Look and Sound Like Thoughts

A Spider-Man movie can make New York look like an actual city, then add an impossible superhero. Or it can do something much stranger. It can make the entire city behave like a comic book, and then ask what it feels like to grow up inside that book.

That second idea sounds like a slogan until you watch *Spider-Man: Into the Spider-Verse*, released in 2018. Cars do not always need their precise photographic contours. Buildings can lean. Printed dots become shadows. Speed lines replace blurry motion. A face can acquire a few hand-drawn marks because an expression needs them, not because the underlying digital model happens to have a wrinkle there. The film does not pretend to be a live-action Spider-Man that has merely been rendered as animation. Its art changes the rules of the world.

And the really clever part is that the changes help tell the story of Miles Morales.

Miles is a teenager in Brooklyn. He loves his neighborhood, struggles to fit into a new school, sketches graffiti with his uncle Aaron, and acquires spider-powers just as a catastrophe starts throwing Spider-people from other realities into his world. There is a Peter Parker who looks like a perfect established superhero, and another Peter who is older, broke, exhausted and not especially interested in being anyone's teacher. Miles will have to become Spider-Man without being an imitation of either. That is the premise. The interesting question is how the filmmaking makes the feeling of not quite fitting visible, and audible.

## An animated film that stops trying to look smooth

In conventional computer animation, a system often makes movement smoother by calculating a new image for every displayed film frame. A common cinematic frame rate is twenty-four frames per second. In traditional hand-drawn animation, an artist may make a drawing last for two frames rather than replacing it on every frame. Animators call that working on twos: twelve distinct drawings in a second instead of twenty-four. The new film's team used that principle as a central visual tool, though not an iron rule imposed on every object in every shot.

The distinction matters because perfectly smooth movement is not always the same thing as expressive movement. A comic artist chooses the one pose that makes a punch, a leap, or a moment of confusion legible. A frame between those poses may make physical movement more continuous while weakening the striking silhouette. Sony Pictures Imageworks developed ways to work with stepped animation, move between timings as needed, and keep cameras and effects cooperating when characters were held for different intervals.

The head of character animation, Josh Beveridge, and visual effects supervisor Danny Dimian have explained how this decision followed from a desire to make every image feel deliberately illustrated. Their account is more interesting than the popular trivia version that says Miles simply moves at half the frame rate and gets promoted to full speed once he becomes a hero. Frame timing varies by sequence and performance. In one technical demonstration, the artists describe Miles and his mentor Peter moving in and out of sync during the forest web-swinging lesson. The frame rhythm participates in the comedy and the awkwardness as their coordination changes. You can watch their relationship getting less clumsy without a character stopping to deliver a speech about teamwork.

This is a lesson in the difference between a technique and a gimmick. If all you know is that the movie uses twos, you have learned a production fact. If you notice how a character's movement communicates confidence, hesitation or friction, you have understood why the production fact matters.

The rendering makes the same argument. Older printed comics could show halftone dots, hatch marks, slight separations between ink colors, and crisp graphic boundaries. Instead of hiding these signs of manufacture, the film makes them part of the image. Color misregistration can stand in for the soft focus familiar from a camera lens. Hard lines can give objects a clear identity in space. Individual illustrative effects can suggest shock, force or atmosphere without trying to simulate the exact behavior of every particle.

Sony's artists did not merely select a ready-made filter labeled comic book. The production combined digital modeling, customized rendering, compositing, hand-drawn work and tools that let artists place lines on animated faces. Some facial lines were tied to predictable surface geometry; others required a human choice. The studio even described systems that learned to predict line placement from an artist's corrections. The point was not to remove the artist. It was to make the technical machinery capable of carrying an artist's intention.

A useful way to hear the achievement is to imagine the alternative. Suppose every character and building had the correct geometric proportions, every shadow had ordinary physical softness, and every motion was perfectly interpolated. The film might remain legible and exciting. But it would lose some of the sensation of reading a page in which an artist has decided exactly how impossible the world should be.

## Miles learning to fall upward

The film's most widely discussed image is Miles's leap from a tall building. He commits to becoming Spider-Man, jumps outward, and the camera turns the apparent fall into a rise. The skyline is a dizzying graphic arrangement; glass fractures around the departure; the composition makes the city feel both gigantic and available. The scene is remembered as a triumphant jump, but its emotional force depends on what came before it.

Earlier, Miles tries to learn the movement before he possesses the confidence. He gets stuck to walls. He cannot reliably manage the release-and-catch rhythm of swinging. Peter B. Parker, whose own life is a shambles, can perform the athletic part almost automatically. Miles is the character who needs help; Peter is the character who badly needs to learn how to give it. The training scenes are therefore not incidental comedy on the way to the final stunt. They make the moment of independent action intelligible.

Now consider the music. Daniel Pemberton's score mixes orchestral writing, electronic textures and record scratching. The film also uses songs closely associated with Miles's social world. In the leap sequence, the song *What's Up Danger*, performed by Blackway and Black Caviar, connects with Pemberton's score. The sound team has discussed how the song gives way to orchestral material as the scene turns heroic. Neither music nor image is simply illustrating a sentence that says Miles is brave. Rhythm, held poses, camera movement, graphic color and the sense of a young person finally choosing his own movement cooperate to make courage feel physical.

Notice the movie's generosity toward failure. It gives Miles awkward experiments before mastery. It lets Peter be a qualified teacher of swinging and a deeply unqualified teacher of living. It lets Gwen Stacy be better prepared in a fight while carrying her own grief. And it lets the audience spend time with wonderfully ill-matched visitors: a black-and-white noir detective, an anime-influenced girl with a robot, and a cartoon pig who operates under a more permissive set of physical laws than everyone else.

The joke is visual as well as verbal. A film that demanded one consistent style for all these characters would have to flatten them. *Into the Spider-Verse* instead makes inconsistency a condition of the story. They belong together because they do not look as though they should.

## The sound of a universe being remixed

It is easy to treat a soundtrack as wallpaper and miss the artistic logic. Pemberton described the first film's musical approach in terms of three major ingredients: electronics, turntables and orchestra. The turntable element is not simply a prepackaged hip-hop beat placed under an otherwise conventional score. Recorded material can be manipulated as an instrument. The composer worked with scratch DJ Blakey, and the team used scratching as part of the score's actual construction.

Sound design makes the same creative choice. The *Into the Spider-Verse* sound crew described building distinct identities for characters and environments, and blending effects with music rather than putting them in separate boxes. The machine surrounding Peni Parker sounds different from the rough metal equipment of Scorpion. The city has presence; the multiverse has an instability you can hear. A heroic moment can transition between a song a character might actually enjoy and a film-score gesture that makes the moment larger than ordinary life.

This is not a minor bonus for an animated film. It helps distinguish Miles from an abstract symbol. He lives somewhere; he has tastes; he has a relationship to music and drawing before the costume makes him recognizable to the wider world. When the superhero story expands into a multiverse, those tastes remain a thread tying him to Brooklyn.

The danger with a film this inventive is that viewers can mistake virtuosity for its entire purpose. The real achievement is matching the invention to emotional stakes. A great effect need not call attention to itself at every instant. Sometimes the cleverest tool is a face with one line in the right place.

## Across the Spider-Verse makes style into a dispute

The 2023 sequel could have repeated the first movie's texture and increased the number of cameos. Instead, *Across the Spider-Verse* turns the existence of different graphic worlds into an argument about how stories are allowed to work. Its creative team included directors Joaquim Dos Santos, Kemp Powers and Justin K. Thompson, with writing credited to Phil Lord, Christopher Miller and David Callaham. Production designer Patrick O'Keefe and the artists at Sony Pictures Animation and Imageworks gave different places and characters markedly different pictorial languages.

Gwen's home reality can look like watercolor that reflects emotional changes. The animators and illustrators worked with tools including Rebelle to create painted effects that move with the scene. Gwen's relationship with her police-officer father is painful partly because neither can easily say what the other needs to hear. The changing background allows an emotion to color the room without claiming that the room has literally changed in the story world. The picture can be subjective.

Spider-Punk, Hobie Brown, is almost the opposite proposition. His image refuses to settle into one clean commercial design. He is built from collage, posters, anarchic textures and incompatible visual tempos. Artists varied timing across parts of the character; his jacket could be held longer than his body, and another element could move on yet another schedule. If a computer animation system is built around the expectation that every part of a character moves consistently, Hobie is an extremely elaborate way of saying no.

That is not just because he likes punk music. The film gives Hobie an attitude toward authority and conformity. He questions who wrote the rules that other Spider-people are prepared to obey. Even when he is standing around, the artwork makes him difficult to capture in a fixed position. Form and personality agree.

Then there is the Spot, who begins as a visual joke: a villain whose pale body is marked by dark holes, and whose ability to open portals makes ordinary geography unreliable. He could have remained a slapstick opponent. As he becomes more threatening, negative space turns into a frightening source of power. The same graphic idea that was funny in a small convenience-store fight can become unsettling when its scale and stakes change. It is a lesson in using one well-chosen visual rule more than once.

The sequel's biggest narrative argument concerns so-called canon events. Miguel O'Hara leads a society of Spider-people who believe certain traumatic events must occur to preserve the integrity of their worlds. Miles refuses to accept that the people he loves are expendable in order to obey a pattern. This is not a rule the listener needs to accept as objective truth just because Miguel says it. The film stages it as a dispute. The entire visual structure has prepared us for a hero who belongs to a family of stories without needing to submit to one official drawing of how the family must look.

## The gamble of visual abundance

There are costs to this approach. *Across the Spider-Verse* is dense enough that a viewer may miss a vital expression while admiring the background, or may lose the emotional line beneath the cameos and visual jokes. Its ending also postpones resolution. A film can have an extraordinarily expressive surface and still leave some audiences frustrated by where the story pauses. These are legitimate criticisms, not evidence that the artwork is irrelevant.

But the two films offer something that superhero adaptations had often treated as a contradiction. They are expensive, technically elaborate productions that deliberately preserve evidence of drawing, printing, collage and imperfection. They let the audience notice the craft rather than masking every handmade choice behind seamless realism.

And that craft is part of what they say about Spider-Man. Peter Parker can be a great mentor even when he is a mess. Gwen can occupy a world that responds to emotion. Hobie can refuse to hold still for the same frames as everybody else. Miles can inherit a symbol without repeating the path of the person who wore it before him.

A movie about the right to draw yourself into a story has found a form in which there is visibly more than one way to draw. Once you see that connection, the spider emblem stops being the only graphic element that carries the character. Sometimes the character is in the missing frame, the misaligned color, the scratch of a record, or the leap that looks like a fall until the whole image turns around.
