# Spider-Man Through the Decades: The Hero Who Keeps Getting Another First Day

*Series:* Spider-Man: The Comics, the Character, and the Culture  
*Lecture ID:* 023000  
*Section:* continuity-and-fandom  
*Editorial form:* The complete track's extended, concluding essay: a single decade-spanning history and critical argument. Each era connects creative work, the experience of encountering Spider-Man, changing distribution and media, and the recurring question of whether Peter Parker is allowed to grow. Not a plot-by-plot retelling.

There is a fairly simple way to make Spider-Man fans disagree. Ask them how old Peter Parker is supposed to be.

Not how old he was when the spider bit him. That is comparatively easy. Ask how old he ought to be now. Should Peter still be struggling to make rent and explain himself to Aunt May? Should he be married, raising children, and worrying about what his powers are teaching them? Should Spider-Man be a mantle shared by several people? Or does the character lose something essential when he stops being the overwhelmed young man we first met?

Each answer comes with a surprisingly large slice of comics history attached.

In 1962, Spider-Man was a teenage novelty at the back of an anthology comic. By 1987, Marvel had put his wedding on the page and reenacted it at a baseball stadium. In 1990, a new Spider-Man number one could sell roughly two and a half million copies in a market that increasingly treated comics as collectibles. By 2002, a film was introducing a version of his origin to people who had never opened a Marvel issue. In 2011, Miles Morales became a new Spider-Man. And in 2024, another comic began with an adult Peter who was already married with two children before gaining his powers.

Those developments are not all episodes in one continuous fictional life. They are decisions made by artists, writers, editors, publishers, filmmakers, animators, and game developers under changing conditions. Occasionally they are answers to a story problem. Occasionally they are answers to a business problem. Frequently they are both.

We are going to follow Spider-Man decade by decade, but not to recite every costume change, death, resurrection, or villain's second cousin. The more interesting question is what each generation thought Spider-Man could become, what it wanted to keep, and what it was prepared to throw away. Some of the best parts of the story are about a character growing up. Some are about a company discovering that growing up is harder to market than it sounds.

There is no single experience of being a fan in any decade. A child watching a cartoon in 1994 did not have the same experience as somebody tracking four interlocking comic titles, and neither one necessarily cared about collector prices. Think of what follows as a set of doors into the character, not a claim that everybody walked through the same one.

## The 1960s: a teenager who was allowed to be difficult

Start in 1962. You pick up a comic called *Amazing Fantasy*, issue fifteen. You are not buying the first chapter of a sixty-year franchise. As far as a reader can tell, you are buying an issue of an anthology with several short stories. One introduces a boy named Peter Parker.

The extraordinary part is not simply that Peter is a teenager. Teenagers had appeared in superhero comics before. It is that Peter is not automatically likable. His classmates dislike him. He can be proud, resentful, brilliant, and convinced that the world owes him an apology. The spider bite gives him power, and his first reasonably developed plan for that power is show business.

This is not an inspirational origin in which a noble child receives the ability to do what he already wanted. Peter makes a bad choice about a burglar he could have stopped. Then Uncle Ben is murdered, and Peter discovers that the escaped burglar is responsible. The story ends with a lesson about responsibility, but it has earned that lesson by letting the hero be selfish first.

Stan Lee wrote the story and Steve Ditko drew it. Ditko's contribution was not simply to put a recognizable costume on a figure. His Peter looks like someone whose shoulders are trying to escape from a room full of other people. His Spider-Man twists around buildings rather than flying past them in the clean straight lines of a conventional champion. The costume covers every inch of the face. We see emotion through posture, through which limb is stretched and which is folded, through a body that seems always to be catching itself in midair.

There is an illuminating archival detail. The Library of Congress holds the original artwork for *Amazing Fantasy* fifteen, including page art with visible production markings and corrections. This character did not descend from a cloud of corporate mythology, already assembled. He passed through hands, pencils, ink, lettering, and edits. The famous moral line is presented by the story's narrator, not in the original comic as a speech Uncle Ben gives Peter. Later versions would reorganize that memory because it works so neatly as a piece of dialogue. That small shift tells you how a cultural icon gets polished after the fact.

*The Amazing Spider-Man* received its own first issue in 1963. Even the attempt to join the Fantastic Four is a joke about money. Peter imagines that joining a famous superhero team might be a paying job. The Fantastic Four, inconveniently, do not operate like a firm hiring a new employee. A gifted kid has discovered that the superhero economy has terrible career counseling.

That gag about joining the Fantastic Four is a useful measure of how odd this hero was. A conventional superhero team might be an institution a young hero dreams of joining because he admires its values. Peter wonders what it pays. He is not incapable of admiration, but ordinary material need gets to the question first. The early comics do not treat embarrassment, money, resentment, and the wish to impress people as things a heroic identity will automatically cure. Instead they put those feelings in the foreground and let the plot tangle around them.

There is another reason those old pages still repay attention: they let readers see thinking as a physical process. Consider the sequence in the Master Planner story, culminating in *Amazing Spider-Man* number thirty-three, cover-dated February 1966. Doctor Octopus's scheme has led Peter to a medicine that can save Aunt May. Then a mass of machinery collapses over him, and water threatens to fill the space. The famous pages are not primarily interesting because we discover that Spider-Man can lift something very heavy. We already know he is fantastically strong. They are interesting because Ditko makes lifting take time. Peter struggles, tries to gather himself, thinks of May and Ben, and keeps trying. The composition makes his burden occupy the page. A reader has to spend time with the effort. The image of an arm straining beneath wreckage is a picture of power; the sequence of panels is a picture of refusal to quit.

The scene is a small demonstration of what comic-book time can do. A film can make a weight rise slowly; a comic can ask the reader to move from one still effort to another and supply the movement between them. Every page turn becomes a tiny wager on whether Peter can find a little more strength. The eventual triumph is not the absence of weakness. It is the result of having been allowed to look weak for long enough that success means something. It would be easy for a later retelling to reproduce the collapsed machinery and lose the central accomplishment by making it one spectacular gesture.

Keep that image in mind as the decades pass. The hero who cannot get out from under the machine is one of the original arguments for why Spider-Man belongs to readers. He is strong enough to lift the burden, but only after the story has made us feel how close he is to failing. This is why the most enduring Spider-Man scenes are often not a new display of maximum strength. They are moments when a remarkable person has to decide whether to try again.

Over the decade the cast around Peter grows: Aunt May, newspaper publisher J. Jonah Jameson, Betty Brant, Flash Thompson, Harry Osborn, Gwen Stacy, and Mary Jane Watson. Their exact relationships change, sometimes quickly, but their practical function is consistent. None has to cooperate with Peter's secret schedule. A date, a job, a friendship, or a family conversation has its own clock. The villain does not need to understand any of these things to ruin them.

The enemies are memorable in part because they are solutions to different visual and psychological problems. Doctor Octopus is a scientist whose metal arms turn intelligence into physical arrogance. The Green Goblin is a hidden personality and a threat to Peter's private life. Mysterio turns the reliability of what Spider-Man sees against him. They do not merely punch at different strengths. They change the sort of story being told.

Then, in 1966, John Romita Senior takes over the regular penciling after Ditko. This is more than an art-style change. The world grows glossier, faces become more glamorous, and Peter's college social life begins to look increasingly like a drama that might carry a comic by itself. Romita's first issue on the title, *Amazing Spider-Man* thirty-nine, is also the issue in which Norman Osborn is exposed as the Green Goblin. Marvel has made a villain's secret identity and a major change in the comic's creative identity part of the same reading experience.

In 1967, Spider-Man arrives in television animation, acquiring a theme song that may be better remembered than particular episodes. The comic is no longer the only doorway. A person can recognize Spider-Man without knowing the difference between Ditko and Romita, and a person who has read the comics can be startled by what a moving, voiced version changes.

What would this decade's Spider-Man have offered a reader? Not a carefully maintained universe in which every piece of continuity had to be explained in advance. A teenager whose problems were ordinary enough to recognize and whose powers made those problems more difficult. The joke of Spider-Man was that the costume was an escape from being Peter Parker, except that putting it on made him even more Peter Parker than before.

## The 1970s: the comic learns that it can hurt its hero

The next decade is sometimes described as Spider-Man becoming darker. That is partly true, but it hides two separate developments: Marvel was testing the limits of what mainstream comics could address, and Spider-Man was discovering that important losses might not be reversible by the last page.

The turn toward consequence actually begins before the decade's most famous tragedy. In *Amazing Spider-Man* number ninety, released in 1970, police captain George Stacy is killed after pushing a child away from falling debris during Spider-Man's battle with Doctor Octopus. George is Gwen Stacy's father, and he has become a sympathetic adult presence in Peter's complicated life. Near the end he asks Peter to take care of Gwen, revealing that he understands more about the masked hero than Peter realized.

It is a different sort of scene from the origin. Uncle Ben's death is a catastrophe Peter understands as the direct consequence of a choice not to act. Captain Stacy's death takes place while Spider-Man is acting. A bystander can die in the wake of a rescue effort, and people watching can mistake the cause. That distinction is cruel, but important: accepting responsibility does not give a superhero control over outcomes. The famous motto is not a magic device that makes the world fair when it is obeyed. It is a standard against which choices are judged even when luck, ignorance, and other people's actions are not under the hero's command.

The death of Gwen a few years later therefore does not arrive in a comic that has never contemplated the collateral costs of a battle. It strikes a still harder point in the same argument. The person Peter is trying most desperately to save can be the person he loses. The series keeps the ability to joke, flirt, and stage bright fights, but its audience has increasingly been taught that a superhero's private history can accumulate grief. This will matter immensely once an expanding franchise starts arguing with itself about which parts of that history must be kept.

The first example is less about violence than about an argument with the institution that approved comic books. In 1971, *Amazing Spider-Man* issues ninety-six through ninety-eight included an anti-drug story involving Harry Osborn. Stan Lee had been encouraged to address the subject through a government request, yet the Comics Code Authority would not approve the depiction of drug use, even in a cautionary context. Marvel published the issues without the Code seal. The Code was later revised.

A detail worth appreciating is the absurdity of the predicament. A commercial comic was trying to show that drugs could be harmful. The rule designed to protect young readers prevented even a negative depiction of drugs. Spider-Man, a character who had always mixed personal troubles with public obligations, proved particularly useful for testing the rule. The episode did not suddenly make comics free of censorship, nor was it the only factor in broader cultural changes. It demonstrated a real limit of the existing system and a publisher's willingness to cross it.

Then comes 1973. Gerry Conway writes, Gil Kane draws, and John Romita Senior inks *Amazing Spider-Man* issue one hundred twenty-one. The Green Goblin kidnaps Gwen Stacy, Peter's girlfriend, and a battle ends with Gwen dead. The story is famous enough that even some fans who have never read the original issue know its outcome.

But consider what sort of ending this is for the young man introduced eleven years earlier. Peter has already failed someone he loved. The whole character is built around the belief that he must not allow such a failure again. Yet here is another catastrophe, in a story where tremendous power does not restore the person at the center of it. Peter cannot punch the fact of Gwen's death until it changes its mind.

Fans have spent decades discussing the mechanics of the fall and Spider-Man's attempt to catch her. The comics have not always handled that question consistently. Yet the story's larger significance does not depend on any one forensic answer. A superhero comic had killed a central character and made the resulting grief part of Peter's continuing life.

One of the story's most important consequences happens after the action, in the quiet. Mary Jane, previously easy for Peter to dismiss as carefree, chooses not to leave him alone. Mary Jane makes a decision that does not erase Gwen and is not simply an invitation to the next romance. A supporting character can surprise Peter by being more serious than his judgment of her.

Meanwhile the broader series is getting bigger. The Punisher first appears in *Amazing Spider-Man* one hundred twenty-nine, a 1974 comic, as an armed vigilante initially set against Spider-Man. Place them side by side and you have an argument about heroism. Peter is a man who knows that violence is tempting and keeps looking for restraint. The Punisher takes a much harsher path. Their proximity makes Spider-Man's morality visible by contrast.

Artist Ross Andru's run also gives the city considerable weight. Rooftops and streets are not abstract diagonals behind the fight. They feel like places with corners, distances, and people. In that concrete city, Peter's responsibility means protecting people whose lives keep unfolding while he fights.

By 1976 the character has a second ongoing title, *Peter Parker, the Spectacular Spider-Man*. More pages mean more places for relationships, villains and continuity to develop. They also mean more moving parts for readers to follow. The series is becoming not one narrow channel but an expanding monthly presence.

It is worth paying attention to the time between installments. Serial comics do not merely tell a long story; they teach readers to live alongside it. A twist that is explained twenty pages later in a collected edition may once have been an argument between friends that lasted a month. A cliffhanger can become an imagined possibility, complete with elaborate theories, before the actual answer arrives. If you read a twenty-year stretch in collected volumes, the continuity is easy to summarize as a string of events. If you met those events as they appeared, the long spaces between them were part of the experience.

That is especially relevant to a hero whose life is defined by postponed obligations. The reader is waiting for the answer to a mystery while Peter is waiting to find out what a friend thinks of him. The two kinds of waiting can reinforce one another, or the editorial machinery can keep a plot going after its emotional question has been answered. Later decades would discover both possibilities. The 1970s offer a useful lesson: continuity is not just a database of what has happened. It is a way of creating anticipation, memory, and the sense that characters might not be exactly the people they were when you first met them.

For a fan meeting Spider-Man in this decade, there are competing promises. There is still the wisecracking man in a bright suit. There is also an increasingly complicated soap opera and the possibility that a story will change the emotional rules for what follows. The tragedy has a cost, but so does the commercial success: the bigger the character becomes, the harder it will be to treat one comic as the whole experience.

## The 1980s: the black suit, a wedding, and two definitions of change

Imagine opening a Spider-Man comic in 1984 and seeing him swing back into New York in black. No familiar red-and-blue web pattern, no need to look at the face to identify him. The silhouette itself has become strange.

In *Amazing Spider-Man* two hundred fifty-two, the new costume has already arrived. Readers would not see the full account of how Peter acquired it until *Marvel Super Heroes Secret Wars* number eight appeared later. Publication order and story order have parted company: Spider-Man comes home from an adventure whose origin is still being published in another series. In a modest way, that is an early demonstration of the crossover logic that later comics would take much further. You can encounter the consequence before you have bought the explanation.

The suit is elegant, and it makes him recognizable with even less information than usual. But the apparent upgrade is a trap. In the developing story, the costume turns out to be a living alien entity, the symbiote, which Peter has to reject. A few years later it becomes the basis for Venom, most famously in the Eddie Brock form that emerges with David Michelinie's writing and Todd McFarlane's art in 1988. Peter's discarded design choice has become someone else's grievance, with teeth.

That is a distinctly Spider-Man reversal. He gets something that looks like an answer to one problem and eventually discovers that it has generated three more. The fact that the black suit became an enduringly marketable image does not cancel the story's anxiety about it. Both things can be true. Comics can create a visually successful costume and then write an entire horror story about why the hero should not keep it.

In 1987, two events reveal almost opposite impulses inside the series.

One is *Kraven's Last Hunt*, written by J. M. DeMatteis and drawn by Mike Zeck. Kraven hunts Spider-Man, buries him, and tries to prove that he can be a better version of the man he has defeated. The premise is grim; its final moral distinction is not. Kraven can imitate the costume and overpower Peter, but he cannot reproduce Peter's refusal to treat other people as things to be mastered. The story ran through several Spider-Man titles, demonstrating the ambitions of a line that could tell a tightly designed story across more than one monthly comic.

The other event is a wedding. Peter Parker and Mary Jane Watson marry in *Amazing Spider-Man Annual* twenty-one. Marvel promotes this fictional milestone with a real-costumed ceremony at Shea Stadium, officiated by Stan Lee. It was covered as a media event, not simply another issue of a comic. The bride's dress was designed by fashion designer Willi Smith, and the publicity linked a comic-book plot, a newspaper comic strip, and a baseball stadium crowd.

Stop at that image for a moment: Spider-Man, whose original problem was that he could barely get a date and keep a job, has become a groom at home plate in front of a crowd. The shy boy from a short story has acquired a public wedding big enough to require a promotional strategy.

Readers can disagree about whether the marriage was good for the ongoing series. At the time, Marvel did not treat the change as invisible or reversible. Peter and Mary Jane now had a shared household, new obligations, and a relationship that was not identical to the recurring will-they-or-won't-they romance. The comic had made an irreversible-sounding promise about adulthood. It was also committing the publisher to maintain that change across future stories.

The contrast is revealing. *Kraven's Last Hunt* explores what the costume means when it is removed from Peter. The wedding explores what Peter might become when he is not defined only by missing the next date. Both ask whether there is a person underneath the reliable brand. Both will haunt the decades to come.

And all the while the way comics reach readers is changing. Specialist comic shops and the direct market are increasingly important, alongside and often replacing the older image of buying an issue from a newsstand rack. A devoted reader can follow many related titles, meet other collectors, track creative teams, and take continuity seriously as an active hobby. That offers depth, but it raises the cost of staying current. The Spider-Man world becomes richer and less self-contained at the same time.

The very shape of that reading habit helps explain why two strikingly different 1987 stories could exist within the same franchise. A fan following the monthly issues may enjoy a compressed, unnerving six-part story across related titles and also take pleasure in an apparently permanent marriage. One treats Spider-Man as a set of symbols to be tested almost to destruction. The other treats him as a person with a future that ought to become more complicated.

A marriage is not inherently the end of a romantic story. It changes the available stories. There is now the possibility of two adults making bad choices together, of loving someone without always agreeing with them, of the work of sustaining affection once the dramatic first declaration is over. Mary Jane does not have to become an accessory who says goodbye at the door; a strong writer can give her ambitions, frustrations, friends, and decisions that the superhero plot cannot simply absorb. The possible difficulty for a publisher is different. A single hero with a permanent new status quo cannot endlessly reproduce every courtship premise that made him familiar in the first place. A wedding can expand a character's life while narrowing one particularly profitable kind of repeatable opening.

That difference, between what makes a character richer and what makes a series easy to restart, is not a judgment that every married Spider-Man story must be better. A poor marriage story can be just as lifeless as a poor dating story. It is a recognition that the editorial preference for one life stage is a choice about kinds of conflict, not an eternal law of the hero. Later publishers would sometimes resolve the conflict by separating versions of Peter into different books. In the 1980s the main series tried to live with the change it had made.

## The 1990s: a hero turns into an event

The 1990s begin with a Spider-Man looking as though his body has discovered a few joints nobody else possesses. Todd McFarlane draws huge expressive eyes, webs like elaborate strands of spaghetti, fingers splayed across walls, and action poses that make the page look like it has been caught in a wind tunnel.

There are earlier Spider-Man artists who did wonderful things with movement, and McFarlane did not invent exaggeration. But his particular exaggerations became a new visual vocabulary. You can often identify a Spider-Man influenced by McFarlane from the webbing alone. It is one reason the art of the late eighties spills into the early nineties so conspicuously: a change on the page has become a visible identity for the whole franchise.

Then comes a comic simply called *Spider-Man*, issue one, in 1990. McFarlane writes and draws it, and the issue sells around two and a half million copies across editions and reprints. The number needs careful handling. Copies ordered or sold into the market are not proof that two and a half million different readers sat down and read the story. Collectors bought multiple editions, and publishers had discovered the sales potential of variant presentations and a huge number one on the cover.

A historical account of the launch describes a California store staging a midnight release complete with searchlights. That is marvelous and a little alarming. We are no longer talking about a boy buying one anthology issue because its cover looks interesting. We are talking about a mass-market event constructed partly around the belief that owning the object will matter in the future.

That is the commercial half of the decade. It is important not to let it flatten the creative half. Artists such as Erik Larsen and Mark Bagley each bring their own energy to Spider-Man, while stories introduce and elaborate figures like Carnage, the offspring of the Venom symbiote bonded with serial killer Cletus Kasady. The 1990s are full of striking images, melodrama, horror-inflected threats and opportunities for fans to find their favorite version of the character.

They are also the decade in which an editorial strategy becomes almost impossible to separate from a story premise: the Clone Saga.

To make its basic conflict intelligible, go back to an old idea. Peter Parker has a clone, created by the Jackal. The clone possesses Peter's memories and believes himself to be Peter. Years later he returns under the name Ben Reilly and eventually as the Scarlet Spider, with a distinctive hooded costume. The series then asks a question that sounds explosive: what if the Peter we have followed all this time is the copy, and Ben is the original?

This is a tremendous hook for a single story. It makes the reader's own attachment part of the plot. Whom have you been cheering for? If two men share the relevant memories and moral convictions, which one has a claim to be Spider-Man? And if the supposed replacement acts with genuine kindness, does biology settle the question?

But a monthly superhero line has other needs. The mystery stretches across multiple titles and escalating reveals. Ben wears the costume as Spider-Man for a time. Eventually the story restores Peter's status as the original and places Norman Osborn behind the scheme. Readers who followed the sprawling sequence might remember both enjoyable character moments and an exhausting sense that any apparent answer could be reversed.

The lesson is not simply that long stories are bad, or that Ben Reilly was an error. His endurance as a character suggests that the concept had emotional life beyond the marketing hook. The problem is the gap between raising a question and deciding when a series has truly answered it. You can make readers passionately interested in a secret; that does not guarantee they will enjoy watching the secret become an indefinite mechanism for selling the next issue.

Now put beside Ben Reilly another invention from the same decade: Miguel O'Hara, the Spider-Man of the year 2099. His original series began in 1992, written by Peter David and drawn by Rick Leonardi. The opening setting is a future New York shaped by powerful corporations, including Alchemax. Miguel is not a teenage Peter waking up in a slightly shinier Queens, and he is not a claimant demanding that we stop loving Peter Parker. He is a different man, in a different social world, carrying a familiar kind of responsibility under conditions that make the original Spider-Man's worries look new.

Miguel O'Hara looks recognizably like Spider-Man from a distance, yet the familiar silhouette moves through a corporate future rather than Peter Parker's neighborhood. The costume has its own geometry, and the towers around it belong to a world in which Alchemax can shape a person's working life as well as the city. Miguel's altered biology and troubled relationship to that company give the mask a new set of pressures. The shift to 2099 does more than supply futuristic equipment: it creates a different person whose responsibilities grow from a different social order.

The contrast with the Clone Saga is revealing. The clone story focuses attention on who possesses the original history. The 2099 story asks what could be created if we stopped requiring the character to possess that history at all. This is not a clean division between one bad experiment and one good experiment; fans can appreciate Ben Reilly for precisely the human dilemmas the saga raised. But it shows that the nineties already contained a path toward Spider-Man as a family of stories, decades before the animated Spider-Verse made that idea common movie vocabulary.

And look at the physical comics in a shop. The first *Spider-Man 2099* issue arrived with a foil cover, a conspicuous feature of the period's marketing. The same object could sell a visual collectible and contain a story about an oppressive commercial future. That apparent contradiction is part of the decade's personality. Commercial packaging does not make artistic invention impossible. It simply means that the art and the sales pitch are now visibly negotiating with each other on the cover.

Spider-Man also becomes a major Saturday-morning and after-school presence in 1994 with *Spider-Man: The Animated Series*. The show introduces viewers to a sweeping gallery of villains, continuing stories, and a Peter who sounds and behaves like a young adult with an enormous number of obligations. To a television viewer this may be their definitive Spider-Man, even while comic readers are negotiating a confusing clone identity puzzle. Those audiences share the mask but not necessarily the storyline.

There are other changes around the franchise: comics sales boom, then the speculative market cools and the industry struggles. Marvel goes through a bankruptcy reorganization in the late 1990s. It would be careless to blame a single Spider-Man plot for an industry-wide crisis. The larger point is that the financial climate in which the franchise operates has become unstable, and creators and editors are working under pressures that go well beyond whether a story is satisfying.

If the 1980s asked whether Peter could have a permanent adult life, the 1990s asked something more unsettling: if the identity is recognizable enough, how much of Peter himself can be replaced? The Clone Saga makes that a fictional question. The era's merchandising, covers and spin-offs make it an industrial one.

## The 2000s: two Peters walk into the twenty-first century

Here is a good way to explain the 2000s without becoming trapped in a chronology chart. For much of the decade you can encounter two Peters, both published by Marvel, whose lives have different amounts of accumulated history.

In 2000, Brian Michael Bendis and Mark Bagley launch *Ultimate Spider-Man*. It starts over with a teenage Peter Parker in a contemporary world. The supporting cast and origin are recognizable, but the pacing, dialogue, school life and sequence of revelations are reimagined. This is not the original Peter traveling backward in time. It is a separate continuity, explicitly inviting a reader to start fresh.

The decision is more interesting than a mere attempt to modernize the hairstyles. By 2000, someone opening the long-running *Amazing Spider-Man* has decades of editorial history behind almost every person in the room. Gwen's death matters. Mary Jane's marriage matters. The Clone Saga matters, even when its status is embarrassing. A new reader can follow a good story without mastering all of that, but the book cannot pretend its past does not exist.

The Ultimate series offers a different bargain: you are allowed to meet the characters at the beginning again, and the writers get to decide which pieces of the established mythology arrive first. Uncle Ben can have time on the page before the tragedy. The Green Goblin can be redesigned. Conversations can occupy space that an older action-packed comic might have handled in a caption. This is a reboot as an invitation, not simply a correction.

Meanwhile, the continuing mainline Peter moves further into adulthood and strange mythology. J. Michael Straczynski's run, beginning in 2001 with art by John Romita Junior, explores whether Spider-Man's abilities have a larger mystical significance. It also gives Aunt May a more direct role in Peter's secret life. For a reader who loves the weight of accumulated experience, these stories offer something the beginning-again line cannot: consequences that can draw upon years of feeling as though you know the characters.

A particularly difficult issue appears after the September 11 attacks. *Amazing Spider-Man* volume two, issue thirty-six, presents a response by Straczynski and Romita Junior to a real catastrophe in New York. This is a moment when an escapist character's familiar city is not merely fictional scenery. The comic tries to speak from within the superhero tradition while the actual event exceeds the power of any superhero explanation. Its significance lies partly in that unresolved difficulty.

And then, in 2002, Sam Raimi's *Spider-Man* arrives in movie theaters with Tobey Maguire as Peter Parker. Millions of viewers can acquire the emotional structure of the character in one sitting. The screenplay and performances make Peter awkward, affectionate and sometimes selfish. The film gives him organic webbing instead of Ditko's mechanical web-shooters. That change alone could fill a fan argument, but it points to an adaptation principle: some artists will preserve the mechanism; others will preserve the dramatic effect.

The movie makes superhero cinema a central part of Spider-Man's public life. Raimi's *Spider-Man 2* in 2004 turns the exhausting overlap between responsibility and ordinary life into the basis of a blockbuster. Peter is brilliant at saving strangers and apparently terrible at turning up where his friends expect him. The train rescue captures the two sides at once: enormous public spectacle followed by something unexpectedly intimate between the hero and the people he has protected.

By now it is possible to be deeply attached to Spider-Man from films and know almost nothing about how the Clone Saga ended. This is not lesser fandom. It is the consequence of a character whose primary route into popular culture is no longer a single serialized comic.

It also changes the emotional scale of a Spider-Man story. In a comic, a missed appointment can be narrated in a caption, set beside a fistfight, and allowed to stay in the reader's mind as the real wound. A movie has to make that missed appointment legible through actors, editing, recurring situations, and the time it chooses to spend on people who cannot throw a punch. Raimi's second film takes great pleasure in showing Peter almost getting something he wants and then being interrupted. The pattern would sound tedious if described as a production schedule. On screen, with a harried performance and the right cuts, it becomes a running form of comedy until the cost is unmistakable.

This is an important distinction between fidelity and success. A film that follows the comic's event order but does not understand Peter's social embarrassment can be less faithful to the character's experience than one that changes how he fires webs. An animated series that borrows an entire rogues' gallery but fails to give its Peter a recognizable private life may reproduce a lot of Marvel trivia without reproducing much Spider-Man. Conversely, a game that never reenacts the original burglar encounter can still make responsibility felt by giving the player a wonderful way to travel and then asking them to interrupt the pleasure for someone else. Different media have different ways of making the central tension tangible.

And the 2000s are a good reminder that the comics do not vanish when movies arrive. The Ultimate line is not merely marketing support for a film. It is an experiment in pacing and serialized characterization that could only work as its own comic. The mainline title can make a shocking decision about an established marriage precisely because it is burdened with a history that the new movie audience does not need to know. The same mask is doing distinct artistic work in several places at once. That abundance can become exhausting; it can also be creatively productive.

Yet the comics keep making their own choices. The mainline Peter publicly unmasks during *Civil War* in 2006. Suddenly the secret identity that helped organize decades of stories is absent. One can imagine powerful new consequences for every relationship, every enemy, and every bit of mundane work Peter performs. The possibilities are enormous; so are the difficulties of sustaining them in an open-ended serial.

In 2007, *One More Day* ends with a bargain involving Mephisto, Peter, and Mary Jane. The continuity is altered so that their marriage has not happened. The lead-up also addresses the consequences of Peter's exposed identity and Aunt May's mortal danger. Readers who have only heard the premise should understand why it angered people: this is not simply a breakup between fictional characters. It retroactively changes a relationship the publisher had once promoted as a landmark event.

From one perspective, restoring a single Peter with a secret identity opens room for the missed dates, financial problems and romantic uncertainty that help Spider-Man stories work. From another, it asks long-time readers to surrender the consequences of stories they had invested in for twenty years. The debate is not settled by declaring that superheroes should always be young or that every change should be forever. Both rules would make an ongoing series much less interesting.

In 2008, *Brand New Day* begins a renewed status quo for the mainline *Amazing Spider-Man*. Readers are being invited to begin again, but by a very different method from the Ultimate project. One continuity was explicitly separate. The other continuity has rewritten something central inside the world that existing readers thought they knew.

So the decade's two Peters are two experiments in accessibility. The Ultimate line says, here is a new doorway. The *One More Day* revision says, we are moving the furniture in the old house. A newcomer might be delighted by either. A long-time reader could reasonably see a substantial difference.

## The 2010s: Spider-Man becomes a question about who gets the mask

At the beginning of the next decade, Spider-Man's identity grows beyond one fictional Peter. This is not the first time the suit has been worn by somebody else, but the new figures do more than temporarily replace him in an editorial emergency.

In 2011, after the death of the Ultimate universe's Peter Parker, Miles Morales makes his debut in *Ultimate Fallout* number four. His subsequent series comes from writer Brian Michael Bendis and artist Sara Pichelli. Miles is a teenager with family, friendships, fears and pressures of his own. He is not simply Peter with a different face. His situation is shaped by knowing that there has been a Spider-Man before him and by deciding what it means to inherit the responsibility of someone he never chose to replace.

That distinction makes the origin interesting. The 1962 Peter discovered power first and then understood responsibility through a terrible personal failure. Miles initially has the example of a hero whose absence is already a public event. He must decide what to do while the meaning of the costume is already defined by other people. An origin can work differently when the symbol exists before the person adopts it.

The main Marvel universe is trying an almost perversely different identity experiment. In 2013, the *Superior Spider-Man* era has Doctor Octopus occupying Peter Parker's body and attempting to be a more effective superhero than Peter. Otto has intelligence, ambition, technology and a professional belief that he can optimize the whole operation. What he does not possess is Peter's humane judgment. The story turns the old joke about Peter's messy life inside out. Perhaps an efficient Spider-Man can organize crime-fighting beautifully and still misunderstand what makes the job heroic.

Notice the pattern. The 1990s used Ben Reilly to ask which Spider-Man is authentic. The 2010s ask whether authenticity depends on the moral choices made inside the costume. Miles and Otto are not interchangeable case studies, of course. One is a young hero discovering his vocation; the other is an adversary attempting to appropriate it. Put together, however, they make a useful argument: Spider-Man is neither merely Peter's DNA nor merely a logo that works on anyone.

In 2014 and 2015, Marvel's comics use the *Spider-Verse* event to gather Spider-themed heroes from multiple realities. The very abundance of versions becomes part of the attraction. One can meet Spider-Gwen, a version of Gwen Stacy from a different universe who is a superhero herself. Fans no longer need to treat alternate timelines solely as a way of returning to the original Peter. Some alternatives have lives worth following on their own terms.

Miles subsequently enters the main Marvel continuity after the 2015 *Secret Wars* event, so the arrangement of Spider-people changes again. Peter and Miles can coexist in the same broad fictional world. Their coexistence answers an assumed dilemma: Miles can be Spider-Man without Peter dying, disappearing, or having his history erased.

At the same time, other kinds of adaptation are expanding the audience. Tom Holland's Peter joins the Marvel Cinematic Universe in 2016 and stars in *Spider-Man: Homecoming* in 2017. This Peter starts his screen career already looking up to a world of established superheroes. The problem is not merely learning how to use powers; it is resisting the temptation to become a miniature version of another hero. The franchise has changed enough that Peter can be the new kid in a superhero world, rather than the first remarkable person the audience has ever seen.

Insomniac's *Marvel's Spider-Man* game arrives in 2018 and makes a different promise: you can be the person doing the swinging. An experienced Peter is already Spider-Man. He is not starting with the spider bite. The game must make travel itself expressive and enjoyable, then make the dramatic story matter after the player has spent time learning the body's rhythm and the city's geometry. For some fans, the sensation of clearing a rooftop is as central to Spider-Man as a famous comic panel.

And in the same year, the animated film *Spider-Man: Into the Spider-Verse* finds a new visual language for Miles Morales. The film is not a straightforward adaptation of the 2014 comics event or a retelling of Miles's original comic issue for issue. It uses combinations of illustrated textures, comic-page devices, motion, and character-specific visual ideas to make the story of becoming Spider-Man an experience in form as well as plot.

Its most memorable triumph is not simply proving that another teenager can wear the costume. It makes the moment of choosing the costume feel like a change in how the world moves around him. Animation, sound and the direction of the image become part of character development. The format itself helps answer the question the story asks.

The difference between Miles and an anonymous new costume becomes particularly clear if we compare the ways these stories begin. For the original Peter, the astonishing power arrives inside a life already marked by school hierarchy, loneliness, Aunt May and Uncle Ben. The responsibility eventually follows a disastrous personal choice. For the early comics Miles, Peter is already a public example. Miles's question is whether a frightened kid can measure up to that memory without becoming a copy of it. The animated film then reshapes the starting conditions around his parents, his school life, his neighborhood, and other Spider-people who have arrived with their own complicated histories.

A logo can be printed on a hundred jackets. Character cannot. If Miles had no relationships outside the mask, nothing important to lose, and no peculiar way of speaking or moving, an audience could applaud the novelty once and then run out of reasons to return. The successful versions of Miles solve that problem by refusing to make his main narrative job the celebration of a new suit. His family relationships are not a decorative background. They create obligations and secrets that are his rather than Peter's. Miles's defining leap is not into another hero's vacancy. He accepts a responsibility that will alter the shape of his own life.

The decade is therefore doing something more complex than simply multiplying Spider-Men. It is testing whether one of the genre's strongest premises can support differences of upbringing, voice, artistic technique and moral emphasis. *Superior Spider-Man* answers by showing how badly the job goes when somebody can simulate the operations of Spider-Man without learning the ethics. *Into the Spider-Verse* answers by making a fresh voice and a fresh visual style inseparable from the hero's decision to act. Their tones could hardly be further apart, but both make the same useful demand: the person in the mask must be more than the ability to imitate a familiar picture.

If the earliest Spider-Man had been a teenager who could not fit into the heroic mold, the 2010s permit the mold to belong to more than one person. That does not eliminate the original Peter or the arguments about his marriage and age. It makes those arguments part of a larger landscape, in which different versions can take different risks without all of them being made the permanent answer.

## The 2020s: several futures at once

By the 2020s, asking for the current Spider-Man sounds like a request for a clarification. The films, the comics, the games, and the animated stories do not line up as chapters in a single biography. That is increasingly a feature rather than a defect.

In 2020, *Marvel's Spider-Man: Miles Morales* gives Miles a starring videogame of his own. This is not a menu option to play the original game in a different suit. Miles has a family and neighborhood world, a distinctive fighting vocabulary, and his own claims on what protecting New York involves. The player gets to learn his version of the job from inside it.

The 2021 film *Spider-Man: No Way Home* does something almost unimaginable to a comics fan reading in the sixties: it turns the existence of several earlier screen interpretations into the point of a blockbuster. Different Peters can meet without one having to be declared invalid. The film draws emotional power from the fact that audiences remember different eras of Spider-Man movies. It also relies on those memories being distinct. An earlier movie does not vanish merely because a studio has cast the character again.

Then *Spider-Man: Across the Spider-Verse* arrives in 2023, continuing Miles's animated story while letting visual styles collide and coexist. Insomniac's *Marvel's Spider-Man 2* arrives in the same year, putting both Peter and Miles in a larger playable city. The practical point is almost comical: there are enough versions of Spider-Man that different audiences can disagree about which one they mean without disagreeing that they love the character.

But the most revealing 2020s development may be a comic that plays a trick on the entire history we have just traced.

In 2024, Jonathan Hickman and Marco Checchetto begin a new *Ultimate Spider-Man*. The title sounds familiar if you remember the Bendis and Bagley series from 2000. It is not a continuation of that teenage origin. This is a different Ultimate universe, deliberately altered, in which Peter Parker did not get his spider powers as a teenager. By the time the opportunity to become Spider-Man reaches him, he is a grown man married to Mary Jane with two children.

Think about how many editorial arguments are packed into that starting point. The original Spider-Man could not escape the obligations of being a teenager. The 1987 Spider-Man married Mary Jane. The 2007 Spider-Man lost that marriage from his established continuity. The 2000 Ultimate line had offered a new teenager as a starting point. Now another Ultimate comic makes the existence of a stable family the premise rather than the reward that a series might one day permit.

That does not mean the 2024 series is a referendum whose result settles every debate about the original comic. It is not the long-running mainline Peter secretly receiving his marriage back. It is a separate telling with different history and different threats. It does, however, show that the adult-life question can generate stories rather than simply close them off. What if Spider-Man's first heroic obligation has to fit inside commitments he has already made as a husband and father? What if responsibility arrives not at the point when a young person enters adulthood, but long after someone has already learned what it costs to care for people?

The Hickman and Checchetto run concludes with issue twenty-four in 2026. Whatever readers think of its ending, the project's existence is a strong demonstration that reinvention does not always mean returning Peter to age fifteen. You can restart a hero at another life stage. The novelty is not a new name on the costume; it is a new answer to the question of what life the costume interrupts.

At the same time, the main *Amazing Spider-Man* series has not become this alternate Peter. A new volume launched in 2025 with Joe Kelly writing, while the familiar mainline character continued to deal with work, antagonists, and a complicated personal status quo. Peter can therefore be a struggling, long-running serial superhero in one publishing line and a married father beginning a heroic career in another. Neither version is a correction to the other's fictional biography, because they are not the same continuity.

This situation can be frustrating if what you want is one definitive Peter who grows continuously and never has a reset. It can be liberating if what you want is an exploration of paths the central series has not committed to taking. Both reactions make sense. The industry's solution to many old Spider-Man arguments is no longer always to choose one winning answer. Sometimes it is to give several answers separate shelves.

There is a cost to that solution, and it is worth being precise about it. Two versions of Peter Parker can lead different lives without contradicting each other, but that does not automatically satisfy a reader who wanted the older version to be allowed to grow. An alternative universe is a demonstration of possibility, not compensation for a decision made in a favorite ongoing comic. The family life of one Peter does not restore the marriage erased from another Peter's remembered publication history. Fans who object to an editorial reset are not necessarily objecting to the idea of alternatives; they may be objecting to the apparent loss of consequences in the story where their investment began.

At the same time, a separate continuity can be a genuine artistic gift. It frees creators from the exhausting expectation that every new possibility must be reconciled with a decades-old timeline. A world in which Peter receives his powers as an adult can ask questions the original origin could not without tearing up its own foundations. A Miles Morales story can focus on Miles's community rather than spending every issue proving his right to the name. A game can let Peter and Miles share the city and make their movement styles distinct. The test is whether freedom from continuity produces actual invention or merely a set of familiar faces shuffled into a new arrangement.

We should resist the comforting idea that readers must choose between pure continuity and endless reinvention. A serial can preserve meaningful consequences while opening new entry points. A self-contained alternate world can be coherent across its own story. A familiar character can change meaningfully without ceasing to be recognizable. These are difficult creative accomplishments, but they are not mutually exclusive in principle. In fact Spider-Man's history offers convincing examples of all three, alongside reminders of how often the industry settles for easier solutions.

The same multiplication brings a risk. When every version of Spider-Man can appear somewhere, the mask can become an endlessly reusable mark of recognition. References to earlier stories can substitute for doing the hard work of inventing a new one. A return appearance can be thrilling and empty at the same time. A new Spider-person still needs a reason to matter beyond the fact that the costume has different colors or that an audience remembers an old theme.

That is a present-tense creative challenge, not a prediction about how the rest of the decade must unfold. We are standing in 2026, not looking back from its end. There is no reason to pretend that the final shape of the 2020s Spider-Man is already fixed.

## What sixty-four years have actually preserved

We have crossed more than six decades of publication, and it is tempting to make a comfortable claim at the end: Spider-Man is timeless. But the history we just followed makes that description misleading. The character has been remodeled repeatedly. His face has been drawn by artists with profoundly different ideas of movement and beauty. He has lived as a schoolboy, a student, a husband, a teacher, a scientist, an employer, and a parent in different continuities. He has been a figure in a comic shop, a movie screen, a television schedule and a videogame map. Some of these versions contradict one another about details that fans care about intensely.

There is an opposite temptation, and it is just as unsatisfying. If the costume can mean anything, perhaps it means nothing in particular. Perhaps Spider-Man is simply a commercially durable shape into which another story can be poured, and the entire argument about Peter's age or his marriage is nostalgia for a specific childhood. That explanation fits the existence of the merchandise better than it fits the stories people actually return to.

Consider how many beloved Spider-Man scenes involve a form of vulnerability the hero could have avoided by not getting involved. The original Peter might have kept earning money from his powers without asking whom his choices were hurting. The Peter beneath Ditko's machinery could have surrendered to the weight. The young man in Gwen Stacy's story could have decided that trying and failing was reason never to love again. A married Peter can insist that a difficult relationship is not a problem to be solved by resetting his calendar. Miles can see the danger of a heroic identity and put on the mask anyway. Different eras disagree about the details, but they keep returning to what a person owes other people after learning how much harm and help that person is capable of.

That is not a prescription for a hero who must always sacrifice everything. In fact, one of the most interesting criticisms of Spider-Man stories is that they can confuse responsibility with compulsive self-punishment. If Peter repeatedly destroys every friendship and opportunity because he cannot allow anyone else to help, a writer ought to ask whether the behavior is noble or a symptom of the character's guilt. Protecting New York does not make every failed date a moral achievement. Sometimes the responsible thing is to be honest with a partner. Sometimes it is to trust a fellow hero. Sometimes it is to go home rather than invent another reason why the people you love will have to wait.

Peter's age changes the kind of story he can inhabit; it does not decide whether that story will work. A teenage Peter can be a wonderfully effective protagonist because he is making decisions before he has developed much judgment. An adult Peter can be effective because he has enough history to know better and still faces choices without safe answers. A father who has already built a family does not necessarily have fewer obligations than a teenager. He may have more, and they may contradict one another in ways a first crush never could. Growing up should change the questions a story asks, not automatically eliminate the capacity for surprise.

What the most durable Spider-Man stories preserve, then, is not a fixed list of facts. It is a relationship between power, judgment, and ordinary dependence. The hero can do remarkable things. The hero is not the only person with a life worth protecting. The world is full of people whose time, work, privacy, grief and affection matter even when a larger-than-life conflict interrupts them. Those people are not just hostages to be positioned between Peter and a villain. At their best, Aunt May, Mary Jane, Jonah, Harry, Robbie, Gwen, Felicia and many others make decisions that force Peter to revise his picture of the world. When the supporting cast becomes a set of switches to operate the superhero plot, the story loses much of its human scale.

The same applies to the villains. Doctor Octopus is memorable partly because he exposes the arrogance of someone who believes intelligence licenses domination. Norman Osborn can strike at the border between Peter's public and private lives. Venom can turn a costume and an injury into a terrible parody of intimacy. Kraven can prove how little the external accomplishments of Spider-Man mean without the choices of the man who made them. These are not interchangeable opponents lined up for an endless tournament. Their best stories reveal a limitation in Peter or an idea that Peter must resist. If every villain is merely stronger than the previous one, there are only so many places the spectacle can go. If every villain threatens a different part of a person's life, the series has room to keep changing.

And there is the city. A web-swing is one of the most joyous things a superhero can do. The motion works on a page when an artist persuades us that one body is part of an enormous constructed space. It works in a film when editing, performance and sound make the drop between buildings a decision. It works in a game when our hands learn a rhythm of speed and height. The pleasure is immediate. But the city that makes this movement possible is also the city that interrupts it. Someone calls for help from a street Peter could have flown over. A stranger sees the mask and draws the wrong conclusion. A police officer, a neighbor or a reporter has a point of view the superhero cannot simply override. Spider-Man is at his best when New York is not a private obstacle course built for his enjoyment.

Another thing the decades preserve is disagreement. The Lee and Ditko era is not the same as Romita's. The married Peter of the late eighties is not the same dramatic proposition as the single Peter of *Brand New Day*. The Bendis and Bagley *Ultimate Spider-Man* and the 2024 adult-Peter *Ultimate Spider-Man* use the same title for nearly opposite entrance strategies. Miles Morales is not an editorial footnote to Peter. His most successful stories would be diminished if we treated him as a mere argument that anyone can put on a red mask. Fans who prefer a particular period often have good reasons, because the alternatives genuinely emphasize different values.

A person who came through the 1994 cartoon may hear Spider-Man first as a specific voice carrying serial melodrama. Someone whose first experience was the Raimi films may remember the train full of strangers and the exhausting effort of trying to live two lives. Someone who learned to swing through Insomniac's New York may know the character first as a feeling in their hands. Someone who kept buying the monthly comics through the marriage may regard Peter and Mary Jane's shared adult life as the payoff of years of characterization. And a reader whose first Spider-Man is Miles may feel no obligation to treat that point of entry as a secondary one. Those perspectives do not reduce to who has memorized the most issue numbers.

You can test a telling of Spider-Man against this history without asking it to recite the history correctly. Start with a simple question: What does this version think is difficult about doing the right thing? Not what is difficult about hitting the villain. What is difficult about making a judgment and living with the result? Then ask what the telling believes Spider-Man wants when nobody needs to be rescued. Does he want admiration, belonging, love, money, freedom, a quiet evening, the chance to be a good parent? If he wants nothing outside the costume, the central conflict becomes thin. If he wants everything and does not care whom he hurts, the hero becomes hollow in a different direction.

Next ask how the chosen medium helps make that struggle felt. Does a comics artist use space, posture and the delay between panels to show effort? Does an actor make a familiar lie painfully embarrassing? Does a screenwriter give the people around Peter reasons to stay, leave, or argue that are not all about the villain's schedule? Does animation make Miles's growing confidence visible before anybody announces it? Does the videogame let a player enjoy freedom and then interrupt it with someone else's need? These are ways of telling the story, not decorative flourishes after the real story has been written.

Finally ask whether the work allows its own decisions to count. A long-running comic cannot preserve every change forever. Some reversals are necessary for a particular story, and new readers deserve doors into a complicated fictional universe. But when a story invites an audience to care about a commitment, a loss or a personal victory, it is taking on a small obligation of its own. It should not casually deny the meaning of that event merely because the next issue needs an easy starting position. This does not require a continuity with no mistakes. It requires taking emotional consequences seriously, even when the details of the world change.

These questions also explain why we do not need a final, official Spider-Man to end a history of the character. The most interesting version is not necessarily the oldest, the most powerful, the most connected to other franchises, or the one that owns the largest share of a reader's nostalgia. It is the version that understands why the person inside the suit has something at stake when the suit comes on. A new adaptation is free to change the web-shooters, the supporting cast, the age, the home, and even the person under the mask. That freedom is exciting. The challenge is to use it to tell a story instead of merely displaying the range of possible costumes.

Return, for a moment, to that early drawing of Peter trapped beneath the machinery. You can now see it not only as a classic comic-book image but as a small model for an extraordinary cultural history. A teenager who was originally written to be prickly and selfish has become a hero that people from very different decades can recognize. Some of those people want him to remain young. Others want him to age. Some follow the comics; some have never read one. Some know a version of Spider-Man who does not even share Peter's name. Yet across these differences, the enduring moment is not a pose of effortless victory. It is the moment someone who might fail decides that another person's life is worth the effort.

The future will produce another first issue, another costume, another performance, another argument over what ought to remain permanent. Some of those new stories will be excellent. Some will be marketing exercises with a decent cover. Nothing in sixty-four years of publication gives us a reason to expect perfection from what comes next, and nothing demands that we settle the debate about Peter Parker once and for all.

But there is a good ending available to this story right now. Peter does not become Spider-Man because he has found a life without problems. Miles does not become Spider-Man because the original hero has left a vacancy in a product line. The mask matters when someone chooses to accept a responsibility that cannot be reduced to fame, strength or the right to wear it. The people who make that choice will be different, because the world around them is different. The choice must still mean something.

There is no need to leave this history hanging on the promise of a next reboot. We have seen the boy at the beginning, the adults he might become, the people who inherit his example, the city that keeps asking something of him, and the artists who give that demand a visible form. The last image can be a simple one: a figure high above New York, poised between one building and another. The swing looks like freedom. Somewhere below, somebody is going to need help. The story begins again when he decides what to do.

### Interpretive limits

Labels like “darker decade,” “collector boom,” “return to basics,” and “what it felt like to be a fan” simplify overlapping trends. In particular, the 1990s sales/collectibles market had many causes, and no single title explains Marvel's later financial difficulties. Statements about what an era *means* are critical readings rather than quotes from individual creators. The ending of *Kraven's Last Hunt*, the heroine's death in the 1973 Goblin story, the *One More Day* rewrite, Miles's original circumstances, and Otto's takeover are discussed with spoilers because their narrative effects are essential to the historical analysis.
