# Miles Morales Was Not Built to Be Peter Parker Two

*Series:* Spider-Man: The Comics, the Character, and the Culture  
*Lecture ID:* 014000  
*Section:* characters-and-conflicts  
*Editorial form:* Comics-first character study with explicit adaptation comparisons.  
*Spoilers:* Peter Parker's fate in the original Ultimate universe; Miles's origin, his conflict with Aaron Davis, and the move into the main Marvel continuity. Film references concern *Into the Spider-Verse* and *Across the Spider-Verse*.

Picture a thirteen-year-old who has just won a school lottery. He is one of the lucky students admitted to a highly sought-after academy. His parents are delighted and worried. The boy is not sure he deserves what he has been given. Meanwhile, he has acquired powers so improbable that winning a school place ought to be the second-strangest thing on his mind.

The boy is Miles Morales. And the school lottery is the detail I would like to begin with, rather than the spider bite.

A spider bite immediately invites comparison with Peter Parker. A lottery makes a different kind of promise. It is not about what Miles can do. It is about what opportunities he has, what people expect of him, and how a child learns to make decisions in a family where those decisions have consequences. The extraordinary powers arrive inside a life that is already complicated.

When Miles debuted in 2011, one obvious question was whether Marvel was simply making a replacement Peter Parker. It was a question the publishing premise encouraged. But if we stop at that question, we miss what the early comics were actually building. Miles is not interesting because every feature of Peter's story has been reproduced with a new name. He is interesting because he inherits an impossible job before he has learned what kind of person he wants to be.

## A world with a vacant mask

Marvel's Ultimate line began in 2000 as an alternate continuity, separate from the long-running main Marvel universe. Its original *Ultimate Spider-Man* starred a new version of Peter Parker, written by Brian Michael Bendis and initially drawn by Mark Bagley. Readers could begin with Peter in high school without learning decades of accumulated adventures. That younger Peter eventually died in his world's superhero conflict. Miles's debut in *Ultimate Fallout* number four, published in August 2011, followed that loss. Bendis and Sara Pichelli are the central creators of Miles; the original issue also includes other stories and credited contributors, so an issue's whole credit list should not be mistaken for the authorship of every individual segment.

Miles originally succeeds a Peter Parker from the Ultimate universe, not the same Peter who has appeared in *The Amazing Spider-Man* since the nineteen-sixties. A new reader who knows only the films may imagine a single timeline in which the familiar Peter dies and hands his mask to Miles. The early comics are doing something more precise: they let one fictional world absorb the death of its Spider-Man, then ask whether that world can recognize a successor.

Miles's first solo series, *Ultimate Comics Spider-Man*, began in September 2011 with Bendis writing and Pichelli drawing. The early artwork gives Miles an immediately recognizable physical presence. The proportions of his body, the pose of his shoulders, and the hesitation of a young hero who has not yet mastered his movement are not incidental. We can recognize the icon of Spider-Man and still see that the person inside has a different relation to the mask.

The new costume helps. Miles's black-and-red design does not hide the fact that he inherits the spider symbol; it makes the inheritance visible through a redesign. A costume is a remarkably efficient form of storytelling when the audience has spent years learning its visual language. Shift the colors, alter the stance, and the eye reads both continuity and change.

It would be tempting to make Miles's moral origin a simple copy of Uncle Ben's death. The original Ultimate comics take another route. Miles has powers before Peter dies, and initially chooses not to become a superhero. After the death, he wonders whether acting sooner could have made a difference. His guilt grows out of hesitation in the presence of another person's public sacrifice. He does not need the same uncle or the same accident in order to feel an obligation. In fact, the difference is part of the point.

Responsibility is not a ritual that must happen exactly once in the same sequence to everyone who wears the costume. It is the problem each person must recognize in circumstances that are his own.

## A friend who does not exist to be impressed

Miles's best friend Ganke Lee knows his secret early. Ganke is not just a roommate waiting to be endangered by a supervillain. He is an enthusiast, a confidant, and sometimes the person who asks the kind of excited questions that a reader might ask. He helps turn a bewildering physical transformation into something two young people can actually discuss.

That relationship changes the rhythm of a Spider-Man story. Peter Parker's secret traditionally isolates him. He lies to people he loves, invents increasingly improbable excuses, and discovers that a joke told behind a mask is easier than an honest sentence at home. Miles has concealment problems too, but he has a friend who can share the impossible information almost from the start. He does not have to process all of it alone.

If you were designing "Peter Parker Two," one economical method would be to reproduce Peter's childhood dynamics, his supporting characters and his conflicts. Miles's friendship with Ganke is evidence of a more interesting choice. The comics give him a confidant with his own temperament and a willingness to be involved. It is not necessary to pretend that their friendship is always perfectly written or that later stories never repeat an old Spider-Man beat. The stronger claim is that the original framework creates a different social possibility.

There is another important relationship: Miles's parents. Rio Morales is his mother, and his father Jefferson is understandably worried about what kind of future his son might have. Uncle Aaron Davis, Jefferson's brother, is charming to Miles and involved in crime as the Prowler. Miles's parents disapprove of Aaron, but that does not prevent the boy from seeking him out.

The family conflict is not simply that one adult is good and another is bad. Aaron can be affectionate toward Miles and still make destructive choices. Jefferson can be trying to protect his child and still carry assumptions about masked vigilantes that make telling him the truth dangerous. The adults bring old loyalties and grievances into Miles's new life. Their decisions are not merely reactions to the boy's powers; they have troubles and commitments of their own.

In the early comics, the bite comes through a spider associated with Aaron's criminal activity at Oscorp. Later Miles must confront Aaron as the Prowler, a person he loves who also attempts to use him. The first conflict is not "Can this new Spider-Man beat a green monster?" It is "What does a child do when an adult he trusts asks him to cross a line?"

That is a deeply Spider-Man-shaped question, but it is not Peter's exact story. The pressure comes from an existing relationship, from competing loyalties, and from Miles learning that affection is not the same as permission to do harm.

## A different set of tools

Miles has many of Peter's powers: wall-crawling, agility, strength, a danger-sense. But he also has camouflage that can make him nearly invisible, and a bioelectric attack commonly called a venom blast. Here it is important to make a correction for people who arrived through another part of the franchise: this venom has nothing to do with the alien symbiote known as Venom. The name describes an electrical sting, not an extraterrestrial roommate.

The abilities alter the design of action. Peter's most familiar fights are kinetic arguments: avoid a blow, swing around the enemy, use the environment, find a clever way to get the villain off balance. Miles can disappear, which makes a scene about hiding, surprise or uncertainty. He can deliver an electrical stun, which gives him an option Peter does not possess in the same way. Readers and game players can recognize the inherited spider vocabulary and watch it branch.

Pichelli's art is important here. A power is not distinctive merely because a handbook lists it. It must change how a page or scene reads. A nearly invisible person in a crowded setting produces one kind of visual suspense. An abrupt electric strike produces a different beat. Miles's costume, body language and powers help make his scenes legible as his own.

The early comics also move beyond inheritance by letting Miles meet a living Peter Parker from another reality. In *Spider-Men* from 2012, the established Earth-616 Peter encounters the young Ultimate hero. There is comedy in the logistics of two people answering to Spider-Man, but there is also something more interesting: Peter can serve as a model without being the only legitimate version of the role. Mentorship is a relationship, not a transfer of legal ownership.

After the 2015 *Secret Wars* event, Miles becomes part of Marvel's main continuity. Two Spider-Men can therefore share a world, not just meet as visitors from separate ones. The premise changes from replacement to coexistence. It raises its own questions about how a publisher can support two ongoing interpretations, sometimes in the same city, without declaring that one of them is merely redundant.

## The films are not the comics, and that is all right

The animated *Spider-Verse* films brought Miles to an audience vastly larger than the original comics readership. They also changed important elements. *Into the Spider-Verse* makes a multiverse collision part of his first heroic adventure, gives the older Peter a damaged personal life, and builds a spectacular visual language around Miles's confidence. His uncle Aaron's role and fate in the film are not identical to the early Ultimate comics. You should not assume that a remembered movie scene explains the comic's sequence.

But the films recognize the strongest part of the character's design. Miles has to decide whether he is allowed to become Spider-Man without first becoming Peter Parker. His leap into the city works because it is both action and a statement of identity. The animation changes from a boy struggling to coordinate his limbs to a body that can claim its place in the frame.

*Across the Spider-Verse* pushes the same argument into a conflict with a larger Spider-society that believes heroic stories must follow certain tragic patterns. Whatever one thinks of that film's cliffhanger, the creative question is not obscure: do the people who came before you have the authority to decide what kind of hero you are allowed to be?

That question resonates with a publishing tradition that often treats established continuity as a set of rules. It also explains why two fans can have different favorite versions of Miles without either being mistaken about his appeal. The early Bendis-Pichelli comics emphasize family conflict and the weight of stepping into a dead hero's place. The animated films emphasize visual self-definition and resistance to an imposed destiny. The Insomniac games give Miles a neighborhood and abilities that players can inhabit. Each takes a different route toward a related question.

There is room for criticism. A storyline can rely too heavily on whether Miles deserves to be Spider-Man, making him spend yet another adventure asking permission to exist. Film adaptations can overshadow the comics that supplied the initial character and many of his relationships. And any franchise with multiple versions runs the risk of treating one origin as a mandatory template even when it was originally invented to make room for variation.

The best way to take Miles seriously is not to insist that he is entirely unrelated to Peter. Of course he is related. The mask is a shared language, and the sense of responsibility is an inheritance. It is also not to insist that every difference is automatically a creative triumph. The useful question is whether the differences allow a fully functioning character, with relationships and decisions that matter even when Peter is absent.

Return to the boy who won the school lottery. He has responsibilities before the costume enters his life. He has a family with conflicts he cannot simply punch away. He has a friend with whom he can laugh about the impossible. He makes choices for reasons that are not identical to those of the man who wore the mask first.

Miles Morales works when "another Spider-Man" stops being the entire description and starts being the beginning of a story.
