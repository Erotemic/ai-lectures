# How to Draw a Web-Swing: Spider-Man's Artists and the City Beneath Him

*Series:* Spider-Man: The Comics, the Character, and the Culture

You can draw Spider-Man in a way that makes him look strong. You can draw him in a way that makes him look fast. And you can draw him in a way that makes him look like the only animal on the planet whose skeleton was invented after the rest of him.

Those are not quite the same drawing. Spider-Man's costume is one of the most recognizable graphic designs in comics, but the shape of the person inside it has never been fixed. Different artists have given him a different center of gravity, a different relationship to the buildings, even a different attitude toward the idea of falling. If you took the dialogue out of a comic and left only the figure swinging through the city, you could often guess the decade, and perhaps the artist.

Why is he so rewarding to draw? The obvious answer is the costume. It has bold colors, an unmistakable emblem, and a mask that allows emotions to be implied through posture. The better answer is the environment. Spider-Man does not ordinarily fly. He has to travel through a three-dimensional obstacle course made of buildings, traffic, ledges and gaps. Each web line suggests a connection to something beyond the frame. His body is pulled in one direction while his attention may be directed in another. A good Spider-Man drawing has to explain not only where he is, but where he has been and where he might go next.

Consider a simple imaginary panel. Spider-Man is at the far left, a thin white web leaving his right hand and disappearing beyond the top right corner. His legs trail behind him, and a row of windows slants downward through the background. Without showing the next panel, the artist has given you a direction of travel, an attachment point somewhere overhead, and a feeling of speed. Change the pose so his legs are tucked tightly beneath him and his free hand points toward a wall. The same figure now seems ready to land or reverse direction. Change the city from a legible building facade to a blur of abstract shapes, and the action feels different again.

The artwork is not a decorative version of an action script. It is the action.

## Ditko draws a creature, not a flying athlete

Steve Ditko's early Spider-Man is sometimes surprising to people raised on polished movie posters. His Peter Parker can look pinched, intense and uncomfortable. In costume, he does not always form the heroic silhouette of a strong man launching himself through the sky. He may cling with a flat palm, fold his body around a corner, or perch in an angular position that would be unsettling on a normal human being. The spider part of the premise is not merely a chemical accident that grants powers; it changes the way the figure occupies space.

It is useful to compare Spider-Man with a generic caped superhero. A flying hero can float against a sky with the arms held forward. The body and the intention point roughly the same way. Spider-Man, by contrast, often seems to be negotiating with surfaces. His shoulders can be turned away from the direction of movement. The feet are available to grab a wall. The back bends because his movement depends on catching and releasing. A building is not just scenery; it is half the apparatus that makes his powers useful.

The smaller eye shapes in many early drawings also leave the artist with an interesting problem. How do you make a masked character expressive when the eyes cannot change like a living face? By shifting the head, curving the torso and making the arms speak. A hand planted above the head can imply caution. The same hand extended toward an unseen threat can imply urgency. Readers learn to infer mood from anatomy that is itself delightfully improbable.

In *The Amazing Spider-Man* number thirty-three, the famous Master Planner escape works for reasons that are almost the opposite of a web-swing. Peter is trapped beneath heavy machinery and cannot move. Ditko's sequence forces the reader to experience increasing effort across a succession of images. It is a reminder that comics movement includes the representation of resistance. An artist who can make stillness heavy can make motion feel earned. The earlier Ditko essay in this series explores that scene's creative history in detail; here its importance is the contrast between a body with nowhere to go and a body usually able to make the entire city into a route.

## Romita gives the body a public life

John Romita Senior's arrival on *The Amazing Spider-Man* with number thirty-nine in 1966 changed more than the shape of a jaw. His background in romance comics mattered. Faces became smoother, fashions more contemporary, emotional encounters more polished, and Peter's world more socially inviting. The change is visible when Mary Jane Watson finally receives her full entrance in issue forty-two. She is not simply a plot device added after the fight. Her presence announces a comic that wants the reader to care about social chemistry as seriously as acrobatics.

This is not a claim that Ditko did not understand emotion or that Romita stopped drawing Spider-Man as an acrobat. The point is that a series' drawing style affects which pleasures it can sustain. A pinched, isolated young hero inhabits one kind of city. A more handsome, increasingly socially connected Peter inhabits another. Romita's strength was to make personal relationships feel like an equally legitimate source of drama.

The web-swing gives Peter a physical escape from social embarrassment, so its balance of grace and vulnerability is part of the storytelling. After a terrible conversation, he can put on the mask and cross the city in enormous arcs. When the artwork makes him physically graceful, the contrast with his badly managed human life becomes comic relief and emotional compensation. A comic can make you enjoy the swing not merely because it is fast but because it provides the relief Peter cannot find at the dinner table.

Romita spoke in interviews about how the romance material was becoming central to Spider-Man's popularity. The artist's influence is therefore not reducible to a before-and-after chart of eye lenses or muscles. The city grows more appealing because the people in it grow more dramatically expressive.

## Ross Andru finds a real anchor point

For a different answer to how Spider-Man should move, look at Ross Andru's work on the title through much of the nineteen seventies. Andru had an acute interest in New York's actual built environment. His colleague Gerry Conway recalled that Andru photographed buildings and views from rooftops to help compose Spider-Man's adventures. Writer and editor Paul Levitz also remembered Andru climbing onto rooftops with a camera, seeking perspectives that would make the city convincing.

This produces a special pleasure. A web-swing is inherently fantastic, but its buildings can feel specific. A facade recedes at a believable angle. A row of windows remains consistent while the characters change position. You can imagine an intersection existing beyond the panel instead of the world ending at its border. Spider-Man's movement acquires geography.

There is a piece of drawing craft that is easy to miss here. If the artist moves the scenery randomly from panel to panel, the reader cannot tell whether the character accelerated, reversed, or simply teleported. Andru was praised by Conway for his ability to maintain a spatial relationship while action unfolded across several images. In some sequences, a background can remain effectively fixed while figures move through it. The environment becomes a measuring instrument. The reader senses how far a body has traveled because the surrounding architecture refuses to lie.

Andru's pursuit of recognizable buildings could have consequences outside the page. Accounts of a Spider-Man story involving a real house in Far Rockaway describe complaints after readers connected the fictional location to an actual residence. The exact legal outcome is reported inconsistently, so it is better not to turn it into a confident courtroom anecdote. What is well supported is the underlying artistic habit: Andru wanted to put Spider-Man somewhere that looked like the actual city.

A fan can learn a good deal by comparing an Andru street scene with a panel built mostly around an enormous costumed figure against speed lines. Both can be effective. One sells the space that makes the adventure possible. The other sells the immediate sensation of an athletic body cutting through it.

## McFarlane makes the spider impossible again

Then comes Todd McFarlane's run on *The Amazing Spider-Man*, beginning with issue two hundred ninety-eight in 1988. If you remember only one detail, it may be the webbing. The lines multiplying around the figure look less like a single tidy cable and more like a spectacular snarl. The mask's eyes grow. The poses stretch. Spider-Man can appear all elbows, knees and twisting ribs, a wiry creature whose anatomy seems to have volunteered for a different set of rules.

McFarlane did not pretend this was a medical drawing. In later interviews he discussed deliberately sacrificing anatomical correctness for a more exciting pose. The impossibility was part of the idea. A body with a spine arranged for spectacular movement does not ask permission from a textbook skeleton.

There is a lovely wrinkle in the history of his web lines. In an interview with Jim Salicrup, McFarlane credited an earlier Mike Golden drawing as a significant inspiration for the extravagant webbing, while also describing his own memories of Ditko's style as larger and more exaggerated than Ditko had actually drawn it. This is how visual traditions evolve: through direct study, homage, imperfect memory and the creative exaggeration of influences. An artist remembers a spider-like oddity and turns the dial much further than the remembered original.

McFarlane's choices were not instantly accepted as a universal house style. He recalled resistance to the large eyes and dense webbing. Later versions of the character inherited many of those traits. But the point is not that he conclusively improved the design. It is that a powerful interpretation can change what later audiences think the design always was. A kid who first meets Spider-Man through one artist may become convinced that every authentic version needs those enormous eyes.

There is also a danger to the virtuoso pose. If every panel puts the hero in the most extreme arrangement possible, the book can lose the quiet differences between resting, balancing, accelerating and striking. A spectacular cover and a clear action sequence solve related but different problems. The most flexible artists know when a simple silhouette will carry more information than an intricate contortion.

## Bagley and Pichelli draw legibility into a new generation

Mark Bagley was central to the look of *Ultimate Spider-Man*, the series launched in 2000 by Brian Michael Bendis and collaborators. Its project was to introduce a contemporary version of Peter Parker without assuming that a new reader knew decades of previous continuity. Bagley's drawing had to carry not just action but conversations, embarrassment, family tensions and the slow development of a teenager. If the only memorable panels were Peter flying over the skyline, the project would fail. The reader needed to recognize Peter when he was in a classroom, at home or trying to conceal the reason he had just vanished.

Think about what that asks of a comics artist. A superhero pose can be designed as an isolated beautiful image. A long conversation needs continuity: where each person stands, how their expression changes, what a pause looks like, whether one hand suddenly shifting reveals nervousness. Comics storytelling depends on those almost invisible decisions. Bagley's energetic action became one strand in a version of Spider-Man that also needed to be accessible as adolescent drama.

Sara Pichelli, working with Bendis on the early Miles Morales comics, faced a related but distinct task. Miles's debut followed the death of the Ultimate Universe's Peter Parker; he inherited a symbol already charged with public expectation. Pichelli's drawings, with collaborators including colorist Justin Ponsor and letterer Cory Petit on *Ultimate Comics Spider-Man* number one, help establish an individual young character instead of a spare Peter with an alternate color scheme.

If you set Miles's black-and-red costume beside the classic red-and-blue, both are instantly readable as Spider-Man. The family resemblance is real. But posture, pacing and costume design give Miles a new visual identity. The artist has to make him recognizably part of a tradition while allowing him to seem uncertain, younger, and eventually confident on his own terms. This is another reason to study the figure between fights. The new Spider-Man is most interesting when the drawing is not yet willing to make him look as comfortable as a veteran.

## The hidden fourth character in every swing

There are at least three things in a compelling web-swing panel: a body, a line of action, and a city that makes the line meaningful. There is also a fourth, invisible participant: the reader, who supplies the movement between still pictures. The artist decides which moments to give us. We invent the motion connecting them, and we do so remarkably quickly when the geometry is clear.

That is why art-credit arguments are not pedantry. In the most meaningful sense, who draws Spider-Man helps determine what Spider-Man can do. Ditko emphasizes contact and unease. Romita gives Peter's world social charisma. Andru gives the city weight and coherent geography. McFarlane makes anatomy an arena for spectacle. Bagley and Pichelli demonstrate how speed must coexist with the grammar of conversations and growing up. These are tendencies within rich bodies of work, not boxes every panel must fit.

The next time you open a Spider-Man comic, try a simple experiment. Find a swing, cover the words, and follow the direction of the body and the web. Can you tell where the next movement goes? Do the buildings locate the hero, or do they act as a dizzying abstract stage? Does the figure look like a gymnast, a dancer, an insect, or a man who has realized one second too late that he chose the wrong building?

All four may be excellent Spider-Man drawings. The artist's choice tells you which kind of Spider-Man you are reading before he has said a single word.
