# Clones, Marriage, and the Problem of Letting Peter Parker Grow Up

Suppose you have followed Peter Parker for years. You watched him leave high school. You watched relationships begin and fail. You watched him marry Mary Jane Watson. You invested in the idea that these things counted. Then a new storyline arrives, and it asks whether the Peter whose life you have been reading was actually a clone.

Later, the same publishing universe asks you to accept a bargain with a supernatural being that removes the marriage from his history. These are not the same plot, and they did not happen for identical reasons. But they belong in the same conversation because they expose the toughest mechanical problem of a long-running superhero: how does a character grow for decades and still remain commercially recognizable as the person readers met at the beginning?

In normal life, a person becomes older. They move away, change jobs, break up, marry, have children, grieve, and accumulate history. A serialized character may do all those things while remaining within reach of a reader who discovers the title next month. Eventually the series must decide whether to carry the full weight of that accumulated life or rearrange the weight until a familiar status quo becomes possible again.

Fans argue about the Clone Saga and *One More Day* because the arguments are not only about twists in fiction. They are about the bargain between readers and an ongoing series. When does continuity reward attention? When does it start to imprison the writers? When does a reset create new stories, and when does it tell the audience that the emotional investment of the last twenty years was negotiable?

There is no sensible way to answer those questions by declaring that comics should never change. But dismissing every change as inconsequential because the characters are fictional ignores the relationship readers have built with a continuing story. The conflict lies between continuity as an inheritance and change as a condition of keeping that story alive.

## The clone who was not supposed to be a footnote

Spider-Man had a clone story long before the 1990s saga. In *The Amazing Spider-Man* number one hundred forty-nine, published in 1975, the Jackal's experiments had put Peter Parker into a situation involving an apparent duplicate. That could have remained a strange episode from an old comic, one of the many science-fiction dangers Peter escaped and later writers could revisit.

Instead, years later the idea returned with enormous consequences. The clone who had survived became Ben Reilly, named for Uncle Ben and Aunt May's maiden name. He possessed Peter's memories and much of his moral framework, yet lacked a stable claim on the life those memories described. This is genuinely potent science fiction. If two people remember the same childhood, does either own it more completely? If both believe in responsibility, which one deserves to wear the mask? And can a character begin as somebody else's copy and still become a person in his own right?

Ben was not interesting only because readers might confuse him with Peter. He had a distinct life on the road, different relationships, and a way of trying to build identity after his origin had been stolen from him. His Scarlet Spider costume made that difference visible. The famous red outfit with a blue sleeveless hoodie is wonderfully confident for something so consciously homemade. It says: I know the symbol matters, but I am not simply borrowing Peter's whole wardrobe.

The 1990s storyline developed the terrible possibility that Peter was the clone and Ben the original. That is the kind of premise capable of making a long-time reader sit bolt upright. It is also capable of making a long-time reader ask a question you probably do not want hanging over the entire series: then whose life have I been reading?

A novel can reveal that a narrator is unreliable and then end. A superhero serial has no such convenient boundary. A monthly franchise has to live with the revelation, including its consequences for customers who have bought hundreds of prior issues. The clone idea attacks the most valuable form of continuity: the belief that the ongoing protagonist is the person to whom those memories and choices belong.

Marvel's published character history of Ben Reilly traces how the saga eventually revealed Ben to be the clone, with Norman Osborn having manipulated the evidence and participants. Ben dies while saving Peter, his body degenerating and resolving the biological question. That settles the plot at one level. It does not settle all the readers' feelings. People had spent time caring about Ben in his own right. An explanation that says he was not the original does not mean he was not a character.

This is why the easiest jokes about the Clone Saga miss something. The story became sprawling and tangled, and criticism of its reversals is fair. But the premise contained an excellent existential problem. Ben was a way to ask whether responsibility could survive an identity crisis. An exhausting serial publication history and a worthwhile character concept can coexist. The same comic can generate an iconic hoodie and an argument about what on earth is going on.

## A marriage is not just a scene in a wedding issue

Peter Parker and Mary Jane Watson married in *Amazing Spider-Man Annual* number twenty-one in 1987. The ceremony itself is easy to locate in an issue guide. Its larger importance is harder to put in a catalog entry.

A marriage is not only a romantic milestone. It changes what stories are available. If Peter can confide in a partner who knows his identity, certain familiar misunderstandings become less plausible. In their place come different conflicts: sharing risk, making decisions as a couple, balancing heroic obligations with long-term commitment, and accepting that another adult has independent priorities. The narrative possibilities are not necessarily smaller. They are different.

Fans disagree about whether that difference is good for Spider-Man, and the disagreement often contains a hidden assumption about what the character is supposed to be. Is he fundamentally a teenager trying to preserve an ordinary life while superheroes interrupt it? Is he an adult whose life should accumulate commitments and consequences? Can he be both, at different points in a career? If you think the first quality must dominate forever, marriage looks like a threat to the engine. If you think the second is essential, undoing marriage looks like a refusal to let the character's experiences count.

There is also Mary Jane to consider. A relationship is not merely a setting that determines Peter's difficulty level. She has her own career, judgment, risks, and ways of understanding their shared life. When a story asks whether the marriage should continue, it is asking about two characters, even if the logo on the cover names one. A critique that treats her only as an obstacle preventing Peter from dating other people has already lost a substantial part of the fiction.

The long-running comic has a practical business challenge as well. Each issue is simultaneously the continuation of old stories and a possible first issue for somebody. Sometimes a new creative team wants access to familiar circumstances. Sometimes an editor wants a broad audience to recognize the hero they know from outside the comics. Those are understandable pressures in serial publishing. But when we explain an individual historical decision, we need evidence for what specific people actually said. It would be too easy to invent a single backstage conversation that supposedly explains years of editorial policy.

## One More Day: the solution that became another problem

Then comes *One More Day*, the four-part 2007 storyline involving *The Amazing Spider-Man*, *Friendly Neighborhood Spider-Man*, and *The Sensational Spider-Man*. The story was written by J. Michael Straczynski, with Joe Quesada deeply involved as writer and artist. Peter's public unmasking during the Civil War era has put loved ones at risk. Aunt May is gravely wounded. A supernatural bargain offered by Mephisto promises to save her, but its cost is the marriage between Peter and Mary Jane.

The reaction becomes intelligible without a page-by-page account of the fictional mechanism. The event makes the continuity itself an object that can be traded. It is not simply that a couple divorces, separates, or discovers their relationship has changed. A force inside the fictional universe changes the history of that relationship. That is a much more aggressive narrative operation.

Why does it bother some readers so intensely? Because a marriage is not one fact on a character sheet. It is a framework in which countless earlier scenes were understood. Alter that framework, and the reader has to decide how to remember those scenes. Their emotional meaning may survive even if the revised continuity denies the legal or historical event that once organized them.

There is also a moral discomfort that belongs to Spider-Man specifically. His founding story says that power brings responsibility. His most characteristic mode of heroism is to accept a cost rather than pass suffering to somebody else. A cosmic bargain that exchanges one kind of obligation for another can be read as an especially cruel test of that ethic. It can also feel to a reader like the storytelling apparatus is doing the difficult decision for him. The distinction is one reason the debate remains interesting. Some readers focus on the impossible dilemma; others focus on the reset it makes available.

The aftermath is part of the analysis too. *Brand New Day* presented a version of Peter with a different day-to-day status quo and new storytelling opportunities. There are readers who like the accessible, energetic stories that followed without liking the mechanism that brought them about. That is a coherent position. Judging the result and judging the transition need not be the same judgment.

Likewise, an admirer of the married Peter can still appreciate a good story written after the reset. Treating a publication era as morally radioactive because of its starting premise is a poor way to assess individual comics. The more interesting criticism asks what new situations the change actually enabled, which possibilities disappeared, and whether the resulting series made full use of the trade.

## The real argument is about time

There is a revealing difference between a film franchise and a monthly comic series. A film actor ages in the world we inhabit; the performance carries that aging, even when later actors restart the role. In a comic, a character can endure decades of publication while experiencing a far shorter and more elastic life. The calendar outside the bookstore and the calendar inside Peter Parker's apartment do not agree.

This creates an advantage. A teenager can discover Spider-Man for the first time without needing an explanation of every old issue. It also creates a vulnerability. Peter's progress can be repeatedly softened or reversed, making him feel less like a person and more like a set of conditions that must be restored whenever he escapes them. The character becomes trapped between the promise that consequences matter and the franchise's need to remain available.

The Clone Saga and *One More Day* choose very different mechanisms, but both expose this struggle. The clone revelation threatened the identity and ownership of Peter's earlier life. The Mephisto bargain altered the history of a relationship that had defined much of his adult life. Neither conflict is fundamentally about whether Spider-Man can beat a supervillain. They are arguments about how much history the name Spider-Man can carry.

A particularly revealing counterexample is *Spider-Man: Life Story*, the 2019 limited series by Chip Zdarsky and Mark Bagley. It imagines a Peter Parker who ages as the decades pass. Its central experiment is to let the historical pressure of time keep arriving. Decisions do not have to be undone just because another real-world decade has passed. The premise produces different constraints, but it also reveals how much emotional material is available when the character cannot indefinitely return to a familiar age and state of life.

Of course, the limited series can attempt that experiment partly because it has a destination. An ongoing title is a different kind of object. The limited series can promise consequence with the knowledge that it will end. The monthly superhero book must remain open to a future writer it has not met yet. That does not excuse every reset, but it explains why the tension cannot be solved by a clever single editorial decree.

## What would a good reset owe the reader?

Suppose we grant that a publisher sometimes needs to revise continuity. That still leaves standards by which a revision can be judged. Did it generate compelling new stories, or merely erase an inconvenience? Did it respect the emotional weight of what came before, even while changing the fictional facts? Did it give supporting characters reasons to exist beyond restoring the hero to a familiar pose? And did it make the character easier to understand by a new reader without punishing the reader who had followed him for years?

Those are not questions with a single correct answer. Different fans value different versions of Peter. Someone who grew up with married Peter and Mary Jane may experience *One More Day* as a break in a relationship with the character. Someone who arrived during a later run may love the stories that followed and consider that preceding rupture distant background. Neither reaction is a failure of reading comprehension. Their entry points gave them different expectations about what Spider-Man was allowed to become.

The Clone Saga adds another layer: a character conceived as an alternative to Peter can earn loyalty that survives the return of the original. Ben Reilly is proof that the issue was never merely which body had the correct laboratory result. Readers can accept the biological answer and remain attached to the person who once looked like an impostor. That is one of the reasons superhero continuity is more than bookkeeping. It records a community's changing affection for fictional people.

A good long-running Spider-Man comic does not need to age Peter exactly one year for every calendar year. Nor must it preserve every event forever. But it should be aware that readers do not experience an issue as a database update. They experience it as something that happened to people they know. The strongest continuations understand why those people matter before rewriting the terms of their lives.

This is the secret identity of the status quo. It looks like a neutral default setting: Peter is young, worried about money, romantically entangled, and always one disaster away from a worse week. But that default is a creative choice, one that becomes more visible each time the story rearranges reality to protect it.

Spider-Man's most famous lesson concerns responsibility. For a series with this much history, that word applies outside the fiction too. Creators inherit a character, a set of possibilities, and the trust of readers. They are free to make bold choices. The interesting argument is what those choices owe the accumulated life that made the character worth inheriting.
