# Peter Parker's First Decade: School, Work, Love, and the Green Goblin

The great trick of the early Spider-Man comics is that the superhero doesn't get to leave the story when the villain loses. The costume comes off. Peter goes home. And the problem he was trying not to think about before the fight is waiting for him, generally worse than when he left it.

That's a good reason to revisit the first decade of *The Amazing Spider-Man*, from the debut in 1962 to the death of Gwen Stacy in 1973. Not because every issue is indispensable, and certainly not because we need to list every fistfight. It is because the comic gradually invented something more durable than an escalating roster of colorful enemies. It invented a supporting life that could change.

Here is enough of the original premise to get oriented. Peter Parker is a brilliant, awkward teenager, raised by Aunt May and Uncle Ben. A radioactive spider gives him remarkable abilities, but he initially uses them to pursue fame and money. After declining to stop a thief, he learns that the same criminal has murdered Uncle Ben. He decides he has a duty to use his abilities to help. The rest of the series asks what that decision costs when you still have school, work, friends, and people you love.

## The Fantastic Four will not be paying your bills

In *The Amazing Spider-Man* number one, the character finally has a magazine built around him. One of its finest early jokes is a visit to the Fantastic Four. Peter has enormous new powers and knows there are already celebrated superheroes in the world. Why not join a successful team? Surely they have some sort of benefits package.

Instead, he discovers that this is not how his fellow superheroes operate. The Fantastic Four encounter isn't a clean job interview, and a place on the team doesn't materialize. Peter's practical problem remains painfully intact: his family needs money.

That financial pressure is central to an arrangement fans still recognize instantly. Peter can take extraordinary pictures of Spider-Man in action because, of course, he knows exactly where Spider-Man will be. J. Jonah Jameson, the man running the *Daily Bugle*, will buy the photographs while devoting considerable energy to denouncing the masked subject. The hero's public defender, photographer, and public-relations disaster can all be the same young man, depending on what mask he has on and which room he enters.

What a beautifully efficient comic setup. A teenage genius with superhuman reflexes finds a way to monetize those reflexes by accepting low-security freelance work from a man who thinks his heroic alter ego is a menace. Peter doesn't merely have an enemy in the press. He has to keep selling that enemy a product.

This isn't a documentary about the economic life of a real teenager; the transactions are shaped for comedy and melodrama. But the feeling beneath them is reliable. Doing something important is not the same as being paid for it. Saving somebody does not tell Aunt May how the bills will be covered. The world does not suspend ordinary obligations when the hero shows up.

## High school, college, and the cruel comedy of being misread

At school, Flash Thompson makes a marvelous mistake over and over. He admires Spider-Man and mocks Peter Parker. The reader knows those are the same person, so every insult comes with a secret counterargument. Peter can't deliver it. Revealing why Flash is wrong would cost more than the satisfaction of proving it.

But the social life in these comics is more than a running misunderstanding. Early relationships include Betty Brant at the newspaper and the classmates surrounding Peter in high school. Aunt May remains an emotional anchor and an ongoing worry. Over time, the series refuses to keep everybody in the same arrangement. People grow closer, drift apart, make assumptions, recover from them, or simply become more complicated than they first appeared.

A major hinge arrives in *The Amazing Spider-Man* number thirty-one, when Peter begins studying at Empire State University. The issue introduces Gwen Stacy and Harry Osborn. The immediate irony is that Peter's intelligence, the trait that might finally win him some respect, is still competing with the demands of Spider-Man. A student distracted by a family crisis and secret emergencies may look unfriendly to the people he would most like to know.

College gives the comic a different social temperature. Harry becomes a friend with problems and family connections of his own. Gwen is neither simply an unreachable prize nor yet the symbol her later fate will make her. She is part of a changing set of relationships in which Peter can genuinely hurt other people by hiding where he's been, even when the hidden reasons are admirable.

And then there is Mary Jane Watson, whose existence the comic teases before giving the reader a full view of her in issue forty-two. The setup is practically sitcom machinery: Aunt May keeps trying to introduce Peter to someone, and he expects a date arranged by his aunt to be dreadful. John Romita Senior turns the eventual reveal into a famously confident arrival. The joke depends on the years of Peter's social anxiety that came before it. His private life is no longer populated only by people who overlook him.

This is what a long-running comic can do that a compressed origin movie has a harder time doing. The audience shares the experience of meeting somebody, getting the wrong impression, and slowly noticing a different person. A single panel can become iconic because readers have been made to wait for it.

## Thirty-three: a heroic pose that hurts

One of the strongest scenes from these years is not primarily about defeating a villain. It is about refusing to stop.

The Master Planner storyline culminates in *The Amazing Spider-Man* number thirty-three. Aunt May is gravely ill, and Peter needs a serum. He has followed the trail to Doctor Octopus, the brilliant scientist with four mechanical arms. Now he's trapped beneath collapsed machinery as water enters the chamber. The serum is nearby. His aunt's life is at stake. He can see what he needs, but he cannot reach it.

On a plot summary, the solution sounds almost insultingly simple: Spider-Man lifts something heavy. If you know how to reduce comics to plot, that is all you need to say. If you know why people return to this particular sequence, it is nowhere near enough.

Steve Ditko makes the lifting take time. He draws pressure before progress, effort before achievement. Spider-Man's body looks folded and almost defeated. The room seems to press back at him. Then the image gradually permits more space. The visual effect is not merely that our hero is strong. It is that he is close to the limit of his strength and still has a reason to try.

The sequence speaks directly to the origin without replaying it. Peter once failed to help because he couldn't be bothered. Here he may fail despite exerting everything he has. The question is not whether he can guarantee the rescue; it's whether he will make the attempt.

If you only know Spider-Man from a film, you've almost certainly seen descendants of this kind of moment: a vehicle to hold, a structure to lift, people who depend on a body that may not quite be strong enough. The comics made one of the strongest versions of it by allowing the reader to experience effort panel after panel.

## A new artist changes the weather

Steve Ditko left the series after issue thirty-eight. John Romita Senior took over as the principal artist beginning with issue thirty-nine. The difference is more than a collector's note about who held the pencil.

Ditko's Peter could be all angles and discomfort, and the whole comic had a nervous intensity that suited a socially isolated boy. Romita's work brought another kind of energy: fuller faces, a smoother romantic appeal, a flair for expressive character entrances. It would be too simple to claim that one artist drew awkwardness and the other drew glamour. Both could handle drama and action. But following the handoff over a few issues makes the change in emphasis hard to miss. Peter's life becomes a stronger romantic soap opera without abandoning its superhero machinery.

Issue thirty-nine also begins the revelation that the Green Goblin is Norman Osborn, Harry's father. That is one of the series' decisive inventions. The Goblin is no longer only an extraordinary maniac throwing explosives at a masked enemy. He is connected to the person Peter shares meals and college life with. Norman has wealth, adult authority, and a respectable public position. He is also capable of violence that makes his son's life more precarious. The more the series lets Peter's personal world grow, the more dangerous it becomes for a villain to enter it.

Norman's relationship with Harry makes the situation painful rather than just clever. Peter can see the danger of the father in a way Harry cannot, or cannot easily confront. A secret identity stops Peter from speaking plainly. It also makes him a witness to the ways a troubled family can contain genuine love, fear, and destructive behavior at once.

## When a comic about a superhero ran into the Comics Code

The 1971 drug story is one of those pieces of comics history that sounds as if somebody invented it to make a point about censorship. The U.S. Department of Health, Education, and Welfare approached Stan Lee about communicating the dangers of drug abuse. Lee wrote the subject into *The Amazing Spider-Man* issues ninety-six through ninety-eight. Harry Osborn's trouble with drugs became part of the story of Peter's friends and Norman's dangerous double life.

The Comics Code Authority, the industry's self-regulatory review body, refused its seal because the comics depicted drug use even though the intention was cautionary. Marvel published the issues without the seal. There is a satisfying absurdity here: a comic produced in response to a public-health request collided with an approval system that could not accommodate the depiction of the problem the story was trying to address.

But don't flatten the episode into the myth that one defiant comic instantly forced the entire Code to change. The historical record shows that a revision process was already underway and that the handling of drug depictions remained complicated. The incident exposed the shortcomings of the system and became part of that history. It also reveals something about Spider-Man's expanding role. His series was considered a plausible place to talk to young readers about real social problems, and it could place those problems inside the life of a character the audience already knew.

## One hundred twenty-one: the issue that will not stay safely in the past

Now we arrive at the part of this decade that has followed Spider-Man into nearly every discussion of superhero storytelling. By 1973, Peter and Gwen Stacy have a relationship the series has spent years developing. Norman Osborn, the Green Goblin, is tied to Peter's own social circle. The stage is set for a fight that reaches outside the safe boundary between superhero adventure and domestic life.

Then *The Amazing Spider-Man* number one hundred twenty-one changes the terms. Norman abducts Gwen, and she falls during the confrontation. Spider-Man tries to catch her. She dies. Readers have argued about the exact physical mechanics of the failed rescue, and later stories and adaptations have approached the event differently. The central fact is harsher than any mechanical debate: Spider-Man acts, and Gwen is not saved.

Gerry Conway wrote the issue; Gil Kane penciled it, with John Romita Senior inking. Naming the artists and writer keeps the ending from seeming like an inevitability laid down in a blueprint from 1962. It was a consequential choice in a series whose readers had lived with Gwen and Peter for years. The next issue brings the confrontation with Norman, himself apparently killed. But a villain's apparent defeat cannot give Peter back what he has lost.

The origin taught Peter that refusing to help could haunt him. Gwen's death asks what happens when he does help, and it still is not enough. That is a different injury to the hero's confidence. It makes the web line itself—the instrument of rescue—part of an argument about the limits of rescue.

Don't reduce Gwen solely to the job she performs in Peter's development. The early comics sometimes gave supporting characters narrower lives than their later reputation suggests; they also gave readers time to know them. Part of what makes the death troubling is the tension between an effective emotional story for Peter and a creative decision to end Gwen's story. Fans can admire the power of the issue and still argue about its costs.

In the aftermath, Mary Jane lets the brightness and evasion of her usual manner fall away. We glimpse the person beneath the performance. Suddenly, Peter's evolving private life is not just about whom he dates. It is about whom he can let close when his world is damaged.

## What the first decade bought the series

Peter begins as a lonely high-school student with a terrible secret and a new obligation. He becomes a college student with friends, romances, recurring enemies, family problems, and jobs that keep insisting on being jobs. The cast gathers its own history. By the time Gwen dies, the series has accumulated enough memory for one issue to change the way readers look at years of earlier material.

None of this means the first decade is solemn. It is often ridiculous in the best superhero sense. There are grotesque costumes, unlikely plans, jokes in fights, and the ongoing spectacle of a boy trying to sell pictures of himself to a man who cannot stand him. But its silliness and tragedy share the same fundamental invention: a hero whose ordinary world doesn't disappear when the action begins.

That is the achievement worth taking from these early comics. They made us care who Peter was going to see after the fight, whether he would make it to class, and what his friends would think when he arrived. The Goblin mattered because Harry mattered. The serum mattered because May mattered. Gwen's death mattered because the comic had given Peter a life in which losing her meant losing something that could not be restored by the next heroic victory. The first decade did not just give Spider-Man adventures. It made those adventures have an afterward.
