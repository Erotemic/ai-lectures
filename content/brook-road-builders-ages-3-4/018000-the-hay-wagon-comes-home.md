# The Hay Wagon Comes Home

Molly had never climbed Pancake Hill.

She had heard about it many times.

Rex said it was a wonderful hill with a wonderful name.

Gus said it was just the right size for a bear.

Jack said it was a low, wide mound in Pine Park.

Molly wanted to know who was right.

"All of us," Rex had told her.

Today she was going to find out.

But first, Green Gate Farm had a job to finish.

Farmer Ruth's field was dotted with little rectangular hay bales.

The baler had done its work. Now the bales needed a ride to the red barn.

Early in the morning, the grown-up farm crew loaded the low wagon. One bale went on, then another, then another.

From the safe yard beside the barn, Molly and Rex watched the stack slowly grow.

"It's a tower of grass bricks," Gus said.

"A very careful tower," Jack added.

The adults stacked and secured the load for the trip. Aunt Jo had brought the friends over to see the last part of the job.

"Where does the hay go?" Rex asked.

"Into the barn," said Farmer Ruth. "We keep it dry there so the sheep and goats can eat it later."

"Does the hen get a grass brick?" Rex asked.

One of the three hens pecked beside the gate.

"Our hens have their own feed," said Farmer Ruth. "They don't need a whole bale of hay."

The tractor rolled up and pulled the wagon out of the field.

Its big back wheels turned slowly.

The hay stack traveled with it, held steady on the wagon bed.

"It's a moving little house!" Gus said.

"For grass," said Molly.

"For very sleepy grass," Rex added.

The tractor and wagon took the farm lane, not the Little Bridge.

The Little Bridge was for walkers, paws, and hooves. It wasn't a road for loads of hay.

Aunt Jo pointed to the field and then the barn. "Look. The long way around is the right way for the wagon."

Jack drew both paths in his blue notebook. The farm road was a wide curved line. The footbridge path was a smaller line with two paw prints beside it.

The adults unloaded the bales at the barn.

They stacked them carefully on a dry raised storage base, out of the weather.

The hay was going into the dry barn, up off the bare ground, where rain could not soak it.

Molly watched from the porch with her blue rope toy between her paws.

"Is all the hay finished?" she asked.

"This cutting is finished," Farmer Ruth said. "The field can grow again."

"But what about my visit?" Molly asked.

Farmer Ruth smiled. "I haven't forgotten. Come on."

Molly carried her rope toy to its place by the farmhouse door and left it there.

Then she went with Farmer Ruth, Aunt Jo, Rex, Gus, and Jack through the green gate.

They walked across the Little Bridge.

Molly felt the wooden boards beneath her white front paws.

"I've stood on that end before," she told Rex.

"Today you get to see the other end," he said.

They crossed the brook, passed the short new path, and entered Pine Park.

There was the sign with the pine tree, the little hill, and Rex's pawprint.

There was the bench beneath the one tall pine.

And there was Pancake Hill.

Molly ran her eyes over it from bottom to top.

"It's not a mountain," she said.

"I never said mountain," Rex replied.

Gus adjusted his green cap. "It is exactly big enough for me."

Jack patted the grass beside the path.

"It's a good place to look at the sky too," he said.

Molly climbed the gentle hill with Rex beside her. They stopped at the top and looked back toward the bridge.

From here she could see the path that led home.

"Now I know your part of the world," she said.

Rex gave a happy little wag.

Molly gave one back, smaller and slower—their secret wave from the hayfield.

When the shadows lengthened, Farmer Ruth took Molly home to the farm, and Aunt Jo brought the others back along Brook Road.

The machines were parked. The hay was indoors. The field lay quiet after its haircut.

Rex settled on his rug beside the wooden dump truck and thought of two puppies on a little hill, each knowing the way to the other's home.
