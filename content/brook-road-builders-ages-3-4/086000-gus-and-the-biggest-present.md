# Gus and the Biggest Present

Gus had a birthday problem.

He wanted to give Jack the biggest present in Brook Road.

He had found a piece of blue ribbon in his house. The ribbon was longer than his arm. Surely, he thought, a big present needed a long ribbon.

At first he considered a boulder.

Mr. Fred had many boulders at the gravel yard, and Gus knew that some were enormous. But a boulder would not fit through Jack's front door. Also, it would be a strange thing to put on a birthday cake.

Then Gus considered a tractor.

Farmer Ruth had a red one. It had big back wheels and an engine and a seat high above the ground.

"No," said Molly, when Gus told her his idea at the farm gate. "Farmer Ruth needs that tractor."

"I only wanted to borrow it until after the candles."

"You can't wrap a tractor in one blue ribbon."

Gus held up his ribbon and imagined it stretched across the entire machine.

"I could try."

Molly shook her head so hard that her ears flapped.

Instead, they visited Dan at the parcel shed near the Red Door Garage. Dan was sorting boxes beside his blue delivery truck.

"Do you have a box," asked Gus, "that could hold something very, very big?"

Dan pointed to a flattened box leaning against the wall. It had once carried a small chair. The box was almost as tall as Gus.

"Will this do?"

Gus stood beside it, smiling. "Yes. I think it will."

Dan gave him the clean empty box. He carried it across the yard, then put it down to catch his breath.

The box was not heavy. It was just awkward. Every time Gus turned a corner, one of the cardboard flaps turned a corner somewhere else.

Rex joined him at the garage gate.

"What have you got?"

"An enormous present."

Rex sniffed it. "It smells like an empty box."

"It is an enormous present box," Gus said carefully.

"What goes inside?"

That was the problem.

At Ms. Pam's garage, Mr. Lee was putting away pieces of wood left over from small carpentry jobs. There were short smooth boards on a workbench and a set of four matching little wheels in a closed box.

"We're making something for Jack," Mr. Lee said. "A small pull wagon for his notebook and things he collects. Your friends asked if I could build it with them."

Gus peered over the bench. It wasn't even as long as one of his arms.

"But Jack has been to the Moon," Gus said. "Shouldn't his birthday present be enormous?"

Ms. Pam was checking the wheel fittings. "This wagon has to go along a path. If it were enormous, Jack would have trouble pulling it."

Gus looked at the wide box. He pictured a wagon the size of a house. A wagon that needed a dump truck to carry it. A wagon with its own little garage.

"Could I help wrap the small one?" he asked.

"Certainly," said Mr. Lee. "After the wheels are fitted and everything is checked."

Gus flattened his big cardboard box on the garage floor. Rex put one paw on the edge to hold it steady. Molly sat beside the blue ribbon, which had tangled around her front paws.

"You have become a present," Gus told her.

"I would like to become untangled," said Molly.

Rex helped slide the ribbon loose.

When the frame was ready, Mr. Lee let Gus sand one already-smoothed edge with a small block of fine paper under his supervision. Gus rubbed carefully. He did not have to lift a big machine or carry a huge stone.

He ran his paw across the edge.

"That feels nice."

"It should," Mr. Lee said. "Small things deserve good work too."

In the afternoon, Gus joined Rex at the house with the blue front step. They kept the wagon a secret.

Jack was visiting and sitting on Rex's rug with the old wooden blocks. He had made a low bridge over a pretend river, and his notebook was open beside it.

"Want to play?" he asked.

Gus forgot for a moment that he was holding a very long blue ribbon.

"Yes!"

They spent a long time getting Rex's wooden dump truck across the little block bridge. The wheels did not fit the first time. Jack moved one block. Rex tried again. This time the truck went across with a soft clack.

"Perfect," said Gus. "It is a very small bridge."

Jack smiled. "You don't need to make everything big."

Gus glanced down at the ribbon still trailing behind him.

He might have been thinking the same thing.

That evening, after Jack went home from Rex's house, Gus returned to the garage. Mr. Lee and Ms. Pam had set aside the wagon so that its last wheels could be checked the next day.

Gus brought his enormous empty box, his long ribbon, and one small piece of paper for a tag.

On the tag, he drew five candles.

The box, he decided, could be as big as he liked.

The present inside would be just the right size.
