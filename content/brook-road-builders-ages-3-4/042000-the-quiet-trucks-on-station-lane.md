# The Quiet Trucks on Station Lane

By the end of the firehouse open day, Gus had one important problem.

He couldn't decide which truck he liked best.

"The one with the enormous ladder," he said.

Then he changed his mind.

"No. The one with the hose."

Then he changed it again.

"Can I like both?"

"Of course," said Aunt Jo.

"Good," Gus replied. "That saves me a great deal of thinking."

The two red firehouse doors stood open in the warm evening light. Captain Ann's crew moved quietly between the vehicles.

The firefighters checked their tools and put everything back where it belonged.

The long ladder lay folded safely along its truck. The hose used for the practice target had been taken care of. The huge boots stood ready on their rack.

Rex sniffed the air.

Clean pavement. Rubber hose. Warm metal.

And a very faint smell of somebody's lunch.

"Perhaps there really is a biscuit cupboard," he whispered to Molly.

Molly gave their little tail-wag greeting.

"If you find it, tell me."

Captain Ann came to say goodbye. She was back in her navy station shirt, carrying her yellow helmet.

"Did you learn which truck does which job?" she asked.

Gus raised one paw at once.

"One carried the pump and hoses. The other carried the long ladder."

"Exactly," said Captain Ann.

"And your boots have their own parking lot," Molly added.

Captain Ann laughed.

"That's one way to describe it."

Jack knelt beside the visitor bench and opened his blue notebook to the map he had started earlier in the week.

There was Brook Road, Rex's blue step, and the curve toward Pine Park.

There was the little pedestrian bridge across the brook, and the longer road by which trucks reached Green Gate Farm.

He had also drawn the Red Door Garage to the west and Dan's parcel shed beside it.

Now he drew a small line branching north, between Rex's home and the park.

Station Lane.

At its end he drew two red rectangles for the firehouse doors.

Beside one he drew a short, boxy engine.

Beside the other he drew a long truck with a folded ladder on top.

"That one is the ladder truck," Rex said, touching the page with his nose.

"Not with your nose!" Jack cried, too late.

A little dark spot appeared near the truck's front wheel.

Gus studied it.

"That's an excellent tire."

Jack looked at the nose mark for a moment.

Then he drew a second tire beside it.

"Now it matches."

Captain Ann admired the finished map.

"You remembered both roads."

"The long farm road is for trucks," Jack said. "Little Bridge is for walkers and paws."

"And the fire engines turn into Station Lane," said Rex.

"Yes," said Captain Ann. "We keep the drive clear so they can come and go when they're needed."

The children watched the last visitors depart. Captain Ann closed the compartments on the engine. The crews checked the parked vehicles. Then the two great red doors came down, one after the other.

Down, down, down.

The firehouse became a quiet brick building again.

Farmer Ruth called to Molly. It was time to walk home by Pine Park and Little Bridge.

Rex pressed his nose gently against Molly's.

"See you at Pancake Hill?"

"Or at the farm," Molly said.

They wagged their tails once each.

Gus pulled his green cap down over his ears. Aunt Jo took Rex, Gus and Jack back along Brook Road beneath the darkening sky.

At home, Rex parked his repaired wooden dump truck between two blocks. He put a second block beside it for a pretend fire engine.

Jack laid the blue notebook on his desk with the new Station Lane page open.

Gus went home to hang his cap on its hook.

Farther away, Molly curled up on her farmhouse rug while the sheep settled near the barn.

The town had one more familiar place now: a firehouse with two red doors, a shorter engine, a longer ladder truck, and a firefighter named Captain Ann.

The trucks were quiet for the night.

And on the blue front step, Rex had already fallen asleep.
