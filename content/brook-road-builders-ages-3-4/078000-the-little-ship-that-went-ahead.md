# The Little Ship That Went Ahead

The first rocket to leave Star Harbor did not have any passengers.

It was a smaller rocket carrying a robotic cargo lander called Scout. Inside Scout were folded solar panels, a little wheeled rover, an antenna, and tools for making measurements on the Moon.

"A delivery truck for the Moon," Rex said.

Dan the donkey, who had driven his blue box truck to the visitor center with supplies, nodded. "I approve of a good delivery."

The friends watched from a distant control-room gallery. The weather was clear. Teams checked the small rocket, the pad, the radio links, and the flight plan.

"Where is the driver?" Gus asked.

"Scout is uncrewed," Dr. Ada said. "Computers and the people in mission control guide its work."

"It will need a very long road," Gus said.

"It won't use a road after launch," Jack reminded him.

The countdown reached its final numbers.

Three. Two. One.

On the big screen, the little rocket rose from its pad. Bright exhaust shone beneath it. A rolling roar arrived across the distant coast. Rex's ears stood up. He had seen rockets in pictures, but this was a rocket actually leaving the ground.

"It's going!" Molly shouted.

The rocket climbed into the blue sky, smaller and smaller, until Jack could barely see it. A tracking screen showed its path. The launch had been the work of many teams, from road builders to engineers to radio operators.

"Where is Scout now?" Rex asked.

"On its journey," Dr. Ada said. "The cargo ship will travel toward the Moon, and we will wait for its signal."

The first day passed. Then another. The friends returned home between visits. Rex slept beside his wooden dump truck. Gus helped Farmer Ruth carry a small basket at the farm. Jack looked at the Moon every evening and marked a little dot on his notebook page.

On the third day, they gathered in the visitor room again. Scout was close to the Moon. Its cameras sent pictures of gray plains and craters. Dr. Ada showed them the selected landing area on the Moon's near side, where the sun was shining.

"Why the sunny place?" Jack asked.

"The rover will use solar panels," Dr. Ada said. "Sunlight can help make electrical power. We planned its work for the conditions there."

Scout began to descend. On the screen, a tiny picture of a four-legged robotic lander moved toward a gray surface. There was no parachute. On the Moon there was almost no atmosphere to help slow a spacecraft, so Scout used its own engine to descend.

The little feet touched the ground.

For a moment, everyone waited.

Then a green signal appeared. Scout had landed and was speaking to Earth by radio.

Gus jumped once and sat down again. "The Moon got a package!"

"And the package is working," said Max.

The lander's small antenna was ready. The rover remained folded and safely fastened until the ground team told it to open. The adult engineers would check its systems before it moved.

Rex looked out toward the big white Star One rocket on the other pad. His own seat waited inside its cabin. Scout had gone first and made a careful little place for the explorers to visit.

That night the Moon shone above Brook Road as it always had. But Jack knew there was a new object on its surface: a small ship from Star Harbor, with a rover waiting to roll.

It made the Moon seem both far away and, somehow, a little closer.
