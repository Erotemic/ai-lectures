# Jack and the Long Lines in the Field

Jack liked lines.

Straight lines. Curvy lines. Lines that joined two places on his map.

He liked the dotted line showing the path from Pine Park to the Little Bridge.

But the lines in Green Gate Farm's field were different.

They weren't there yesterday.

"Somebody drew on the field," Jack said when he arrived at the red barn.

Molly bounced beside him. "A very big somebody?"

"With a very big pencil?" Gus asked.

Aunt Jo laughed. "Let's look first."

Across the farm lane the cut hay had dried in the sun and moving air over the past days.

The grass was no longer fresh and green the way it had been just after mowing.

Today Farmer Ruth was driving the tractor again, pulling a hay rake.

Its turning wheels had many long, thin teeth.

The rake gathered the loose grass into long, tidy lines.

One line. Another line. Another.

"It is combing the field," Gus said.

"The mower gave it a haircut," Rex replied. "Now it gets its hair combed."

"A very fancy field," Molly said.

The friends watched from the farmyard bench.

At one end of the field the loose grass still looked scattered.

At the other it sat in long rows.

Jack opened his blue notebook to two fresh pages.

On the left he drew little bits of grass in every direction.

On the right he drew several long, wavy lines.

"The same grass," he said, "but in different places."

"Where did it all go?" Rex asked.

Jack showed him the drawing. "Into the rows. The rake didn't make the grass disappear."

Rex leaned close.

"Good. I don't like it when good smells disappear."

After the tractor passed the far edge of the field, Farmer Ruth finished the job and came back on foot.

"Are you making a field map?" she asked Jack.

"Sort of. But I can't tell where I would walk."

"We aren't walking through the working field today," she said. "Those rows are for the baler to pick up later."

"Can the baler follow a line?" asked Gus.

"It can follow the rows of hay," Farmer Ruth replied. "But first I need to check whether it's dry enough."

Molly looked at the rows as if they were paths.

"In my dream," she said, "each row is a road to a different place."

"Where does the first one go?" asked Rex.

"To a mountain of biscuits."

"The second?"

"To a pond made of gravy."

Gus considered that. "My cap would make a very good gravy boat."

"It would make a very green one," said Jack.

"What about the last row?" Rex asked.

Molly thought for a moment.

"Home," she said. "The last road comes home."

The children spent the afternoon in the farmyard, far from the moving machines.

Jack made a little maze on a piece of paper.

It had three lines, two turns, and one enormous pretend biscuit at the end.

Rex followed the lines with his nose.

He got lost at the first turn.

"I found my own paw," he announced.

"A good thing to find," said Aunt Jo.

When it was time to go, Farmer Ruth was still checking the hay before any baling began.

The long rows stayed in the field.

The tractor went quiet beside the barn.

As the friends crossed the Little Bridge, Jack tucked his notebook under his arm.

He liked the rows in the field. But he liked Molly's last imaginary road best.

The one that came home.
