# Jack and the Things That Would Float

Jack placed a pencil on the table in the cabin simulator. The pencil stayed where he put it.

"It isn't floating," Gus observed.

"We're still on Earth," Jack said.

"Then the simulator is doing a very good job of being a table."

Max laughed. Their training room looked like the spacecraft inside, but it did not actually leave the ground. There were screens with pretend views of Earth and the Moon. There were radios, lights, and harmless practice controls.

"When our spacecraft is traveling in orbit, things inside can seem to float," Max explained. "We must keep them where we can find them."

Rex inspected a little pouch fixed beside his seat. He tucked a soft toy bone inside and closed the flap. He opened the flap. The bone stayed where it belonged.

"Excellent," Rex said. "My bone knows how to fly."

Molly tried the same pouch with a small cloth ball. She gave it one gentle pat. The ball did not roll away.

Jack asked Max how floating could happen if Earth still pulled on the spacecraft.

"Earth still has gravity," Max said. "The ship and everything inside are moving together. In orbit, they're continually falling around Earth. That's why things can feel weightless."

Jack thought about that for a long time. He drew a little circle for Earth and a curved line for the ship. He labeled it with one word: together.

Then came the practice flight.

Dr. Ada's voice sounded from the speaker. "Cabin trainer, ready for the checklist?"

Max checked the displays. Aunt Jo helped everyone fasten their harnesses. Rex found his handhold. Molly checked her pouch. Gus counted six seats. Jack waited for his turn to answer.

The screens changed to show a starry sky. A recorded rumble played softly from the speakers.

"We are pretending to leave Earth," Max said.

"I am pretending very well," Gus replied.

A little amber light came on at Jack's practice station. He looked at the picture next to it. The practice door was shown as not fully closed.

"Max," Jack said, "the door picture isn't green."

Max looked at the training display. "Good observation. Let's stop and check."

They did. It was a deliberate training problem. The practice door switch had not been set to the closed position. Max corrected it with the training instructor, and everyone tried the sequence again.

This time every practice check passed.

"Would you have gone anyway?" Molly asked.

"No," Max said. "On a real mission we don't ignore a check just because we want to go."

After lunch they visited the visitor gallery to watch the white Star One rocket at its pad. It was still connected to ground equipment. The engineers were continuing their work, and the crew had more training to do.

Gus put his green cap back on. "My cap is not trained for space."

"It doesn't need to be," Aunt Jo said. "It can stay in your cabin bag."

Jack closed his blue notebook. The page showed Earth, an orbit, six seats, and a little green light. It was not a map of a road. It was a map of the things they would need to remember.

Back in Brook Road, Rex's wooden dump truck waited by the rug. Nothing in the little town was floating. The bench at Pine Park stayed on the ground. Farmer Ruth's hay stayed in the barn.

That was fine with Jack. The stars could wait until the team was ready.
