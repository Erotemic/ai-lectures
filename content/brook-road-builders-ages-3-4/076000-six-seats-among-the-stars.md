# Six Seats Among the Stars

Rex had a question about space.

"Where do we sit?"

Max showed him the answer inside a full-size cabin trainer at Star Harbor. There were six seats, each with a harness made for its passenger. Some seats were shaped for people. Some had supports and straps suited to smaller animal bodies.

"One for Rex. One for Molly. One for Gus. One for Jack," Max said. "One for Aunt Jo. And one for me."

Gus climbed carefully into the practice seat that was his size. "Mine is very comfortable. It needs a biscuit drawer."

"Food goes in a closed storage pouch," Aunt Jo replied.

Rex inspected the pouch. "An acceptable drawer."

The cabin was bright and calm. It had windows, handholds, places to stow supplies, and an air system. The real spacecraft would carry its own breathable air and water for the journey. The crew would not open a window to take a breath in space.

"There aren't trees to make fresh air out there," Jack said.

"Or a pond to drink from," Molly added.

"And no biscuit shop," Rex said sadly.

Max showed them the cabin's displays and the radios. Dr. Ada was on the other end of a practice call from mission control.

"Star One trainer, can you hear us?" she asked.

"We can hear you," said Max.

Gus leaned toward the microphone. "The biscuit drawer is working."

"That's useful news," Dr. Ada replied.

They practiced the small tasks that belonged to the travelers: buckling their harnesses, listening for a call, finding a handhold, and knowing where Aunt Jo was. Max handled the controls. The friends could ask questions but would not steer the ship.

Rex's ears folded against the padded headrest. He tried to turn around and look at Molly, but the harness held him snugly in place.

"I cannot make a one-wag greeting from here," he said.

"You can blink," Molly replied.

They both blinked once.

Later, in another room, they tried on practice helmets. The clear face pieces let them see one another. For a Moon walk they would need properly fitted protective suits with air, temperature control, and radios. The grown-up specialists would check everything before anyone went outside the lander.

Gus peered through his helmet. "My cap won't fit on top."

"It can wait safely inside the spacecraft," Aunt Jo said.

"Then the Moon will have to recognize me without it."

Jack drew the six seats. He added a small closed food pouch and a rectangle for the radio. He did not try to draw every pipe and wire. There were too many.

As they left the practice cabin, a worker brought in a checklist as thick as a book. Max smiled at it.

"Does somebody read all that?" Jack asked.

"Different people check different parts, and then we check together," Max said. "Practice makes it easier to know what to do."

On the ride home Rex slept for a little while with his head against Aunt Jo's arm. He woke when they reached his blue front step.

The real flight was still ahead. For now he had learned something important: a rocket trip was not just a big engine and a tall tower. It also needed a good place for six travelers to sit, breathe, rest, and talk to the people at home.
