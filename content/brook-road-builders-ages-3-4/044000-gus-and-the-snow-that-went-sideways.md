# Gus and the Snow That Went Sideways

Gus had counted eleven snow lumps on the way to Rex's house.

Twelve, if he counted the one on his own green cap.

He arrived with Jack and shook himself at Aunt Jo's door.

Plop.

A small lump fell from his cap onto the cleared step.

"Twelve," he said with satisfaction.

Rex sniffed it.

"Does snow smell like anything?" asked Jack.

"Cold," Rex decided.

"That's not a smell," said Gus.

"It is if your nose is cold enough."

Aunt Jo opened the door, and the friends watched the morning through the front window. The orange plow had come back along Brook Road. This time it was clearing a strip beside yesterday's tracks.

The truck rolled slowly past the turn to Station Lane. Its blade was turned a little sideways, so the moving snow slipped along it toward the curb.

Gus stared.

"Wait. The truck is going forward. Why is the snow going over there?"

He pointed hard at the side of the road.

"Maybe the snow has changed its mind," offered Rex.

Jack squinted at the blade.

"I think the angle matters."

"An angle?" Gus asked. "Like a triangle?"

"A little like the slant on a triangle."

The plow kept moving along the road, while the line of snow beside it grew longer.

At the firehouse corner, a second crew had cleared a way into the station apron. Captain Ann's two red trucks still stood inside their bays, ready for their ordinary jobs.

"The orange truck isn't bringing the snow to the firehouse," Rex said.

"Good," said Gus. "They don't need a giant snow present."

When the plow had gone and Aunt Jo said the road work was finished near the house, Jack found a shallow baking tray.

He tore a sheet of scrap paper into soft little white squares. They were much nicer indoors than bringing a real snowdrift onto the kitchen table.

He put a row of scraps at one end of the tray.

"This card is our plow," he said, holding up a stiff strip of cardboard.

First he held it straight across the tray and pushed gently.

The scraps bunched up ahead of it.

"That's a snow mustache," said Gus.

Then Jack tipped the cardboard so one end pointed farther forward than the other.

He pushed again.

The white squares traveled along the slant and gathered at one side.

"They went sideways!" cried Rex.

Gus leaned close.

"And nobody asked them to."

"The card made a path for them," Jack said.

He tried it the other way. The scraps gathered on the other side.

Gus borrowed the cardboard and gave it a much steeper angle.

A few paper squares shot toward his sleeve.

"Oh! My sleeve has become the curb."

Rex put his nose near the tray and sneezed.

One square landed on Gus's cap.

"Thirteen," Gus said.

They left the tray on the table while Aunt Jo swept up the scraps. Outside, the road looked wider, but the snowbanks were still there beside it.

"The plow didn't make the snow vanish," Jack said.

"No," said Gus. "It gave the snow a different address."

At home, Gus hung his green cap by the door. The little white paper square came with it, tucked beneath the brim.

He decided to leave it there until morning.

Even a bear's cap, it seemed, could have a tiny snowbank.

**Editorial note:** These stories depict talking animals and an invented town. The road plow, spreader, tractor blade and snowblower are distinct adult-operated winter-maintenance machines. The children observe from warm homes or marked pedestrian places and never operate powered equipment. The linked sources support only the specific physical facts; they do not establish measured educational, developmental or sleep effects.
