# Rex and the Pipes Under the Pad

Rex liked pipes because sometimes they carried interesting smells. The pipe beneath the farm road carried water. The gutter on Farmer Ruth's repaired shelter carried rain away from the door.

The pipes at Star Harbor were different.

"That one is enormous," Rex said.

Beyond the visitor glass, a truck had delivered a broad metal pipe. Workers in helmets guided it into a carefully prepared place beside the launch pad. Farther off stood a tall water tank.

"What is all that water for?" Molly asked.

"Is the rocket going to have a bath?" asked Gus.

Dr. Ada shook her head. "When a rocket starts its engines, there is a great deal of heat and an enormous sound. Water helps protect the launch equipment. It reduces some of the effects of that sound and heat."

Gus looked at Rex. "The rocket is going to make the loudest splash without going swimming."

The friends had come to watch a water-system test. It was a quiet day for rockets, because no rocket stood on the pad yet. But workers were checking the equipment that a rocket would need later.

A little light flashed in the control room. Dr. Ada listened to a voice on the radio. Then, far across the fenced area, water poured from great outlets near the pad. It splashed, rushed, and ran toward the channels built to manage it.

"That is more than Jack's rain gauge could hold," Rex said.

"Much more," Jack replied.

He opened his blue notebook to the page about the rain that had once fallen on Brook Road. Water had traveled along a curb, into a drain, and through the farm-road culvert. Here, the engineers had built channels on purpose for a very different job.

"Where does this water go afterward?" he asked.

Dr. Ada showed him the plan for catching and managing the test water. "It goes where the site team has designed it to go. We don't let it simply run through the visitor path or out into the work roads."

Rex stood on his paws and listened. The rushing water sounded like a great waterfall. Then it stopped. The equipment crew began checking the pipes, valves, and drains.

"Was that the launch?" Gus asked.

"No," said Dr. Ada. "That was one system passing a test."

They walked to a display inside the visitor building. There were pictures of more things a rocket needed: electric power, cooling, communication lines, and pipes connecting the ground equipment to the vehicle before launch.

"And they all have to work?" Molly asked.

"Yes," said Dr. Ada. "A rocket cannot borrow electricity from the sun in the middle of being put together. The people and machines on the ground must get it ready."

Gus thought about the four orange cones once delivered to Ms. Pam's garage. "Do rockets need orange cones?"

"They need very clear boundaries," said Dr. Ada.

"Then they need enormous cones."

At lunchtime Dan arrived in his familiar blue box truck. This time he carried ordinary supplies for the visitor center, not a rocket engine. He unloaded them at a marked delivery bay.

"My truck gets to help a rocket by bringing paper towels," he said.

Rex considered that. "A big adventure still needs small deliveries."

When they reached Brook Road that evening, the rain gauge stood quietly at Pine Park. The small storm drain and the farm culvert were right where Jack had drawn them. Star Harbor's great pipes were far away, and its water test was finished.

Rex dreamed of a rocket so tall it could drink from a cloud. But in the morning he remembered the better answer: it was the people on the ground who knew where every pipe went.
