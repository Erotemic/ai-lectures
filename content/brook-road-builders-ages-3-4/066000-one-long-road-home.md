# One Long Road Home

On the first bright afternoon after the picture board was finished, Aunt Jo spread a picnic blanket beside the bench at Pine Park.

Rex knew the spot. He knew the tree. He knew the path that curled around Pancake Hill.

But there was one new thing at the park gate.

A map of his neighborhood.

Jack stood beside it with his blue notebook. He was trying not to look too pleased, which was difficult because his cheeks kept smiling.

"Ready?" Aunt Jo asked.

"Ready," said Jack.

"Ready for what?" asked Gus.

"For a picnic," said Rex.

Gus nodded. "I have been ready for a picnic since breakfast."

The four friends waited near the gate: Rex with his red collar and one dark ear, Gus in his green cap, Jack holding the notebook, and Molly with her two white front paws. Molly had come from Green Gate Farm with Farmer Ruth. They had walked over Little Bridge, as they always did.

Molly and Rex exchanged one wag.

Aunt Jo had invited a few neighbors to see the new map, eat a small snack, and spend time together. Nobody needed to bring a working truck into the park. There was a proper pull-off and service lane for deliveries, and the paths belonged to walkers.

The first visitor was Dan the donkey. He had parked his blue box truck back by his parcel shed and come on foot.

"Did your truck get lost?" Gus asked.

"No," said Dan. "It knows where it sleeps."

"Do trucks sleep?" Rex wondered.

"Mine rests," Dan said. "Especially when I stop asking it to carry boxes."

Next came Ms. Pam, her striped raccoon tail swinging behind her. She had closed the red door of the garage for her afternoon break.

"How's that little wooden dump truck?" she asked Rex.

"All four wheels still work!" Rex said.

He wished he had brought it to show her, then remembered he could show her another time. That was a nice thing about having neighbors. You did not have to fit everything into one afternoon.

Captain Ann joined them from the firehouse on Station Lane, wearing ordinary clothes instead of her big protective boots. Gus looked at her feet.

"Your boots shrank," he said.

"These are my walking shoes," Ann replied.

"Good. You'd need a very long picnic blanket for the other ones."

Mr. Lee arrived with his measuring tape tucked into a pocket, though he had no measuring to do today. Mr. Fred, the badger from the aggregate yard, carried a cloth bag holding three large, smooth stones for the children to examine after the snack.

"Not from the moving machines," he said. "These are just big, smooth stones I keep for visitors."

Gus peered into the bag. "Pancake Hill has cousins."

Farmer Ruth smiled. "I suppose every hill does."

Aunt Jo gathered everyone around the new map board. It showed Brook Road, the garage, the firehouse, the parcel shed, the service road to the gravel yard, Pine Park, the brook, Little Bridge, and Green Gate Farm. The solid line stood for vehicle roads. The dotted line stood for the walking route through the park and over the footbridge.

Jack pointed to the little red barn.

"Molly lives over here."

Molly put a white paw beside Rex's paw, right on the gravel path beneath the board.

"And Rex lives over there," she said, pointing her nose west.

"Where is my cap?" Gus asked.

Jack sighed, but he was smiling.

"On your head."

"Good. The map is accurate, then."

Dan studied the solid line curling around the farm-road bend. "That's the route I use for deliveries," he said.

Ms. Pam pointed to the little red door. "And there's where the tow truck waits when it isn't helping someone."

Captain Ann found Station Lane and its two red doors. Mr. Fred found the three stones that stood for the gravel yard. Farmer Ruth found the green gate at the end of the dotted path.

No one had to explain every machine or remember every job at once. The pictures were enough to make the places recognizable.

Then everyone went to the picnic blanket.

Aunt Jo had sandwiches and slices of apple. Farmer Ruth had brought a few little rolls in a basket. Gus sat down and looked very carefully at his roll.

"Is this a tiny hay bale?" he asked.

"It's bread," said Rex.

"Good. I was afraid I might need a tractor to eat it."

Molly settled in the shade beside Rex. The breeze stirred the pine branches. Somewhere beyond the northern service lane a distant truck made a low, steady sound, but it did not come through the park.

After the snack Jack opened his notebook. The first pages showed a yellow digger and the gravel piles that had become a path. Other pages showed the little bridge, a tractor, a tow truck, a delivery truck, rainwater going into a drain, a fire engine, snow on the roads, stones moving along a conveyor, and the new roof by the farm's wagon door.

He turned to a blank page and drew the picture board at the park gate. Beneath it he drew four tiny figures: a puppy, a bear, a boy, and another puppy.

Rex peered over his arm.

"Which puppy is me?"

"The one beside the blue front step," Jack said.

"Good," Rex replied. "You got my good ear."

Gus looked at the bear. "You got my cap too."

This time, everyone agreed.

When the afternoon grew quiet, Farmer Ruth and Molly walked back toward Little Bridge. Rex went with them as far as the beginning of the path, Aunt Jo beside him. The puppies gave their one-wag goodbye. Across the brook, the red barn and its dry little shelter waited in the sunshine.

Ms. Pam went home to the Red Door Garage. Dan returned to his parcel shed. Captain Ann walked north toward Station Lane. Mr. Lee and Mr. Fred went their separate ways along Brook Road.

Jack put his blue notebook under his arm. Gus tipped his green cap. Rex looked once more at the picture board.

The map was new, but the places on it were not. He had been to all of them, with these friends, on one day or another.

Aunt Jo took his paw and they walked west. They passed the firehouse lane, the houses, and finally Rex's own blue front step.

Inside, his wooden dump truck waited beside the rug, with all four wheels ready for another pretend delivery.

Rex parked it carefully by the wall. The town outside was growing quieter. Somewhere far off, the blue box truck had stopped humming. The brook would still be moving under its bridge, and the pine tree would still be standing by Pancake Hill.

He climbed into bed, curled his tail around his feet, and thought about the little map.

Tomorrow the roads would still lead to his friends.

For tonight, he knew his way home.
