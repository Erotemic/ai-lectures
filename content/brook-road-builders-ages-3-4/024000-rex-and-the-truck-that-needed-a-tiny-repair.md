# Rex and the Truck That Needed a Tiny Repair

Rex's little wooden dump truck had climbed the biggest mountain in the house.

The mountain was a sofa cushion.

It had crossed the longest bridge.

The bridge was two books placed end to end on the rug.

It had delivered the heaviest load.

The load was one pinecone.

Then the truck stopped.

"Come on," Rex told it.

He gave it a gentle push.

Three wheels rolled.

One did not.

"Oh," said Rex.

Gus leaned over the cushion mountain. "Your truck is on strike."

"What does that mean?" Jack asked.

"It means it would like a biscuit," Gus said.

"I don't think wooden trucks eat biscuits."

"Then it's a very difficult strike," said Gus.

Molly, visiting with Farmer Ruth, came to look. She rested her chin on the rug, eye to eye with the little truck.

"Maybe it needs the Red Door Garage," she said.

Rex looked toward the window. The garage was farther down Brook Road, but the little toy was right here.

"Could we take it on Ms. Pam's tow truck?" he asked.

Aunt Jo smiled. "Her flatbed is for much bigger jobs. Let's have a look at this one first."

Jack fetched his blue notebook, opened to the picture of the tilted flatbed, and pointed to the long ramp.

"The real flatbed truck carried Aunt Jo's van when it could not drive."

"And the van needed a new tire," Gus added.

"Does the toy need a new tire?" asked Molly.

Aunt Jo turned the wooden truck over on the low table.

"Let's find out why this wheel won't turn."

The children watched as she turned the free wheels one by one.

Whirr.

Whirr.

Whirr.

The last one stopped at once.

"Something is caught around its axle," she said.

There, tucked between wheel and wood, was a little strip of ribbon from an old game.

"That isn't a broken tire," Jack said.

"It's a trapped ribbon!" Molly cried.

Gus looked delighted. "Then the truck has captured a snake."

"A very quiet, very flat snake," Rex said.

Aunt Jo removed the ribbon and checked that the wheel was sound. Nothing had to be replaced.

Rex gave the truck another gentle push.

All four wheels rolled along the table.

He smiled so widely that his light ear tipped sideways.

"Fixed!"

"Now it can go back to delivering pinecones," Molly said.

Gus retrieved the pinecone from the sofa cushion.

"Important delivery," he announced. "One prickly loaf of bread."

Jack turned to another page in his notebook, where he had drawn the white service truck with its many little doors.

"The service truck took tools all the way to the farm instead."

"Because the red tractor couldn't start," Rex remembered.

"So sometimes a vehicle goes to the garage," Jack said. "And sometimes the repair comes to the vehicle."

"And sometimes Aunt Jo fixes the truck on the rug," Gus finished.

"That is the smallest garage of all," said Molly.

They made a tiny pretend parking place out of two large blocks. Rex drove the wooden dump truck between them, unloaded the pinecone, and backed away.

Beep, beep, he said softly.

The pinecone stayed put.

Outside, the day was beginning to fade. Farmer Ruth and Molly walked home by the familiar Little Bridge. The real trucks were parked for the night at the garage, and Aunt Jo's yellow van was home on Brook Road.

Jack drew four round toy wheels and a small ribbon beside them, then closed his blue notebook.

Gus put his green cap on its hook.

Rex set his little wooden dump truck next to his rug, with its wheels all facing forward.

He curled up beside it.

Tomorrow the truck could climb the cushion mountain again.

Tonight, the mountain and the truck could rest.
