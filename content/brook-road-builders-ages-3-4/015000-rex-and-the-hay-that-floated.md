# Rex and the Hay That Floated

Rex dreamed that the hayfield had learned to wag its tail.

It was a very large tail, made of long green grass.

It wagged so hard that all the clouds went sailing away.

Then Rex woke up and saw that his own tail was wagging against his rug.

"Oh," he said. "Just me."

Aunt Jo was already getting ready to visit Green Gate Farm.

When they reached the Little Bridge, Molly was on the other side.

She had brought her blue rope toy to the green gate, but she wasn't looking at the toy.

She was looking past the red barn.

"The field is moving," she told Rex.

Rex looked at the grassy hill beyond the farm.

"Fields don't walk," he said.

"This one is doing something. Come see."

Aunt Jo led them to the bench beside the barn. Jack and Gus were already there with Farmer Ruth.

A different machine was attached to the tractor today.

It was wide and low, with spinning arms that looked from a distance like turning stars.

"What's that?" Rex asked.

"A hay tedder," said Farmer Ruth. "Earlier this week the grass was cut. Today we're helping it dry."

Rex tried the word quietly.

"Ted-der."

"That sounds like somebody's uncle," Gus said. "Hello, Uncle Tedder."

The tractor moved across the field.

Its engine hummed. The turning arms lifted the cut grass and tossed it loosely back down.

A thin, untidy blanket of grass seemed to ripple behind it.

Not a great cloud. Not a storm. Just little bits lifting and settling.

"It's shaking out a very big blanket," Jack said.

"I knew it," Rex whispered to Molly. "The field is waving."

"It's a friendly field," Molly whispered back.

They both wagged their tails.

Gus leaned forward to watch the turning arms.

"If I slept under that blanket, would I smell like hay?"

"You already smell like hay," Rex said.

"Do I?"

"And porridge."

"That's breakfast," Gus replied, pleased.

Jack studied the grass before and after the tractor passed.

He noticed that the grass was lying differently afterward. It wasn't all pressed together in thick strips.

"It looks fluffier," he said.

"The sun and the air can reach more of it now," Farmer Ruth explained.

"Is it finished?" Molly asked.

"Not yet. We'll check how it dries, then gather it into rows another day."

That was the part Rex liked least.

Not yet.

A great big tractor could do so much. Why couldn't it do everything at once?

He looked at Molly. Molly looked at him.

"Maybe we could help by wagging," he said.

They stood side by side and wagged toward the field.

Left. Right. Left. Right.

Gus held up one paw.

"I think the field is waving back."

A light breeze went through the loose grass. A little ripple crossed the ground.

Rex barked once in delight.

Jack drew the tedder in his notebook, using circles for the spinning arms and tiny lines for the grass.

Then he drew two tails beneath it.

"Scientific observation?" Aunt Jo asked, with a smile.

"Two kinds," Jack said. "A machine lifting grass, and two puppies pretending they can help."

"I can put the puppies on the second page," Rex offered.

"You are the puppies," Jack said.

"Oh, right."

When the tractor stopped, the field lay in loose, drying grass. No bales yet. No hay wagon moving yet.

Molly and Rex walked back to the porch. They left the field to the sun and air.

Before Aunt Jo led Rex home, Molly made one tiny wag, almost too small to see.

Rex answered with one of his own.

That was their new way of saying, "See you soon."

That evening, a breeze rustled the tree outside Rex's window.

He watched the leaves move a little, then settle.

His tail stayed still against the rug, but he remembered the wave anyway.
