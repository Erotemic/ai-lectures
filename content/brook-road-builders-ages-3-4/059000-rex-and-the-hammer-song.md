# Rex and the Hammer Song

Tap. Tap-tap.

Rex opened his eyes.

Tap-tap. Tap.

He was lying on Farmer Ruth's porch beside Molly. The two puppies had been very busy doing nothing at all when the sound began.

"A woodpecker," Rex said.

"A very big woodpecker," Molly added.

Tap-tap-tap.

Rex sat up. "A woodpecker in work boots."

"And a tool belt," said Molly.

Then Mr. Lee walked around the side of the red barn, carrying a toolbox. His beaver tail swept a little dust off the ground.

"I heard that," he said with a smile. "No woodpecker today. Just the crew fastening the new little roof."

"You make excellent tapping noises," Rex told him.

"Thank you. We try."

Gus and Jack had arrived with Aunt Jo. Gus's green cap sat a little crooked because he had been pretending it was a roof for his head.

"Is the barn finished?" he asked.

"Nearly," Mr. Lee said. "The frame is up, and we've put the new roof pieces in place. Now we're checking the edges and making sure rain goes where it should."

The friends stayed with the adults on the porch side of the barrier. From there they could see the side shelter without getting near the tools or working platform.

"Yesterday it looked like a bare wooden drawing," Jack said.

"And now it's wearing its real hat," Gus replied.

Rex thought that was a rather good description. The new roof rested above the little side space where Farmer Ruth would soon park the wheelbarrow again.

Tap.

Clink.

A soft whir from a tool.

Rex tilted his head for each new sound.

A hen walked across the yard, stopped by the barrier, and made a much louder noise.

BUK-BUK-BUK.

"She thinks the hammer is trying to talk to her," Molly said.

"Maybe it is," Gus replied. "What does a hammer say to a hen?"

"Tap," said Rex.

"And what does a hen say back?"

BUK-BUK-BUK.

Even Jack laughed at that.

When the fastening work was finished, Mr. Lee brought out a small sample piece of bent metal for the children to see. He held it in his own hands, safely away from the active area.

"This is the sort of shape we use where one part of a roof meets another," he said. "The piece at our barn is already fitted in place."

Where the little roof met the taller barn wall, the builders fitted flashing to help rain travel out instead of slipping into the gap.

Jack remembered the wet corner Molly had found. He flipped to the page where he had drawn the first drip.

"So the water won't go behind the roof edge the way it did before?"

"That's what the new detail is designed to prevent," said Mr. Lee. "We'll inspect it before the shelter goes back into use."

"If the water goes down the roof," Molly said, "where does it go next?"

Mr. Lee pointed to the lower edge. The crew had fastened a narrow gutter there.

At the lower edge, a gutter would catch the water and guide it down a short pipe away from the wagon door.

It ended by a small patch of splash stones beyond the walking approach, not at the place where the old wet mark had formed.

Jack drew another arrow in his notebook. Down the red roof. Along the gutter. Down the pipe. Out to the stones.

"That's a better route," he said.

"A very short road for raindrops," Gus observed.

"Without a traffic light," Rex added.

"Or a bridge," said Molly.

They all looked toward Little Bridge, far beyond the field. It was still exactly where it belonged, crossing the brook for pedestrians. No rainwater needed to drive a truck over it.

Farmer Ruth came out with a clean cloth and the farm's water jug. "We've nearly finished checking everything," she said. "The work barrier stays a little longer, and then we can use the side shelter again."

The hens had gathered by their feeder. The tractor sat in the dry main barn. From a safe distance, the new little roof looked as though it had been there for years.

Mr. Lee gathered his tools. The tapping stopped.

Rex waited a moment.

"I think I liked the hammer song," he said.

"There's another song," Molly answered.

"What's that?"

From the barnyard came three hens arguing very seriously over one perfectly ordinary piece of straw.

BUK. BUK-BUK. BUK.

"Oh," said Rex. "That one has a chorus."

The afternoon sun warmed the new roof, and the little side shelter waited for the next rain without making any sound at all.
