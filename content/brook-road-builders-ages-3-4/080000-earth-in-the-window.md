# Earth in the Window

In space, Gus's green cap wanted to float away.

He took it carefully from his bag, let it go for one tiny moment, and watched it hang in the cabin air.

"My cap has learned to fly," he said.

Aunt Jo caught it and tucked it under a strap. "Then we'd better teach it to park."

The Star One spacecraft was on its way to the Moon. After leaving Earth orbit, its engine had given it the push needed for the long journey. It did not need to roar every minute; most of the time the ship traveled quietly, while the crew checked its course and the systems kept them comfortable.

Rex discovered that a small soft bone could float too. Molly's cloth ball floated beside it, slow as a dandelion seed. The puppies kept the toys inside a clear pouch once playtime was over.

Jack looked out the window. Earth was turning behind them, blue and white and brown, with great swirls of cloud over the ocean.

"I thought I'd see Brook Road," Rex said.

"It's much too small from here," Jack replied.

"Even the big gravel yard?"

"Even Mr. Fred's gravel yard."

They all took turns looking. Somewhere on that blue world were Pine Park, the little footbridge, the farm road with its culvert, the garage with a red door, the firehouse, and Star Harbor by the sea. None of those places had disappeared. They were simply too small to see from so far away.

Max spoke with Dr. Ada by radio. There was a small delay between a message leaving Earth and reaching the spacecraft. Radio waves traveled quickly, but the Moon was very far away.

"Mission control, can you hear us?" Max asked.

"Loud and clear," came Dr. Ada's answer a moment later.

Gus leaned toward his microphone. "My cap is behaving."

"Thank you for the report," Dr. Ada replied.

The crew ate lunch from closed containers. There were little pieces of fruit that had to be kept in their bag, water in drink pouches, and food Max had chosen after a great deal of planning. Rex's special meal was in a sealed packet labeled with a picture of his dark ear.

"That's me," he said proudly.

"Your lunch has a portrait," Gus observed.

The lights changed gently as the crew prepared to rest. Max showed them the sleep spaces, where soft straps kept sleepers from drifting through the cabin. Aunt Jo helped Rex settle into his. Molly curled against her own padded support. Gus's cap stayed in the little storage pocket by his shoulder.

Jack had one last question.

"If we're going to the Moon, why isn't it getting bigger quickly?"

"It's very far away," Max said. "We'll travel for days. Tomorrow it will look a little different."

Jack opened his blue notebook and drew Earth as it looked that day. He remembered the first map at Pine Park, which had seemed so big when it showed the whole neighborhood. Then he drew a tiny dot on the blue circle.

"That's where we began," he said.

Rex looked at the dot. "You forgot the biscuit shop."

"There isn't one on the map."

"Then I hope we packed enough."

They laughed quietly. The cabin lights dimmed. Outside, the stars kept their places, and the dark sky did not blink.

The ship continued toward the Moon while the crew slept, with the grown-up flight team and people on Earth watching the journey together.
