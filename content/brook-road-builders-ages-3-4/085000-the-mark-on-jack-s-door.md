# The Mark on Jack's Door

The morning after the last of the welcome-home visitors had gone, Jack stood in the kitchen doorway with his socks on.

His dad was holding a pencil.

"Heels against the wall," Dad said.

Jack put his heels against the wall. He stood so straight that his toes curled up inside his socks.

"You may stand normally," said Dad.

Jack let his toes go.

Dad placed a flat book gently on top of Jack's head and made a short pencil line on the wooden doorframe.

There were other lines lower down. One was beside a little scribble that Jack had made a long time ago, when writing his own name had been difficult.

Jack touched the newest line. "Is that how tall I am now?"

"That's where the top of your head reached this morning."

Jack stretched on tiptoe. "And now?"

"Now you are cheating the ruler."

Jack laughed and put his heels down again.

In a few days it would be his birthday. Jack was going to be five.

He had been on the Moon. He had seen a rocket launch, traveled through space, and watched a little robot roll over gray dust. But standing in the kitchen, looking at a pencil line, he found himself wondering about a much smaller thing.

"Will I be taller by my birthday?"

"Probably not enough to see," Dad said. "But we'll be able to make another mark someday."

Jack considered that. Tomorrow sounded very soon. Someday sounded very far away.

He took his blue notebook to Pine Park.

Rex was there, sniffing the gravel beside the entrance sign. One of his ears was brown and the other was light, and they did not always point the same way. Gus was sitting on the grass with his green cap on backward. Molly had come over from Green Gate Farm and was trying to persuade a fallen leaf to run away from her.

"I'm going to be five," Jack announced.

Gus sat up. "Five what?"

"Five years old."

"Ah." Gus thought about it. "Will you be five times as tall?"

"I hope not," said Rex. "You would never fit through my front door."

Jack laughed. "I measured this morning. I am not even one time taller."

He opened his notebook to show them a drawing of the doorframe with its little pencil lines. Then he flipped back through older pages. There was a yellow excavator at Pine Park, a tractor at the farm, and the huge rocket at Star Harbor. On the newest page was Scout's little rover on the Moon.

"That drawing is much better than your first truck," Molly said.

Jack looked at the first truck. Its wheels were crooked. One wheel had six spokes and the other had seven. He loved it anyway.

"I can draw circles better," he said. "But I like this one."

Gus examined the page. "I liked when the truck dumped gravel. Remember? We thought the pile was a mountain."

"You thought it was a mountain," said Rex. "I thought it smelled interesting."

"A very good mountain," Gus said.

The friends walked the curving path around Pancake Hill. The tall pine tree was still there. It had been tall before the park was built, tall before the first dump truck came, and tall when Jack returned from the Moon.

"Does a tree have birthdays?" Jack asked.

"It has years," said Aunt Jo, who was sitting nearby on the bench. "But it doesn't have cake."

Gus looked offended on behalf of the pine tree.

"Poor tree."

Jack sat beside Aunt Jo. "Will being five feel different?"

Aunt Jo looked at him. "What does being nearly five feel like?"

Jack considered the question. "My shoes feel the same. I still like my notebook. And I'm hungry."

"Those are useful things to know," Aunt Jo said.

Gus climbed onto the bench. "I feel hungry too. Do I have a birthday?"

"Of course you do," said Molly.

"Then I think mine should be at lunchtime."

Jack's dad arrived with a small bag of apples for the walk home. He told the friends there would be a birthday picnic on Saturday afternoon at the park, if everyone wanted to come.

Rex's whole tail answered before his mouth did.

"Yes!"

"I might even have five candles," Jack said.

"That's a lot of candles," Rex replied.

"Not on a very big cake," said Gus.

Jack folded his notebook closed. He was still the same size he had been when he arrived, and his birthday was still going to come.

On the way home he stopped at the little picture map by the park entrance. It showed all the roads they had helped build, the footbridge, and the farm beyond the brook. It did not show the Moon.

Jack knew where the Moon belonged. He had drawn that path in his own notebook.

Back at his house, he touched the new mark on the doorframe once more.

He did not stand on his toes this time.

On Saturday, there would be cake.
