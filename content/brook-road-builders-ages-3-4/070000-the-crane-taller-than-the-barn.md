# The Crane Taller Than the Barn

At Green Gate Farm, the tallest thing Molly knew was the roof of the red barn.

At Star Harbor, she met a crane that seemed to go on and on and on.

Its long boom stood against the sky. A hook hung from cables near the top. The crane's wide supports rested on carefully prepared ground.

"It could hang the whole barn up to dry," Gus said.

"It isn't here for the barn," Molly replied.

"Lucky barn."

Aunt Jo and Dr. Ada had brought the friends to a visitors' shelter beside the giant assembly building. For days, flatbed trucks had been bringing steel sections to the site. Mr. Lee, who knew wood and bridges and doors, had come along to learn about the construction.

"There's something I want you to see," he told Jack.

Across the work area, two workers attached lifting gear to a large steel piece. Other workers checked the load and cleared the lifting area. The crane began to raise it. Slowly, very slowly, the piece left the truck.

"Why don't they just pull it up fast?" Rex asked.

"Because a big load must stay controlled," Mr. Lee said. "The lifting crew makes a plan and checks every part of the operation."

The steel piece crossed a little stretch of open sky. It looked like a bridge made of silver lines. Then it settled into place high on the unfinished tower, where workers fastened it.

"That's one piece," Molly said.

"One of many," Dr. Ada replied.

The new tower would have platforms and connections for a rocket. Next to it stood an enormous assembly building where large rocket parts would be joined together. A thick, slow-moving crawler could one day carry the completed rocket out along a special road.

Jack looked from the crane to the building to the launch tower.

"So a rocket needs more than a place to stand. It needs a place to be put together."

"Exactly," Dr. Ada said.

Just then a breeze picked up. The crane stopped. The steel on its hook was held steady while the lifting team checked the conditions. The friends waited. Rex counted birds over the far fence. Gus counted the imaginary biscuits that would fit in the crane's bucket if it had a bucket.

"It doesn't have a bucket," Jack said.

"That is why it has no biscuits."

The lift resumed only after the workers were satisfied. No one hurried because four friends were watching.

Later, under the visitor shelter, Mr. Lee made a little demonstration with wooden blocks. He stacked one block on another and another. Gus tried to add a fourth while holding a sandwich, and the little tower tipped.

"My tower has become a garage," he announced, looking at the blocks on the table.

"Or just blocks," said Molly.

Mr. Lee built it again with a broad base. "A tall thing needs more than height," he said. "It needs a good plan, a good base, and careful building."

As the afternoon cooled, the friends watched the new steel section gleam against the sea. They could see a long way from the viewing shelter, but there were still walls, pipes, wires, and more tower sections to install.

On the way back to Brook Road, Molly rested her nose against the car window. She imagined her own red barn standing beside the enormous assembly building. The barn would look like a little toy.

When she reached home, the real barn was just the right size again. It held the tractor, the hay, and the quiet sounds of the farm.

The new space tower was far away, getting taller one piece at a time.
