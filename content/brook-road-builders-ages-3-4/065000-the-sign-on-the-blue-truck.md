# The Sign on the Blue Truck

There was a new sound near Pine Park.

Chuk. Chuk. Chuk.

Rex's ears went up.

"A truck?"

"Not quite," Aunt Jo said.

They were standing by the park gate. On the northern service lane, an adult operator was guiding a compact loader toward a prepared work space beside the entrance. The small loader had four wheels and a broad bucket in front. It carried a modest scoop of gravel.

Rex watched from the public path with Gus, Jack, and Molly. The small loader stopped beside two post holes Mr. Lee's crew had prepared earlier. The operator tipped the gravel into the adult work area and drove back along the service lane.

"It brought little stones for the sign posts," Jack said.

Gus looked at the neat pile.

"The gravel yard's youngest mountain."

"It's not a mountain," Rex told him.

"It could grow up," said Gus.

Mr. Lee, the beaver carpenter, was checking the two timber posts. The crew had prepared them in sturdy bases, and the area around the new board was closed until the work was finished. The park's old entrance sign was still in its familiar place. So were the repaired gate-side gravel strip and the rain gauge farther off by the open grass.

Then they heard a second sound.

Rummm, rummm.

A blue truck with a white stripe came along Brook Road and pulled into the proper delivery space by the service lane. Dan stepped out of the cab.

"I have something for a bear, a boy, and two puppies," he called.

"BISCUITS?" asked Rex.

"Not this time."

Dan rolled open the rear door of his box truck. Inside, secured for the ride, was a wide, flat wooden crate.

"Is it a very flat biscuit?" Rex asked hopefully.

"It is the neighborhood map," Dan said.

Aunt Jo and the other adults helped unload the crate. Dan operated the truck's liftgate to lower it to the delivery area, and Mr. Lee's crew moved it into the closed work space.

The children stood beyond the barrier with Aunt Jo. Gus rose onto his toes. Molly rose onto her white front paws. Rex tried both, which made his collar jingle.

When Mr. Lee opened the crate, the friends saw a wooden frame with a clear cover. Behind it was their map, painted in sturdy colors and carefully checked by the grown-ups.

"There's the pine," Jack said.

"There's the barn," Molly said.

"And two firehouse doors," Rex said.

Gus leaned forward. "I see my cap."

Everyone looked.

At the bottom of the map was a small green mark, right beside the picture key.

"That's the green gate at the farm," Jack said.

Gus squinted.

"It does look a little like my cap."

"Your cap has a bigger brim," Molly said.

"True," Gus agreed. "Very important detail."

Jack saw the solid road line he had drawn, leading from Brook Road around the long farm route. He saw the dotted walking line past the pine tree and across Little Bridge.

"The map is right," he whispered.

"The board isn't finished yet," Aunt Jo reminded him.

They watched Mr. Lee and his crew set the framed panel onto the prepared supports, adjust its position, and fasten it firmly. Then the adults checked the board's edges, its height, and the space left open for walkers and wheelchairs passing through the gate.

The small loader had already finished its part of the job. Dan closed his truck's rear door and drove back toward his parcel shed. Neither vehicle entered the park's walking loop or crossed Little Bridge.

Gus sat on the nearby bench while the grown-ups finished tidying the work area.

"It has been a good day for trucks," he said.

"Two trucks," Rex corrected.

"One truck and one loader," Jack said.

"Two excellent machines," Gus replied. "And one very important map."

By late afternoon Mr. Lee removed the temporary barrier. Aunt Jo took the friends over to the new board.

It was not very tall, but it was tall enough for a grown-up to read and low enough that Jack could point to its pictures.

Rex touched no part of the board. He stood on the path and stared at the tiny paw-print symbol beside the dotted line.

"That's the way Molly and I go," he said.

"And that's the way Dan's truck goes," Jack said, tracing the solid road line in the air without touching the cover.

"A map with two ways," Molly said.

"And no cap," Gus added.

They laughed.

At the end of the day the park had its old sign, its tall pine, its bench, Pancake Hill, and one new thing: a picture board showing how the familiar places joined together.

Aunt Jo turned them toward home. Rex looked back once and saw the little drawing of the bridge under the clear cover. It could stay there through sun and rain, showing walkers the way to the farm while the blue truck took its longer road.
