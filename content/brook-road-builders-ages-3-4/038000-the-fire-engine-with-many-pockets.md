# The Fire Engine with Many Pockets

Molly came to the firehouse with Farmer Ruth and a very serious expression.

She wanted to see the big red truck.

Rex wanted to see the big red truck.

Both puppies wanted to see it first.

They met by the visitor gate, exchanged their small, secret tail wag, and trotted in together.

"There," said Rex.

The fire engine waited behind one enormous red door. Its wheels were dark and clean. Its windows reflected the blue sky.

Captain Ann stood beside it, ready to show the neighbors around.

Gus had been counting the little side doors.

"Four on this side," he announced. "Or perhaps five. One is hiding behind a wheel."

"Why are there so many?" asked Jack.

"Each compartment holds things we may need," Captain Ann answered. "It helps us keep equipment in the right place."

She opened the first one.

Inside was folded hose.

Molly leaned forward until Farmer Ruth gently called her back to the visiting line.

"It looks like a great big sleeping snake," she said.

"A snake that eats water," Rex replied.

"It doesn't eat anything," Jack said.

"Good," Rex said. "More biscuits for me."

Captain Ann opened another compartment. There were tools fastened in tidy holders.

Gus looked disappointed.

"Still no enormous biscuit."

"Do fire engines carry biscuits?" Rex asked.

"The firefighters sometimes pack lunch," Captain Ann said. "The engine's equipment compartments are for our work."

"Lunch is important work," Gus observed.

Jack was looking at a panel of round dials on the side of the truck.

"What's this?"

"Those are gauges for the pump," Captain Ann replied. "They help the crew check what's happening when they use it."

Jack carefully drew three circles.

"It has more gauges than my little rain tube."

"Quite a few more," said Aunt Jo.

Captain Ann showed them the hose bed at the back, where lengths of hose rested in folded layers.

Rex counted the folds aloud. He lost count when Molly sneezed.

"That's all right," said Molly. "I wasn't counting either."

Then Rex noticed a small capped fitting on the side of the engine.

"Does the truck drink water there?"

"This engine carries some water in its tank," Captain Ann said.

She pointed to the pump panel again.

"The pump helps push that water through the hose," said Captain Ann.

"So the hose is like a road for water," Jack said.

"A very useful road," said Captain Ann. "The crew can also connect to other water supplies when they need more."

Rex looked at Molly.

They had seen roads for dump trucks, roads for delivery trucks, and a culvert that gave rainwater a route under the farm road.

Now they had found a different sort of route.

"Does it have stop signs?" Gus asked.

"No," Captain Ann said. "But it does have a nozzle at the far end. You'll see a water demonstration later."

Before the next visitors came over, she closed and checked each compartment.

Click.

Click.

Click.

Gus listened to the tidy series of sounds.

"The pockets are buttoning up."

The two puppies settled beside Aunt Jo on the grass beyond the visitor markers. Rex was pleased that Molly had seen his side of Brook Road again.

"What's your favorite pocket?" he asked.

"The one with the folded hose," Molly said.

"Mine is the one that might hold biscuits."

"But none does," said Jack.

Rex gave his small tail wag.

"A puppy can hope."

The engine stood quietly in its bay with every tool back in place. At the far end of the yard, the ladder truck waited for its own turn.

## For parents and caregivers - not spoken

**Continuity:** Before: Captain Ann has introduced Station Lane and its two red trucks. It is now the open day, and Rex, Gus, Jack and Aunt Jo have arranged to meet Molly and Farmer Ruth. After: The visitors recognize that the shorter red vehicle is a pumper engine, carrying hose, an onboard water tank and pump, and stored tools. Molly and Rex visit the firehouse together for the first time. No tools are removed from service, and the apparatus is left ready.

**Editorial note:** The talking dogs, bear cub, fox firefighter and community are original fiction. The engine and ladder are distinct adult-operated fire apparatus; the hose and protective-gear demonstrations take place in a calm, supervised open-day setting. Nothing in the narration describes a real incident or asks a preschooler to operate equipment. The references establish only the linked equipment facts, not any measured developmental, behavioral or sleep benefit.
