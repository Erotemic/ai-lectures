# The Baler and the Little Hay Bricks

The first hay brick was not made of bricks.

It was made of grass.

That seemed wrong to Gus.

"You can't build a wall out of grass," he said.

"You can build a little pile of it," Molly answered.

"But a wall?"

"Let's wait and see," said Jack.

They were sitting with Aunt Jo near the red barn at Green Gate Farm.

Farmer Ruth had checked the cut hay over several dry days. Today she said it was ready.

A tractor waited at the field, with a boxy machine hitched behind it.

"A baler," said Aunt Jo.

"A what?" Rex asked.

"A baler. It gathers dried hay and packs it together."

Gus put both paws on his knees.

"I hope it makes something very big."

The driver began moving slowly along the first long row of hay.

The baler picked up the dry grass from the ground.

The machine made a steady clunking sound.

Ka-thunk. Ka-thunk. Ka-thunk.

Rex's tail began to bounce with it.

"My tail knows that song," he said.

Behind the machine, something appeared.

Not a pile.

Not a cloud.

Not a wall.

Out came a small, neat, rectangular bale of hay, held together with twine.

It settled onto the cut field.

"A grass brick!" Gus cried.

Then came another.

And another.

Rex watched the little rectangles resting behind the baler.

"They look like loaves of bread," he said.

"You would need a very big toaster," Molly replied.

"I don't think we should toast hay," Jack said.

"I don't think a sheep would want toasted hay," Aunt Jo agreed.

Gus put his green cap over his eyes, thinking.

"What if we built a hay house for the biggest sheep in the world?"

"We don't have the biggest sheep in the world," said Jack.

"We might," Gus said. "Maybe she's just very far away."

One of the two woolly sheep in the pen gave a sleepy bleat.

"That one sounds tired," Molly said.

"Perhaps she's the smallest biggest sheep," Gus replied.

Jack opened his notebook. First he drew a long wavy line of loose hay. Then he drew an arrow toward a rectangle with little loops of twine around it.

"The baler made the same hay easier to carry," he said.

"It was dry enough today," Farmer Ruth told them when she joined the group for a break. "Now we can pack it into bales and carry it to the barn."

"Does it turn into bricks after it dries?" Rex asked.

"No," she said. "It's still hay. It's just pressed together and tied so it holds its shape."

Rex liked that answer.

A brick of grass was still grass. He could sniff that much.

Gus looked toward the waiting low wagon beside the barn.

"Is that where the grass bricks go?"

"Tomorrow we'll collect them," Farmer Ruth said. "The adults will load the wagon. Then we'll store the hay under cover."

The machine continued making its slow clunking sound in the distance.

A hen scratched at the earth by the barn, completely uninterested in hay bricks.

Jack drew the hen too, because she happened to be standing exactly where his pencil had room.

As the sun went down, the baler went quiet. The bales remained in tidy places on the cut field, waiting to be gathered.

At home, Gus set his green cap on the bedpost.

He made a very small pretend house out of two pillows.

It was not big enough for a giant sheep.

It was just big enough for his head.

So he rested there and went to sleep.
