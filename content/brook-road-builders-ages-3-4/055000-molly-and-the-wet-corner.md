# Molly and the Wet Corner

Molly liked rainy mornings at Green Gate Farm.

She liked the way the red barn looked darker when it was wet. She liked the damp smell of grass. Most of all, she liked the little round drops that hung from the fence rails before they fell.

Plip.

Plip.

That morning, the rain had stopped. Farmer Ruth was opening the big barn doors when Molly heard another sound.

Plip.

It came from the narrow shelter beside the wagon door.

Plip. Plip.

Molly went to see.

The little shelter had a low roof joined to the tall red barn wall. Under it was the spot where Farmer Ruth usually parked the green wheelbarrow between jobs. Today, a dark wet mark spread across the ground there.

Molly put one white front paw near the edge of it.

A drop fell from the roof onto the wet place.

"Someone left the rain on," she said.

Farmer Ruth came around the corner. Her striped-collar goat stood on the other side of the fence, chewing in the way goats do when they have no intention of explaining themselves.

"There shouldn't be a drip under that little roof," Farmer Ruth said.

"Perhaps it forgot where the outside is," Molly suggested.

Ruth bent to look from a safe place. She did not climb up or pull at the boards. "The old edge is wearing out. I'll ask Mr. Lee to look at it. In the meantime, we'll keep this bit closed off."

She moved the wheelbarrow into the dry main barn and set a barrier across the shelter entrance.

Inside, the hay bales from the farm's big harvest were still stacked high and dry. The red tractor stood in its familiar bay. The hens were already making an argument about breakfast, as if the weather had been arranged especially for them.

The next day Rex visited with Aunt Jo, Gus, and Jack. They came from Brook Road on the walking path through Pine Park and over Little Bridge. No trucks came over the little wooden bridge. They never did.

Rex gave Molly one wag. Molly gave one back.

"Come listen," Molly said.

"To what?" asked Rex.

"A tiny roof that goes plip even after the rain stops."

They stood beside Farmer Ruth, outside the marked area. The dripping had nearly finished, but they could see the dark patch beneath the old edge.

Gus squinted at the shelter. "Maybe the barn was drinking water and got the hiccups."

"Barns don't drink," Jack said.

"They might if they had very long straws."

Rex tried to imagine a barn straw. He could only think of a straw the size of a telephone pole, which sounded much too big to fetch.

Mr. Lee, the beaver carpenter, arrived to inspect the shelter with Farmer Ruth. He checked the little roof and its connection to the taller wall. He also found an old wooden edge board that had become soft in one place.

"The main barn is sound," he said. "It's the little side shelter that needs work. We'll take care of that part and give it a new roof edge."

Jack opened his blue notebook. He drew the tall barn as one big rectangle and the side shelter as a smaller shape beside it. Then he drew a drop just where the two roofs met.

"Why does it come inside that little bit?" he asked.

"Water finds a way through gaps," Mr. Lee said. "A roof has to lead rain down and away from the places we want to keep dry."

Jack drew an arrow running down the roof and out beyond its edge. Then he drew another arrow showing where the drip had gone the wrong way.

Loose roof edges and damp marks are things adults check when they inspect old farm buildings.

Gus watched Jack's pencil. "Can you draw a roof that keeps my cap dry?"

"Your cap is on your head," said Rex.

"That's where I keep most of my important things."

Rex looked inside Gus's green cap. There was nothing there.

"I see," he said.

"Exactly. Very tidy," Gus replied.

Farmer Ruth called the friends back toward the porch. The work area would stay closed while Mr. Lee arranged the repair.

As they walked, Molly turned once to look at the wet patch. She had heard a little noise, and now everyone knew what it meant.

The sun reached the red wall. The last drop fell.

Plip.

Then the farm was quiet enough to hear the hens starting a fresh argument, which Molly thought sounded a lot like breakfast again.
