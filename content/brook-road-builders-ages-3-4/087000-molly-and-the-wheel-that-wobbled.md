# Molly and the Wheel That Wobbled

Molly liked the sound of wheels on gravel.

They went crunch, crunch, crunch when a wagon moved. A big truck made a low rumble. A little cart made a quick clatter.

And today, at the Red Door Garage, one little wheel made a sound that didn't belong.

Clunk.

Molly lifted her ears.

Clunk.

"That one," she said.

Ms. Pam stopped pulling the small red wagon across the garage floor. Mr. Lee had finished its wooden body, and Ms. Pam was helping fit the four matching wheels.

"You heard it too," Ms. Pam said.

Rex and Gus were watching beside Aunt Jo. Jack was not there. The wagon was still his birthday surprise.

"It has four wheels," Gus reported. "I counted them twice. Four, four."

"But one is unhappy," Molly said.

"Wheels don't get unhappy," Rex replied.

"This one sounds unhappy."

Ms. Pam put the wagon on a low work stand so she could look closely. She turned each wheel with her hand.

The first turned smoothly. The second turned smoothly. The third turned smoothly.

The fourth rocked a little from side to side.

"There we are," she said. "That fitting needs adjusting."

"Could we give the wagon only three wheels?" Gus asked.

Ms. Pam looked at him. "Not this wagon. It's made to roll on four."

"What if it had five?"

"Then it would be a different wagon."

"What if it had one hundred?"

Rex sat down. "Then we'd be counting until the birthday cake was gone."

Gus considered that and decided against it.

While Ms. Pam corrected the fitting, Aunt Jo spread a long piece of paper across a low table. She brought four toy wheels out of a box and let the friends try rolling them over a little strip of washable paint. They made tracks across the paper, side by side.

Rex made one track with his paw by mistake.

"That's not a wheel," Molly said.

"It's the new puppy model," Rex said. "Very good at turning corners."

Molly added her own pawprint beside it. The two prints were different sizes and shapes.

Then she looked at the four wheel tracks.

"One goes off the paper," she said.

A toy wheel had rolled toward the edge because Gus had given it a crooked push.

"It wanted to visit the floor," Gus said.

"It went where you pushed it," Molly answered.

At the work stand, Ms. Pam spun the last wagon wheel again. It turned straight and quiet.

"That's better," she said.

Mr. Lee lowered the wagon to the floor. Its little red sides were high enough to hold Jack's notebook, but low enough that he could reach inside. A wooden handle folded down at the front.

Molly watched Ms. Pam pull it slowly across the floor.

No clunk.

The little wheels rolled together.

"Now the wagon sounds happy," Molly said.

"Now the wagon is working," Ms. Pam replied, smiling.

They rolled it outside to the garage yard, away from the road. The pavement was smooth. The wagon followed behind Ms. Pam easily.

Then they tried it over a patch of gravel beside the garage. There was a little more resistance. The handle pulled a little harder, and the wheels made a wonderful crunchy sound.

"It is like my legs," Gus said. "Running on the path is easier than running through the tall grass."

"For me too," said Rex.

Molly, who was very good at running through tall grass, did not say anything.

They put a notebook-sized wooden block in the wagon and tried again. It rolled steadily. No one rode inside it. It was a cart for carrying things, not a little seat with wheels.

Ms. Pam parked it beneath the garage roof until the paint was dry. Mr. Lee wiped his hands and counted the wheel fasteners one last time.

"Jack can carry his notebook, pencils, and little treasures in it," he said.

"And perhaps one biscuit," Rex suggested.

"Your biscuit?" asked Molly.

"Only if I am walking beside it."

Molly took one last look at the four wheels. They were ordinary wheels, with ordinary jobs. But they had taken many people to get right: Mr. Lee, Ms. Pam, Aunt Jo, and three very curious young friends.

"One friend is missing," Rex said. "Jack doesn't know yet."

"He will on Saturday," Molly said.

"First we have to hide it inside my enormous box," Gus replied.

The box stood by the garage wall, nearly as tall as Gus. Its blue ribbon reached all the way to the ground.

Molly looked from the little wagon to the giant box.

"That box," she said, "is going to make a lot of noise when we open it."

"Good," said Gus. "Birthdays should have a little noise."

By sunset, the wagon was covered, the tools were put away, and the garage door had closed for the evening.

Behind it, four small wheels waited quietly for their first trip.
