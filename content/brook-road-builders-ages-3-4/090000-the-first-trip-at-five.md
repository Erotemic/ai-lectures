# The First Trip at Five

The day after his birthday, Jack woke up and saw his little red wagon waiting beside the hallway door.

The blue ribbon was folded inside it. His yellow paper crown lay on top.

He took the crown out, put on his shoes, and fetched his notebook.

"Where are you taking it?" Dad asked.

"To see how far it goes."

Dad looked out the window. The sky was clear, and the path to Pine Park would be dry.

"I'll walk with you."

Rex met them near the house with the blue front step. He was carrying his wooden dump truck in his mouth. He put it down before speaking.

"I brought a truck for your wagon."

Jack smiled. "A truck in a wagon is a very good idea."

They placed it next to the notebook. Its wooden wheels knocked gently against the wagon's red side.

Gus came from his house wearing his green cap. Molly joined them from the direction of Little Bridge, with Farmer Ruth walking a little way behind her.

"Is it going to the Moon?" Gus asked the wagon.

"No," said Jack. "It's going to the park."

"Much closer," said Rex.

"And it has much better air," Molly added.

Jack pulled the handle. The wagon followed him along Brook Road with a soft rattle, then turned onto the path toward Pine Park.

At the entrance, Jack stopped beside the picture map. The solid line showed the road for trucks. The dotted line showed the walking way over Little Bridge. He could see the farm, garage, and firehouse in their familiar places.

"We built a lot," Gus said.

"Grown-ups built most of it," Jack reminded him.

"We watched very carefully," Gus replied.

They passed the repaired patch of gravel at the park gate and headed toward the tall pine. For a while the wagon rolled easily. Then one of its little wheels met the edge of a soft patch near the grass.

It stopped.

Jack pulled again. The wagon moved only a tiny bit.

"I'm five," he said. "I thought I'd be able to pull it."

Rex sniffed the wheel. "There is grass in the way."

Gus crouched beside the wagon. "Would you like me to help move it back to the firm path?"

Jack looked at the wheel. He could see that it had dipped off the gravel.

"Yes, please."

With Dad watching, Jack held the handle while Gus helped guide the light wagon back onto the path. Rex and Molly stepped aside. The little wheel found the firm surface again.

"Now," said Jack.

He gave the handle a small pull.

The wagon rolled on.

"It wasn't because I'm too small," Jack said.

"The ground was soft," Molly answered.

"Even a big truck can get stuck," Rex said. "Although Ms. Pam would need a much bigger wagon to bring it home."

They continued to the bench beneath the pine tree. No one hurried. The wagon had a good place to stop there, with its wheels resting on the firm path beside the bench.

Jack took out his notebook and turned the pages.

He found the first digger. There were the dump trucks that had brought the park's gravel. He found the wooden bridge, the hay wagon, the snowplow, and the red fire engine. Then came the huge crane at Star Harbor, the white rocket, and the gray Moon with Scout's little rover.

Rex's toy dump truck was sitting beside him on the bench.

"That one's still my favorite," Rex said.

Jack picked it up. Its repaired wheel turned as smoothly as it had since Ms. Pam helped fix it long ago.

"I remember when we couldn't get it over the block bridge," Jack said.

"We moved the blocks," Rex answered.

"Then it went over."

Gus stretched his paws in the grass. "I remember when Pancake Hill was just a pile of dirt."

"It was never quite a pile," said Jack.

"It was a very excellent pile," Gus replied.

Molly stood by the pine tree and looked toward the path leading to Little Bridge. She remembered standing on the other bank, hearing Rex's bark before the footbridge existed.

"Now I can come over whenever Farmer Ruth brings me," she said.

Jack found a blank page at the back of his notebook.

On it, he drew a small red wagon. He made four wheels that were almost round. Inside it he drew a notebook and a toy dump truck. In front of it he drew one small hand holding the handle.

Then he drew three little friends beside it: a dog with one dark ear, a bear with a cap, and a dog with white front paws.

"There," he said.

"What about the Moon?" asked Gus.

Jack tipped his head back.

The Moon was not visible in the bright morning sky, but he knew it was there, somewhere beyond the blue.

"I already drew the Moon," he said, turning to the older page.

"And what do we draw tomorrow?" asked Rex.

Jack looked at the empty pages still waiting beyond the wagon drawing.

"I don't know yet."

For once, he did not hurry to find out.

They had a little time at the park. They watched a beetle cross a flat stone. Rex tried to follow its path with his nose. Molly settled beside him. Gus leaned back against the bench and let his cap shade his eyes.

When it was time to leave, Jack tucked the notebook back into the wagon and took hold of the handle.

The handle felt warm in Jack's hand. The wagon rolled smoothly along the good path. Gus walked beside him, and Rex's wooden truck bumped gently against the notebook with every turn.

They walked home together, past the picture map and the familiar gate, with the little red wagon rattling gently behind them.

At Jack's house, Dad held the door while Jack brought the wagon inside. He parked it beneath the pencil marks on the doorframe.

The newest mark was only a small line in a long piece of wood.

Jack put his hand beside it, just to see.

Then he went to find his slippers.

The little wagon waited by the door.

Tomorrow there would be another road to take, or another question to ask, or perhaps just another game on the rug.

There was no need to choose tonight.
