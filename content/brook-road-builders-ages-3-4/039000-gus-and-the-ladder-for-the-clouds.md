# Gus and the Ladder for the Clouds

Gus had seen plenty of ladders.

There was one at Farmer Ruth's barn, where grown-ups reached the high shelf.

There was a little step ladder in Aunt Jo's shed.

And there was the ladder he had once made from three sticks and a piece of string.

That last one had never worked.

But the ladder resting on the roof of the firehouse truck was bigger than any of them.

"That," Gus announced, "is a ladder for clouds."

"Clouds don't have doors," said Jack.

"Not on the side facing us," Gus replied.

The crowd gathered at the painted viewing line beside the demonstration yard. Captain Ann and the other firefighters checked that the space around the long red truck was clear.

The truck was parked on firm, level ground. Its engine idled quietly.

Captain Ann waved to the visitors from beside the line.

"Watch the sides first," she said.

Gus looked up at the enormous ladder.

"I thought we were watching the top."

"The sides first," said Captain Ann.

The truck put out strong metal legs, called stabilizers, onto wide pads.

They reached down to the ground on either side of the truck.

"Like a table spreading out its legs," Jack said.

"A very long table," said Gus. "For a very large dinner."

"They help keep the truck steady while the ladder is raised," Captain Ann explained.

Rex sat beside Molly. Their tails made two soft marks in the dust.

The crew checked their equipment, and then the long ladder began to lift.

Slowly.

Higher.

The ladder rose clear of the truck roof.

Gus's mouth fell open.

"It was sleeping flat. Now it's sitting up."

One ladder section slid out from another, and then another.

Jack leaned his notebook against his knee.

"They fit inside each other when they're put away!"

"Like my stack of cups," said Gus. "Except my cups don't reach the trees."

The ladder extended in the open space above the yard. It did not touch a tree or a roof.

The crew controlled it from the truck. Everyone watching stayed behind the visitor line.

A small white cloud drifted past above the ladder tip.

Gus waved.

"Good morning, cloud. Do you need help with your curtains?"

Rex looked up.

"The cloud doesn't have curtains."

"Then I will bring it some," Gus said.

Jack squinted along the ladder.

"It isn't really going to touch the cloud."

"I know," Gus answered. "But a bear may still be polite."

Captain Ann explained that the aerial ladder is useful when firefighters need to reach high places, and that the trained crew checks the ground and everything above the truck before moving it.

Gus listened, then glanced at the narrow rails along each side.

"Could it reach Pancake Hill?"

"Pancake Hill is not very tall," said Rex.

"Exactly. It wouldn't have to get out of bed."

Captain Ann signaled the crew. The ladder began to shorten.

One long section slid neatly back into another.

And another.

Then the whole ladder lowered toward its cradle on the truck roof.

Gus watched until it rested flat again.

"It has gone back to sleep," he whispered.

The crew put the stabilizers away after the ladder was secured.

Jack finished his sketch. It showed a long red truck, four wide supports, and a ladder rising above it.

Gus added one small cloud in the corner.

"That's the cloud's house," he said.

Jack laughed. "Then where's the door?"

Gus drew the tiniest square imaginable.

"On the other side," he answered.

The ladder truck rested with its metal sections tucked together. The sky went on moving quietly above it.

**Editorial note:** The talking dogs, bear cub, fox firefighter and community are original fiction. The engine and ladder are distinct adult-operated fire apparatus; the hose and protective-gear demonstrations take place in a calm, supervised open-day setting. Nothing in the narration describes a real incident or asks a preschooler to operate equipment. The references establish only the linked equipment facts, not any measured developmental, behavioral or sleep benefit.
