# Jack and the Snow That Was Not Even

Jack had brought two things to Pine Park.

His blue notebook.

And a ruler.

Gus had brought one thing.

His green cap.

Rex had brought four paws, two ears, and a nose that was very interested in everything beneath the snow.

"What are we looking for?" asked Gus.

"How deep the snow is," said Jack.

"Very," said Gus, looking at the white field.

"Let's find out."

The friends stood with Aunt Jo at the cleared park gate. Pancake Hill was still there, but now it looked like a pancake covered in whipped cream. The tall pine held little pockets of snow between its branches. The bench had a fat white cushion on its seat.

Gus pointed at the snow against the fence.

"There! That must be the deepest bit in the entire world."

Aunt Jo examined it.

"The wind has piled snow there. We call that a drift."

Jack measured the open snow first, far from the fence and the pine tree.

He carefully lowered his ruler straight down through the soft layer until it reached the ground.

"This spot is much shallower," he said.

Gus moved beside the drift. He didn't climb onto it. He simply looked at the top of it from the cleared edge.

"But there's a mountain over here!"

"The wind made that mountain," Jack replied. "It moved some snow from one place to another."

Rex sniffed the air.

"The wind is a snowplow without wheels."

Gus blinked. "And without a driver."

"And without a horn," Rex said.

"You would make a good horn," Gus told him.

"Woof," said Rex politely.

They laughed so hard that Gus's cap slipped over his eyes.

At the far side of the gate, Mr. Lee the beaver carpenter had arrived with a small machine on two wheels. It had handles, a boxy front, and a tall bent tube that pointed toward an empty grassy verge.

"Is that another snowplow?" Rex wondered.

"It's a snowblower," said Aunt Jo. "Mr. Lee is using it to open the walking path. We'll watch from here."

The machine hummed.

The snowblower drew snow in at the front and sent it out through a tall curved chute.

A soft white arc landed on the empty grass beside the path.

Gus gasped.

"It has a snow sneeze!"

"A very tidy sneeze," Jack said. "It lands in the same place each time."

Mr. Lee worked along the path in short passes, keeping his machine away from visitors and directing the snow toward the open verge. Then he switched it off and checked the path with another grown-up.

The children waited while the adults finished.

When Aunt Jo led them along the newly cleared route, Rex could see the original gravel beneath his paws.

"There you are," he whispered to the path.

The bench was still covered in snow, so they did not sit there. Instead they turned back toward the gate, where the open path met Brook Road.

Jack drew three things in his notebook: the ruler in the ordinary snow, the drift by the fence, and Mr. Lee's curved snowblower chute.

Gus peered at the page.

"Can you draw the wind?"

"Not exactly."

"Draw a hat blowing sideways."

Jack drew a tiny green cap with three lines behind it.

Gus smiled.

"Perfect. That is definitely the wind."

By evening Jack's ruler was dry, his notebook was on his desk, and the snow rested on Pine Park without moving at all.
