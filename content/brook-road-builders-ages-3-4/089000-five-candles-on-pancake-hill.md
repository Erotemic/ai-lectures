# Five Candles on Pancake Hill

Jack woke up on his birthday and checked the doorframe.

He looked at yesterday's pencil mark.

Then he stood beside it with his heels on the floor.

He wasn't suddenly any taller.

"Maybe after breakfast," he told his dad.

Dad smiled. "Happy fifth birthday."

There was a small paper crown on the breakfast table, made from yellow card. Jack put it on and ate his toast. He did not feel like a king. He felt like Jack, with a crown that kept slipping over one eye.

In the afternoon they walked to Pine Park. The path was dry, the tall pine cast a long shadow, and the little picture map still stood beside the gate sign.

Rex was waiting near Pancake Hill. Molly had come across Little Bridge with Farmer Ruth. Gus stood behind an enormous cardboard box with a blue ribbon tied around it.

"Happy birthday!" the friends called.

Gus's green cap slid over his nose when he shouted.

Jack laughed. "Thank you!"

Aunt Jo had spread a picnic blanket beneath the pine. Jack's dad carried a cake in a covered box, very carefully. Ms. Pam had brought a small basket of apples. Dan the donkey had stopped by after his delivery route. Mr. Lee sat on the bench, and Captain Ann visited briefly in her ordinary clothes, without her firefighting boots.

At the edge of the blanket was a large cardboard package.

"Is that for me?" Jack asked.

"It's not a boulder," Gus said quickly.

"I wasn't going to guess boulder."

"Good. Because I considered one."

Everyone made room. Mr. Lee untied the big blue ribbon. Gus opened one flap. Then another. The box made a loud flup sound and fell open.

Inside was a little red wagon with four small wheels and a wooden pull handle.

Jack stared.

"It's for your notebook," Molly said. "And things you want to bring on walks."

"Ms. Pam fixed a wobbly wheel," Rex added. "She made it stop clunking."

"Mr. Lee built the body," Gus said. "I worked on an edge. And I brought the enormous box."

Jack knelt beside the wagon. He ran a hand along the smooth wooden side. On the front, the friends had painted a small yellow Moon and four marks that could be pawprints or boot prints, depending on how you looked at them.

"Can I try it?"

"Yes," said Mr. Lee. "It's for pulling your supplies, not for riding in. Let's try it with your notebook."

Jack put his blue notebook inside. It rested neatly on the wagon floor. Rex placed his old wooden dump truck beside it, just for the trial trip.

Jack pulled the wagon a little way along the firm path. The wheels made a gentle rattle over the gravel.

"It's like a truck," he said.

"A very quiet truck," Rex replied.

"A science truck," said Gus.

"A science wagon," Jack corrected, with satisfaction.

He pulled it back to the picnic blanket and returned Rex's toy truck to its owner.

Then Aunt Jo helped Dad open the cake box.

The cake had white frosting, five little yellow candles, and a small blue icing curve across the top that looked a little like the Moon above Earth.

"Did you bring an entire rocket?" Gus asked.

"Only the cake," said Dad.

"Good. A rocket would not fit on this blanket."

Dad lit the five candles. Their flames moved a little in the afternoon air. Everyone gathered close enough to see.

Jack looked at the five lights.

He remembered being smaller. He remembered the first day he had watched a yellow digger at the park and thought its bucket looked like a giant spoon. He remembered the Moon, where his boots had felt light against the ground.

"Make a wish," Molly whispered.

Jack did. He didn't say it out loud.

Then he blew.

Four candles went out. The fifth stayed lit.

Rex tried to help by making a dramatic huffing noise, but Aunt Jo gently held the cake farther from his nose.

Jack tried again.

The fifth flame went out.

Everyone cheered, even Gus, whose cap fell off altogether.

There were small slices of cake and apples and cups of water. Rex received his own suitable puppy treat from Aunt Jo instead of cake. Molly had one too. After the candles were taken away, Jack ate his slice slowly, sitting with both feet on the grass.

Then Dad held out a folded message from Star Harbor.

Dr. Ada had sent a new picture from Scout's rover on the Moon. The little robot stood beside its science station, with its wheel tracks stretching across gray ground. A short note said the team was still listening to the rover's reports.

"It's working," Jack said softly.

"Even though we came home," Molly said.

Jack placed the picture in his notebook. Later he would draw it carefully. Right now, he wanted to be at his party.

They played a game of follow-the-leader around the low side of Pancake Hill. Gus led first. He made everyone walk as if they were very slow dump trucks. Molly led next and took them around a fallen pine cone. Rex chose a route that involved sniffing the grass every three steps.

When Jack's turn came, he simply walked along the path, pulled his small red wagon, and looked back to see all his friends following.

"Where are we going?" Gus asked.

"Back to the cake," said Jack.

"Excellent route," said Gus.

As the afternoon grew golden, the neighbors went home by their familiar ways. Dan used the road in his truck. Farmer Ruth and Molly walked toward Little Bridge. Ms. Pam went west to the Red Door Garage.

Jack's dad carried the empty cake box. Jack pulled the wagon. It carried his notebook and the folded birthday crown.

At home, he set the wagon beside the door. The little Moon painted on its front faced into the hallway.

"How does five feel?" Dad asked.

Jack thought about it.

"A little like four," he said. "But with more candles."

Dad laughed and gave him a hug.

Before bed, Jack put his notebook safely on its shelf. The new Moon picture was tucked between the pages.

Outside, Brook Road grew quiet.

Five candles were out. Four little wheels were still.

And Jack was five.
