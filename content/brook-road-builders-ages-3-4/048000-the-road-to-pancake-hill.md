# The Road to Pancake Hill

Rex could see Pancake Hill from the park gate.

He could also see something he liked even more.

Two small black-and-white ears on the other side of Little Bridge.

"Molly!"

Molly barked once, happily.

But there was a white blanket between the friends and the bridge rails.

Mr. Lee and Aunt Jo were finishing a narrow path with shovels. They brushed the wooden bridge boards and checked the railings. Farmer Ruth waited with Molly at the farm end until the grown-ups said the little walking route was ready.

"Now," Aunt Jo said, "we can cross on our feet."

Molly trotted across with Farmer Ruth. Rex walked from the park gate with Jack and Gus.

They met beside the first railing and touched noses.

One wag from Rex.

One wag from Molly.

Their little hello was the same as it had been in the warm hayfield months earlier.

"It's cold!" Rex told her.

"I've noticed," said Molly.

Gus wore his green cap. Jack carried his blue notebook under his coat. Behind them the adults' path led safely into Pine Park.

The road plow had cleared the driving lanes; the smaller snowblower had opened the park path.

Beyond the houses, an adult crew was finishing the last little side lane. The orange plow was nearly done with its route. The spreading truck had already returned to the town depot.

Molly looked back toward Green Gate Farm.

"Our red tractor made a path on the farm road," she said.

"The big red one from hay season?" asked Gus.

"The same one. With a snow blade on it now."

The long farm road was ready for vehicles, but Little Bridge was still a bridge for feet and paws.

"Good," said Rex. "A truck would make us very crowded."

They followed Aunt Jo toward Pancake Hill. The low mound had become a white pillow. Gus knelt beside its edge and pressed his mittened hand into the fresh snow.

"I declare this Pancake Hill's winter hat," he said.

"Does Pancake Hill need a hat?" Molly asked.

"Not anymore. It already has one."

The puppies left two little trails of paw prints in a cleared patch beside the hill. They watched the marks appear: four toes and a pad, four toes and a pad, then another set beside them.

Jack opened his notebook on the sheltered side of the bench, where Aunt Jo helped keep the pages from blowing.

He drew the white road, the orange plow, the red spreading truck at the garage bend, the tractor's blade at the farm, and Mr. Lee's curved snowblower at the park.

Rex pointed with one paw at the drawing of Little Bridge.

"Don't put the tractor there."

"I won't," Jack said. "That's our walking route."

He drew a small pair of paw prints crossing the bridge instead.

Gus watched him draw a white line through the park.

"Where's the big snow sandwich?"

"The ridge beside the road," Jack said.

"Good. Don't forget the filling."

Jack drew a snowbank beside the road. He did not draw any cheese.

When the grown-ups called it time to go, the children followed the clear path toward the gate. Molly and Farmer Ruth headed back toward the bridge; Rex, Gus, and Jack went west with Aunt Jo.

At the park entrance, Rex looked over his shoulder.

The footpaths were dark lines through the snow, and their paw prints were little marks beside them. Pancake Hill wore its white hat. The tall pine stood still.

Nothing had vanished under the snow after all. The roads and paths had been there, waiting for people to find them again.

At home, Jack laid the winter route drawing beside his older pages about summer gravel and rain. Rex parked his wooden dump truck on the blue-step windowsill. Gus hung his cap on its peg.

Across the brook, Molly went to sleep near her blue rope toy.

The machines were parked. The paths were quiet. And the town, white from end to end, was ready for another ordinary morning.
