# The Truck That Came Home Light

When Rex first met Dan's blue box truck, the space behind the cab had been full of parcels.

Now Dan opened the back at his little parcel shed, and the space was almost empty.

"Where did the boxes go?" asked Gus.

"We delivered them," Dan said.

"All of them?"

"All the boxes on this week's list."

Rex looked inside from the marked waiting place, with Aunt Jo beside him.

He could see the clean floor and the straps Dan used to hold things in place when the truck carried a load.

"The truck had a box with a roof, so the parcels could travel inside."

Jack traced the tall square shape in the air with his pencil.

"The blue truck carried things in one room," he said. "Then the room got emptier."

"That's a useful sort of room," said Gus. "My room gets fuller every day."

Dan handed Aunt Jo a clean, unused carton that wasn't needed for his next deliveries. "You said the children liked making things out of boxes," he said.

"We do," Rex said at once.

Back at the blue-step house, they unfolded the carton on the rug. Jack drew a road on a long sheet of paper. Gus found three blocks and a pinecone. Rex fetched his small wooden dump truck, its wheel working perfectly since Aunt Jo had freed the old ribbon.

"This block is the garage," Jack said.

"It needs a red door," said Gus.

They put a red square of paper against the block.

"This pinecone is Pine Park," Rex announced, sniffing it.

"That's a very prickly park," Gus said.

They put the pinecone beside a curved pencil line for the walking path, near a tiny brown mound that was Pancake Hill.

The third block became Green Gate Farm, with a green paper gate and a small blue dot for Molly's rope toy.

Gus stood the empty carton on its side.

"This is Dan's truck. The biggest truck I can fit in our house."

"And here's my real little one," Rex said, rolling the wooden dump truck beside it.

He made a soft engine noise.

Brrrrum.

"Delivery! One pinecone!"

"You can't deliver Pine Park," Jack protested.

"Not the whole park," said Rex. "Just the pinecone part."

Jack drew one long line from the garage to the farm, going around the brook. He drew a shorter walking line from Pine Park across Little Bridge.

"The blue truck had gone to the farm by the long road, not over Little Bridge."

"I know," Rex said. "Little Bridge is for paws, shoes, and hooves."

"And for goats with shopping bags," Gus added.

Jack drew a little goat near the footbridge, carrying a bag that appeared to contain an enormous carrot.

They remembered what Dan had carried. Four orange cones for Ms. Pam. A clear rain gauge for the park. Feed for the hens. A green wheelbarrow for Farmer Ruth.

"Where is the rain?" asked Rex.

"Not here yet," Jack said. "The tube is ready when it comes."

Aunt Jo looked out the window toward the west end of Brook Road.

Dan was putting the blue truck away beneath the roof of his small shed.

The engine went quiet.

The children came to the doorstep together, still holding the paper road.

"Goodnight, truck!" Rex called, though it was much too far away for Dan to hear.

Gus waved his cap anyway.

After their game, Jack folded the route drawing into the back of his blue notebook. Rex folded the clean carton flat and stood it behind the rug.

The wooden dump truck parked beside his blocks.

Across town, the orange cones stood in the garage shed, the gauge waited for rain, and the green wheelbarrow rested near the barn.

Every parcel was where it belonged.

And the blue truck, which had carried them all, was home.
