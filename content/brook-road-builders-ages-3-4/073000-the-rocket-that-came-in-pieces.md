# The Rocket That Came in Pieces

Gus expected the rocket to arrive in one enormous box.

He stood beside Aunt Jo on the visitor platform at Star Harbor, watching the road. A special truck came over the rise, moving so slowly that Rex could have walked beside it. Its long trailer carried a round white piece wrapped in protective covering.

"That's only part of it," said Dr. Ada.

Gus looked disappointed. "I ordered the whole rocket."

"You didn't order anything," Jack reminded him.

"Then perhaps that is why they sent part."

Another truck followed. And then another. Some carried long metal sections. Some carried boxes. One had a covered load marked with a picture of a round window.

Dan's blue box truck would have looked like a little toy beside the largest of them.

"The pieces came from different factories," Dr. Ada said. "People have been building and checking them for a long time. Now the engineers will bring them together in the assembly building."

Rex pressed his nose against the railing. "Will the rocket have a tipping bed?"

"No," said Max, the pilot, who had joined them. "It has a different job from a dump truck."

"Does it get to keep the stones?"

"It doesn't carry stones to the Moon."

"That is good," Rex said. "Mr. Fred might miss them."

Through the open assembly-building doors, the friends could see tall cranes and work platforms. The heavy transporters delivered their loads to carefully marked places. Workers checked the papers, the labels, the lifting gear, and the parts themselves. Then each section moved into the building.

Jack opened his blue notebook. On one page he drew a big rocket as one long shape. On the next page he drew the pieces: a lower section with engines, an upper section, a cabin with windows, and equipment that would help the travelers near the Moon.

"Why are there parts that come off later?" he asked.

Max pointed to the drawing. "A rocket uses a lot of energy to leave Earth. Some parts finish their work and separate. The spacecraft doesn't need to carry every empty part all the way to the Moon."

Gus stared at the paper. "Like when I finish a sandwich and don't carry the plate to the farm?"

"Not exactly," said Jack.

"But it is still a good sandwich rule."

The rocket would be called Star One. It was being prepared for a journey that would take the six travelers toward the Moon and bring them home again. Its spacious cabin had seats for Rex, Gus, Jack, Molly, Aunt Jo, and Max. The Moon lander had room for the same six and equipment for a short visit to the surface. This was a very advanced spacecraft, and grown-up teams had designed it for all its passengers.

Molly counted the six places on the drawing twice. "No one is left behind?"

"No one," Aunt Jo said. "And nobody flies until every check is complete."

The last truck for the day brought a smaller metal piece shaped like a bell. Its cover had a bright yellow label. A lifting crew took it inside.

"Is that the rocket's dinner bell?" Gus asked.

"It's part of an engine," Dr. Ada said.

Rex's ears lifted. "Then the rocket does have a bell."

They went home while the work inside the assembly building continued. At Rex's blue front step, the familiar wooden dump truck waited beside the rug. Its four wheels still turned from the repair Ms. Pam had made.

Rex looked at his small truck, then remembered the long line of enormous trailers.

For a rocket to fly high, a remarkable number of wheels had to roll on the ground first.
