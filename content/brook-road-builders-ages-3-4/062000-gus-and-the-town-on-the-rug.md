# Gus and the Town on the Rug

On the next morning, it rained just enough to make the grass shine.

Not a storm. Not even a big shower. Just a soft patter that made Rex's blue front step smell damp.

"Good day for a town," Gus announced.

Aunt Jo looked up from the kitchen. "A town?"

"A whole town. On the rug."

Rex hurried to fetch the wooden dump truck that Ms. Pam had helped him repair. All four wheels still turned. Jack came over with his blue notebook and two pencils. Gus brought his green cap, although he had no intention of taking it off.

They spread Rex's old cardboard play carton flat across the rug. The carton had come from one of Dan's earlier deliveries. It had been folded away behind the rug for just such a day.

"Here is Brook Road," Jack said, drawing a line across the cardboard.

Rex put a blue block near one end. "My front step."

Gus put a red block at the other end. "Garage."

"That's Ms. Pam's red door," Jack said. "Pine Park goes farther along the road."

They laid a green towel beside the cardboard for the park. A pinecone became the tall pine tree. Three small stones formed Pancake Hill.

Gus regarded the stones. "Too small."

He added one more.

"Now it is a proper pancake."

Jack drew the brook beside the park. He set two wooden blocks a short distance apart and placed a flat craft board across them.

"Little Bridge," he said.

On the other side went a red toy barn.

"Molly's house!" Rex cried.

"Molly lives at the farm," Jack said. "The barn is for the farm animals and machines."

"Then I shall build Molly a cushion," said Gus.

He put a little square of cloth by the barn, and Rex decided that was right.

Now Gus picked up Rex's dump truck.

"The truck is delivering a very important load of pebbles. To the farm!"

He filled the tiny bed with three chunky wooden cubes from Rex's block box. The dump truck rolled along the cardboard road. Rrrr, rrrr.

Gus steered toward the little craft-board bridge.

"Stop!" Jack said.

Gus froze with one paw over the toy truck.

"Why?" he asked.

"That's our walking bridge. Dan's big truck can't go over it."

"But this truck is little," said Gus.

"This one can pretend. But if we're making a map of the real town, we need the road that the real trucks use."

Gus considered the problem. Then he picked up the bridge and set it on top of his head.

"There," he said. "Now I'm a bear with a bridge hat. No truck can cross my head."

"You already have a cap," Rex pointed out.

"Two hats are better than one when the town is busy."

Aunt Jo laughed from the table.

Jack found another long strip of cardboard. He started it by the garage, curved it around the outside of the rug, and brought it back toward the farm.

"The long farm road," he said.

"Does it go near the culvert?" Rex asked. He remembered the little tunnel where water passed under the road after the rain.

"Yes. We'll put that near this bend."

Gus rolled the dump truck all the way around. It took more turns, but the wheels stayed on a road the whole time.

At the end, he lifted the truck's little tipping bed. The three cubes tumbled out beside the red barn.

"Delivery!" he boomed in his best small-dump-truck voice.

Rex sniffed a cube.

"Not a biscuit," he said, disappointed.

"Good," Gus replied. "The pretend farm ordered cubes."

Jack drew a solid line beside the cardboard roadway and a dotted line beside the short path and bridge.

"Two ways," he said. "The roads can carry a truck. The path is for people and animals walking."

"Can my bear feet use the path?" Gus asked.

"Yes."

"Can my cap?"

"If it's on your head."

"Excellent. I was hoping you would say that."

They added small pictures for the other places they knew: two red doors for the firehouse on Station Lane, a blue truck for Dan's parcel shed, and a little pile of stones for Mr. Fred's gravel yard.

The town was becoming crowded. A toy wheel loader stood by the stones. Rex's dump truck waited near the farm. Gus borrowed a round block to stand for the road roller, and a bent spoon became an imaginary excavator.

Then Rex walked across the green towel to get a better view.

"Careful," Jack said, "you're standing in Pine Park."

"I know," Rex replied. "I'm the biggest dog in town."

He looked down at the blue block that was meant to be his house. It was smaller than his paw.

"I may need a bigger front step."

By afternoon the rain had stopped. They put the little buildings back in their boxes but kept the flattened cardboard map. Aunt Jo helped Jack roll it gently, without crumpling his lines.

"Tomorrow," she said, "we can show your idea to Mr. Lee. A real map would need to be clear enough for neighbors to use."

Rex parked his wooden dump truck by the rug. Gus set his green cap beside his bed.

The little town had fitted onto one piece of cardboard. The real town did not fit onto a rug at all, but Jack liked knowing that both had the same bridge, the same roads, and the same red barn at the far end.
