# A Bark from the Other Side

The first time Rex heard the other dog, he was sitting under the big pine tree in Pine Park.

He had just finished sniffing one end of the bench.

It was a good end. It smelled of pine needles and yesterday's rain.

Then, from somewhere beyond Pancake Hill, came a sound.

"Woof! Woof-woof!"

Rex lifted his dark ear. Then he lifted his light ear.

"That isn't me," he said.

"It isn't me either," said Gus, the little bear. "I've never been very good at woofing."

Gus tried.

"WOOF!"

A squirrel looked down from a branch and hurried away.

"See?" said Gus. "Far too loud."

Jack closed his blue notebook. "Let's find out who it is."

Aunt Jo walked with them along the park's curving path. They went past the pine tree, past Pancake Hill, and toward the grass at the far edge of the park.

Beyond the grass ran a narrow brook. It was not a lake and not a pond. It was a little ribbon of moving water.

On the other side stood a green gate, a red barn, and a small black-and-white dog with white front paws.

"Hello!" the dog called.

"HELLO!" Rex called back, much louder.

"I'm Molly!"

"I'm Rex!"

"I can hear that!" Molly said.

Rex wagged his tail. Molly wagged hers. The tails could not reach each other across the water, but they seemed to be having a fine conversation anyway.

A grown-up goat came to the green gate and waved.

"That's Farmer Ruth," Aunt Jo explained. "Molly lives with her at Green Gate Farm."

Behind the gate a hen pecked at the ground, and two woolly sheep stood beside the red barn.

"I would like to meet those sheep," said Gus.

"I would like to meet that dog," said Rex.

"I would like to know how to get there," said Jack.

"By the long farm road at the moment," said Aunt Jo. "But we're making a shorter walking way."

She pointed to a small sign beside a new blue work fence, well beyond the park's finished path.

A drawing on the sign showed two banks, a gap, and a little bridge.

"A bridge!" said Jack.

"For trucks?" asked Gus.

"For feet and paws," said Aunt Jo. "Big trucks will keep using the farm road."

A small yellow excavator sat inside the new fenced work area. Beside it, a grown-up beaver in work boots was putting bright markers into the ground.

"That's Mr. Lee," Aunt Jo said. "He helps build wooden things."

The excavator's driver lowered the bucket to dig a place for the bridge support. Its tracks stayed on the firm work ground. It was smaller than the great digger that had helped make Pine Park, but it still went:

Rrrrum. Scrunch. Lift.

Jack watched the arm. "It's another excavator, just a little one."

"A baby digger," said Gus.

"It isn't a baby," Rex said. "It has a grown-up driving it."

"Then a grown-up digger wearing very small shoes," Gus decided.

Molly called across the brook again.

"Are you coming over?"

"Not yet," Rex answered. "They're building the bridge."

"When it's ready," Molly said, "I'll show you my blue rope toy."

"And I'll show you how fast my tail goes!"

"I can already see that!"

Jack opened his notebook to a clean page. He drew the pine tree on one side, the green gate on the other, and the little brook between them.

He drew a line above the water, but made it dotted, not solid.

"The bridge isn't there yet," he said.

"Neither are we," said Gus. "We're on this side."

"Quite right," said Aunt Jo. "And it's time for lunch on this side."

They walked home along Brook Road.

That evening, Rex lay on his rug beside his wooden toy dump truck.

He listened to the house settle. He listened to Aunt Jo wash a cup in the kitchen.

Then he thought of a white-pawed dog at a green gate, and a bridge that would one day join two sides of a little brook.

His dark ear went flat. His light ear followed.

Somewhere across the water, Molly was probably getting sleepy too.
