# The Little Stones Come Home

Rex was waiting at the Pine Park gate when the green truck arrived.

He could hear it before he could see it.

Rrrrrrrrr.

"The stones are coming!" he called.

Gus pushed his green cap back to look. Jack opened his blue notebook to the drawing of the truck on the giant scale. Molly stood with Farmer Ruth on the walking path, her white front paws planted neatly beside Rex's.

They had all met to see what happened to the stones next.

The dump truck came along the northern service lane, stopped in the work area, and raised its bed to tip the stone out.

A gray little mound grew behind the truck.

"That's not a mountain," Gus said.

"No," said Jack. "It's not very big."

"It's a baby mountain," Gus decided. "It hasn't learned how to be tall yet."

Rex sniffed the air. The fresh stone smelled like damp earth and dust from far away.

"That smells like the stone yard."

Molly wagged once. "You remembered it with your nose."

The adult crew had kept the worn strip beside the park gate marked off. The rest of the walking path was open, but the friends stayed by the bench with Aunt Jo and Farmer Ruth while the workers did the repair.

A compact loader carried small bucketfuls from the new pile to the patch. One of the workers spread the mixture in an even layer.

"Do they need every stone to point the same way?" Jack asked.

"No," Aunt Jo said. "The mix contains little stones and finer pieces. They settle together as the crew spreads and compacts them."

Rex looked at the fresh surface.

"It still looks like a lot of pebbles."

"That's what paths can be made of," said Molly.

Then came a small roller, its metal drum round as an enormous rolling pin.

Gus stood up.

"I remember that machine!"

A small roller pressed the spread stone mix into a firm surface, just as a roller had helped make the original park path.

The roller made a slow pass. Then another. The loose mix settled into the repaired strip.

"It's pressing the baby mountain flat," Gus whispered.

"It won't grow up to be a big mountain now," Rex said.

"That's all right," said Gus. "It has found a different job."

When the adults finished, they checked the surface and cleared the work signs. Aunt Jo invited the friends to walk on the repaired little strip.

Rex took one step.

Then another.

His paws landed on a firm, level patch beside the familiar path.

Molly came next. They gave each other their one-wag greeting, though Rex was so delighted he nearly gave two.

Jack crouched to look at the tiny stones held together in the surface. The patch was not a pile anymore. It was part of the entrance.

"That's a lot of work for one little place," he said.

"It is," said Aunt Jo. "And the machines all had their own part."

At Pancake Hill, the children sat in the grass after the crew had packed up. The spring air smelled of new leaves. The little wooden bridge still crossed to the farm, and the tall pine watched over its bench.

Jack laid his notebook open.

On an old page: two heaps of gravel, a dump truck with its bed raised, and a small green cap that was much too little for the pile.

On the new pages: a quarry rock, the crusher, the shaking screen, a long conveyor, different stockpiles, the yellow loader, the giant scale, and the green truck.

He drew one last short arrow to the repaired patch by the gate.

"Now I know where those little stones came from," Jack said.

"A great big rock," Rex replied.

"Several big rocks," said Jack.

"A mountain of rocks," Gus offered.

Molly lay down in the grass. "And now they make a place for paws to walk."

Rex rested his chin beside hers. A faint rumble drifted down from the service lane as the last truck headed away.

Pancake Hill was still the same little hill. The tall pine was still the same tree. The park had one new patch made from old, old rock.

When Aunt Jo called everyone home, the friends rose slowly. Jack closed his notebook, and Rex took one final sniff of the firm little path.

The machines were going home too. Their work was finished, and evening was settling over Brook Road.
