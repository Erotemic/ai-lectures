# The Machine with the Stretching Arm

"It has enormous tires," said Gus.

"It has a yellow body," said Jack.

"It has a long nose," said Molly.

Rex stared at the machine parked beside the work area at Green Gate Farm.

"I don't think that is a nose."

The machine was yellow, with four thick tires and a glass cab. Out from its body reached a long metal arm. At the end were two sturdy forks.

Mr. Lee was standing beside Farmer Ruth on the far side of the barrier, checking the next part of the barn repair.

"That's a telehandler," he told the friends.

"A tell-a-what?" Gus asked.

"Telehandler," Jack repeated slowly.

"Can it handle the telephone?" asked Rex.

"Not that sort of handle," Mr. Lee said, laughing.

"Then why is it called tele?" Gus wanted to know.

"Because its arm can reach out a long way. Tele means far. Watch the part that stretches."

A telehandler has an arm called a boom that can reach forward and upward.

The three Brook Road friends had come with Aunt Jo. Molly watched with Farmer Ruth from the farm porch, which was outside the marked work zone. A pair of hens wandered past the fence and stopped as if they had bought tickets to a very important show.

The machine operator climbed into the cab. Other workers checked the work area and made sure the path was clear.

There was a pallet of new roof panels bundled together near the adult work platform. The crew had already put the replacement wooden shelter frame in place and braced it. The materials needed to go up to the working level, but the shelter itself was not a driveway for a big truck.

"Could the truck drive right under the roof?" Gus asked.

"It isn't going to," Aunt Jo said. "It can reach from where it stands."

The forks moved beneath the pallet. The machine lifted it clear. Then the arm began to rise.

Higher.

And a little farther.

Rex's ears rose with it, one dark and one light.

"My ears are stretching," he whispered.

"Your ears are the same size," Molly said.

"They feel taller."

The forks carried the secured pallet of roof panels toward the place where the builders needed them.

The operator made careful, small movements, guided by the adult crew. Nobody stood underneath the raised pallet. When the pallet reached the working platform, the crew waited until it was set down before going near it.

"The tires didn't travel up there," Jack said.

"Just the arm reached farther."

"Like someone stretching across the table for the last apple slice," Gus said.

"Without putting their feet on the table," said Aunt Jo.

"I would never put my feet on the table," Gus replied.

Rex remembered the apple experiment at Jack's house. Gus had nearly put his nose into the apple bowl, which was not exactly the same thing.

Mr. Lee checked the next pieces with his crew. Soon the telehandler drew back its arm and lowered its empty forks.

The work area looked different now. The wooden posts and sloping frame of the little side shelter were standing where the old worn pieces had been. Up at the working level waited a neat bundle of panels.

"It looks like the barn has grown a little shoulder," Molly said.

"A dry shoulder," Farmer Ruth replied. "Once the roof is properly fastened and finished."

One hen clucked loudly.

"She's giving the machine a review," said Rex.

"What did she say?" asked Gus.

"She says the yellow thing is not a chicken," Molly translated.

"Very fair," Jack said.

The operator parked the telehandler, with its forks resting down and its arm tucked in. The adults checked what had been delivered. The day was not finished, but the lifting part was.

Later the friends sat under the farm's porch roof with cups of water and slices of pear. Farmer Ruth brought Molly's blue rope toy out of the way of the grown-up work.

Jack drew two pictures. In the first, the telehandler's arm was short. In the second, it had stretched farther out to reach the platform.

Gus examined the pictures.

"I could use one of those to fetch my hat off a high shelf."

"You could use a chair and a grown-up," said Aunt Jo.

"Or," Rex said, "you could put your hat somewhere you can reach."

Gus pulled his cap down around his ears.

"I think that is the best machine of all," he said.

The afternoon grew still. Beyond the porch, the red barn stood with its new little frame, waiting quietly for the roof that would soon cover it.
