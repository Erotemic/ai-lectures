# Jack's Bridge for an Acorn

For several days, there was no crane beside the brook and no bridge to walk on.

The grown-ups were giving the new concrete supports the time they needed.

Jack kept looking at the dotted line in his blue notebook.

"I wonder what holds up a bridge," he said.

"A very strong duck," suggested Gus.

"I've met ducks," said Rex. "They're smaller than you think."

Jack put his notebook on Rex's kitchen table.

"Maybe we can make a little bridge and see."

Aunt Jo gave them two wooden blocks, two sheets of paper, and a little acorn from the yard.

Rex brought his wooden toy dump truck, although he did not know whether it would fit.

"What are the blocks?" Gus asked.

"The banks," said Jack.

He put one on the left side of a blue towel and one on the right.

"What's the towel?" Rex asked.

"The brook."

Gus looked closely.

"That's the tidiest brook I've ever seen. Not one muddy puddle."

Jack laid a flat sheet of paper across the two blocks.

"That's a bridge," he said.

Rex gave the paper an interested sniff.

"Smells like paper."

"That part is right," said Jack.

Jack carefully set the acorn in the middle.

The paper bent downward.

The acorn rolled a little, then slipped off into the blue towel.

Plink.

"The acorn has gone swimming!" cried Gus.

"It's a towel," said Rex.

"It's a very dry swim," Gus agreed.

Jack picked up the acorn.

"The paper bent. But I wonder if it always has to."

"Can we use a bigger block?" asked Rex.

"We can try that later," said Jack. "First I want to try the other paper."

Gus folded the second sheet back and forth with Jack, making small ridges all along it.

Zig. Zag. Zig. Zag.

When they set the folded sheet between the same two blocks, it looked like a tiny row of rooftops.

Jack placed the same acorn in the middle.

It stayed there.

Rex waited for the splash.

Nothing splashed.

He waited a little longer.

"The acorn isn't going swimming," he said.

Gus peeked underneath.

"Maybe the roof ridges are holding it up."

"It bends less now," Jack said. "We changed the shape."

They tried one more acorn, very carefully. The folded paper held both for a moment.

"Two passengers!" Gus announced. "Acorn Express is leaving the station."

Rex pushed his big wooden dump truck toward the bridge.

"What about this passenger?"

Jack looked at the heavy wooden truck. Then at the thin paper.

"That one's too big for our little test," he said. "We'd need a stronger bridge."

So they made a truck bridge out of wide wooden blocks instead.

Rex rolled his dump truck across it with great importance.

"Beep," he said.

Gus sat beside the towel and wore his green cap very straight.

"Welcome to the bridge," he announced. "No acorns in the water."

After lunch, they walked with Aunt Jo to Pine Park. The path still curved around Pancake Hill, and the bench was still under the same old pine tree.

Farther away, beyond the work fence, the new supports stood at the brook. The builders were checking their work. There was still no wooden walkway above them.

Across the water, Molly sat on Farmer Ruth's porch with a blue rope toy beside her paws.

"What did you do today?" she called.

"Built a bridge for two acorns!" Rex called back.

"Did it work?"

"The second one did!"

"Can a puppy cross it?"

"Only if the puppy is the size of an acorn," said Gus.

Molly looked at Rex. Rex looked at Molly.

"We're going to need the real bridge," Rex admitted.

That night Jack opened his notebook again.

He drew one flat paper bridge with a little bend. Beside it he drew a paper bridge with zigzag folds and two acorns on top.

He didn't write that all bridges had to look like paper. He just drew what he had seen.

Outside, the brook kept moving between its two banks.

Inside, the little paper bridge sat quietly on the table, holding one last acorn until morning.
