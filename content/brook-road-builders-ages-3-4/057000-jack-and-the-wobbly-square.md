# Jack and the Wobbly Square

Jack had a question that would not stay in his notebook.

It kept hopping out between the pages.

"Why did Mr. Lee order a board that goes sideways?"

He had drawn the side of the barn shelter. There were upright posts. There was a top beam. And between two corners he had drawn a board going diagonally.

"Perhaps the board is lost," Rex said.

"Boards don't get lost," Jack answered.

Gus, who had once lost his green cap while it was still on his head, decided not to comment.

They were at Jack's kitchen table. Aunt Jo had brought over a bowl of sliced apples and a handful of short craft sticks with soft connectors for a little experiment.

"Mr. Lee said the sloping board helps keep the shelter steady," Jack said. "I thought straight up-and-down boards would be enough."

"I thought apples would be enough," Gus said, taking another slice.

Jack put four sticks on the table. With Aunt Jo's help he joined their ends loosely to make a square.

"Four sides," he said.

"One door for an invisible sheep," said Gus.

He peered through the empty middle. "Hello, sheep. Do wipe your hooves."

Rex sniffed the square. It smelled like craft wood and nothing like sheep.

Jack pressed one top corner very gently.

The square leaned to one side.

"Oh," he said.

It was no longer very square.

The corners could move, so the square leaned sideways and turned into a slanty shape.

"Now it's a sleepy square," Gus said. "It's lying down."

Jack pushed it back. It leaned the other way.

"Why didn't the sticks stay where they were?" Molly asked. Farmer Ruth had brought her from the farm for the afternoon, and Molly was sitting close to Rex with her white front paws tucked neatly together.

"Because these little joints move," Jack replied. "I made them loose on purpose. I thought that wouldn't matter, but it does."

He examined the drawing in his blue notebook again. The sloping board crossed from one corner to another.

"Let's try that part."

Aunt Jo handed him a fifth craft stick. Jack put it across the square, corner to corner, and they fixed it with the model connectors.

He pressed gently on the top corner again.

This time the shape resisted.

With a diagonal brace across it, the little frame was harder to push out of shape.

"It has a stripe," Rex said.

"It has two little triangles," Jack said.

Gus looked closely. "The invisible sheep has moved into a triangle house."

"Does the sheep like it?" Molly asked.

"Very much. The front door is extremely pointy."

Jack laughed. "The stick across the middle changes how the frame moves. Mr. Lee uses real lumber and carefully chosen joints for the actual shelter. This is just our little model."

"I am glad," said Rex. "I don't think the real roof should be held together with things from Jack's kitchen table."

They built another square with the same four loose joints. Gus made that one lean so far it looked like a walking window.

"It's going to visit the apple bowl," he said.

"Not through the apples," said Aunt Jo.

Jack tried pushing both shapes again. The loose one changed shape easily. The braced one did not change as much.

He drew them side by side in his notebook. Under the first he wrote WOBBLY. Under the second he drew the extra sloping stick.

He left room to add what Mr. Lee built at the farm.

That afternoon the four friends walked with the adults to Green Gate Farm by the familiar footpath and Little Bridge. Mr. Lee had prepared the new wood frame pieces in the adult work area. The old shelter was still closed, and the main red barn still held the dry hay and the tractor.

Jack looked at the ready timber through the fence.

One piece went straight.

One piece went up.

And one piece waited to go slanting across.

Gus waved to the empty space beneath the unfinished shelter.

"Good evening, invisible sheep!" he called.

Farmer Ruth's real sheep looked up from the pasture.

The real sheep, at least, knew exactly where their home was.

By bedtime Jack's two little frames rested on the kitchen shelf. He had put his notebook beside them, open to the page with the crooked square and the straight one.

He did not need to solve the whole barn tonight.

He had answered one small question, and there would be more to see tomorrow.
