# Operator algebra, adjoints, spectra, and Fredholm alternatives

## Banach and C-Star Algebras: Where Functional Calculus Lives

What do we mean when we ask for a function of an operator? For a number, taking its square root is familiar. For a diagonal matrix, taking its square root means applying the square-root function to each diagonal entry. But the very same question makes sense for an operator acting on infinitely many coordinates. We want the answer not to depend on guessing eigenvectors. We want to know what kind of functions are allowed, and how to recognize the result.

An algebra is a space where we can add and multiply elements. An ordinary function algebra gives the clearest picture: add two continuous functions point by point, or multiply their values at each point. Suppose the functions live on a closed interval. We can measure their size by the largest absolute value they attain. This maximum norm makes the continuous functions into a complete space: if a sequence of functions becomes uniformly closer together, its limit remains continuous. It is both an algebra and a Banach space. We call it a Banach algebra because multiplication behaves well under its norm.

Matrices offer another Banach algebra. The objects are matrices rather than functions, but matrix multiplication still makes sense, and the operator norm measures their effect on vectors. This example introduces an important complication: matrix multiplication is not generally commutative. The order of two matrices matters. Nevertheless a single matrix and its powers commute with each other. That is why polynomials of one matrix are a natural starting point for functional calculus.

Take a two-by-two diagonal matrix with entries two and five. Its square has entries four and twenty-five. If we want to apply a continuous function, say the square root, the desired result has diagonal entries square root of two and square root of five. For this normal matrix, the construction is exactly what the continuous functional calculus formalizes. Replacing the finite list of diagonal entries with a whole spectrum is the main conceptual change.

Now introduce the adjoint, the operator analogue of conjugate transpose. An algebra equipped with an adjoint and a compatible complete norm can satisfy the C-star identity: the size of the product of an element and its adjoint is the square of the size of that element. For bounded operators on a Hilbert space, this identity follows from inner-product geometry. This extra structure is what makes the theory especially powerful.

A self-adjoint operator equals its own adjoint. A normal operator commutes with its adjoint. The small algebra generated by a normal operator, its adjoint and the identity is commutative, even if the surrounding operator algebra is not. The Gelfand representation tells us that a commutative unital C-star algebra can be treated, without loss of norm information, as continuous functions on a compact space. In the algebra generated by one normal operator, that compact space can be identified with its spectrum. In other words, working with a normal operator is, in a precise sense, working with the function that assigns each spectral point its own value.

This gives a reliable answer to our opening question. We evaluate a continuous function of a normal operator by first evaluating that function on the spectrum, then carrying it back through the representation. The identity function becomes the original operator. A constant function becomes a multiple of the identity. Products of functions correspond to products of their operators. And the operator norm is exactly the largest size of the function on the spectrum.

There are limits to this pleasant picture. For a nonnormal matrix, a continuous function defined only on its eigenvalues does not specify a useful calculus with the same norm control. For an unbounded self-adjoint operator, the spectral measure allows more functions, but their operator domains must be handled separately. A holomorphic calculus for a bounded operator works in greater generality because analytic functions can be recovered from resolvent integrals. Those are related, but different constructions. A responsible calculation begins by checking the type of operator and the regularity of the proposed function.

The practical lesson is that functional calculus is not a collection of notational tricks. It is a consequence of algebra, topology, and the geometry of the adjoint working together. The basic examples—continuous functions, matrices, and bounded operators—show why the abstract machinery is worth building.

## Adjoints: Moving an Operator Across an Inner Product

Imagine a microphone that records two numbers: the left-channel signal and the right-channel signal. A mixing device outputs their sum in one channel and their difference in another. It is a linear transformation, so a matrix describes it. But suppose that instead of asking what the device outputs, you ask what combination of input signals is being measured when someone listens to a particular output channel. That is the question answered by the adjoint.

A linear operator takes a vector to another vector. An inner product lets us measure how strongly two vectors align. If we apply an operator to the first vector and then take an inner product with the second, the adjoint tells us how to move that transformation across the inner product. For every input x and every test vector y, the inner product of T x with y equals the inner product of x with T adjoint y.

That sentence is worth hearing twice in different language. First transform the signal and then measure it against y. Or transform the measuring instrument backward by the adjoint and apply the resulting measurement directly to the original signal. The numerical result agrees exactly.

### What transposition misses

In ordinary real Euclidean space, the adjoint of a matrix is its transpose. The row of a matrix that computes one output coordinate becomes a column describing the corresponding input measurement. For a complex vector space there is another operation: complex conjugation. The adjoint is the conjugate transpose, because the complex inner product conjugates one of its arguments.

For example, multiplying a complex number by the imaginary unit rotates its plane by a quarter turn. The adjoint multiplies by the negative imaginary unit, undoing that rotation inside an inner product. An ordinary transpose would not record the reversal of complex phase.

In an infinite-dimensional Hilbert space we may have no finite matrix. Yet the same definition works, and a bounded operator has exactly one bounded adjoint. The reason is that, for fixed y, the rule assigning to x the inner product of T x with y is a continuous linear measurement. The representation theorem for Hilbert spaces says every such measurement comes from a unique vector. Call that vector T adjoint y. This is how the adjoint exists without coordinates.

### An example with an endless sequence

Take a square-summable sequence and shift every entry one place to the right, putting zero into the first position. The right-shift operator does not change the length of a sequence. It simply changes where its entries live.

What is its adjoint? It is the left shift: remove the first entry and slide the remaining entries toward the beginning. To check, pair a right-shifted sequence with a second sequence. The first coordinate contributes nothing, and every later coordinate pairs with the preceding input coordinate. That is exactly the pairing of the original input with the left-shifted test sequence.

This one example makes an important warning visible. The right shift preserves length, so T adjoint followed by T is the identity. But T followed by T adjoint is not the identity: it erases the first coordinate. An operator may preserve every input norm without being onto. Its adjoint records that asymmetry.

### Adjoint geometry of ranges and kernels

The kernel of an operator consists of inputs that disappear. The range consists of outputs that are actually attainable. The adjoint relates these two ideas through perpendicularity.

Suppose y is perpendicular to every output T x. Then every inner product of T x with y is zero. Move T across the pairing: every inner product of x with T adjoint y is zero. That forces T adjoint y to be zero. Conversely, if T adjoint y vanishes, y is perpendicular to every output.

So the vectors perpendicular to the range of T are exactly the kernel of T adjoint. This gives a geometric criterion for solvability. A proposed output must be perpendicular to every obstruction in the adjoint kernel if it is to lie in the closure of the range. The word closure matters: in an infinite-dimensional space, the range need not be closed, so satisfying every orthogonality test may mean an output can be approximated arbitrarily well without being attained exactly.

This distinction shows up in inverse problems. A measurement can be compatible with all linear constraints and still be attainable only as a limit of increasingly unstable approximate inputs.

### Products, projections, and norms

The adjoint reverses the order of a composition. If you apply T and then S, the adjoint first moves S backward and then T. It is the same reversal that happens when taking the transpose of a matrix product.

An orthogonal projection equals its own adjoint. That equality expresses the fact that projecting a vector onto a closed subspace is the same operation whether you examine how inputs act against test vectors or reverse the measurement. A projection along an oblique direction generally is not self-adjoint.

There is another formula we will need repeatedly. For every bounded T, the operator T adjoint T measures squared output lengths: its inner product with x on both sides equals the squared norm of T x. Consequently T adjoint T can never yield a negative quadratic measurement. That property is called positivity.

Moreover, the operator norm of T adjoint T is the square of the norm of T. One direction follows from the ordinary norm bound for compositions. The other follows by testing the inner product of T adjoint T with unit inputs whose transformed lengths approach the operator norm. This is the C-star identity, and it will eventually make functional calculus precise.

### The reason to learn adjoints first

It is tempting to define a function of an operator by writing a power series and hoping for the best. But for self-adjoint operators, we can do better. The adjoint gives us the notion of self-adjointness. Self-adjointness leads to real spectral values. Positivity leads to square roots and inequalities. Those structures make it possible to define a wide class of scalar functions of an operator and know that the result behaves correctly.

The adjoint is therefore not merely a way to reverse matrix arrows. It is the bridge from a transformation to the geometry of its measurements. It tells us what the transformation does to energy, orthogonality, solvability, and ultimately spectrum.

## Self-Adjoint and Positive Operators: The Geometry of Energy

Take a spring attached to a moving object. Its stored energy is proportional to the square of its displacement. If the spring acts in several directions, the energy can be a quadratic expression involving interactions between coordinates. The matrix in that expression should be symmetric: swapping the two input directions does not change the energy. In complex Hilbert spaces, the corresponding symmetry is self-adjointness.

An operator A is self-adjoint when A equals its adjoint. This means the inner product of A x with y equals the inner product of x with A y for all x and y. One immediate consequence is that the scalar inner product of A x with x is real. We can interpret it as a measurable quantity, even though it need not be positive.

An operator is positive when that quantity is nonnegative for every x. Positive operators are self-adjoint by definition in the standard complex Hilbert-space formulation. The phrase positive here does not mean that the entries of a matrix are positive. It means that every vector produces a nonnegative quadratic measurement.

### Rotate to the revealing coordinates

Consider the two-by-two real matrix with two on the diagonal and one off the diagonal. Apply it to the vector whose entries are both one. The output is three times the input. Apply it to the vector whose entries are one and negative one. The output is the input itself. These two perpendicular directions are eigenvectors, with eigenvalues three and one.

In the coordinates formed by those directions, the matrix is diagonal. Its energy on a vector with coordinates a and b is three a squared plus b squared. It cannot be negative, even though the original off-diagonal entries couple the two coordinates.

This is the simplest form of the finite-dimensional spectral theorem: every self-adjoint matrix admits an orthonormal basis of eigenvectors, and all its eigenvalues are real. The positivity of such a matrix is equivalent to nonnegativity of those eigenvalues.

Why are eigenvalues real? If A x equals lambda x for a nonzero vector, then the inner product of A x with x equals lambda times the squared norm of x. Self-adjointness makes the left side real, so lambda must be real. Why are different eigenspaces perpendicular? Evaluate the same mixed inner product in two ways, moving A from one slot to the other. Distinct real eigenvalues force the inner product to vanish.

Those short arguments explain why diagonalization is especially clean for self-adjoint matrices. General matrices may have complex eigenvalues, nonorthogonal eigenvectors, or too few eigenvectors to form a basis.

### A positive operator need not look positive

Let T be any bounded linear operator on a Hilbert space. Form T adjoint T. For any x, the quadratic measurement associated with this product is exactly the squared length of T x. It is never negative. Thus T adjoint T is positive.

This construction appears whenever a measurement procedure has an associated least-squares error. If T maps an unknown signal to predicted observations, the quantity measuring squared prediction length is governed by T adjoint T. Its positivity is built into the geometry, not into the numerical signs of a chosen matrix.

Orthogonal projections give another example. Project onto a closed subspace and then compare the result with the original vector. The quadratic measurement of the projection is the squared length of the projected component. Every orthogonal projection is positive and self-adjoint. Its only possible eigenvalues in finite dimensions are zero and one.

But a self-adjoint operator need not be positive. A reflection across a line has one eigenvalue equal to one and another equal to negative one. It preserves lengths and is self-adjoint, yet its quadratic measurement can be negative. Self-adjointness expresses symmetry; positivity adds an ordering condition.

### Positive square roots

For a positive matrix, computing a square root becomes natural after diagonalization. Take its nonnegative eigenvalues, replace each by its ordinary nonnegative square root, and retain the same eigenvectors. The resulting matrix B is positive and satisfies B composed with B equals A.

For our example with eigenvalues three and one, the positive square root has eigenvalues square root of three and one. There is no need to guess entries and solve a nonlinear system of matrix equations. The spectral coordinates turn the construction into scalar arithmetic.

Among positive square roots the answer is unique. There may be other square roots that are not positive, because signs can be changed on eigenspaces. The positivity condition selects the natural one.

In infinite dimensions, a positive bounded self-adjoint operator also has a unique positive bounded square root. Proving this in general will require a more flexible way to apply scalar functions to operators. The familiar matrix construction tells us what that larger theory must recover.

### Ordering operators by their energy

If A and B are self-adjoint, we say A is no greater than B when B minus A is positive. This means that the quadratic measurement of A never exceeds that of B on any vector.

For positive operators, the order is about all vectors at once. Looking at matrix entries does not tell the whole story. Even multiplying positive operators together need not produce a positive operator if they fail to commute. To see the issue, remember that a positive operator must be self-adjoint. The product AB has adjoint BA, and those may differ.

This noncommutativity is one reason functional calculus is subtle. A function of a single self-adjoint operator behaves like ordinary scalar algebra, because all polynomial expressions in that operator commute with one another. Functions of two different operators are a different problem.

### A multiplication operator with no eigenvectors

Return to the Hilbert space of square-integrable functions on the unit interval. Define A to multiply a function by its position t. The quadratic measurement is the integral of t times the squared magnitude of the function. Since t lies between zero and one, A is positive. It is also self-adjoint.

Yet it has no nonzero eigenvectors. If multiplying by t always gave a fixed scalar lambda times the original function, then the function could be nonzero only where t equals lambda. A single point has zero measure, so the resulting square-integrable vector would be zero.

The operator is positive, bounded, and self-adjoint, but it has no eigenbasis. Nevertheless we know what its positive square root should do: multiply by the ordinary square root of t. This example tells us that applying functions to operators must eventually work without diagonalizing into eigenvectors.

That is the transition from matrix spectral theory to functional calculus. The goal is not to abandon the familiar diagonal picture; it is to preserve exactly what made the picture useful when no list of eigenvectors exists.

## Spectra and Resolvents: What If an Operator Cannot Be Inverted?

Suppose a matrix has diagonal entries one and three. If you try to invert the matrix after subtracting one times the identity, the first diagonal entry becomes zero, and inversion fails. If you subtract three, the second entry becomes zero. At any other complex number, both diagonal entries remain nonzero and inversion works.

The values one and three are eigenvalues. But a more general question underlies the calculation: for which numbers z does A minus z times the identity have a bounded inverse defined on the entire space? The set where the answer is no is called the spectrum. For finite matrices, it is exactly the set of eigenvalues. For infinite-dimensional operators, that equality can fail.

### The resolvent is an inverse with a parameter

When A minus z times the identity has a bounded inverse, that inverse is called a resolvent operator. Thinking of z as a tunable parameter turns inversion into a family of problems. We can inspect where the inverse exists and how large its norm becomes as z approaches the spectrum.

For our diagonal matrix the inverse is also diagonal. Its entries are one divided by one minus z and one divided by three minus z. When z approaches one, the first factor becomes large. The inverse does not suddenly misbehave at a mysterious point: its failure is anticipated by increasingly severe amplification nearby.

If A is self-adjoint, the spectrum is real. For a complex parameter with a nonzero imaginary part, the inverse exists, and its norm is bounded by the reciprocal of the magnitude of that imaginary part. This estimate is a fundamental safeguard when working with self-adjoint differential operators.

### An operator with spectrum but no eigenvalues

Now use the space of square-integrable functions on the interval from zero to one. Let A multiply any function by the coordinate t.

To solve the equation A minus z times the identity, applied to f, equals g, the formal answer is to divide g by t minus z. If z lies outside the interval, that denominator stays safely away from zero. Division is bounded on L two. So z is outside the spectrum.

If z lies inside the interval, the denominator becomes arbitrarily small near the point t equals z. Dividing can amplify functions supported near that point by as much as we wish. There is no bounded inverse.

Thus the spectrum is the entire interval from zero to one. But this operator has no nonzero eigenvectors. An eigenvector associated with a value z would need to be supported only where t equals z, which is a set of measure zero.

This is the first decisive lesson: spectral values need not appear as eigenvalues. They can instead describe positions where an operator is arbitrarily close to failing an eigenvector equation on carefully chosen unit inputs.

### Approximate eigenvectors

Fix a number lambda between zero and one. Choose a small interval around lambda and build a function supported there, normalized so its L-two norm is one. On that support, t lies close to lambda. Therefore A applied to the function is close, in L-two norm, to lambda times the function.

As the support shrinks toward lambda, the discrepancy tends to zero. These functions are approximate eigenvectors. They do not converge to a genuine nonzero eigenvector; their shapes concentrate more and more tightly. Each one shows that the operator almost acts like multiplication by lambda on a particular unit vector.

For a bounded self-adjoint operator, every spectral value has such approximate eigenvectors. This fact replaces the finite-dimensional picture in which spectral values are always associated with actual eigenvectors.

There is another kind of infinite-dimensional spectral failure for general, non-self-adjoint operators. An operator can be one-to-one and bounded below while failing to be onto. The unilateral right shift is an example at the parameter zero. It has no nonzero kernel, but its range misses vectors with nonzero first coordinate. Its spectrum therefore contains zero even though zero is not an eigenvalue. Later, when we restrict ourselves to self-adjoint operators, the relationship between spectrum and approximate eigenvectors becomes cleaner.

### Why the distance to spectrum controls the inverse

For the multiplication operator, the inverse at a point z outside the interval is multiplication by one divided by t minus z. Its operator norm is the largest magnitude of that multiplier, interpreted in the essential-supremum sense. This is precisely one divided by the distance from z to the interval.

The same equality holds for any bounded normal operator, including every bounded self-adjoint one: the resolvent norm equals the reciprocal of the distance from z to its spectrum. This is an exceptionally rigid law. It says that the worst possible amplification of an attempted inverse can be read off directly from geometric distance in the complex plane.

Do not use that equality blindly for every matrix. A non-normal matrix can have a very large resolvent even when z is not particularly close to an eigenvalue. Such sensitivity underlies pseudospectra and transient growth in differential equations.

### A function of the operator should respect its spectrum

Take a polynomial p. We already know how to form p of A: replace the scalar variable by A, and use operator composition. For a self-adjoint A, its spectrum passes through the polynomial. In other words, the spectrum of p of A is the collection of values p takes on the spectrum of A.

For the multiplication operator, this is intuitive. If p is the square function, then p of A multiplies by t squared, and its spectrum ranges from zero to one. If p is t squared plus two, the spectrum ranges from two to three.

This is called spectral mapping. Functional calculus extends this relationship to more general functions. The spectrum supplies the domain on which those functions matter. If two scalar functions agree on the spectrum, they should describe the same function of A, even if they differ elsewhere.

The spectrum is therefore not just a list of special frequencies. It is the correct collection of scalar values against which we test an operator and through which we apply scalar functions to it. Eigenvalues are one way that spectral values show themselves, but they are not the whole story.

## Matrix Functions: Turning Scalar Formulas into Operator Formulas

You know how to square a matrix: multiply it by itself. You also know how to add a multiple of the identity. But what does it mean to take the exponential of a matrix? Or to take its square root? Or to evaluate a function that has no simple polynomial expression?

For a self-adjoint matrix, the answer starts with one geometric idea. Find a set of perpendicular eigenvector directions. In each of those directions, the matrix acts by multiplication by an ordinary real number. A scalar function can then act on that number. Finally, put the directions back together.

This is the finite-dimensional functional calculus. It is not merely a notation for manipulating matrix formulas. It turns a collection of scalar instructions into a linear transformation with well-defined action on every vector.

### Follow a vector through its spectral pieces

Consider a matrix that stretches one diagonal direction by a factor of three and the perpendicular diagonal direction by a factor of one. We encountered a concrete example: a two-by-two matrix with twos on the diagonal and ones off the diagonal.

Any vector in the plane decomposes uniquely into a piece along the first direction and a piece along the second. Applying the matrix multiplies the first piece by three and the second piece by one. Applying the matrix twice multiplies them by nine and one. The same decomposition works for every polynomial in the matrix.

Now replace the square function by the square-root function. We simply multiply the first component by the square root of three and leave the second component unchanged. This defines a positive square root of the matrix without ever guessing its entries.

For the exponential, multiply the two components by e cubed and e to the first power. For the reciprocal, multiply them by one third and one, provided no eigenvalue is zero. The rules are scalar once we have the perpendicular decomposition.

### Spectral projections as switches

We can make that construction coordinate free. Let P one be the orthogonal projection onto the eigenspace of eigenvalue three, and P two the projection onto the eigenspace of eigenvalue one. Then the original operator is three times P one plus one times P two.

A function f of the operator is f of three times P one plus f of one times P two. The projections behave like switches selecting independent frequency channels. They add up to the identity, and projecting onto one channel after another gives zero.

This formula explains why the same eigenvectors work for every function of the matrix. The function changes only the scalar multiplier assigned to each spectral channel. It does not change the spectral directions themselves.

A scalar function only needs to be defined at the eigenvalues. If two functions disagree everywhere except at one and three, but agree at those two points, they define the same function of this matrix. That is the finite version of saying that the spectrum is the correct domain for functional calculus.

### Compute an inverse without a symbolic trick

Let us compute a more interesting function. Take f of t to be one divided by t plus one. For our matrix with eigenvalues three and one, the spectral multipliers are one fourth and one half.

Reassemble the operator from the two projections. The resulting matrix has three eighths on the diagonal and negative one eighth off the diagonal. If you multiply it by the original matrix plus the identity, you obtain the identity exactly.

So f of A really is the inverse of A plus I. This may seem obvious from notation, but the spectral construction tells us why it is true and when it can fail. If the scalar denominator vanished at an eigenvalue, there would be no bounded inverse. The functional formula would not rescue an impossible division.

### Two kinds of exponential

An exponential of a self-adjoint matrix is positive and invertible, since its eigenvalue multipliers are positive. An exponential of i times a self-adjoint matrix behaves differently: each eigenvalue multiplier lies on the unit circle in the complex plane. Its magnitude is one, so the operator preserves lengths. In other words, it is unitary.

This contrast is central in applications. Exponentials with negative real spectral multipliers can damp signals. Exponentials with imaginary spectral multipliers can rotate their phases without losing energy. These two types of evolution will later reappear as the heat equation and the Schrödinger equation.

A different warning: the matrix exponential of A plus B does not generally equal the product of the matrix exponentials of A and B. For ordinary numbers it does. For matrices it requires commuting operators, or additional special structure. The obstacle is not the exponential function; it is noncommutativity between distinct operators.

### What if a matrix is not self-adjoint?

For a diagonalizable but non-self-adjoint matrix, we can still apply a scalar function on its eigenvalues by using an invertible eigenvector matrix and conjugating a diagonal matrix back. But those eigenvectors need not be perpendicular. The resulting numerical behavior may be sensitive to small perturbations.

For a matrix with a Jordan block, knowing values of f at the eigenvalues is no longer enough. Derivatives of f may be required. Consider a two-by-two matrix with a single eigenvalue zero and a one just above the diagonal. Its square is zero. The exponential is therefore exactly the identity plus the matrix. More generally an analytic function on this Jordan block depends on its value and first derivative at zero.

So functional calculus has different versions with different assumptions. The self-adjoint version treats very general continuous functions because perpendicular spectral geometry keeps everything controlled. For arbitrary matrices, analytic functions give a powerful alternative.

### The infinite-dimensional challenge

For a finite self-adjoint matrix, the spectral pieces are finitely many, and everything is built by addition. What changes in infinite dimensions? There may be infinitely many eigenvectors; an eigenvalue list may have limit points; and some self-adjoint operators have no eigenvectors at all.

But the matrix construction gives us a specification to preserve. Functions of an operator should respect sums and products of functions. Real functions should give self-adjoint outputs. Nonnegative functions should give positive outputs. The output should depend only on the function's values on the spectrum. And approximation of scalar functions should produce controlled approximation of operators.

These requirements will guide a construction that works even when the spectral pieces cannot be numbered one by one. The finite matrix formula is not discarded. It becomes the model for a much broader theorem.

## Compact Operators and Continuous Spectrum: Two Ways Infinity Enters

There are two very different ways an infinite-dimensional operator can fail to behave like a finite matrix. It can have infinitely many eigenvectors with eigenvalues accumulating toward zero. Or it can have spectral values without a single nonzero eigenvector. Functional calculus must handle both.

The first situation is close to diagonalization. The second forces us to replace lists of eigenvectors by measures and spectral projections. Understanding the difference makes the general spectral theorem less mysterious.

### What compact means for an operator

In a finite-dimensional space, a bounded set has compact closure. In an infinite-dimensional Hilbert space, a bounded set can spread in infinitely many independent directions and have no convergent subsequence.

An operator T is called compact if it sends every bounded sequence to a sequence with a convergent subsequence. In particular, the image of the unit ball has compact closure. A compact operator cannot transport infinitely many orthogonal unit directions to outputs whose sizes all stay bounded away from zero; that would create a sequence of well-separated outputs with no convergent subsequence.

Finite-rank operators are compact. They send every vector into a fixed finite-dimensional space, where the ordinary finite-dimensional compactness principle applies. Norm limits of finite-rank operators are compact as well.

An example of a compact infinite-dimensional operator is diagonal multiplication on square-summable sequences by one, one half, one third, one fourth, and so on. Each coordinate is attenuated, with higher coordinates attenuated more strongly. Truncating after the first N coordinates gives a finite-rank operator, and the error in operator norm is one divided by N plus one. That error tends to zero, proving compactness.

### The compact self-adjoint spectral theorem

The diagonal example has a complete orthonormal collection of eigenvectors, one for each coordinate. The associated eigenvalues approach zero. Zero belongs to the spectrum because the inverse would need to multiply the N-th coordinate by N, which is not bounded. Yet zero is not an eigenvalue: no nonzero sequence is annihilated by the diagonal operator.

This teaches us to distinguish a spectral limit point from an eigenvalue. A compact self-adjoint operator on a Hilbert space has a particularly useful spectral theorem: its nonzero spectrum consists of eigenvalues of finite multiplicity, with no possible accumulation point other than zero. Its nonzero eigenspaces are mutually perpendicular, and together with the kernel they span the Hilbert space.

Such an operator therefore behaves like an infinite diagonal matrix, at least after choosing the right orthonormal coordinates. We can apply scalar functions by modifying the diagonal multipliers, taking care of the limit at zero.

Notice why the word compact is doing essential work. The theorem does not say that every bounded self-adjoint operator has an eigenbasis. The next example disproves that claim.

### Multiplication on an interval

Let A act on square-integrable functions between zero and one by multiplying a function at position t by t. A is bounded, self-adjoint, and positive. It has no eigenvectors, because an eigenfunction would have to live on one point of the interval, a set of measure zero.

It is also not compact. To see why, choose infinitely many disjoint small intervals within the half of the domain between one half and one. Put a normalized indicator function on each interval. These input functions have unit norm and are mutually perpendicular. After multiplying by t, their supports remain disjoint and their output norms remain at least one half. No subsequence of the outputs can converge, because every pair is separated by a definite distance.

Here the spectrum is the entire interval from zero to one. It is not a collection of isolated eigenvalues converging to zero. It is a continuous range of approximate eigenvalues, even though the operator has no actual eigenvectors.

### Why both examples are important

One might respond by saying that continuous spectrum is just a defect of our notation. Could we choose a different kind of basis? The useful answer is yes, but not by pretending that point masses are ordinary L-two eigenvectors. We can instead describe the operator as multiplication by a measurable function, sometimes after a unitary change of representation. In that description, scalar functional calculus is simply multiplication by a scalar function composed with that measurable multiplier.

The compact diagonal example is multiplication by a function on a discrete measure space. The interval example is multiplication by a function on a continuous measure space. The same general principle handles both: each spectral region carries some component of the vector, and a scalar function changes the multiplier on that region.

This is the conceptual content of the spectral theorem for bounded self-adjoint operators. The theorem can be presented as a multiplication-operator representation or as a projection-valued measure. The two formulations encode the same spectral action in different languages.

### The distinction from ordinary approximation

Compactness is not merely a statement that an operator can be computed accurately on every fixed input. Recall projections that keep the first N coordinates of a sequence. These projections converge strongly to the identity, meaning they approximate every fixed vector. But they do not converge to the identity in operator norm, because a unit vector can always sit entirely in a coordinate beyond N.

The identity operator on an infinite-dimensional Hilbert space is not compact. Its unit-ball image is still the whole unit ball. Thus a sequence of compact operators can converge strongly to a noncompact operator; compactness is preserved by operator-norm limits, not by arbitrary strong limits.

These distinctions between norm convergence, strong convergence, and spectral structure appear again in functional calculus. Uniform approximation of scalar functions will produce operator-norm approximation. More general measurable approximations often produce only strong convergence. We should not confuse the two.

### The lesson about infinity

Finite-dimensional spectral theory asks for eigenvalues and eigenvectors. Infinite-dimensional spectral theory asks a broader question: how is a vector's squared magnitude distributed across spectral values, and what happens when we multiply that distribution by a scalar function?

Compact self-adjoint operators supply the reassuring case where an orthonormal eigenbasis still works. Multiplication on an interval supplies the reason a full spectral calculus is necessary. A satisfactory theory must embrace both without changing its basic rules.

## Compact Operators, Fredholm Alternatives, and Integral Equations

A system of one thousand equations can be solved by matrix elimination, at least in principle. A system with infinitely many unknowns is different. Even a linear equation that seems perfectly harmless can have an inverse that amplifies small errors without bound. Compact operators occupy a middle ground: they act on infinite-dimensional spaces, yet they preserve enough of the finite-dimensional picture to recover a useful existence theory.

A compact operator is one that sends the unit ball into a set whose closure is compact. That means every bounded sequence of inputs has a subsequence whose outputs converge. Notice the asymmetry: the input sequence itself need not have any convergent subsequence. The operator is doing the compression.

Consider a diagonal operation on square-summable sequences. Keep the first coordinate unchanged, divide the second by two, divide the third by three, and so on. The outputs of high-coordinate basis vectors approach zero. If we discard all coordinates beyond N, we get a finite-rank operator. The difference between the original operation and the truncation has norm at most one divided by N plus one. Thus the full operator is a norm limit of finite-rank operators and is compact.

Contrast that with the identity. Apply it to the sequence of standard basis vectors: each vector has norm one and any two are separated by the square root of two. No subsequence converges. So identity is not compact on an infinite-dimensional Hilbert space. Compactness is a strong form of smoothing or loss of fine-scale detail, not merely boundedness.

Now turn to an integral equation. Imagine an unknown function u that contributes to an observed value at x by an average over all other locations. We can write the output as the integral of a kernel K of x and y multiplied by u of y. If K is square integrable over the pair of variables, this integral operator is compact on the corresponding L two space. One can see why by approximating the kernel by a finite sum of separated terms: one function of x times another function of y. Each separated term extracts a single scalar measurement from u and produces a fixed output shape. A finite sum has finite-dimensional range. Approximate the kernel in square-integral norm, and the resulting operators approach in operator norm.

The central equation is u minus K applied to u equals f. This looks like a perturbed identity. In finite dimensions, either the associated matrix has an inverse or the homogeneous equation has a nonzero solution. For a compact K, the Fredholm alternative recovers essentially this dichotomy even though the space is infinite dimensional.

Specifically, for a compact operator K on a Hilbert space, if the equation u minus K u equals zero has only the zero solution, then one minus K is invertible. In other words, uniqueness for the homogeneous equation forces existence and uniqueness for every forcing f. When the homogeneous equation has nonzero solutions, compatibility conditions appear. The forcing must be orthogonal to solutions of the adjoint homogeneous equation.

The adjoint condition is worth hearing slowly. Suppose v is annihilated by one minus the adjoint of K. If f equals u minus K u, take the inner product of f with v. Move K onto the other side of the inner product. The result is the inner product of u with v minus the adjoint of K applied to v, which is zero. Hence any solution f must be orthogonal to every such v. This is not an arbitrary technical constraint. It expresses a conservation law in the linear algebra of the problem: there are directions of the output space that the operator cannot reach.

A concrete rank-one example makes the whole theory visible. On L two of the interval from zero to one, let K send every function to its average, treated as a constant function. Apply K twice: taking the average of a constant changes nothing. So K is a projection. If we try to solve u minus K u equals f, take averages of both sides. The left side has average zero. Thus the forcing must have average zero. If that holds, u can be chosen equal to f plus any constant. We have infinitely many possible constants, one-dimensional nonuniqueness, and one linear compatibility condition. The kernel of one minus K is exactly the constant functions.

Now scale K by a number smaller than one in magnitude. The perturbed operator one minus alpha K is invertible. On constant functions it multiplies by one minus alpha; on mean-zero functions it acts as the identity. Its inverse can therefore be written down without any infinite series. As alpha approaches one, the inverse becomes large on constants. We can see the instability approaching the resonance where the homogeneous equation develops a solution.

This is the practical role of the Fredholm alternative in boundary-value problems and integral equations. A compact perturbation of identity has a finite-dimensional obstruction to solvability rather than an uncontrolled infinite-dimensional failure. But the theorem does not say every integral operator is compact, nor that a compact operator itself is invertible. Indeed, a compact operator on an infinite-dimensional space cannot be both boundedly invertible and onto the same space. If it were, the identity would be compact as a composition of its inverse with it, contradicting the basis-vector example.

The right mental model is not that infinity disappears. It is that a smoothing operation can isolate the troublesome directions into a finite-dimensional part. We can then analyze resonance, compatibility, and stability much as we would for a matrix, while retaining the functional setting needed for real differential and integral equations.

### Banach and C-Star Algebras: Where Functional Calculus Lives

**Track:** Analysis from First Principles. **Background:** L-p spaces, basic Hilbert spaces and weak derivatives as introduced earlier in this course. **Central question:** What assumptions make this construction rigorous?

#### Mathematical companion (not spoken)

A complex unital Banach algebra \(A\) satisfies \(\|ab\|\le\|a\|\|b\|\). For \(\|a\|<1\), the Neumann series \((1-a)^{-1}=\sum_{n\ge0}a^n\) converges in norm, with \(\|(1-a)^{-1}\|\le(1-\|a\|)^{-1}\).

A C*-algebra has an involution \(a\mapsto a^*\) and \(\|a^*a\|=\|a\|^2\). For compact Hausdorff \(K\), \(C(K)\) is a commutative unital C*-algebra under \(f^*(x)=\overline{f(x)}\), \(\|f\|=\sup_K|f|\). A unital commutative C*-algebra is *-isometrically isomorphic to \(C(\widehat A)\), where \(\widehat A\) is its character space.

For a bounded normal operator \(N\) on a complex Hilbert space, there is a unique unital isometric *-homomorphism \(C(\sigma(N))\to B(H)\), \(f\mapsto f(N)\), sending \(z\mapsto z\) to \(N\). Therefore \(\|f(N)\|=\max_{\lambda\in\sigma(N)}|f(\lambda)|\). This does **not** extend verbatim to arbitrary nonnormal operators.

Example: if \(D=\mathrm{diag}(2,5)\), then \(\sqrt D=\mathrm{diag}(\sqrt2,\sqrt5)\) and \(\|\sqrt D\|=\sqrt5\).

##### Exercises with answers

1. **Neumann inversion.** If \(\|T\|\le1/3\), bound \(\|(I-T)^{-1}\|\). **Answer:** At most \(3/2\).
2. **Function calculus.** For \(D=\mathrm{diag}(2,5)\), compute \((D-2I)(D-5I)\). **Answer:** Zero, by evaluating at the two spectral values.
3. **Algebraic caution.** Is every element of \(M_2(\mathbb C)\) normal? **Answer:** No; the Jordan block \(\begin{psmallmatrix}0&1\\0&0\end{psmallmatrix}\) is not normal.

### Adjoints: Moving an Operator Across an Inner Product

**Track:** Analysis from First Principles. **Background:** Hilbert spaces, bounded operators, and orthogonal projection. **Question:** How can one operator reveal what another does to lengths, angles, and measurements?

#### Mathematical companion (not spoken)

Throughout, the complex Hilbert-space inner product is linear in its first argument. For bounded \(T:H\to K\), its Hilbert adjoint is the unique \(T^*:K\to H\) such that
\[
\langle Tx,y\rangle_K=\langle x,T^*y\rangle_H.
\]
Existence and uniqueness follow from Riesz representation; \(\|T^*\|=\|T\|\). We have \((ST)^*=T^*S^*\), \((T^*)^*=T\), and
\[
(\operatorname{ran}T)^\perp=\ker T^*,\qquad
\overline{\operatorname{ran}T}=(\ker T^*)^\perp.
\]
For the unilateral right shift \(S:\ell^2(\mathbb N)\to\ell^2(\mathbb N)\), \(S^*(x_1,x_2,\ldots)=(x_2,x_3,\ldots)\), \(S^*S=I\), and \(SS^*=I-P_{e_1}\).

The C-star identity is \(\|T^*T\|=\|T\|^2\). Proof: \(\|Tx\|^2=\langle T^*Tx,x\rangle\le\|T^*T\|\|x\|^2\); hence \(\|T\|^2\le\|T^*T\|\). Reverse via \(\|T^*T\|\le\|T^*\|\|T\|=\|T\|^2\).

##### Exercises with answers

1. **Two-channel mixer.** Let \(T=\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\1&-1\end{pmatrix}\) on \(\mathbb R^2\). Find its adjoint and norm. **Answer:** \(T^*=T\) and \(T^*T=2I\), so \(\|T\|=\sqrt2\).
2. **Shift asymmetry.** Why is the right shift not unitary? **Answer:** It is an isometry, \(S^*S=I\), but \(SS^*\ne I\); its range has zero first coordinate.
3. **Range test.** If \(\ker T^*=\{0\}\), is T necessarily onto? **Answer:** No. Its range is dense; surjectivity requires more, such as closedness of the range.
4. **Order reversal.** Prove \((ST)^*=T^*S^*\) from the pairing. **Answer:** Apply the adjoint identity twice and use uniqueness.

### Self-Adjoint and Positive Operators: The Geometry of Energy

**Track:** Analysis from First Principles. **Background:** Inner products, adjoints, matrices, bounded operators. **Question:** Why do real measurements and nonnegative energy lead naturally to self-adjoint operators?

#### Mathematical companion (not spoken)

A bounded \(A:H\to H\) is self-adjoint if \(A=A^*\). It is positive, written \(A\ge0\), if \(A=A^*\) and \(\langle Ax,x\rangle\ge0\) for every \(x\). Over a complex Hilbert space the reality of all quadratic forms \(\langle Ax,x\rangle\) itself implies self-adjointness by polarization.

For \(A=\begin{pmatrix}2&1\\1&2\end{pmatrix}\), normalized eigenvectors \(u_+=(1,1)/\sqrt2\), \(u_-=(1,-1)/\sqrt2\) have eigenvalues \(3\) and \(1\). Thus
\[
A=3u_+u_+^*+u_-u_-^*,\qquad A^{1/2}=\sqrt3\,u_+u_+^*+u_-u_-^*.
\]
For an orthogonal projection \(P=P^*=P^2\), \(\langle Px,x\rangle=\|Px\|^2\). For \(T\in\mathcal B(H)\), \(T^*T\ge0\).

For \(A=M_t\) on \(L^2([0,1])\), \(\langle Af,f\rangle=\int_0^1 t|f(t)|^2dt\), \(0\le A\le I\), and \(A^{1/2}=M_{\sqrt t}\). The latter statement is an explicit multiplication-operator calculation, not an appeal to a complete eigenbasis.

##### Exercises with answers

1. **Negative energy.** Find a unit vector on which the reflection \(\operatorname{diag}(1,-1)\) has negative quadratic measurement. **Answer:** \((0,1)\), giving \(-1\).
2. **Square root.** Determine the positive square root of \(\operatorname{diag}(4,9)\). **Answer:** \(\operatorname{diag}(2,3)\).
3. **Not entrywise.** Is \(\begin{pmatrix}1&2\\2&1\end{pmatrix}\) positive? **Answer:** No; eigenvalues are \(3,-1\).
4. **Products.** Explain why positivity of A and B does not alone ensure positivity of AB. **Answer:** AB may fail to be self-adjoint if AB differs from BA.

### Spectra and Resolvents: What If an Operator Cannot Be Inverted?

**Track:** Analysis from First Principles. **Background:** Bounded operators, inverses, complex numbers, and inner products. **Question:** Why are eigenvalues insufficient to describe infinite-dimensional operators?

#### Mathematical companion (not spoken)

For bounded \(T\in\mathcal B(H)\), the resolvent set is \(\rho(T)=\{z\in\mathbb C:T-zI\text{ has a bounded everywhere-defined inverse}\}\); the spectrum is \(\sigma(T)=\mathbb C\setminus\rho(T)\). (Equivalently one may use \(zI-T\), changing the resolvent's sign.) In a nonzero complex Banach space, \(\sigma(T)\) is nonempty and compact.

For \(M_t\) on \(L^2[0,1]\):
\[
\sigma(M_t)=[0,1],\qquad \sigma_p(M_t)=\varnothing,\qquad
\|(M_t-zI)^{-1}\|=\frac1{\operatorname{dist}(z,[0,1])}
\quad(z\notin[0,1]).
\]
For real \(\lambda\in[0,1]\), normalized \(f_\epsilon=\mathbf1_{I_\epsilon}/\sqrt{|I_\epsilon|}\) with \(I_\epsilon=[0,1]\cap(\lambda-\epsilon,\lambda+\epsilon)\) satisfies \(\|(M_t-\lambda I)f_\epsilon\|\le\epsilon\). Hence the spectrum contains continuous-spectrum points without eigenvectors.

For normal \(T\), \(\|(T-zI)^{-1}\|=1/\operatorname{dist}(z,\sigma(T))\). For arbitrary operators, only the lower bound \(\|(T-zI)^{-1}\|\ge1/\operatorname{dist}(z,\sigma(T))\) is universally available. A Jordan block \(J=\begin{psmallmatrix}0&1\\0&0\end{psmallmatrix}\) has \(\sigma(J)=\{0\}\) but \(\|(zI-J)^{-1}\|\) grows on the order of \(|z|^{-2}\) near zero.

##### Exercises with answers

1. **Matrix resolvent.** Find the spectrum of \(\operatorname{diag}(1,3)\). **Answer:** \(\{1,3\}\).
2. **Continuous spectrum.** Explain why \(M_t\) has no eigenvalue equal to one half. **Answer:** An eigenfunction would vanish almost everywhere except possibly at a singleton, hence be zero in \(L^2\).
3. **Shift range.** Is the unilateral right shift invertible? **Answer:** No. It has no preimage of \(e_1\), so zero belongs to its spectrum even though its kernel is zero.
4. **Mapping.** What is \(\sigma(M_t^2+2I)\)? **Answer:** \([2,3]\).

### Matrix Functions: Turning Scalar Formulas into Operator Formulas

**Track:** Analysis from First Principles. **Background:** Eigenvectors, self-adjoint matrices, inverses, and elementary scalar functions. **Question:** What does it mean to take an exponential, square root, or reciprocal of a matrix?

#### Mathematical companion (not spoken)

If \(A=A^*\in M_n(\mathbb C)\), write \(A=\sum_{j=1}^m\lambda_jP_j\) with distinct real \(\lambda_j\) and mutually orthogonal spectral projections \(P_j\), \(\sum P_j=I\). Any function \(f:\sigma(A)\to\mathbb C\) defines
\[
f(A)=\sum_{j=1}^m f(\lambda_j)P_j.
\]
The calculus satisfies \((fg)(A)=f(A)g(A)\), \(\overline f(A)=f(A)^*\), \(\sigma(f(A))=f(\sigma(A))\), and \(\|f(A)\|=\max_{\lambda\in\sigma(A)}|f(\lambda)|\).

For \(A=\begin{psmallmatrix}2&1\\1&2\end{psmallmatrix}\), the explicit projections are \(P_+=\tfrac12\begin{psmallmatrix}1&1\\1&1\end{psmallmatrix}\) and \(P_-=\tfrac12\begin{psmallmatrix}1&-1\\-1&1\end{psmallmatrix}\). Thus
\[
(A+I)^{-1}=\tfrac14P_++\tfrac12P_-=\tfrac18\begin{pmatrix}3&-1\\-1&3\end{pmatrix}.
\]
For a nilpotent Jordan block \(N=\begin{psmallmatrix}0&1\\0&0\end{psmallmatrix}\), \(N^2=0\) and \(f(N)=f(0)I+f'(0)N\) for analytic f near zero. This is **not** continuous functional calculus for arbitrary nonnormal matrices.

##### Exercises with answers

1. **Polynomial.** For \(A=\operatorname{diag}(2,5)\), find \(A^2-3A+I\). **Answer:** \(\operatorname{diag}(-1,11)\).
2. **Heat.** If \(A\ge0\), what is the norm of \(e^{-tA}\) for \(t\ge0\)? **Answer:** \(\max_{\lambda\in\sigma(A)}e^{-t\lambda}=e^{-t\min\sigma(A)}\) in finite dimensions; it is at most one.
3. **Unitary.** Why is \(e^{itA}\) unitary for self-adjoint A and real t? **Answer:** In an orthonormal eigenbasis each diagonal multiplier has modulus one.
4. **Jordan.** Evaluate \(e^N\) for \(N^2=0\). **Answer:** \(I+N\).

### Compact Operators and Continuous Spectrum: Two Ways Infinity Enters

**Track:** Analysis from First Principles. **Background:** Eigenvectors, spectral values, compactness, and Hilbert spaces. **Question:** When does the matrix eigenvector picture survive, and when does it fail?

#### Mathematical companion (not spoken)

A bounded operator \(K:H\to H\) is compact if it maps the unit ball to a relatively compact set. For \(D:\ell^2\to\ell^2\), \((Dx)_n=x_n/n\), let \(D_N\) retain only the first \(N\) weighted coordinates. Then \(D_N\) has finite rank and \(\|D-D_N\|=1/(N+1)\to0\). Its spectrum is \(\{1/n:n\in\mathbb N\}\cup\{0\}\), while \(\ker D=0\).

For a compact self-adjoint \(K\), the nonzero spectral points are real eigenvalues of finite multiplicity and can accumulate only at zero. There is an orthonormal basis of eigenvectors after including a basis of \(\ker K\). This does not imply zero must be an eigenvalue.

For \(M_t\) on \(L^2[0,1]\), select disjoint measurable positive-measure subsets \(I_n\subset[1/2,1]\) and \(e_n=\mathbf1_{I_n}/\sqrt{|I_n|}\). Then \(\|M_te_n\|\ge1/2\) and the outputs are orthogonal, so no subsequence converges. Thus \(M_t\) is not compact.

##### Exercises with answers

1. **Compact diagonal.** Is \(\operatorname{diag}(1,1/2,1/3,\ldots)\) invertible as a bounded operator on \(\ell^2\)? **Answer:** No; its coordinatewise inverse would be unbounded and zero belongs to its spectrum.
2. **Zero eigenvalue.** Does zero belong to the point spectrum of that diagonal operator? **Answer:** No; the kernel is trivial.
3. **Identity.** Why is the identity not compact on an infinite-dimensional Hilbert space? **Answer:** An orthonormal sequence lies in its unit-ball image and has no convergent subsequence.
4. **Strong approximation.** Does strong convergence of finite-rank projections to I imply I is compact? **Answer:** No; compactness is closed under operator-norm limits, not arbitrary strong limits.

### Compact Operators, Fredholm Alternatives, and Integral Equations

**Track:** Analysis from First Principles. **Background:** Hilbert-space operators, spectra and bounded inverse theorem. **Central question:** Why does a nearly finite-dimensional operator behave differently from an arbitrary infinite-dimensional one?

#### Mathematical companion (not spoken)

For compact \(K:H\to H\) on a Hilbert space, the Fredholm alternative for \(I-K\) states: its kernel and cokernel are finite dimensional, its range is closed, and
\[ \operatorname{ran}(I-K)=\ker(I-K^*)^\perp. \]
The Fredholm index is zero: \(\dim\ker(I-K)=\dim\ker(I-K^*)\). In particular, injectivity is equivalent to surjectivity. More generally \(I-\lambda K\), \(\lambda\in\mathbb C\), is Fredholm of index zero.

If \(k\in L^2((0,1)^2)\), the Hilbert–Schmidt operator \((Ku)(x)=\int_0^1k(x,y)u(y)\,dy\) is compact. Finite-rank approximations arise from dense finite sums \(\sum_j a_j(x)b_j(y)\). Hilbert–Schmidt norm controls operator norm.

For \(Pu=(\int_0^1u)\mathbf 1\), \(P^2=P=P^*\), \(\operatorname{ran}P=\operatorname{span}\{\mathbf1\}\). The equation \((I-P)u=f\) is solvable iff \(\int_0^1f=0\), and then \(u=f+c\). If \(\alpha\ne1\),
\[ (I-\alpha P)^{-1}=I+\frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha}P. \]

##### Exercises with answers

1. **Compact diagonal.** Why is \(K e_n=n^{-1}e_n\) compact on \(\ell^2\)? **Answer:** Truncate at N; the operator norm error is \((N+1)^{-1}\).
2. **Solvability.** What must \(f\) satisfy to solve \((I-P)u=f\)? **Answer:** Zero integral over the interval.
3. **Near resonance.** What happens to the inverse bound as real \(\alpha\to1\)? **Answer:** The eigenvalue on constants is \(1-\alpha\), hence inverse norm grows at least \(|1-\alpha|^{-1}\).
