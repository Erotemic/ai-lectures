# Choose conductors and make durable connections

## Copper, Aluminum, and the Life of a Connection

Two wires can carry the same current with nearly the same loss, even though one is copper and one is aluminum. But put either wire into the wrong terminal, or assemble the connection poorly, and the problem changes completely. The resistance of the long conductor may be small while the resistance of a few millimeters at its end dominates the heating.

That is why selecting a conductor is never simply a vote for one metal. We have to distinguish the metal's bulk electrical properties, its mechanical behavior, its environment, and the connection system that makes it part of a circuit.

### Resistance lives in geometry as well as material

Think of a conductor as a long cylinder. A longer cylinder gives moving charge more material to pass through. A wider cross section provides more parallel paths. The familiar expression is resistance equals resistivity times length divided by cross-sectional area.

Copper has a lower electrical resistivity than aluminum at the same reference temperature. In round-number engineering terms, aluminum needs roughly one and a half to one and two-thirds times as much cross-sectional area to offer the same resistance over the same distance. The precise ratio depends on the actual copper and aluminum alloys and their condition, not just their chemical symbols.

Imagine two hypothetical one-hundred-meter conductors. One has a chosen copper cross section; the other uses an aluminum cross section about sixty percent larger. If the material resistivities and geometries are selected accordingly, the total resistance can be about the same. For the same current, the power dissipated in each conductor is then about the same as well.

But the aluminum conductor is not the same shape. It is thicker. It needs a termination with a compatible conductor range. It may fit into a different raceway configuration. Its weight, flexibility, cost, and mechanical handling may favor a different design. For power distribution over long distances and in some larger feeders, aluminum's low density and economic advantages can matter considerably. For smaller equipment conductors, connection and size constraints may favor copper. Those are engineering tradeoffs, not moral properties of metals.

Electrical size nomenclature adds another trap. A conductor's gauge, nominal cross section, and actual outside diameter are different things. Insulation increases outside diameter without increasing metallic conduction area. A stranded conductor may have a different overall geometry than a solid conductor of the same nominal metallic area. A wire size on a carton therefore does not by itself tell you whether a particular connector, bend, lug, or conduit is suitable.

### A metal changes resistance as it warms

The copper or aluminum conductor carries current, and current produces heat wherever there is resistance. As the metal warms, its resistivity generally rises. Within an ordinary temperature range, a linear temperature coefficient provides a useful approximation. The resistance at a higher temperature is the reference resistance multiplied by one plus a coefficient times the temperature increase.

That creates a feedback loop. A current produces heat. The higher temperature raises resistance. At a fixed current, the increased resistance raises the heat generated per second. The conductor also transfers heat to its surroundings, so temperature eventually approaches an equilibrium if the installation can reject enough heat.

There is an important distinction here. At fixed current, increasing resistance increases heat production. At fixed voltage across an ideal resistor, increasing resistance decreases total power instead. Neither simplified statement describes every real load, because many modern power supplies regulate power and change their current draw as supply voltage changes. Always state what quantity the thought experiment holds constant.

Temperature also changes the dimensions of metal. Copper and aluminum expand and contract as they warm and cool, and not by identical amounts. If a conductor is restrained in a terminal, repeated cycling can alter the stresses within the contact. Some aluminum alloys and termination systems have different mechanical relaxation behavior than copper. The connection therefore needs a compatible design that maintains contact pressure across its expected lifetime.

This is where mechanical engineering enters electrical engineering. An electrical joint must remain a low-resistance metal-to-metal contact while carrying current, enduring temperature cycles, resisting movement and surviving its environment.

### Why a connection is not just two pieces of metal touching

Zoom into a conductor pressed against a terminal. Even apparently smooth metal surfaces meet through many microscopic high points. Real current crosses the places where actual electrical contact exists. Pressure, surface finish, oxides, corrosion, and movement can change how many of those microscopic paths remain effective.

The connection has a contact resistance in addition to the resistance of the conductor. Contact resistance can begin very small, but a poor contact can concentrate heat into a much shorter region than a cable run. A modest current through a localized high-resistance joint can therefore overheat the joint while the rest of the cable stays comparatively cool.

Oxide films complicate the story. Aluminum develops a persistent surface oxide in air, but that fact alone cannot establish that a particular modern termination will fail. Copper also has surface chemistry, and engineers can make reliable connections with either metal. Reliability depends on a conductor and connector being evaluated as a combination: the material, dimensions, preparation, environment, and intended service all belong to the same decision.

Think of a connector as a system with at least four specifications. First, what conductor materials does it accept? Second, which sizes and stranding classes? Third, which temperature and electrical ratings? Fourth, how is it intended to be assembled, including the specified tool and fastening method? A terminal marked for copper is not automatically suitable for aluminum. Nor does a connector that accepts both metals necessarily permit them to be placed together under one arbitrary clamping surface.

Terminals may use screws, spring pressure, crimped barrels or other engineered contact mechanisms. Correct contact pressure matters, but more force is not automatically better. Overtightening can deform a conductor or damage a terminal; undertightening can leave insufficient pressure. Compression connectors may rely on particular matched dies and tools. The product's listing and instructions are part of the electrical design.

### The specific history of older aluminum branch wiring

There is a historical episode worth separating from the broader question of whether aluminum is a useful conductor. During the 1960s and early 1970s, some North American homes were wired with solid aluminum conductors on ordinary small branch circuits. The United States Consumer Product Safety Commission later investigated overheating connections associated with installations from that period.

Those investigations implicated connection deterioration at devices and splices. The commission's published historical evidence does not establish that all aluminum wiring of every alloy, size, or era is inherently defective. It documents a particular population of older residential branch-wiring installations and a particular failure mechanism.

This distinction matters when evaluating an older building. A solid aluminum branch conductor from a particular vintage is a different evidence category from a contemporary large aluminum feeder installed with listed aluminum-rated lugs. Both deserve accurate identification, but one should not infer the condition of the second from a fire statistic about the first.

It is also an attribution lesson. The consumer hazard findings come from the Commission and the studies it commissioned, not from a generic claim that copper is always safe. Modern connector requirements come from standards developed by standards organizations and technical committees, such as the trinational UL, CSA and ANCE wire-connector standard. Those are different forms of evidence answering different questions.

### Choosing the conductor is choosing the whole path

Suppose an electrical designer has to carry a known current over a long run. The first comparison might be copper against aluminum using resistance and mass. But a responsible comparison continues: allowable operating temperature, installed geometry, insulation and cable type, compatible terminations, mechanical support, voltage drop, and the available fault-clearing arrangement.

What if the conductor is small enough electrically but the terminal is rated for a lower temperature? What if its resistance is acceptable at twenty degrees but the actual operating temperature is substantially higher? What if aluminum saves weight but the receiving equipment does not accept the intended conductor size or alloy? Each question can change the answer without changing Ohm's law.

The most useful habit is to avoid asking, 'Is aluminum good or bad?' Ask instead: 'What alloy and geometry are we discussing? Where does the heat go? How are the ends joined? What operating environment and thermal history must this assembly survive?'

A conductor is not a metal sold by the meter. It is a current path with boundaries. In a durable electrical system, the boundaries deserve as much engineering attention as the length between them.

## Insulation, Cable Assemblies, Raceways, and Enclosures

Conductor material alone does not specify the installed path. Insulation, containment, and environmental protection define other parts of that path.

Imagine three pieces of wire, all with copper inside, all the same nominal size. The first is an insulated conductor intended to be pulled through an approved raceway. The second is part of a factory-made cable assembly that includes an outer sheath and several conductors. The third is a flexible appliance cord. Electrically, all three can carry current. Mechanically and legally, they are not interchangeable.

Their different construction reflects something fundamental about electrical design: the metal carries the current, but the surrounding materials establish where that current may flow, what the wiring can survive, and how a building or machine can be assembled and maintained.

### Start at the center and work outward

At the center is the conductor, often copper or aluminum. Over it there may be electrical insulation. The insulation separates it from neighboring conductors and accessible material under the voltage and environmental conditions for which it was evaluated.

Outside that insulation, a cable can have fillers, binders, shielding, a metallic armor or sheath, and an outer jacket. These layers do different jobs. A dielectric layer resists electrical breakdown. A jacket can protect against abrasion, moisture, chemicals or sunlight, depending on its formulation and rating. Armor or a metallic sheath can provide mechanical protection, and in some systems it participates in an engineered protective bonding path. Shielding can shape electric fields or reduce electromagnetic interference; it is not automatically a protective conductor.

People often call every outer layer 'insulation,' but this blurs a useful distinction. A polymer can be mechanically tough without having the required electrical insulation rating. A cable jacket can be waterproof in one exposure condition without making each conductor inside suitable for every wet-location installation. A metallic sheath can provide mechanical protection without being permitted to replace a separate protective conductor. Each layer has an assigned function.

### Temperature is a property of the construction

Insulation materials age under heat. Their electrical and mechanical properties change with prolonged temperature exposure and with chemical and moisture conditions. Some thermoplastic compounds soften when heated. Cross-linked materials are constructed differently and can support other operating-temperature ratings. That is one reason cable markings and standards specify temperature, voltage, and environment rather than simply 'plastic coating.'

For example, common insulation families include polyvinyl chloride, or PVC; cross-linked polyethylene, or XLPE; and ethylene propylene rubber, or EPR. Those names describe families, not a universal permitted temperature or installation method. A particular cable product has an evaluated construction and marking. The same broad polymer family may appear in different cables with different ratings.

An insulation temperature rating also does not authorize using a conductor at that temperature if a terminal, connected device, wiring method or applicable rule imposes a lower limit. We have already met the problem at a joint: the whole electrical path has to satisfy its most restrictive relevant condition.

A second distinction is between continuous thermal endurance and short fault withstand. A material may tolerate a brief higher temperature during a fault than it can sustain during normal operation. That does not make the fault temperature a normal-current rating.

### The words wire, cable, and cord have work to do

In everyday conversation, wire means almost anything with metal inside. In engineering language, an **insulated wire** can be one conductor with insulation. A **cable** often includes one or more conductors in an assembly with additional construction. A **flexible cord** is made for particular uses that involve connection to portable equipment, movement, or certain machine arrangements.

That distinction appears directly in wiring rules. A cable assembly approved for fixed wiring in a wall is not automatically intended to serve as the moving power cord for a tool. A cord suited to an appliance is not generally a substitute for a concealed permanent branch-circuit wiring method. A conductor intended for use in a raceway does not necessarily have all the physical protection of a complete building cable assembly when used by itself.

In U.S. systems, people will encounter markings such as THHN, THWN-2, XHHW-2, NM and MC. The markings refer to particular product categories and evaluations. A suffix or letter matters: UL's published wire and cable application guide, for example, distinguishes wet- and dry-location temperature ratings and notes how a 'dash two' suffix is used on certain building wires. The visible letters are the beginning of product identification, not a license to infer every allowed application.

In other jurisdictions, cable classes and nomenclature differ. IEC standards may define conductor constructions and nominal cross-sectional sizes in metric terms while another product standard defines insulation and cable performance. A label meaningful under one national scheme should not be silently translated into an equivalent code approval under another.

### What a raceway does

A raceway is an intentional channel for conductors and sometimes cables. In North American practice that category includes different forms of conduit and tubing. Electrical metallic tubing, rigid metallic conduit, rigid nonmetallic conduit, and flexible raceways have different mechanical, environmental and electrical characteristics. Some are threaded; some use compression or set-screw fittings; some are selected for exposure to water, sunlight or corrosion; others are intended for more protected environments.

The raceway creates a route that can protect wiring from physical damage and make conductors replaceable or accessible in ways a concealed cable run may not. But the raceway also shapes thermal conditions. Several current-carrying conductors grouped in a conduit cannot reject heat in exactly the same way as the same conductors laid separately in free air. This affects allowable current and becomes central when we study ampacity.

There is a geometry problem, too. A raceway has limited cross-sectional space, and pulling conductors around bends can damage insulation or require significant tension. Fill, bend radius, pull points, support, and fitting compatibility are engineering variables. A physically larger raceway can make an installation easier to maintain, but increasing size alone does not resolve every electrical, structural or fire-resistance requirement.

Metallic raceways have an additional question: electrical continuity and bonding. A metal tube is not necessarily a reliable fault-current return path merely because it looks continuous. The raceway type, the connectors and fittings, the equipment, and the applicable installation rules determine how the protective path is established.

### Enclosures are environmental interfaces

Follow the raceway to a box. A box is not just a place where wires happen to meet. It protects a connection, contains the assembly, provides space and access, and may be part of the protective bonding arrangement. Its entrance fittings, covers, mounting and drainage conditions matter as much as the box material.

The environment may be dry, damp, subject to driving rain, washdown water, condensation, dirt, dust, salt or mechanical impact. An enclosure's suitability must reflect the expected exposure, not merely its location inside or outside a building. An outdoor wall under an eave has different water exposure from an enclosure hit by a hose, and both differ from equipment subject to immersion.

You will see at least two prominent enclosure-rating languages. The IEC **IP code**, defined by IEC 60529, classifies specified protection against access to hazardous parts, entry of solid objects and entry of water. In the common two-digit form, the first digit addresses solids and access, and the second addresses water. The familiar phrase 'IP sixty-seven' is therefore shorthand for particular tests and degrees of protection, not for an appliance that is invulnerable to weather.

In North America, **NEMA enclosure Types** identify environmental performance under a different test and classification scheme. Types such as 3R, 4, 4X and 12 are not simply the numbers of an IP code with different letters. Their specifications do not have a universal one-to-one conversion, and material properties such as corrosion resistance can distinguish types that appear similar from a water-splash perspective.

Even a correctly rated empty enclosure can lose its intended protective behavior after an unsuitable cable entry, damaged gasket, missing cover or poorly located drain. Environmental integrity belongs to the assembled system.

### Wiring also passes through a building

A cable route encounters framing, masonry, thermal insulation, fire-rated walls, movement joints, and other building systems. A penetration may need physical edge protection and firestopping that preserves the original assembly's fire-resistance performance. A route through wet soil or exposed sunlight changes material requirements. A crowded path with data cables or other services may introduce mechanical, electrical-interference, or separation concerns.

This is why an electrical plan needs more than a line connecting two boxes. It needs a wiring method and an understanding of the path. Does the conductor remain in a permitted enclosure? What moisture conditions occur? Can heat escape? Are conductors identifiable and maintainable? What happens where the route crosses another construction system?

The correct answer is often an assembly, not a component. A conductor, its insulation, a jacket or raceway, properly selected fittings, and an enclosure all cooperate. Interchange any one part without revisiting the entire assembly, and the original reasoning can stop being valid.

### Read markings as a specification, not as decoration

A good materials review moves from general materials to the exact product. It records conductor material and size, cable construction, voltage class, temperature, environmental ratings, permitted wiring method, raceway and fitting compatibility, and the requirements of the box or equipment into which the wiring terminates.

The design question is not 'Can I fit this wire through that tube?' It is 'Does the resulting complete system carry the current, survive its environment, preserve insulation and protective continuity, and remain usable over its service life?'

Once you see wiring as layered construction, a surprising number of installation puzzles become easier to reason about. The cable carries the electricity, but the assembly makes it a dependable part of a building.

## Ampacity: Heat, Installation Method, and Derating

The installed construction now supplies the thermal conditions needed to interpret a conductor’s current-carrying capacity.

Picture two identical copper conductors carrying the same current. One hangs in open air where a breeze can remove heat. The other is enclosed with several loaded conductors inside thermal insulation. Would you expect them to reach the same temperature?

There is no reason they should. Their electrical resistance may be identical at the start, but their ability to reject heat is different. That observation is the foundation of ampacity: the current that a conductor is permitted to carry under stated conditions is not simply a number stamped onto the metal.

A conductor has an electrical resistance. A wiring installation has a thermal environment. Ampacity joins the two.

### A wire is a heater and a heat exchanger

Whenever current passes through resistance, the conductor generates heat. For a waveform characterized correctly by its root-mean-square current and a compatible resistance model, the familiar approximation is current squared times resistance.

Double the current and, if resistance is held fixed, the heating becomes four times as large. Current is therefore a powerful thermal variable. A small increase near an installation's limit can matter more than intuition suggests.

But a conductor is not thermally isolated. It sends energy to its insulation, the neighboring cables, the conduit, the building material, the ground, and the surrounding air. The steady operating temperature occurs where heat production and heat removal balance. If the environment becomes hotter, the temperature difference driving heat removal shrinks. If more loaded conductors are placed together, each may warm its neighbors and make the shared region less effective at shedding heat.

A simple classroom model expresses this as a thermal resistance from conductor to ambient. Temperature rise equals power dissipation times thermal resistance. The model is useful because it shows why the same electrical loss can produce different conductor temperatures. A bundled or insulated installation may have greater effective thermal resistance than a freely cooled one.

This is a model, not the general formula for a wiring code's ampacity tables. Real cables have multiple heat-flow paths and construction details; buried circuits also depend on soil properties. Standards and validated engineering methods account for those effects more carefully.

### What an ampacity table assumes

Open an ampacity table and you will find a current value associated with conductor material, area, insulation temperature class, and some installation conditions. That value is meaningful only with its table headings, footnotes, reference conditions and adjustment rules.

One table may assume conductors in air at a specified ambient temperature. Another describes a reference installation method, such as conductors in conduit in a thermally insulated wall, in conduit on a surface, in free air on a tray, or buried in the ground. The IEC installation guidance groups these into different reference methods because their heat transfer differs.

Consider an air-cooled cable in a hot rooftop conduit. A reference current rating determined at thirty degrees Celsius does not describe that hotter condition without an adjustment. Likewise, taking several separately rated circuits and tightly grouping them in a raceway changes their thermal situation. Some of the reference tables assume two loaded conductors; others assume three. Whether a neutral carries significant current under the actual waveform can also change the analysis.

This is what engineers mean by **correction factors** and **adjustment factors**. We begin with a rating under specified reference conditions and modify that rating to account for conditions that differ. In some standard methods the factors multiply; in other applications additional constraints and special rules intervene. It is a mistake to treat one simple product as a complete design algorithm.

### The physical meaning of derating

Suppose a conductor has a hypothetical reference ampacity of thirty amperes in a given reference installation. The environment has a temperature correction factor of zero point nine one, and the grouping of circuits has a factor of zero point eight. In the simplified model, thirty times zero point nine one times zero point eight is twenty-one point eight four amperes.

That arithmetic says only this: under those hypothetical factors, the adjusted value is lower than the reference value. It does not establish that any real thirty-ampere conductor is suitable for twenty-one point eight four amperes in an actual building. We have not established its insulation type, terminal limits, protection, conductor count, wiring method, product marking or adopted jurisdictional rules.

The lesson is the shape of the reasoning. A cable that looks large enough from its printed size can be underspecified after accounting for its thermal environment. Conversely, simply multiplying generic factors found online can be just as wrong if they belong to a different cable family or reference method.

There is another nonobvious feature. The applicable conductor's insulation rating may be ninety degrees Celsius, while an equipment termination accepts only a lower temperature condition. You cannot automatically carry the highest column value all the way through to the terminal. Table methods sometimes allow the higher insulation temperature to be used as the starting basis for adjustment while separately enforcing a lower terminal limit. That distinction is subtle and edition-sensitive; its exact implementation belongs to the adopted code and connected equipment instructions.

### Geometry and loaded conductors

Why does grouping matter? Imagine a single wire losing heat to the surrounding air. Now imagine four loaded circuits crowded into a small raceway. Their total heat production raises the temperature of the raceway and its contents. The innermost conductor may have no direct exposure to cool air. Each conductor changes the others' boundary conditions.

The correct number of **current-carrying conductors** is an electrical question, not simply a visual count of colored wires. A protective conductor is not normally intended to carry load current. A neutral may carry only imbalance in some balanced linear three-phase situations, but nonlinear loads can send harmonic currents through it, and special arrangements can make the neutral heavily loaded. You cannot declare every white or blue conductor thermally irrelevant just because of its color.

Physical arrangement matters as well. Cables lying separately on a ventilated ladder, a tight bundle in a wall cavity, and cables inside a buried duct can have very different thermal behavior. Even where the same cable is used, a change of routing may require the current-carrying assessment to be repeated.

### Buried cable is a thermal problem in soil

With buried conductors, heat has to move through a duct or cable jacket and into soil. Soil thermal resistivity matters. Dry, poorly conducting soil can hold heat near a cable; soil moisture and temperature can vary seasonally and around energized installations. A wet trench on installation day is not a guarantee of that soil's behavior years later.

IEC-oriented guidance accordingly distinguishes air temperature from ground temperature, installation depth, grouping and soil thermal properties. More advanced methods, including the IEC 60287 cable-current-rating series, model thermal paths in greater detail. That is particularly important for large or critical cable systems.

It is tempting to say that underground means cool. Sometimes it does. But a buried cable is not automatically better cooled than one in free air. The answer depends on the actual thermal path and operating conditions.

### The current varies with time

So far we have described a steady-state heating model. Real loads are intermittent. Motors draw starting currents, thermostats cycle, and electronically controlled equipment changes operating states. A conductor and its surroundings have thermal mass, so temperature does not jump instantly to the steady-state value at each current fluctuation.

A simple thermal-capacitance model captures that lag. The rate of temperature change depends on the difference between heat being generated and heat escaping. This explains why a brief pulse and the same current applied continuously can have very different thermal outcomes.

But an installation's allowable loading cannot be invented from that observation alone. Protection devices, cable insulation, terminals, equipment standards, and code-defined continuous-load treatments create conditions that must all be respected. A current rating for continuous operation cannot be casually reinterpreted as a permission to overload a circuit for an arbitrary fraction of each hour.

The deeper principle is that **time, temperature and current belong together**. A realistic load study records both peak current and the duration and repetition of those peaks.

### Ampacity is not the whole cable design

Imagine a thirty-meter run and a very long two-hundred-meter run built from the same conductor, under similar thermal conditions, carrying the same current. Their per-meter heat generation might be similar, so the reference ampacity model might permit the same continuous current. But the long circuit has much more total resistance. Its voltage drop can be far greater.

That means there are at least two independent sizing checks: can the conductor carry the current within its thermal and product limits, and can it deliver suitable voltage at the load under expected operating conditions? Other checks include fault protection and withstand, mechanical strength, terminal compatibility, and available installation space.

Those requirements need not point to the same wire size. A conductor sized by ampacity may be too small for voltage regulation over a long route; a conductor enlarged to reduce voltage drop may create an equipment-termination compatibility question.

An engineer therefore does not ask merely, 'What is the ampacity of this gauge?' The useful question is, 'What is the allowable current of this **wiring system** under its actual thermal environment and applicable constraints?'

When you understand that distinction, cable tables become much less mysterious. They are compressed models of electrical heating, heat flow, materials and installation geometry. Reading them well requires restoring the assumptions that made each number meaningful.

## Voltage Drop, Distance, and the Cost of Current

A building can have a circuit that never overheats and still performs poorly. A long feeder may deliver enough current without exceeding its thermal rating, yet the voltage at its far end may sag whenever a large load starts. Lights may flicker, a motor may struggle to accelerate, or an electronic converter may change the current it draws.

Nothing about this contradicts the ampacity calculation. Ampacity asks whether the wiring can carry current under its thermal conditions. Voltage drop asks how much of the source's voltage is consumed by impedance along the path before the load receives its share.

Both questions concern the same conductor, but they lead to different sizing constraints.

### Trace the complete current loop

Imagine a direct-current source supplying a resistor at the end of a long pair of wires. Current travels out along one wire and returns along the other. Each wire has resistance. Their resistances add around the loop.

Suppose the distance to the load is thirty meters. For a two-conductor circuit whose supply and return use equal paths, the current traverses sixty meters of conductor in total. In a simplified resistance calculation, using thirty meters only once would miss half of the wiring's resistance.

That is why the first step is to draw the current path. In a North American center-tapped system, a load connected line to neutral has a particular supply-and-return geometry; a load connected between the two outer conductors has another. In a balanced three-phase circuit, current flows through multiple phase conductors and is described with phasors rather than a single two-wire loop. A neutral's presence on the drawing is not proof that it carries the same current as a phase conductor in every operating condition.

The calculation must follow the actual circuit topology. There is no universal 'multiply length by two' rule that applies unchanged to every three-phase or multiwire circuit.

### Resistance turns into voltage loss

For a simple DC or predominantly resistive circuit, the voltage lost along a wire is current times wire resistance. The conductor's resistance is its resistivity times length divided by metallic cross-sectional area.

Put these together and several trends become immediate. Double the run length, and resistance and voltage drop double if everything else remains the same. Double the metallic cross section, and both are approximately halved. Double the current, and the voltage drop doubles at fixed resistance—but the power lost as heat becomes four times as large.

This last relationship is important when looking at efficiency. Voltage drop tells you how much voltage is missing at the load. Current squared times resistance tells you the power being dissipated along the cable. That lost energy has to go somewhere: it becomes heat distributed along the wiring and associated connections.

A circuit can therefore waste substantial power even when it remains within its permitted thermal loading. That is especially relevant to systems operating for many hours, where small continuous losses become meaningful energy costs.

### Work through a hypothetical long run

Consider a pure resistive model at twenty degrees Celsius. The conductor metal has a resistivity of approximately one point six eight times ten to the minus eight ohm meters, a value commonly used for copper in introductory physics. A two-wire circuit has a thirty-meter one-way run. Each conductor has a metallic cross section of two point five square millimeters. Let the current be twelve amperes.

The total loop length is sixty meters. The approximate loop resistance is zero point four zero three ohms. Multiply by twelve amperes and the voltage drop is about four point eight four volts. Relative to a nominal one-hundred-twenty-volt supply, that is about four percent. The total heat dissipated along both conductors is about fifty-eight watts.

Now keep the current and length fixed, but increase the metallic area to four square millimeters. The loop resistance falls in proportion to area, and the idealized drop becomes about three volts. This is an electrical comparison, not a recommendation to select either size for a building. The hypothetical example has not established ampacity, cable type, permitted conductor sizes, connection compatibility, or local voltage-drop criteria.

Notice the larger lesson: the conductor area that carries a current safely can be smaller than the conductor area that delivers acceptable voltage to a distant load. In a long system, the voltage criterion may become the stronger constraint.

### A warm conductor drops more voltage

The earlier calculation used reference-temperature copper resistance. But current heats a conductor, and copper's resistance increases as it warms. If the metal temperature rises from twenty degrees to seventy degrees Celsius, a simple linear approximation using a temperature coefficient near zero point zero zero three nine per degree gives an increase of about nineteen and a half percent in resistance.

At the same twelve-ampere current, the hypothetical four point eight four volt loss becomes about five point seven eight volts. The precise number in a real cable depends on the metal alloy, actual temperature distribution, manufacturing tolerance, and termination effects. But the direction is reliable: a cold-conductor calculation tends to understate resistance-related drop at hotter operating temperatures.

The calculation and the thermal design are coupled. Current creates heat. Heat changes resistance. Resistance changes the voltage drop and loss. This is one reason careful conductor sizing uses temperature assumptions that match the installation, not merely convenient room-temperature values.

### Alternating current adds phase

At mains frequency, copper conductors still have resistance, but the complete electrical behavior of a cable also includes reactance. Alternating current creates time-varying magnetic fields. A conductor arrangement has inductance, and that inductance creates a voltage component that is not simply in phase with the current.

The general object is the circuit's complex impedance. For a sufficiently simple linear sinusoidal model, impedance has a real part, resistance, and an imaginary part, reactance. The load has a power factor that describes the relationship of its fundamental current to voltage, although harmonics require additional care.

For many short, relatively small building circuits at ordinary mains frequency, the resistive component is dominant enough that a first approximation based on resistance is useful. As circuits become longer, conductor sizes larger, or current and phase characteristics different, reactance may matter. Schneider Electric's installation guide provides separate single-phase and balanced three-phase formulas that include resistance, reactance, current, circuit length and phase angle.

A balanced three-phase system also has a geometric factor: its voltage relationships are not simply those of a two-conductor loop. Engineers often see a square-root-of-three factor in three-phase line-to-line voltage-drop approximations. That factor arises from phasor geometry under stated balance and waveform assumptions. It must not be pasted into a DC calculation or an unbalanced nonlinear waveform without reconsidering the model.

### The source itself can sag

Imagine that measurements at a distribution panel already show reduced voltage when a large motor starts. The motor circuit adds some additional voltage drop along its cable. Improving only that cable cannot restore the voltage lost upstream in the service transformer, feeder, utility system, generator or inverter.

To analyze voltage at the load, separate source voltage from downstream line impedance. At a given instant, the receiving equipment sees the voltage remaining after the current-dependent drops along the route. The source may also change its terminal voltage with load.

A motor can complicate the story because its starting current may be much higher than its steady running current. Starting current increases the drop, which reduces the voltage available to create starting torque. The outcome depends on the motor, control method, source stiffness and mechanical load. An inverter-driven motor or electronically regulated appliance may behave differently from an across-the-line induction motor.

It is also unsafe analytically to assume every load keeps current fixed as voltage changes. A simple resistance draws less current at lower voltage. A constant-power converter, over its regulated operating range, tends to draw more current when input voltage falls. Real equipment has limits and protection behavior. Different load models can therefore produce different predictions from the same wiring resistance.

### Voltage-drop criteria are not one eternal percentage

You may have heard that a branch circuit should stay below three percent voltage drop, or that feeder plus branch should stay below five percent. Those figures appear in editions and commentaries associated with North American code guidance, often as efficiency-oriented informational notes rather than a universal enforceable rule for every circuit. Other applications and jurisdictions use different criteria, and some equipment requires a tighter voltage tolerance for reliable operation.

The IEC 60364-5-52 family addresses voltage drop in wiring-system selection. Its consolidated 2024 publisher description notes changes to Clause 525 and references the applicable annex. That scope statement is not enough to determine an installed system's permitted percentage without reviewing the relevant complete adopted text.

The technically useful habit is to state a criterion together with its source and scope: the equipment's permitted voltage range, the power system's operating profile, the chosen engineering guide, and the actual adopted local requirement. A percentage without its context is less valuable than the load behavior it was intended to protect.

### Size for the system, not for one formula

An engineering comparison should check at least four different dimensions. First, the conductor's thermal current-carrying capability under its installation conditions. Second, the voltage it delivers under normal, starting, and other relevant load states. Third, its ability to withstand and permit proper clearing of faults in the actual source and protective-device arrangement. Fourth, mechanical and connection compatibility, including size, flexibility, raceway or cable construction, and terminal limits.

Energy efficiency adds an economic dimension. Enlarging a conductor can reduce resistive losses, but increases material cost, space and possibly installation complexity. The best tradeoff depends on duty cycle, energy price, material costs and expected service life. The math is not reducible to choosing the largest conductor that fits.

The conceptual shift is to stop thinking of a wire as a perfect line on a diagram. It has resistance, impedance, temperature, geometry and cost. When current flows, it changes the voltage available to everything downstream. Understanding that relationship turns voltage drop from a mysterious complaint into a calculable property of the whole current path.

## Connections Under Pressure: Terminals, Torque, Splices, and Strain Relief

The current path also passes through connections, where contact conditions can dominate the behavior of an otherwise suitable conductor.

A copper conductor can carry current for many meters with very little trouble, yet the last few millimeters, where it enters a terminal, can become the hottest part of the circuit. There may be more metal in that terminal than in the wire. Why would the larger piece be the problem?

Because a connection does not behave like a continuous piece of metal. Two nominally smooth metal surfaces touch only at microscopic high points. Current must squeeze through those points, cross an interface, and spread out again. The electrical resistance of this interface depends on its material, cleanliness, geometry, and especially the mechanical condition that keeps the surfaces together. A reliable electrical joint is simultaneously an electrical component and a mechanical assembly.

The useful way to understand a termination is to ask three questions. What establishes the electrical contact? What maintains that contact through temperature changes and movement? And where do forces on the cable go, other than through the conducting joint?

### Contact area is not the area you can see

Imagine pressing two flat metal plates together. Through a magnifying glass, their surfaces look polished. At a much smaller scale they are hills and valleys. Most of the apparent area is separated by tiny gaps, films, or oxides. Electrical current concentrates at the spots where conducting material actually meets. Engineers describe the resulting restriction as constriction resistance, often considered together with film and interface resistance as contact resistance.

Clamping force can enlarge the true contact regions and help break or control some surface films. But force is not the only design variable. The conductor may be round or stranded; a terminal may be a screw clamp, pressure plate, spring cage, crimp barrel, compression lug, or other engineered geometry. Its surface finishes and permissible conductor constructions matter. Two connections with the same conductor and nominal force can have different electrical and mechanical behavior.

Contact resistance also creates a feedback loop. A simple circuit has the familiar heating term: current squared times resistance. If a connection's effective resistance increases, more energy is dissipated at the joint. Heating changes dimensions and material properties. Repeated expansion and contraction can alter contact pressure. Under some conditions, damage and resistance can reinforce one another.

A numerical example makes the location of the problem clear. At twenty amperes, an idealized joint with two milliohms of resistance dissipates zero point eight watts. If degradation raises that resistance to twenty milliohms, the same current produces eight watts. Those figures are deliberately hypothetical, not a rating or an acceptable resistance specification. They show why a small resistance in a confined connection can matter thermally.

The ordinary branch-circuit breaker observes total circuit current. A resistive joint in series with a load can run hot while drawing less than the breaker's overcurrent threshold. That is one reason a healthy protective-device rating does not prove the quality of every splice.

### Why specify torque?

A screw-type terminal uses a rotating fastener to create pressure on a conductor or pressure plate. Torque is the moment applied to that fastener. The temptation is to think that a particular torque uniquely produces a particular contact force. It does not. Part of the applied moment overcomes thread friction and friction under the fastener head or pressure-bearing surface. Geometry, lubrication, finish, thread condition, and tool behavior affect the result.

Torque nevertheless gives manufacturers a practical, repeatable installation variable for a joint that has been designed and evaluated with particular conductors. The point is not that more torque is always better. Too little tightening may leave inadequate pressure or a joint susceptible to movement. Excessive tightening may damage strands, deform a pressure plate, strip threads, or exceed the mechanical limits of the connector. Both directions can harm the connection.

A published torque value therefore belongs to a specified terminal and its instructions. It is not a property of copper wire in the abstract. The manufacturer's marking may also prescribe conductor material, size range, number of conductors, solid or stranded construction, preparation, and particular fitting types. A terminal approved for one copper conductor is not automatically suitable for two, or for aluminum, or for very fine flexible strands.

The American workplace rule in OSHA's electrical standard is useful here because it states the general engineering obligations distinctly. Electrical connections must suit the conductor material; a terminal for multiple conductors must be identified for that use; and listed or labeled equipment is to be installed according to instructions included in its listing or labeling. These are requirements in the covered workplace setting, not a claim that OSHA itself governs every private domestic installation. Product evaluation standards such as UL 486A-486B concern connector designs and tests; their existence is not a substitute for the particular connector's instructions.

It is tempting to recheck an old screw by turning it and reading a torque wrench. That reading is not a trustworthy reconstruction of the torque originally applied. Static friction, settling, oxidation, and movement change the relationship between a present wrench reading and the connection's history. Maintenance decisions need an equipment-specific method rather than the assumption that a passing retorque reading proves the contact was sound.

### The connector is a system

Consider several connection families. In a screw or pressure-plate terminal, tightening creates the contact force. In a spring-clamp connector, a purpose-built spring maintains force over an allowed range of conductors and temperatures. In a crimp or compression lug, a specified connector, tool, die, and deformation create the joint. Some connectors join multiple wires; some accept exactly one conductor. Each family has a different installation and inspection logic.

The visible appearance of two connectors is not a reliable way to infer interchangeability. A generic-looking lug may have a specific conductor-class limitation. An unapproved ferrule may alter the geometry that a terminal was tested with. A compression die chosen by eye may produce a joint that looks crimped but fails to develop the intended mechanical and electrical contact. Likewise, the presence of a spring does not make a connection universally maintenance-free; its suitability is established for a stated product range and use.

Dissimilar metals introduce another layer. Copper and aluminum have different mechanical, thermal, and surface-chemistry behavior. Connecting them reliably requires an interface identified for the materials and conditions. An antioxidant compound is not a magic substitute for a suitable connector, and adding a compound contrary to product instructions can alter the tested joint. This is a product-compatibility problem, not merely a choice of which metal has lower resistivity.

The historical attention to aluminum branch-circuit connections in parts of North American housing is an example of this system perspective. Problems in particular conductor-and-device combinations prompted investigations and repair guidance. The accurate lesson is not that every aluminum conductor is defective. It is that material, connector construction, thermal cycling, and approval conditions cannot be separated when evaluating a joint.

### Where does the cable's weight go?

A termination can be electrically correct and still fail because it is carrying a mechanical load it was never designed to support. Think of a suspended cable entering an enclosure. Its weight may pull continuously on the terminal. A vibrating machine adds cyclical movement. A sharp cable entry can concentrate stress in the insulation. Pulling a cord can transmit force directly to its conductors if there is no appropriate strain relief.

A strain-relief fitting, cable clamp, or approved entry device is meant to transfer the relevant forces to the cable's sheath and the enclosure or structure, not to the electrical contact itself. The design must be compatible with the cable diameter, construction, environmental rating, and expected movement. A gland may also help preserve an enclosure's environmental classification; a random hole with a tight-looking cable does not necessarily do that.

Mechanical support is not the same as squeezing a cable as hard as possible. Excessive compression can damage insulation or change the geometry of conductors within the sheath. Supports must resist movement while respecting the cable construction, bend radius, and manufacturer's installation limits.

The result is a sequence of distinct functions. The electrical terminal conducts current. The strain-relief fitting manages pulling and bending forces. The enclosure contains and protects the joint. The surrounding wiring method supports the route. Good design avoids forcing one component to perform the job of another.

### Reading a termination specification like an engineer

Suppose a drawing shows a feeder entering a distribution enclosure. The feeder has a conductor material and size, insulation rating, strand class, and expected operating temperature. The enclosure has terminals with markings and a manufacturer document. An engineer must reconcile the two sets of requirements, not merely verify that the wire physically fits under the clamp.

The review questions are concrete. Is the conductor material accepted? Is its size within the terminal's identified range? Is the number of conductors per opening permitted? Are the strand class and connector type compatible? Are termination temperature limits consistent with the cable and current-rating calculation? Is an appropriate mechanical entry provided? Are the tools and tightening or compression method specified? What evidence of the completed work is meaningful for this equipment?

Notice that neither a torque value nor a continuity beep answers all these questions. A low-current continuity test can confirm a path, yet say almost nothing about how a joint behaves under load and thermal cycling. A thermal image can locate a temperature difference under particular loading and environmental conditions, but it cannot alone identify the cause or prove that a cooler connection is correctly constructed. Better observations are useful; they are not substitutes for the mechanical and product requirements.

The final lesson of a termination is that the smallest part of an electrical system can have the largest local consequence. Conductivity supplies the path, contact engineering preserves it, torque or another specified joining method establishes the initial condition, and strain relief protects that condition. Treating those as separate but coordinated functions explains both what makes a reliable joint and why superficially similar joints can age so differently.

### Copper, Aluminum, and the Life of a Connection

#### Non-spoken technical companion

##### Material and geometry model

For a homogeneous uniform conductor with length `L` and metallic cross section `A`:

\[
R(T)=\rho(T)\frac{L}{A},\qquad P_{\mathrm{loss}}=I_{\mathrm{rms}}^2R.
\]

Reference properties from OpenStax *University Physics* Vol. 2 §9.3 (idealized pure-material approximations at 20°C, not alloy-specific cable tables):

| Property | Copper | Aluminum |
|---|---:|---:|
| Resistivity at 20°C | `1.68e-8 Ω·m` | `2.65e-8 Ω·m` |
| Approximate temperature coefficient | `0.0039 /°C` | `0.0039 /°C` |
| Equal-resistance area ratio `A_Al/A_Cu` | — | `2.65/1.68 ≈ 1.577` |

In an equal-length model, a `16 mm²` copper conductor and a `25.2 mm²` aluminum conductor have approximately equal **reference-temperature resistance**. Real conductor alloys, strand packing and manufacturing limits change the comparisons. Do not turn this computed area into a standardized commercial size.

The linear model `R(T) ≈ R(T0)[1 + α(T−T0)]` assumes a restricted temperature interval and fixed geometry; it is not a connector heat-rise or cable ampacity standard.

##### An equal-current but unequal-connection thought experiment

A conductor has bulk resistance `0.05 Ω`. At `10 A` its dissipation is `5 W`. A connection adds `0.002 Ω`, dissipating `0.2 W` over a small joint volume; if degradation instead raises that localized resistance to `0.05 Ω`, the joint dissipates `5 W`, **not** an additional `0.2 W`. This hypothetical shows concentration of heat, not an acceptability threshold. Actual joint temperature depends on geometry, conduction, convection and contact stability.

##### Paper exercises with answers

1. **Equal resistance:** If `ρ_Al/ρ_Cu=1.577`, what aluminum area matches `10 mm²` copper at equal length and reference temperature? **Answer:** approximately `15.77 mm²`; not an approved conductor-selection result.
2. **Temperature:** Starting from `R20=0.40 Ω` and `α=0.0039/°C`, compute `R70`. **Answer:** `0.40 × (1+0.0039×50)=0.478 Ω`.
3. **Localized heating:** If a connection resistance changes from `1 mΩ` to `15 mΩ`, how does its dissipation change at fixed current? **Answer:** fifteenfold, assuming the current stays fixed.
4. **Evidence classification:** Can a CPSC statistic on pre-1972 outlet connections be applied to all 2026 aluminum feeder cables? **Answer:** no; population, conductor type, connector system and operating conditions differ.
5. **Product reasoning:** A terminal carries a temperature number and a material marking. What additional information is needed? **Answer:** conductor size/stranding range, number of conductors per terminal, torque or matched crimp system, wet/corrosive suitability, product-specific instructions and intended equipment application.

##### Layered wiring-system model

```text
current-carrying metal
   -> conductor insulation (voltage and thermal endurance)
   -> optional filler / shield / armor (construction-specific functions)
   -> cable jacket or raceway (construction and environment)

```

An optional layer is not automatically present and cannot be assigned another layer's function without product-level evidence.

##### Examples of non-interchangeability

- A conductor marked for wet locations does not by itself validate a dry-location-rated terminal or unsuitable enclosure.
- A jacket with sunlight resistance does not make a particular cable approved for direct burial.
- A continuous metallic conduit can support bonding only when the chosen system and fittings establish the required reliable continuity.
- A corrosion-resistant enclosure material does not prove that its field-drilled entries preserve the specified IP or NEMA type.
- A high conductor insulation temperature does not override a lower temperature limit at the termination.

##### Additional questions

1. Does a higher insulation temperature label necessarily permit a higher branch-circuit current? **No:** terminations, wiring method, number of loaded conductors, product restrictions and local code also constrain the result.
2. Is a metallic shield the same as protective earth? **No:** shield function and protective conductor qualification are distinct.
3. Why might an outdoor enclosure with an IP mark still fail in service? **Because:** gasket condition, penetrations, condensation, installation orientation and chemical exposure affect the complete assembly.

5. Does raceway fill equal ampacity correction? **No:** fill and heat dissipation are related but separate requirements with different models.

### Ampacity: Heat, Installation Method, and Derating

**Track:** Electrical Systems: Principles, Practice, Codes, and Judgment. **Learning surface:** simplified thermal models, installation-method drawings, manufacturer tables, and paper calculations.

#### Non-spoken technical companion

##### Simplified lumped thermal model

\[
P_{\text{heat}}=I_{\mathrm{rms}}^2R(T), \qquad
\Delta T=P_{\text{heat}}\,\Theta_{\text{thermal}},
\]

where `Θ_thermal` is an idealized thermal resistance in K/W. For transients:

\[
C_{\text{thermal}}\frac{dT}{dt}=P_{\text{heat}}(t)-\frac{T-T_a}{\Theta_{\text{thermal}}}.
\]

This model is explanatory. It does not replace IEC 60287, IEC 60364-5-52, NEC ampacity rules, manufacturer specifications, or circuit-protection coordination. Temperature feedback enters through `R(T)`.

##### Derating example with deliberately hypothetical coefficients

A **fictional** base table gives `I_ref = 30 A`. Invented correction factors are `k_ambient = 0.91`, `k_grouping = 0.80`. Then:

`I_adjusted = 30 × 0.91 × 0.80 = 21.84 A`.

The factors demonstrate multiplication but are **not a selected code table row** and cannot authorize a 21.84 A field loading. Schneider Electric's published IEC-oriented guide shows genuine variation by installation method, ambient temperature, grouping, and thermal-resistivity assumptions. IEC's official public catalogue documents standard scope, not the detailed calculation tables themselves.

##### Thermal and electrical constraints are separate

| Design question | Relevant inputs | Common failure of reasoning |
|---|---|---|
| Reference ampacity | Conductor material, area, insulation, installation method | Taking the number without the table heading |
| Environmental correction | Ambient, cable grouping, soil, thermal insulation | Assuming every route is open air |
| Terminal temperature | Equipment and connector ratings | Treating a 90°C insulation label as a 90°C terminal permission |
| Load current | Waveform, duty, harmonics, simultaneous operation | Treating all neutral currents as zero |
| Voltage regulation | Circuit length, hot resistance, reactance, load behavior | Confusing ampacity with acceptable voltage drop |
| Fault behavior | Fault current, withstand time, device operation | Inferring protection solely from a normal-current table |

##### Exercises with answers

1. **Power scale:** Double current with fixed resistance. **Answer:** four times heating.
2. **Factor sensitivity:** Apply a hypothetical `0.85` factor to `40 A`. **Answer:** `34 A` in that simplified model.
3. **Combined factor:** Why do `0.80 × 0.90` and `0.80 + 0.90` answer different questions? **Answer:** these factors multiply dimensionless reductions; addition is not a valid derating operation.
4. **Route comparison:** Which is likely to have higher thermal resistance: one freely ventilated cable or the same cable in a tightly insulated wall cavity? **Answer:** generally the insulated cavity, but actual ratings depend on the specified construction.
5. **Why isn't longer always hotter per meter?** **Answer:** if conductor size, per-meter resistance, current, and local heat-flow conditions are unchanged, local heating may be similar; total voltage drop grows with total length.
6. **Why does a neutral count require electrical analysis?** **Answer:** actual current and harmonic content, not insulation color alone, determine whether it produces material heating in the grouping model.

### Voltage Drop, Distance, and the Cost of Current

**Track:** Electrical Systems: Principles, Practice, Codes, and Judgment. **Learning surface:** one-line routes, hot-resistance calculations, phasor sketches, efficiency comparisons and load scenarios.

#### Non-spoken technical companion

##### Resistive single-phase/DC loop model

For a two-wire supply/return route with identical conductor material and area and one-way length `L`:

\[
R_{\text{loop}}(T)=2\rho(T)\frac{L}{A},\qquad
\Delta V\approx I R_{\text{loop}},\qquad
P_{\text{loss}}=I^2R_{\text{loop}}.
\]

The factor two accounts for outgoing and returning conductors in this specific topology. It is not a universal formula for all multiwire or three-phase systems.

##### Calculated example, equal current, 20°C reference

Take `ρ_Cu=1.68×10^-8 Ω·m`, `L_one_way=30 m`, `A=2.5 mm² =2.5×10^-6 m²`, `I=12 A`, `V_nominal=120 V`.

- `R_loop = 1.68e-8 × 60 / 2.5e-6 = 0.4032 Ω`.
- `ΔV = 12 × 0.4032 = 4.8384 V`, or `4.032%` of 120 V.
- `P_loss = 12² × 0.4032 = 58.0608 W`.
- At `A=4.0 mm²` instead, `R_loop = 0.252 Ω`, `ΔV=3.024 V`, `P_loss=36.288 W`.
- With `α=0.0039/°C` and hypothetical metal temperature `70°C`, the 2.5 mm² reference resistance becomes `0.4032 × 1.195 = 0.481824 Ω`, `ΔV=5.781888 V`, `4.81824%`.

These are reference-temperature *material* calculations, not standardized wire sizes, allowable currents, or an approved installed cable design. The chosen 12 A is held fixed to expose changes in geometry and temperature.

##### Balanced sinusoidal AC approximation

For a balanced three-phase route, using `R` and `X` **per conductor per unit length** at the relevant operating temperature:

\[
\Delta U_{LL}\approx \sqrt{3}\,I\,L\,(R\cos\varphi+X\sin\varphi).
\]

A single-phase two-wire approximation has the analogous factor 2 for its two conductors. Source: Schneider Electric *Electrical Installation Guide*, *Calculation of voltage drop in steady load conditions*. These are approximate phasor formulas for stated conditions and do not fully describe unbalanced, nonlinear or dynamic equipment.

##### Competing load models

| Model | Current change when received voltage falls | Caveat |
|---|---|---|
| Constant resistance | Current falls approximately with voltage | Resistance may vary with temperature |
| Constant current | Current remains approximately fixed | Only over device operating range |
| Constant input power | Current rises approximately as inverse voltage | Control limits, power factor and harmonics affect the result |
| Direct-on-line motor during start | Potentially large, dynamic current | Source, motor, torque-speed and load models required |

##### Exercise: source versus line loss

A hypothetical source is `118 V` at the feeder origin under load; the cable drops `3 V`. The receiving voltage is approximately `115 V`. Replacing the cable with one that drops `1 V` raises receiving voltage to `117 V` **only if source voltage and load current remain the assumed values**. It cannot automatically recover a missing two volts caused upstream, and a real load could change its current after the replacement.

##### Additional questions

1. At fixed current, how do resistive drop and cable loss scale with doubled length? **Answer:** both double.
2. At fixed length and area, how does doubled current affect voltage drop and loss? **Answer:** drop doubles, loss quadruples in the fixed-resistance approximation.
3. A conductor passes its thermal check but fails the voltage-delivery objective. Is the thermal check contradicted? **No:** they are separate constraints.
4. Why calculate resistance at operating temperature? **Because:** metal resistivity changes with temperature, affecting drop and loss.
5. Why is a three-percent target not a universal rule? **Because:** its authority, edition, application and enforceability differ, while equipment voltage requirements vary.
6. Does adding an extra neutral guarantee lower drop for a phase-to-phase load? **No:** first establish the actual current path and circuit topology.

### Connections Under Pressure: Terminals, Torque, Splices, and Strain Relief

**Track:** Electrical Systems: Principles, Practice, Codes, and Judgment. **Learning surface:** component drawings, product data sheets, resistance calculations, and supervised training assemblies.

#### Non-spoken technical companion

##### Contact-resistance thought experiment

At a fixed current, a connector's local resistance `R_c` dissipates `P_c = I²R_c`. For `I = 20 A`:

| Hypothetical contact resistance | Heat at contact |
|---|---:|
| `2 mΩ = 0.002 Ω` | `0.8 W` |
| `20 mΩ = 0.020 Ω` | `8 W` |
| `100 mΩ = 0.100 Ω` | `40 W` |

These are **constructed arithmetic cases**, not acceptable field readings or real connector performance data. Current, resistance and temperature may change together; actual temperature depends on thermal pathways and duty cycle. A series high-resistance fault need not draw enough total current to trip an ordinary overcurrent device.

##### Why torque is an imperfect proxy for clamp force

A generic fastener approximation is `T ≈ K F d`, where `T` is applied torque, `F` an idealized bolt preload, `d` a characteristic fastener diameter, and `K` bundles friction and geometry. This relation is **not** an installation formula for electrical terminals. Actual terminal designs can use pressure plates, multiple contact points and non-bolt geometries. The manufacturer's product-specific method governs.

##### Compare connector categories on paper

| Connection type | Contact-force mechanism | Specification to retrieve |
|---|---|---|
| Screw/clamp terminal | Fastener, clamp, pressure plate | Conductor range and construction, terminal torque, conductor count |
| Spring-clamp connector | Elastic spring geometry | Allowed conductor type/range and insertion or release method |
| Crimp/compression lug | Defined plastic deformation | Connector, conductor, tool, die, crimp pattern; listing compatibility |
| Multi-conductor splice | Connector-specific mechanism | Number and combination of conductors; material and size |

A product-class comparison does not make the products mutually interchangeable.

##### Exercise: identify missing specifications (desk)

A drawing specifies a hypothetical `20 A` feeder using aluminum conductors into a control enclosure. The terminal product page gives a copper-only conductor range; its face has two screws, but no statement about multiple conductors per opening. Write a materials-query memo rather than choosing a torque. ##### Limits and counterexamples

- High conductor conductivity alone does not establish a low-contact-resistance joint.
- A correctly recorded torque is evidence of using the specified method; it is not a direct measurement of contact pressure or absence of future degradation.
- More force is not always better; excessive force can damage the assembly.
- Thermal scanning and continuity measurements require interpretation and may miss failure modes.
- An installation code's language, a connector test standard, and a particular manufacturer's instructions answer different questions; do not merge their scopes.
