# 022000 — When Earth Stops Being the Underdog

**Track:** Stargate SG-1: Through the Gate. **Audience:** Fans, with context for the series' evolving technology and alliances. **Prerequisites:** Basic familiarity with the Stargate program. **Scope:** Earth's changing military and diplomatic position, early vulnerability, the Asgard alliance, Prometheus, Ancient technology, the fleet era, the Ori conflict, and the Asgard inheritance. **Spoilers:** Major events from the pilot through *Unending*, including *Small Victories*, *Disclosure*, *Lost City*, *New Order*, *Camelot*, and the tenth-season finale.

# When Earth Stops Being the Underdog

In the early years of *Stargate SG-1*, Earth's principal defense against a hostile galaxy is a metal cover. A wormhole opens inside a mountain. The gate's surface fills with light. An officer waits for a radio code and a confirmation that the travelers are friendly. If the wrong people try to come through, the iris keeps them from reaching the room. There are armed guards, alarms and technicians who know exactly how bad an ordinary morning can become.

Earth has nuclear weapons, aircraft, laboratories and soldiers who are very good at improvising. It has almost nothing resembling an interstellar navy. A Goa'uld mothership does not need to win a fair fight against a fighter squadron. A single hostile arrival through the gate can put the personnel in the mountain at risk. When Jack O'Neill asks what Earth can do against a threat, there is often a short and unsatisfying answer. The team may have to find a way to stop the enemy before the enemy arrives.

Near the end of the series, Earth's people serve aboard a starship called the Odyssey. It travels between worlds. It carries technology made by a civilization that once seemed almost unimaginably advanced. Its officers can consider threats far beyond the gate room, and its weapons have become serious participants in interstellar battles. The mountain still exists, but it is no longer the whole boundary between Earth and the galaxy.

That change is one of *SG-1*'s great achievements in long-form storytelling. The series accumulates consequences. A technical discovery does not have to disappear after one episode. An alliance can become a ship, a ship can become a fleet, and a friendship with Thor can change humanity's place among older civilizations. The show allows its protagonists to grow powerful in a universe that was initially designed to make them feel small.

It also creates a difficult artistic problem. An underdog's escape is thrilling partly because escape is all they can reasonably hope to accomplish. Give that same underdog a battleship, and a victory may begin to look like the exercise of superior force. The question changes from whether Earth can survive contact to what Earth ought to do with the power it has acquired. And the answers are not automatically as charming as the people giving them.

## The defense of a single room

The Stargate's earliest adventures repeatedly establish the asymmetry between Earth and its enemies. SG-1 can enter a village occupied by Jaffa soldiers; the Jaffa may have better weapons and the backing of an empire. The team can study a mysterious device; the device may be capable of destroying the base if activated carelessly. The heroes do not understand the ancient technology they encounter well enough to treat it as an equipment upgrade.

This vulnerability is useful to the storytelling. The team must negotiate, deceive, hide, retreat and seek help. The characters' different habits become practical resources. Daniel may know a language or recognize a cultural pattern. Carter may find a physical constraint that makes an impossible escape possible. Teal'c understands the organization and limitations of the enemy's forces. Jack must decide which risks are worth taking when the available information is inadequate. The adventure is built around intelligence and character because those are the assets Earth can reliably supply.

The season-one ending provides a clean example. SG-1 has intelligence of an approaching Goa'uld invasion. The political authorities are prepared to close the Stargate program. The team goes through the gate anyway and ends up aboard an attacking ship. It cannot summon a human fleet or ask another cruiser to take a position alongside it. The story depends on a small group reaching the right place with limited resources and preventing a catastrophe that Earth cannot confront directly.

Even victories can conceal how fragile the position remains. When the Asgard become important allies, their protection is not identical with an Earth defense system. A treaty might discourage a Goa'uld from attacking, but its enforcement depends on the credibility and availability of another civilization. Earth can be safer without becoming independently capable of defending itself. That is a realistic distinction within the show's own imaginative terms: diplomatic protection is power, but it is not the same power as controlling the means of protection.

The iris is a symbol of the predicament. It can prevent some arrivals through the gate. It cannot decide whether a visitor should be believed. It cannot stop an approaching fleet. It cannot make a human team safe on another world. A door is an excellent defensive technology when the danger is trying to pass through that door. Once the scale of danger increases, the same door starts looking like a very small place to hide.

The series could have kept Earth in this position indefinitely. Each week the team might have discovered a different way to avert disaster, returning home as vulnerable as before. Instead, *SG-1* becomes interested in what repeated adventures would do to a society. If the gate is used for years, if reports are written and alliances maintained, if engineers can study devices rather than merely survive them, then humanity ought to learn something. The power of the later seasons comes partly from honoring that implication.

## Learning to borrow power

Earth's rise is often described as an escalation of technology. That is true, but it leaves out the relationships that make the technology accessible. Carter is brilliant, yet even Carter cannot invent the accumulated science of several galaxies over a long weekend. Daniel can translate an inscription, but he does not thereby acquire the manufacturing capacity to build what the inscription describes. The SGC's greatest leaps are usually assisted, negotiated, borrowed, adapted or inherited.

The Asgard alliance is the central example. Thor's appreciation for Jack and SG-1 develops through encounters in which the less advanced humans prove useful. *Small Victories* shows Carter persuading the Asgard to sacrifice a highly sophisticated vessel as part of a plan to defeat the Replicators. Jack and Teal'c deal with an infestation aboard a Russian submarine, where tactics that the Asgard might regard as primitive are urgently practical.

The episode is funny because the Asgard are capable of engineering miracles and still need help from people who sometimes solve problems by firing bullets at them. But the lesson is not that human cleverness has surpassed Asgard science. It is that expertise can become narrow when nearly every problem has been approached with the same inherited assumptions. Human visitors supply a different set of habits, and their willingness to act under uncertainty becomes valuable.

The relationship is reciprocal. The Asgard offer information, defense and later equipment that human engineers could not build unaided. Earth becomes more than a protected minor civilization because the older power begins treating its people as partners. That recognition is one reason *The Fifth Race* has such emotional force. It does not install an instant upgrade. It creates the expectation that humans may someday be able to stand alongside older civilizations and contribute something worth accepting.

There is a moral economy to this progression. Trust becomes a practical resource. A stolen weapon can be used immediately if you can operate it, but it may cost an alliance. An honest promise honored over years can result in help that no amount of theft would obtain. The distinction appears when the rogue NID operation steals technology from the Tollan and others. The SGC has to show its partners that it can be trusted not to treat the entire galaxy as an unattended weapons depot.

This is not pure generosity on either side. Earth wants advanced capabilities partly because it fears destruction. The Asgard want help fighting the Replicators. Both parties have interests. What makes the relationship compelling is that it can be mutual without being merely transactional. Thor likes these particular humans. Jack respects Thor even when he finds alien protocol an excellent target for jokes. Carter can learn from the Asgard while also telling them they have made a mistake. Partnership is not the same as submission to an older and wiser species.

## The day Earth builds a starship

The Prometheus is a landmark because it changes the physical premise of the series. Earth's first major interstellar warship, designated X-303, is constructed through a difficult mixture of human engineering and alien technology. It is not a pristine scientific achievement that has emerged from a classroom. Its systems have limitations. Its early history includes attempted hijacking and the messy politics of who controls a vessel built around knowledge acquired in secret.

The very name creates an appealing science-fiction image. Prometheus steals fire from the gods; Earth builds a ship with technology learned from civilizations that have been playing gods. It is a good name for a machine assembled by people whose curiosity and ambition have outgrown their original equipment. It also invites the question of what happens after the fire is stolen. Possessing it is not the same thing as knowing how to live with it.

A spaceship changes the narrative geography. The gate had previously supplied a remarkable route between particular fixed points. A ship can move between those points and reach places the network does not readily connect. It can rendezvous with allies, patrol space, rescue travelers and fight. It also gives Earth the means to appear above another society's planet with a military vessel. A capacity that is clearly protective when a mothership is approaching Earth may look very different when Earth is the visitor arriving from orbit.

The early Prometheus cannot simply erase the Goa'uld advantage. That restraint is important. It makes the ship feel like a first step, a risky prototype rather than an announcement that the protagonists have won the technology race. The crew does not stop needing alliances. The military does not stop needing Carter to explain a failure. Having a new machine can multiply the ways a mission goes wrong, because the crew is trying to operate an integrated system whose parts are not all products of the same scientific tradition.

*Disclosure* uses the ship to complicate the politics of the program. Other governments learn about the Stargate and object to the United States acquiring technological and strategic benefits in secret. Thor offers to enhance Prometheus with Asgard systems, and his endorsement helps maintain Hammond's leadership. The new warship is not simply a symbol of national pride. It is a bargaining object, an international concern, and a physical expression of an interstellar friendship.

Earth's technological development is accumulating faster than its political arrangements can comfortably absorb. The SGC began as a tightly controlled secret base. A fleet cannot be governed forever as if it were merely a larger iris. Ships require crews, bases, construction, supplies, diplomacy, and decisions about when force may be used. It is possible to build a faster drive before building satisfactory institutions for deciding where that drive should take an armed vessel.

## The ancient weapon under the ice

*Lost City* is the moment when the story's accumulated history becomes a large-scale defense of Earth. Jack absorbs Ancient knowledge, SG-1 seeks a crucial outpost, and the defense against Anubis culminates in Antarctica. The episode is spectacular because the payoff has been prepared over years. Ancient technology has appeared as mystery, temptation, danger and possibility. Here it provides the means to answer an invading fleet.

It is tempting to describe the result as humanity achieving technological parity with its enemies. The details say something more interesting. Earth survives because it discovers and uses equipment left behind by a far older civilization. The Ancient outpost and its weapons are not the product of a sudden breakthrough in human industry. Jack is endangered by the very knowledge that allows the device to be operated. His teammates are not merely excited by their new defensive capacity; they are trying to prevent the person they love from being lost inside it.

The defense is earned dramatically but inherited technologically. That distinction becomes the basis of the next stage of the series. Earth's government may possess access to the outpost, but questions remain about what it can operate, who should control the discovery, and how the rest of the world will respond. In *New Order*, international disagreements over Antarctica appear immediately. A weapon capable of repelling an invasion has become a subject of diplomatic contest.

The Goa'uld respond to the shift in power as well. The System Lords recognize that Earth has deployed a device they cannot easily defeat. Their representatives arrive seeking an arrangement against Ba'al. This is an extraordinary reversal. The empire that once threatened Earth now has factions willing to approach it as a power with something to offer. The humans have not become masters of the galaxy, but their reputation has changed the terms of negotiation.

That reversal is one of the series' funniest achievements. Earth did not necessarily invent the weapon, understand every part of it, or gain the ability to use it again on demand. Yet powerful enemies must take its demonstrated capabilities into account. Military reputation can grow faster than reliable capacity. The SGC is becoming a participant in a political system that other powers have navigated for centuries, and its choices have consequences far beyond the success or failure of a single reconnaissance mission.

The danger is that the possession of an Ancient weapon can make leaders act as if possession proves maturity. A tool that can protect a planet can also make it easier to dismiss the people who object to how the tool is used. The series sometimes celebrates the new status and sometimes shows its costs. It is strongest when it remembers that discovering a weapon and deserving authority over its use are separate achievements.

## A fleet does not make you invulnerable

As *SG-1* moves into its later seasons, the ships become more capable. The Daedalus-class vessels and the Odyssey stand for a new kind of Earth presence in space. They can travel, defend, and join other powers in large battles. Some of their capabilities are inherited or supplied by allies. Their existence nevertheless changes the show's visual grammar. Earlier, the great enemy ship was the thing looming over Earth. Now an Earth vessel may appear through hyperspace to answer a call for help.

The shift could have produced monotonous triumph. The series avoids that by introducing enemies who are not impressed by humanity's new fleet. At the end of season nine, *Camelot* stages a confrontation around an Ori Supergate. Earth and its allies assemble an armada containing advanced human vessels, Goa'uld ships, Jaffa forces, Asgard support and others who have their own reasons for resisting the invasion. They bring the hardware that years of the series have taught us to respect.

The Ori ships arrive and tear through that confidence. Their defenses and weapons outclass much of the assembled force. The result is painful because it is not the old image of four stranded explorers facing an enemy warship. It is a coalition of civilizations with serious capabilities discovering that it is still outmatched. Earth's rise has not ended the possibility of being the underdog; it has changed the scale on which defeat can occur.

There is a valuable humility in this. A warship can solve problems the gate room cannot solve. It cannot resolve every conflict created by a galaxy full of powers. An alliance can put more hulls in space. It cannot guarantee that allies share the same strategy or that the enemy has not crossed a technological threshold none of them anticipated.

The Ori conflict also changes the meaning of heroic action. An SG team can rescue one village through personal courage and improvisation. In a fleet engagement, decisions about what to defend and what to sacrifice may involve entire populations. The camera can still find the crew on a bridge, and the characters can still trade remarks that sound like themselves. But their judgments now reach farther than the line of sight of a soldier standing beside a Stargate.

For some viewers, this is a loss. The original pleasure of the show was a small band of travelers stepping into an unknown place. More ships, more weapons and more galactic stakes can crowd out the intimacy of those discoveries. That criticism has force. The series can sometimes seem to replace wonder with inventory, as if another upgraded vessel proves that the adventure has become more important.

Yet the change also keeps the series from pretending its characters have learned nothing. If the first years establish that Earth will study, adapt and build, then later years must acknowledge what those efforts produced. The challenge for the writers is to find new forms of uncertainty without arbitrarily taking every achievement away. *Camelot* succeeds because it does not erase Earth's gains. It shows that those gains are insufficient against a newly encountered threat.

## An inheritance too heavy to carry easily

The tenth-season finale *Unending* gives Earth's growth an ending that is both generous and devastating. The Asgard, facing the collapse of their own future, decide to end their civilization. Before they do, they transfer a vast inheritance of technology and knowledge to the Odyssey. The alliance that began with a little gray visitor taking an interest in a stubborn human officer becomes an act of civilizational trust.

It would be easy to stage this solely as a triumphant upgrade. The human ship receives new capabilities. The older civilization has recognized humanity as a successor. After years of trying to reach the Asgard's level, Earth has received a gift that makes it a genuine custodian of their achievements.

But the episode places the gift beside its cost. The Asgard are gone. The transfer is an act of farewell, not a ceremony announcing Earth's graduation from apprentice to master. An ally's departure creates a burden that no improved weapons system can offset. Humanity has gained a library, but it has lost the people who could answer a question about why a particular page was written.

Then the Odyssey faces an Ori attack. To survive, Carter activates a time-dilation field. The crew is trapped together while time outside the ship scarcely moves. The crisis that begins as another engineering problem becomes a lifetime. The people inside age, love, argue, lose, and continue living within a vessel that contains a civilization's legacy. Carter keeps working on the problem of escape. Teal'c eventually carries memories of the years that the others will not retain in the same way.

The episode is a remarkable answer to the question of what Earth's power has bought. A warship can protect a planet, and an inherited archive can transform scientific understanding. Neither can remove the human experience of time and loss. The crew can possess technology almost beyond imagining and still find itself confined to a set of rooms, waiting for the person who can solve the problem to finish. The Odyssey has become another gate room, except that the walls contain a much larger portion of the galaxy's history.

The inheritance from the Asgard is also politically different from conquest. Earth is not entitled to the archive because it can threaten the donor. It receives the gift because an older civilization has chosen to entrust it. That makes human power the result not only of ingenuity and military success but of moral reputation. The farewell demands that the recipients be worthy of trust after the people who placed it in them can no longer intervene.

## The next address

There is an obvious story one can tell about the technological rise of Earth: first the iris, then stronger weapons, then Prometheus, then the fleet, then the Asgard archive. It is satisfying because it resembles progress in a game. The character acquires equipment, survives a harder opponent, and reaches the next level.

*Stargate SG-1* is more interesting than that sequence when it follows the consequences. New technology changes how other civilizations perceive Earth. It can make diplomacy possible or make a threat credible. It creates a reason for rivals to steal, for governments to demand access, and for allies to wonder whether humanity is developing the judgment to match its capabilities. It changes the cost of a mistake. A reckless visitor with a rifle can endanger a village. A reckless captain with a warship can endanger worlds.

The series' moral center remains a small group of people willing to argue with one another. Carter cannot make a technological capability good merely by explaining how it works. Daniel cannot make a civilization just by translating its founding myths. Jack cannot make every risk acceptable by being willing to take it personally. Teal'c understands that defeating a tyrant is not the same as ensuring freedom for those the tyrant once controlled. They need one another precisely because technology enlarges the consequences of acting on a partial view.

And sometimes the best gift Earth can offer is still not a weapon. It can be a promise kept, a person rescued, an ally treated as a friend, or a practical solution an older power has overlooked. Those achievements are harder to measure than the speed of a hyperdrive, but they are what bring the Asgard to the point of trusting humanity with their legacy.

At the start, the iris is an act of fear: a narrow defense against a universe too powerful to face. At the end, an Earth ship carries the remnants of another civilization's knowledge through that same universe. Both images have value. The first makes danger intimate. The second gives accumulated history a physical weight.

Earth stops being the underdog in some battles. It never stops being a civilization with reasons to be careful. The Stargate remains open to places whose people are older, stranger, more vulnerable, or more dangerous than anyone on Earth can fully anticipate. The strongest version of the series' optimism is not that humanity will eventually have the biggest ship. It is that, after years of discovering how much force it can wield, humanity might still care about what waits on the other side of the gate.
