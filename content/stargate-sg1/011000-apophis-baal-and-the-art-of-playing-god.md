# 011000 — Apophis, Ba'al, and the Art of Playing God

**Track:** Stargate SG-1: Through the Gate. **Audience:** Fans, with an explanation of the Goa'uld premise. **Prerequisites:** The basic Stargate premise. **Scope:** The Goa'uld as performers, parasites, rulers, rivals and declining imperial powers, with Apophis and Ba'al as contrasting villains. **Spoilers:** Major plot developments from the pilot through season ten, including *The Enemy Within*, *Fair Game*, *Summit*, *Abyss* and *Insiders*.

# Apophis, Ba'al, and the Art of Playing God

Imagine being told that your god has arrived. There are soldiers at the door, not because a priest expects a visitor, but because a man with a gold ornament on his forehead has brought armed men who cannot afford to doubt him. The god walks through the room as if every body there already belongs to him. He wears an expression of complete entitlement. He can command a beam of light from an ornament on his hand, and the command will be obeyed by something that looks to its witnesses like a miracle. Even his voice seems to come from somewhere larger than the body in front of you.

Now imagine the camera moving close enough to disclose the terrible little joke. The god is an animal inside that man's body. He knows how to operate weapons and machines, he can command an army, and he has arranged for generations of people not to know the difference between a machine and a miracle.

This is one of the best decisions *Stargate SG-1* inherited from the original *Stargate* film and expanded into a television mythology. Its chief enemies do not merely have the wrong religion. They have engineered a social order in which telling the truth is dangerous, ignorance is policy, and the people who perform divinity possess enough real power to make disbelief costly.

The Goa'uld are often deliciously theatrical. Their eyes flash. Their voices deepen. They dress as if every day requires a personal triumphal procession. They announce themselves with more confidence than tact. The television series can make them frightening, repulsive, absurd, and even funny without changing their central function: they rule by controlling what other people are permitted to understand.

Two of the most effective villains show different stages of that arrangement. Peter Williams's Apophis gives the early series an enemy whose godhood seems absolute until someone refuses it. Cliff Simon's Ba'al arrives in a galaxy where the old theatrical tricks are increasingly exposed, and he discovers that losing the sacred costume need not mean losing the pleasure of domination. Taken together, they tell the story of an empire built on pretending to be something it is not, and of the violence required to preserve the pretense.

## What a Goa'uld actually steals

Before the Goa'uld can be a metaphor for anything, they are a specific horror story. The symbiote enters a host, takes control of the body, and can use that person's face, voice and memories while suppressing the person who lived there first. The host does not simply become a villain. The host is an occupied person, sometimes fully aware and unable to act.

The early episode *The Enemy Within* makes the mechanism brutally intimate. Charles Kawalsky is not a stranger wearing a convenient mask. He is Jack O'Neill's friend. His face returns through the gate, recognizable, still carrying the emotional weight of service and shared danger. Something inside him has plans of its own. The SGC tries to separate the parasite from the man and cannot obtain a clean rescue. The threat is not merely infiltration; it is that your friend can become a vehicle for someone else's intention while still looking like your friend.

That distinction changes how the show's villains ought to be viewed. The antagonist usually introduced as Apophis is a Goa'uld using a human host. The antagonist in a stolen body is not identical with the body. When the series sometimes forgets to let us see the prisoner behind the monster, it loses one of its own strongest premises. When it remembers, the harm is more intimate than any exploding starship.

Consider Skaara, the young man from Abydos whose friendship with Jack gives the opening adventure a personal center. The Goa'uld Klorel takes Skaara as a host. Jack's objection to that theft cannot be reduced to defeating another member of a hostile faction. He wants the person who used to answer him to be able to answer again. *Pretense*, with its hearing over who may control Skaara's body, gives that desire an institutional shape: a court can ask what possession means even if it cannot easily undo the experience.

The Goa'uld rely on another stolen relationship as well. The Jaffa carry larval symbiotes. Their immune systems become dependent on that arrangement, binding their health and the survival of their children to the imperial system they serve. The Jaffa are not mere believers who could quit the moment someone gave them a persuasive lecture. Their rulers have made their bodies dependent on a technology of domination. The system trains soldiers, creates dependence, and calls the arrangement sacred duty.

This is why the claim to godhood has teeth. The Goa'uld do not only require worship. They organize reproduction, military service, medicine, punishment, and hope around the idea that a god's command is reality. A skeptical Jaffa can be brave and still have to ask where their child will receive a symbiote. A human villager may cease to believe and still be standing in front of an armed patrol. A theatrical ruler is not harmless because the theater has been exposed.

## Apophis knows how to enter a room

Apophis is particularly good at being seen. His costuming and ceremonial gestures make him legible even before we know which other System Lords exist. Peter Williams plays a man who expects the world to defer, and whose anger at disobedience is less an emotional surprise than an administrative response. The voice announces that something greater has spoken; the body makes it clear that the voice is not especially interested in listening.

The strength of that performance becomes apparent when Teal'c turns against him in *Children of the Gods*. Teal'c has been Apophis's First Prime, not a confused peasant encountering a startling device. He has enforced the system for years. He knows its weapons, its cruelty, and the price of disloyalty. When he frees the prisoners instead of murdering them, he has not discovered a clever fact about technology. He has decided to commit treason against the order that organized his adult life.

Apophis's authority must be immense for that decision to have meaning. If he were merely a fool whom anyone could see through, Teal'c's choice would be smaller. The series lets him be arrogant and occasionally strategically incompetent, but he presides over a structure with armies, ships, prisons, wealth and long habits of obedience. Other people have to do the hard work of breaking it. One declaration that he is no god does not make his soldiers disappear.

There is a contradiction in the show's portrayal of Apophis that can be dramatically useful. His self-presentation claims eternal, indivisible power. His conduct reveals fear, rivalry and an appetite for territory. He can be humiliated; he can lose a battle; he can need another Goa'uld's forces. At times he looks less like an immortal god than an ambitious nobleman with poor impulse control. Yet that is precisely what an earthly empire might produce after generations of rulers have been told that their desires are divine commands.

Apophis has a recurring personal stake in destroying SG-1. The team does not just defeat his soldiers. It demonstrates, publicly and repeatedly, that a god can be resisted. Teal'c's defection is a military loss, but it is also a contagious idea. Apophis cannot allow people to see that the First Prime of Apophis now serves the enemies of Apophis. He keeps responding as if humiliation itself were an attack on the cosmic order.

There is pleasure in watching that composure crack. The performance needs our awareness of the gap between grandeur and insecurity. A Goa'uld can be a grave threat and also a pompous tyrant who cannot stand being contradicted by a sarcastic colonel. *SG-1* does not always balance those tones perfectly, but the combination helps the threat endure across years of television. Pure solemnity would make these golden thrones hard to revisit. Pure parody would dissolve the prisoners and soldiers who are suffering under them.

## A galaxy of competing heavens

The System Lords allow the series to turn a single antagonist into a political world. The Goa'uld adopt names and imagery taken from a number of human mythologies. The show's fictional explanation is that rulers on Earth helped generate or appropriate stories that later became religious traditions. For its plots, that gives a recognizable face to otherwise distant aliens; for the scriptwriters, it provides a supply of names, iconography and rivalries.

This is also one of the show's shortcuts. Complex historical religions are treated as a collection of costumes worn by predatory extraterrestrials. Egyptian imagery is the foundation, but the series reaches into other traditions with little sustained interest in what those traditions meant to their practitioners. It is possible to enjoy the audacity of this pulp premise and still recognize the simplification. The interesting fictional idea is not that human belief has been disproved. It is that any regime which forbids investigation can turn a possession of technology into a claim on the soul.

Once the Goa'uld have rival houses, the galaxy acquires a politics. They make treaties because a shared enemy is dangerous, betray treaties because power shifts, and form alliances designed to keep someone else from becoming too strong. In *Fair Game*, the Asgard mediate a proposed non-aggression treaty between Earth and several System Lords. Suddenly the familiar people in gold robes are not only targets for the team's weapons. They are participants in a negotiation with leverage, demands, and rival suspicions.

The price demanded of Earth is revealing. The System Lords want to prevent technological development that could threaten them and to end Earth's use of the Stargate. They are not primarily worried about theological disagreement. They are worried about competition. They recognize, more clearly than some of their worshippers can, that the sources of their advantage can be studied and reproduced.

The treaty negotiation also produces the memorable spectacle of Nirrti using deception against other Goa'uld. Earth survives not by becoming stronger in the room, but by learning the shape of the rivals' mistrust. At least for that episode, diplomacy is something more than an interval between fights. The team discovers that the empire is not a single pyramid. It is a collection of rulers trying to use one another without being used.

*Summit* makes that system almost absurdly literal. Daniel Jackson infiltrates a gathering of System Lords while the Tok'ra plan to poison them. We encounter the Goa'uld as a dangerous collection of personalities meeting to preserve their interests. They have accumulated immense power and still require a conference. The threat from Anubis exposes the limits of the old coalition. If the strongest ruler can eat the others' territories, old legitimacy is little protection.

For a show about ancient gods, these scenes are refreshingly material. Somebody controls ships. Somebody possesses intelligence. Somebody wants an army. Somebody has lost an ally and must decide whether to bargain. Empire is a set of arrangements between people with resources, not merely the ability to say an impressive word in a deeper voice.

## Ba'al and the polite cruelty of an expert

Ba'al is frightening in a different register. Cliff Simon rarely needs Apophis's full ceremonial volume. His Ba'al can converse, smile, and behave as though a negotiation is taking place while holding all the instruments of violence. Sometimes he seems to be enjoying an exchange with Jack more than the practical information he might obtain from it.

*Abyss* is the essential case. Jack has been briefly joined with the Tok'ra Kanan. Kanan used Jack's body to return to a world where he had left a woman endangered. The mission fails; the symbiote abandons Jack, and Ba'al captures him. Jack is tortured in a chamber whose gravity field turns imprisonment into a scientific instrument. Ba'al kills him repeatedly and uses a sarcophagus to bring him back, so that another round can begin.

The sarcophagus is more than a convenient reset button. It shows the underside of one of the Goa'uld's great advantages. A ruler who can reverse death can also make death an inadequate refuge from torture. An invention that might have been a medical miracle becomes an instrument for prolonging a captive's dependence on the captor. Ba'al has taken control not only of Jack's movement but of the apparent end of Jack's suffering.

The episode uses Daniel, now ascended, as a counterweight. He can visit Jack but cannot simply intervene with the physical force that an earlier teammate might have provided. Their conversations are painful partly because Jack is not interested in being offered philosophical serenity by someone who can leave the room. He is being hurt again, now. Daniel's promise of another way out does not erase the realities of the cell.

The story asks whether the possession of great power changes the definition of cruelty. For Ba'al, it plainly does not. He understands the physical and psychological machinery of the procedure. He can see Jack's pain. He continues. The simple fact that Ba'al is technically sophisticated makes him more culpable, not more civilized.

Cliff Simon's performance supports that reading. Ba'al's small reactions suggest someone who feels comfortably superior even when the conversation turns insulting. Jack's sarcasm is both a defense and a challenge. The villain has to endure being spoken to as a man rather than a god, and because he has the power to punish that insult, the banter never entirely becomes harmless comedy.

As the later seasons give Ba'al more space, he adapts to the decline of the old System Lords. He is capable of bargaining with enemies, operating under cover on Earth, and pursuing advantages in situations where godly pageantry would only make him more conspicuous. In *Insiders*, the series exploits his cloning scheme: a number of apparently identical Ba'als enter the story, each potentially lying about who is the original and what the others intend. The setup is funny because the character's confidence appears to be multiplying uncontrollably. It is ominous because a villain who can reproduce his own face makes identity another tactical resource.

The clone plot is not profound by itself. It does, however, expose a truth about this villain. Ba'al's deepest allegiance has always been to Ba'al. A system of divine monarchy is easy to advocate when the monarch is oneself. Give him a chance to escape the collapse of the old hierarchy and he will be willing to change almost everything except his assumption that other lives are for his use.

## What the sarcophagus cannot repair

There is a tendency in science fiction to connect technology with progress as though the two were the same process. The Goa'uld demonstrate otherwise. They have interstellar travel, advanced medicine, energy weapons, and astonishing industrial capacity. Their institutions are organized around domination, secrecy and inheritance. Innovation is constrained by the need to preserve the mystique of rulers who already know everything worth knowing.

The sarcophagus gives this contrast its most intimate form. It preserves bodies while the series associates repeated dependence on it with damaging effects on personality and judgment. The Tok'ra reject routine sarcophagus use in part for those reasons. A system built to preserve the privileged can gradually deform the lives of the people it preserves.

The same is true at political scale. The Goa'uld have lived long enough to forget that their power was assembled. Their history of conquest can appear to them like a natural law. The series keeps returning to the fragility of that illusion: a stolen device can be understood; a small rebellion can survive; a Jaffa can refuse to obey; a human host can be released. But the collapse of godhood is not necessarily the collapse of a fleet. Exposure is the beginning of liberation, not its completion.

It helps that the show rarely gives the Goa'uld a unified plan for living after their lie stops working. Individual rulers might seek a less overt form of power, especially Ba'al. The System Lords as an institution keep trying to restore the vanished agreement that their own dominance should be taken for granted. They are formidable at punishing dissent and less impressive at understanding a social movement that cannot be killed by assassinating its leader.

There is a risk in celebrating their downfall too cleanly. Millions of humans and Jaffa have been recruited or forced into the imperial apparatus. Some serve for survival. Others have become devoted beneficiaries, commanders, or collaborators. Some have committed terrible acts. When the center fails, the consequences fall on people whose lives have been formed by it. A victorious attack on a mothership does not answer how to organize the soldiers who survive, or who will govern territories formerly assigned to the defeated System Lord. Teal'c and Bra'tac's political struggle addresses a problem that the spectacular battles cannot settle.

Nor does Earth's possession of powerful new weapons automatically produce better governance. The series occasionally enjoys its heroes' access to technology so much that it lets us forget how familiar the temptation should be. A civilization that can destroy other worlds may find it easy to describe that capacity as a moral entitlement. The Goa'uld are not a warning that technology is evil. They are a warning about what happens when technological superiority is permitted to stand in for legitimate authority.

## How to win a scene you have already lost

The villainy of Apophis and Ba'al belongs partly to the writing and partly to the way two actors occupy space. Williams lets Apophis carry the memory of ceremonies and worship. Simon lets Ba'al carry the expectation that any conversation can be turned into a transaction, and any person into a hostage. Both have the occasional capacity to look absurd; neither is only a joke.

They also provide different kinds of conflict for SG-1. Against Apophis, the team is often trying to break a public spell: show the prisoners that the god can bleed, the Jaffa that commands can be refused, the planet that a threat can be resisted. Against Ba'al, the conflict becomes more personal and procedural. His influence can survive in secret, in an agreement, in a device, even in a copy of himself. A theatrical empire has learned to negotiate, infiltrate and improvise.

This change keeps the Goa'uld from being merely one season's obstacle repeated with larger ships. Their mythology shifts as Earth learns. The heroes stop being astonished by glowing eyes. Their enemy must find newer ways to intimidate people who no longer automatically tremble. The fact that the old methods no longer work as reliably is itself a sign of political change. Teal'c's choice in the pilot has spread from an impossible betrayal into a movement.

I think the villains are at their most interesting when the series remembers that the enslaved host is still somewhere inside the spectacle. A Goa'uld's personal cleverness is not a defense. His vast reign is not a defense. Apophis's grand entrance and Ba'al's patient smile are compelling television because something human can be taken away behind them. The camera can enjoy the costume without endorsing the power it represents.

The deepest reversal *SG-1* offers is therefore not that gods turn out to be aliens. That was the premise almost from the start. It is that a god who has been obeyed for centuries may have no better claim to command than a frightened animal who has found a weapon and convinced everyone else that the weapon is the universe's final word.

The Stargate opens, and somebody walks through who is prepared to tell him no. Once that becomes possible, the ending of an empire is imaginable. Making it happen is another story entirely.

The show fictionalizes and combines imagery drawn from distinct historical religious traditions; these are its own representations. The episode facts and credits are distinguishable from this essay's reading of theatrical rulership, technology, coercion and power. Claims about motives visible in performances are interpretations; no undocumented actor or writer interview is attributed here.
