# 019000 — Heroes: Who Gets to Tell the Story of a Death?

**Track:** Stargate SG-1: Through the Gate. **Audience:** SG-1 fans interested in close readings and television form. **Prerequisites:** Familiarity with Stargate Command and the original cast. **Scope:** *Heroes, Parts 1 and 2*, documentary construction, military secrecy, medical sacrifice, and the episode's production history. **Spoilers:** Complete spoilers for both parts of *Heroes*, including Janet Fraiser's death; limited discussion of other seasons.

# Heroes: Who Gets to Tell the Story of a Death?

There is a camera inside Stargate Command, and almost nobody wants it there.

This is a curious objection for a show that has been inviting us into Stargate Command for seven years. We have watched the gate turn from almost every angle. We have followed officers down corridors, observed private conversations, witnessed events on distant planets, and seen things that the American president may not yet know. As viewers, we have enjoyed privileged access to the most secret operation on Earth without ever needing to explain what gives us the right to be there.

Then, in *Heroes*, a documentary filmmaker named Emmett Bregman arrives with a camera crew and asks to record what happens at the base. Suddenly access is a problem. Jack O'Neill avoids him. General Hammond tries to keep him within strict limits. Other personnel submit to interviews with varying degrees of amusement or hostility. The question that would normally seem absurd becomes central: what right does this person have to see what we see?

The two-part seventh-season story, written by Robert C. Cooper and directed by Andy Mikita, first aired in February 2004. It is remembered for killing Doctor Janet Fraiser, one of the series' most beloved supporting characters. That memory is justified, but incomplete. *Heroes* is not simply an ordinary Stargate mission that ends in an unexpected death. Its form is built around the tension between watching, recording, withholding, and remembering. The episode prepares us to want a camera's access, then makes us consider the responsibility that comes with it.

A recording can protect the truth from being hidden. It can also expose someone's worst moment to people who did not earn the right to share it. *Heroes* is interested in both possibilities. Its great achievement is that it does not answer the problem by declaring either the soldier or the journalist morally superior.

## An outsider in a familiar room

Emmett Bregman is played by Saul Rubinek. He is quick, impatient, often funny, and willing to make himself thoroughly unwelcome. He has not sneaked into the mountain. His project has presidential authorization: a documentary intended to record the Stargate program for a future in which its existence may become public. The project also has a political dimension. The outgoing administration would prefer the history of the program not to be written entirely by its enemies or successors.

This is important because the film is not a simple act of democratic transparency. It has been commissioned by power. Its subject is an enormous secret whose public has never been consulted about its creation. The people controlling Bregman's access are also the people whose actions he is supposed to evaluate. He cannot merely wave an authorization letter and become independent of them.

At first the resulting scenes are mostly comic. Jack, who has spent years deflecting questions from superior officers, proves very good at deflecting a journalist. Daniel treats the visit as an opportunity to be awkward in new ways. The camera records waiting, empty corridors, and the baffled expressions of personnel who are not sure what the visitor wants. Bregman discovers that an organization can cooperate with a documentary while making sure it films nothing significant.

This is a funny reversal of the usual television contract. In most episodes, if the action is happening in the gate room, the camera is there. When a Jaffa opens fire, we see the firefight. When somebody is wounded, we see the consequence. Bregman's crew may be told that something is happening and still be forced to stand in a corridor, listening to the alarm from a place where nobody will explain it.

The audience now has a strange double perspective. We know enough about the SGC to see why the guards are cautious. We also know that Bregman is asking reasonable questions. An institution capable of conducting operations on other planets does not become exempt from examination simply because its employees are brave.

Bregman's character keeps the tension lively because he can be obnoxious. If he were infinitely courteous and patient, every refusal would seem automatically unjust. If he were a cheap caricature of a predatory reporter, every refusal would seem wise. Rubinek gives him enough vanity, persistence, intelligence, and anger that the audience has to keep revising its judgment.

## A thousand journeys and one ordinary crew

The documentary has been arranged around a milestone: the Stargate program's thousandth trip through the gate. It is the sort of number an institution likes to celebrate. A thousand trips implies competence, accumulated knowledge, a history worth commemorating. It can be converted into a ceremonial account of service and achievement.

The episode promptly complicates that ceremony by taking us to the sort of mission a ceremony might omit. SG-13 investigates ruins on another world. Its commander is Colonel Dave Dixon, played by Adam Baldwin. Its members are capable professionals with their own habits, jokes, and concerns. A young airman, Simon Wells, carries an ultrasound picture of the baby he and his wife are expecting. His fellow soldiers tease him about it.

This small interlude does an extraordinary amount of work. The people on SG-13 are not anonymous casualties waiting for the leading team to arrive and rescue them. They are occupying the same kind of story SG-1 usually occupies, including the familiar mix of archaeological excitement, ordinary military boredom, and banter. We have merely joined it late.

The ruins appear interesting to the expedition's scientist. Dixon replies in the weary spirit of a man who has seen more old alien architecture than he ever expected to. It is a joke the series could have given Jack. Here it belongs to somebody else. That relocation changes the implied size of the world. The Stargate program does not stop existing when the camera leaves SG-1.

Then the mission goes wrong. Hostile Jaffa attack. Wells is badly wounded. Reinforcements are sent, including SG-1 and a medical team led by Janet Fraiser. The cheerful picture of an unborn child has moved from a conversation about a future family into the reason the audience dreads what might happen next.

The episode does not need to lecture us about military sacrifice. It has shown us the kind of ordinary future the endangered airman wants to have. The danger of naming a future too clearly in a television drama is obvious: we begin to expect that it will be taken away. But *Heroes* handles the promise with more care than that. The picture will not turn out to be a cheap announcement of a death. It is one way of establishing why saving someone is worth the risk.

## Two investigations, two kinds of suspicion

After the rescue, a death has occurred. Bregman wants to know who, and what happened. Richard Woolsey, newly introduced as a government investigator, arrives to examine the decisions that led to the mission. The two inquiries are easy to lump together as harassment of grieving professionals. Both men ask uncomfortable questions at a moment when the personnel of Stargate Command desperately want to be left alone.

But they are not asking quite the same questions. Woolsey wants an accounting of command decisions, risk, and responsibility. Bregman wants a visible account of a life and a death. One investigation belongs to official oversight; the other belongs to testimony and public memory. Both can be necessary. Both can become abusive.

Woolsey has a difficult job in a difficult institution. If an officer made the wrong call and someone died, the fact that the officer is likable and the mission was dangerous does not settle the matter. Viewers may dislike Woolsey's tone, and he is vulnerable to the political agendas of those who sent him, but the basic question is legitimate. Who answers for a decision when a person cannot come home to argue about it?

Bregman's problem is stranger. He is looking for evidence that the episode itself possesses but refuses to give to us. We know Jack O'Neill has been seriously wounded. We see signs of grief. The camera and the editing encourage us to join Bregman in trying to reconstruct a missing event. His curiosity is now our curiosity.

This is an ingenious use of a television audience's learned habits. After seven seasons, we know Jack's importance to the series. If he is hurt and the identity of the dead person is being concealed, we can be led to fear that the worst possible thing has happened to him. He becomes a plausible answer to the question because the story has trained us to think of him as central.

The revelation will not simply correct that mistaken inference. It will make us aware of the inference itself. Why did we assume that the death belonged to the person most important to our viewing experience? The episode is not saying that Jack deserves less concern. It is asking why the camera's old hierarchy of importance should become a hierarchy of the value of lives.

## The recording Daniel did not make for us

Daniel Jackson has footage of the mission. He recorded it because the wounded Wells wanted to leave a message for his pregnant wife. In the field, a camera briefly became a means of communication between a person in danger and someone waiting at home.

That purpose is specific and intimate. Wells did not ask to make a documentary for the public. He asked for a message that would survive him if he could not. Daniel helped because he understood the request. When Bregman presses for the tape afterward, Daniel resists. He is not merely protecting classified military information. He is protecting an object created in a moment of fear, under a promise shaped by trust.

On the footage, Janet is treating Wells. She is direct, reassuring, and wholly concentrated on keeping him alive. She tells him in effect that he will make it home. She has not come through the Stargate to let him die on the ground. Then a Jaffa weapon strikes her, and the doctor who was attempting to save him is killed.

There is no elaborate cinematic preparation for her death within the recording. It is a violent interruption of a medical task. One moment she is attending to a patient; the next she is gone. The camera does not choose a noble angle or wait for an expression of acceptance. The film's brutal power comes from refusing the protections fiction usually extends to a beloved character at the instant of sacrifice.

Of course, we should not confuse this designed effect with actual documentary accident. Actors performed it, a director staged it, a writer placed it within the narrative, and editors controlled when we would see it. The absence of ceremonial language is itself an artistic choice. The scene is not a raw window onto history. It is a carefully made representation of what an unprepared observer might witness.

The distinction is essential to the episode's intelligence. Bregman wants footage because it can show something a formal report cannot. But the recorded image is not self-interpreting. The circumstances of its making and the choice to display it are part of its meaning.

Daniel eventually gives Bregman permission to use the footage. He has remembered Janet's care during his own most desperate medical crisis. He realizes that a recording of her dying while trying to save Wells might also be a record of what she did for people, and of what others have had too little opportunity to see. The choice is painful, and the episode does not erase that pain by presenting publication as uncomplicated virtue.

## A filmmaker who learns when not to film

Bregman is insistent because he believes that people who do secret, dangerous work must not disappear into secret records. An institution can bury embarrassment under classification. It can also bury courage, suffering, and moral responsibility. The dead deserve an account not written entirely by the bureaucracy that employed them.

But before Daniel changes his mind, Bregman offers to return the tape without using it. That reversal is one of the episode's most important acts. A journalist who pursues access at all costs might get a sensational film. Bregman becomes worth trusting when he recognizes that acquiring a recording does not settle whether he should broadcast it.

There is a good question hiding inside that decision. What does he owe to Janet, who cannot consent to the use of the image? What does he owe to Cassandra, her daughter? To the colleagues who survived? To the public, if the program's existence one day becomes public? To Wells and his wife, whose intended message has been captured alongside someone else's death?

*Heroes* does not stage a formal hearing to answer each question. It gives us people making imperfect judgments in conversation. Daniel has emotional standing but cannot speak for everyone affected. Bregman has professional obligations but no claim to own the grief of strangers. Hammond has authority but has participated in the secrecy the filmmaker is examining. Each needs something from the others.

This gives the episode a much more adult account of truth-telling than the slogan that a picture is worth a thousand words. A picture may tell us something essential. The decision to show it can still be cruel or necessary, and sometimes both.

The documentary frame also reveals the viewers' own position. We have been demanding the answer along with Bregman. When the answer comes, we may wish we had not wanted to see it so badly. The series has turned a routine desire for narrative resolution into a question about the appetite that brought us to the episode.

## Why the first hour has room for jokes

One striking feature of *Heroes* is the amount of time devoted to awkwardness, small talk, and comparatively low-stakes comedy before the story becomes terrible. In a conventional two-part adventure, every spare moment might be spent raising the power of the enemy or explaining a weapon. Here a journalist wanders around a base being mildly irritating. An off-world commander teases his men. The world appears able to go on having ordinary conversations even when nothing history-changing is taking place.

The difference in tone makes the loss hurt, but that is not its only function. The first hour gives the people at the SGC lives that exist independently of tragedy. Janet can eat a meal with Bregman and exchange thoughts with him as a person, not merely appear in a hospital scene whose job is to establish how much everyone will miss her. Dixon and Wells can enjoy their own running jokes. The audience is permitted to spend time with the institution rather than only with the famous protagonists.

There is also a production story behind the two-part structure. Cooper has recalled that *Heroes* began as a single episode and was developed into two parts after a version ran substantially longer than its broadcast slot. Additional material was written and photographed later. Joseph Mallozzi has similarly described extra scenes and an expanded structure rather than simply padding an existing story. These are creators' retrospective accounts, not an invitation to identify a particular scene as an afterthought by looking at it.

The result works because the additional room lets an alternative point of view mature. Bregman does not have to win our sympathy in the first five minutes. The restrictions on his camera become comprehensible by repetition. The off-world mission is allowed to have a life of its own. When the second half concentrates on injury, inquiry, and loss, the ordinary life of the first half remains in our minds.

As a standalone television piece, the structure demands patience. Some viewers may find Bregman aggravating for too long, and the documentary conceit does not always make the transition between the show's normal camera and the film crew's camera perfectly distinct. Those are fair costs. The slow first half has more purpose than simply delaying an action scene. It establishes what a story about people could look like before one of those people becomes a name on a casualty report.

## The eulogy as a different kind of inventory

At the memorial service, Carter faces a task that resists the sort of achievement list often used to honor a soldier or physician. Janet's importance cannot be reduced to ranks earned, diseases treated, or missions completed. Carter speaks of lives that continue because Janet helped preserve them.

The list changes the scale of the episode again. We have watched a television series whose default measure of accomplishment is a planet saved, a device recovered, an enemy defeated. A military doctor has saved individual people in rooms we have often visited and sometimes barely noticed. Those people have birthdays, relatives, ambitions, and further days in which they might do good for somebody else.

The memorial invites us to count what remains alive rather than only what has been lost. This is more precise than a general claim that Janet was kind or brave. It attaches her character to consequences. A patient left the infirmary because she worked. Another received one more chance because she refused to stop. Wells returns to his family because she stayed to treat him under fire.

The child Wells and his wife welcome is named Janet. That gesture could easily have become an excess of sentimental closure. In the context of this story, it has an additional role. A photograph of an unborn child brought Wells into the episode. The child's arrival later shows that the life Janet saved did not end when the mission ended. The future in that small photograph remains possible, though the person who preserved it cannot be there to see it.

This does not balance an equation in which one life has been subtracted and another added. There is no compensating arithmetic for grief. It is a statement about consequence. The rescue did not make the death worthwhile by some universal standard, but it shows us what Janet was trying to do, and what she succeeded in doing before she died.

The episode allows its characters to mourn without converting each reaction into a formal speech. Teal'c offers Carter help in finding words. Daniel struggles with the recording. Jack, whose injury fueled the audience's earlier fear, survives into a story in which his survival is not the moral center. Hammond must live with a command that can never guarantee all its personnel will return.

## The discomfort of an excellent trick

There is a criticism of *Heroes* worth taking seriously. It obtains a large part of its emotional effect by encouraging the audience to fear for Jack and then revealing that Janet has died instead. The maneuver uses our expectations about who counts as a principal character. Does the episode challenge those expectations, or does it exploit them before claiming the dignity of a memorial?

It does both. The misleading presentation is a dramatic trick. Its success depends on the audience being manipulated. And the series had accumulated so much goodwill for Janet that losing her could feel like losing something more than a fictional character. Some viewers understandably found that choice painful or unwelcome, rather than automatically admirable because it produced tears.

The production history reinforces that this was a contested decision. Writers and producers later recalled arguments over whether Fraiser should be killed. Brad Wright has said that the expectation at the time that the series might be nearing its end influenced the decision, and he later expressed regret about losing her. Teryl Rothery has discussed filming the death and the emotional response around the production. These accounts do not make the final story wrong or right. They help correct the notion that a dramatically powerful outcome was inevitable or universally welcomed by the people who made it.

There is also the danger of treating Janet principally as a device for producing the grief of other characters. The best defense of *Heroes* is not that her death is sad. It is that the episode goes to considerable effort to make her professional action visible and to place the final emphasis on the lives she changed. It lets the dying person be the doctor who had been working all along, not an anonymous victim designated to elevate the show's seriousness.

Yet the objection cannot be dismissed completely. A continuing series loses a relationship, a medical perspective, and a source of everyday humor when it kills a supporting character. The future stories that might have been told with Janet are no less real as creative possibilities because the farewell was well executed. The willingness to make an irreparable choice is part of what gives *Heroes* its force; it is also why reasonable viewers disagree about the cost.

## A documentary within a secret

At the end, Hammond watches Bregman's completed film and begins to understand the outsider's purpose. Jack, once so resistant to being interviewed, is willing to sit down. The gate has not become public. The SGC's complicated relationship with government oversight has not been solved. No camera can guarantee that the people who will eventually watch the documentary will understand it as the participants hope.

What has changed is the possibility of a record that does more than celebrate victory. The institution can be brave and still need witnesses. A commander can be protective of his personnel and still need an outsider to ask questions that insiders have stopped asking. A journalist can be right about the need for an account and still have to learn that the subjects of that account are people, not just material.

The final irony is that we, the television audience, have always possessed the access Bregman spends two hours seeking. We know the people in the briefing room; we have entered the infirmary after visiting worlds whose inhabitants cannot possibly imagine the SGC; we have been shown things that even many employees of the program do not know. *Heroes* invites us to wonder what we have chosen to notice during those visits.

We may remember the brilliant rescue, the machine switched on at the last possible moment, the enemy ship exploding overhead. The people who made those moments possible will often have been visible at the edge of the frame, or not visible at all. The camera turns toward them here, and one of them dies while doing the job we have watched her do for years.

There is no way to make that a fair exchange. There is a way to refuse to let it vanish into a line of classified paperwork. *Heroes* is about the effort to find such a way, and about the humility required to admit that telling a true story is itself an act with consequences.

## Source and interpretation notes (not spoken)

*Heroes, Parts 1 and 2* aired February 13 and 20, 2004, written by Robert C. Cooper and directed by Andy Mikita. The documentary crew is authorized by the outgoing president and is intended to create a record for future disclosure, not an immediate public broadcast. SG-13's Wells is injured; Jack is wounded but survives; Fraiser dies on P3X-666 after treating Wells. Daniel's camera is used for Wells's message to his pregnant wife, and Daniel eventually decides the death footage should be part of Bregman's film. The retrospective production account of an originally single-part script expanded after editing is attributed to Cooper and Mallozzi; the contested decision to kill Fraiser is attributed separately, including later regrets. Claims about viewer complicity, narrative hierarchy, the ethics of looking, and the documentary's form are this essay's criticism, not stated creator intent.
