# 026000 — After the Last Dial: Two Films and a Bigger Franchise

**Track:** Stargate SG-1: Through the Gate. **Audience:** SG-1 fans and listeners interested in how a long-running television series ends. **Prerequisites:** Familiarity with the central team and the Goa'uld and Ori storylines; the essay supplies necessary context. **Scope:** The 2007 finale, the two 2008 follow-up films, and SG-1's relationship to *Stargate Atlantis* and the expanding franchise. **Spoilers:** Complete endings of *Unending*, *The Ark of Truth*, and *Continuum*, including character deaths and the resolution of the Ori war.

# After the Last Dial: Two Films and a Bigger Franchise

There is a peculiar problem with ending a story about a door.

If the door is mysterious, you can close it. If it is dangerous, you can seal it. If the people who walked through it have finally arrived home, you can let them stand together on the safe side and say goodbye. But the Stargate has never worked that way. For ten seasons, the ring in the mountain has promised that an address might lead to a new civilization, a ruined city, an elaborate trap, or a place whose people have a legitimate complaint about Earth's last visit. The point of the door is that the world beyond it does not end when the current adventure does.

So how do you finish *Stargate SG-1* without pretending that the whole galaxy has politely stopped existing?

The answer, in practice, came in three parts. The television series ended with *Unending* in 2007, a story about time and companionship that is strikingly indifferent to conventional victory. Then came two feature-length follow-ups released in 2008. *The Ark of Truth* brought a large intergalactic war to a conclusion. *Continuum* brought an old enemy back for one more elaborate, self-contained adventure. One gave the late series an ending. The other reminded viewers what the earlier series had felt like.

They are not interchangeable films, and treating them as a single extended finale obscures what each one was designed to do. Together, though, they reveal something about the peculiar strengths and limitations of a franchise that had spent years turning a single science-fiction premise into an entire inhabited universe.

## First, a finale that refuses to be a finale

The last regular episode, *Unending*, has the ingredients for a spectacular final confrontation. The Asgard, one of humanity's most powerful and generous allies, are dying. They transfer their technological legacy to Earth. An Ori attack follows. The Odyssey, equipped with Asgard knowledge and weapons, finds itself trapped in a situation where escaping ordinary time becomes the only way to survive. Carter creates a time-dilation field. A moment outside the ship stretches into years inside it.

An episode that might have spent its running time celebrating new weapons instead settles into a small human question: what happens when people who ordinarily measure their lives in emergencies are forced to live together without an available next mission?

The ship becomes a world of aging bodies, routines, romance, irritation and grief. Teal'c tends plants. Daniel and Vala discover how difficult it is to hide behind banter forever. Carter continues the impossible technical work. Mitchell, who is used to directing energy toward a practical objective, has to live without the comfort of an approaching operation. Landry dies during the years inside the field. It is one of the show's boldest changes of scale: the fate of a civilization is reduced to the daily experience of a handful of people in a sealed room.

Carter eventually finds a way out. Teal'c alone carries the long memory into the restored timeline. The others are spared the years they lived together, and also lose them. It is a clever closing move, partly because it resists a false finality. The galaxy is still there. The Ori war is not decisively resolved. There are missions left to fly. But the audience has seen the team do something more demanding than win a fight. They have learned, endured and lost together.

There is a cost to that choice. A viewer who has followed the Ori across the ninth and tenth seasons can reasonably ask where the promised ending went. The television finale leaves the central external conflict unfinished. The answer was intended to arrive in a different format. That makes *Unending* more emotionally interesting than a hurried showdown might have been, but it also makes the film that follows carry the burden of narrative closure.

## A war that does not fit comfortably inside the old gate room

*The Ark of Truth*, written and directed by Robert C. Cooper, takes the Ori conflict seriously as a problem of scale. Earth is no longer fighting a single Goa'uld ship or negotiating with one threatened village. The enemy possesses a religion that spans worlds, fleets capable of overwhelming Earth, ascended power behind its founders, and a network of Priors who can turn a theological command into military obedience.

The action takes SG-1 into the Ori galaxy itself. Daniel has learned of an ancient device called the Ark of Truth, built by the Alterans before their departure. It can force people to see a claim as true. Finding it offers a way to undermine the Ori's authority without simply destroying every follower who has been convinced, coerced or born into the system.

That is a promising premise for Daniel. He has spent his adult life insisting that an unfamiliar person deserves to be understood before they are treated as a target. Against the Ori, he also understands that ideas are not harmless just because they are ideas. A system of belief has been built into a system of conquest. Priors can compel; ordinary believers can kill; people who want to leave may face punishment. An argument about the gods is inseparable from an army advancing through a gate.

The Ark offers something close to a technical fix for an epistemological catastrophe. Instead of winning a debate in every village, the team can demonstrate to the Priors that the Ori deceived them. Those Priors are linked through their staffs, and once the truth spreads, the worship sustaining Adria's ascended power is diminished. Morgan le Fay can then confront her. The war ends through a combination of ancient technology, a confrontation between ascended beings, and a new understanding among the people who had served the Ori.

Notice what is different from the earliest *Stargate* victories. In the original film, Ra's machinery can be turned against him. In early *SG-1*, someone can expose a Goa'uld as an impostor, and a community can begin to reclaim its history. Here the solution is no longer enough to persuade people in a room. The story needs a device that transmits a conclusion across a vast institutional network. The scale of the enemy has grown beyond the scale of the show's most characteristic form of heroism: four people talking someone into a better choice.

Cooper, discussing the film in 2008, acknowledged the moral difficulty of the Ark itself. In his view, the conflict between Daniel's preference for a nonviolent answer and a violent military answer was central to the story, but he did not claim the device made that distinction straightforward. He openly entertained the problem that compelling belief may be another kind of coercion.

This is where the film is more interesting than its tidy ending can initially suggest. The Ori control people partly by controlling what they can believe. The heroes defeat them by using an instrument that also overrides a person's ability to make up their own mind. The content of the message may be true. That does not make the means neutral. A truth imposed by force is a strange victory for a character whose finest moments often come from patient conversation.

The film recognizes some of the problem, but its timetable does not allow the sustained argument a television season might have developed. Perhaps that is one of the unavoidable costs of giving a long-running war a feature-length conclusion. The machinery of resolution has to move faster than the sociology of liberation.

## The two people who make the ending human

The grand intergalactic resolution is not, for me, the film's most persuasive ending. That honor belongs to two people whose lives have been damaged by the Ori: Vala and Tomin.

Tomin's story began as the story of an ordinary man shaped by a powerful religious institution. He was not born as a cartoon villain. He was offered belonging and purpose inside a system designed to exploit both. He becomes a soldier for the Ori, participates in their conquest, and gradually faces the terrible gap between the moral language he has been taught and what he is ordered to do.

In *The Ark of Truth*, he refuses a Prior's command to kill SG-1. His refusal has an immediacy the Ark itself lacks. He does not need an artifact to perform a first act of disobedience. He makes a choice after seeing what obedience requires. Later, he intends to return and help rebuild the society he once served as a warrior. His work will not be completed by a beam of light. It will require people to live with one another after the regime's central claim has collapsed.

Vala's journey is related but different. She has spent years surviving by improvisation, seduction, theft and deflection. She has also experienced life under the Goa'uld as a host to Qetesh, and then the Ori's manipulation of her body and her daughter Adria. Her cheerful insolence is often a means of escaping vulnerability. The late series gradually allows her to choose a community without having to pretend she cannot be hurt by it.

When Tomin offers her a life with him in the Ori galaxy, she does not return to the life that once seemed to define her. She stays with SG-1. The choice is not an announcement that every wound has healed. It is a decision about where she belongs, made by someone who spent much of her life unable to trust that belonging was available.

This gives the ending a moral scale its superweapon lacks. Tomin must face the people he helped harm. Vala must learn to remain somewhere long enough to be known. These are choices no ancient device can make on their behalf.

The film complicates its own resolution with another, less graceful decision. An International Oversight Advisory representative, James Marrick, secretly activates Replicators aboard the Odyssey as a contingency. The ship is far from home, the Ori remain dangerous, and the supposedly useful weapon becomes a threat to the people who brought it. It is an action sequence tied to an old SG-1 lesson: institutions can be so determined to deploy a technological advantage that they forget why the technology was dangerous.

Still, the Replicator subplot is crowded into a film already carrying an Ori campaign, Ancient mythology, Vala's family, and a conclusion to ascended politics. It can feel like a second catastrophe introduced to supply a familiar kind of action. The criticism is not that the Replicators are out of place in the Stargate universe. It is that the story's attention is pulled away from the people whose ideological and political transformations form its heart.

The Ark closes one major conflict. Whether it entirely earns the simplicity of that closure is another question.

## The second ending remembers the original adventure

The other film begins with a very different promise. *Stargate: Continuum* is written by Brad Wright and directed by Martin Wood. Its villain is Ba'al, one of the last great Goa'uld antagonists, and its weapon is not an ascended artifact of enforced truth. It is history.

During what should be a final ceremony marking the removal of Ba'al's symbiote, people begin to vanish. Teal'c is gone. Vala is gone. Jack's presence turns suddenly lethal. Carter, Daniel and Cameron Mitchell escape through the Stargate and discover that they are no longer returning to the world they know.

They emerge aboard the *Achilles*, a ship that was transporting the Egyptian Stargate in 1939. In this altered history, Ba'al has intervened to keep the ring from reaching the United States. The familiar Stargate program was never established. The three survivors have crossed from a reality where their teamwork helped change the galaxy into one where their achievements never happened.

What an economical way to make an enormous series feel new again. After ten seasons of accumulated ships, allies, weapons, special protocols and Ancient discoveries, *Continuum* removes nearly everything. Carter is no longer the scientist whose colleagues know she can save the day. Daniel is again a man whose ideas about ancient history meet disbelief. Mitchell has no ordinary place in this world at all: without the intervention that saved his grandfather, he should never have existed. The film takes characters who have become institutions and makes them strangers.

Ba'al, meanwhile, knows exactly how to exploit accumulated knowledge. He has watched Earth become dangerous; he has learned from the System Lords' failures; he arrives in a timeline where the human resistance that troubled the Goa'uld was never organized. He possesses the advantage of a villain who has been allowed to study the story before rewriting its opening chapter.

It is also a satisfying choice of antagonist. Apophis would likely try to overpower this new Earth. Ba'al wants to manage events, anticipate rivals and arrange for the outcome to be favorable before anyone realizes the contest has begun. He is not simply more theatrical than the other System Lords. He is more adaptable, which is why a story about manipulating history suits him.

But history does not consent to being managed. Qetesh, the Goa'uld who once possessed Vala, appears here in the alternative timeline as a rival with ambitions of her own. The manipulation Ba'al employs against Earth creates new opportunities for those around him. His attempt to outwit the past turns his own empire into a place where allies can betray him and even the people he intends to rule have lives he does not fully control.

## A freezing ship and an enormous amount of continuity

*Continuum* has a genuinely eerie early sequence aboard the frozen *Achilles*. The modern team is standing inside a version of history that should have been a remembered foundation for their own adventure, but the foundation has gone wrong. The ship is cold, damaged and isolated. They are physically inside a counterfactual.

The film's production pursued a kind of realism that contrasts with the warm, familiar studio environments of a typical SG-1 mission. Wright and the crew traveled north to shoot on the Arctic ice with the support of the United States Navy. They filmed near the surfacing of the submarine USS *Alexandria*, with a small crew working in severe conditions. Other frozen sequences were filmed on a refrigerated stage in Vancouver. Brad Wright described the film as an old-fashioned Stargate adventure on a scale he had wanted to make. That production choice is visible in its physical textures: a huge white landscape, the overwhelming isolation of the ice, and the vulnerability of people who have stepped through a miraculous ring and discovered they may freeze to death.

The Arctic footage is not a substitute for character. It is a way of making the central conceit tangible. Our heroes have spent a decade traveling to worlds that may be hostile; now the dangerous alien environment is Earth, made strange by the removal of everything they accomplished.

The altered present also gives the film room for one of the franchise's most persistent questions. Would it be right to erase the lives of the people who have inhabited the new history? Carter, Daniel and Mitchell know a reality in which billions of people are alive under different circumstances and an entire galactic alliance exists. The authorities in the altered world have no independent reason to value the memories of three strangers over the existence of everyone else. A request to reset history is not a harmless engineering proposal. It is a political and moral demand made from a position of unequal knowledge.

*Continuum* treats this chiefly as an obstacle to the adventure rather than developing it into a full debate. The pressure of Ba'al's eventual invasion resolves the practical question. Still, the hesitation is valuable. It shows that a government does not automatically become foolish simply because it refuses to accept what the heroes know to be true.

Then the film performs its final trick. Mitchell travels back to the earlier past, waits through years in which none of his teammates can accompany him, and prevents Ba'al's interference. The team in the restored present remembers none of the ordeal. A photograph quietly reveals that another version of Mitchell lived long enough to save their history.

That is a particularly Stargate kind of ending. There has been an enormous victory, but the people who enjoy it cannot share the memory of how it happened. One man paid in years that the others do not know he spent. The evidence is not a parade or an award. It is a picture that may sit unnoticed in a locker.

## Two films built for different kinds of loyalty

The distinction between the films is not accidental. In a 2008 interview, Wright said the team wanted to give viewers a major conclusion to the Ori story and also an independent, old-fashioned Stargate adventure. Cooper described the film format as providing a little more time and resources for a large-scale version of a television story, though not unlimited freedom. In other words, the films were made with two different audience needs in view: resolution and return.

The release strategy was itself a sign of the industry's changing economics. The television series ended in 2007; both films arrived directly on home video in 2008. They were shot in 2007, with postproduction schedules affecting the order and spacing of their releases. Wright later described the home-video business as an initially successful route for continuing SG-1, and its subsequent deterioration as an obstacle to developing further direct-to-video adventures.

This is not simply trivia about distribution. The form shapes the stories that can be told. A weekly television series can spend a month making the audience impatient for the next revelation, then use a quiet episode to complicate it. A one-off film must promise a complete experience to someone selecting a disc for the evening. *Ark* has to settle an unfinished war. *Continuum* has to create its own beginning, middle and end, even though the weight of ten years of shared memory gives its altered timeline emotional force.

Each format solves one problem and creates another. *Ark* is more consequential for the final mythology, but its resolution is necessarily compressed. *Continuum* is less necessary to understand the future of the galaxy, but arguably more successful at recovering the flavor of a team going somewhere dangerous with incomplete information and trying to work its way home.

One cannot replace the other because an ending to a long-running series has two jobs. It must address the commitments of the fiction, and it must say goodbye to the experience of watching it.

## The franchise outgrows its original team

By the time the films appeared, SG-1 was already part of a larger world. *Stargate Atlantis* had begun in 2004, during SG-1's eighth season. Its expedition left Earth for the lost Ancient city in the Pegasus galaxy. The new series inherited the pleasure of discovery but changed the scale and organization of the adventure. Instead of teams stepping into another world and returning to a familiar base, the protagonists inhabited a remote city that had to become their home.

This difference is easy to underestimate. The SGC's mountain is a place the audience knows belongs to Earth. The expedition in Atlantis is a human outpost surrounded by a galaxy whose people have their own histories, enemies and claims. When SG-1's fleet grows more formidable and Ancient technology becomes less mythical, Atlantis can restore the uncertainty of being far from help. The two series share a universe but organize risk differently.

The same development also exposes a tension in a long-running science-fiction property. If the first show discovers and eventually masters extraordinary technologies, what is left for the next group to discover? A sequel can invent a more dangerous enemy or a more distant galaxy. It can also reconsider the institution that makes the adventure possible: who gets chosen, how much support reaches them, and what kind of community forms beyond the reach of ordinary authority.

*Stargate Universe*, which followed in 2009, would push the franchise toward a different tone and predicament. It does not need to become part of SG-1's ending to show why the original series had created such a productive foundation. The gate, once a device for a single trip, had become a grammar flexible enough to tell several kinds of stories.

The risks are obvious too. Franchises can keep enlarging their mythology until the audience is asked to care about the administration of a fictional universe more than the people in it. A crossover can feel like a reward for sustained attention or like compulsory homework. The most successful SG-1 storytelling rarely relied on a viewer remembering every treaty and lost technology. It relied on recognizing why a particular person would make a particular decision today.

## The door remains open

Neither of the two movies offers a perfect solution to the problem of ending. The Ark resolves a war with a device that troubles the show's own respect for conscience. *Continuum* asks us to enjoy a reset that gives the heroes their history back while making the moral cost of rewriting the alternative history rather brief. Both occasionally strain under the quantity of continuity they carry. Neither can reproduce the leisurely pleasures of ten seasons of rooms, corridors, arguments, discoveries and everyday work.

But there is something generous in their refusal to stage a funeral for SG-1 itself.

The war ends. Ba'al is defeated. The team changes and survives. Daniel remains curious. Carter still prefers an actual explanation to a grand claim. Mitchell wants another mission. Teal'c has acquired more freedom than the child raised under Apophis could have imagined. Vala has found people with whom she chooses to stay. Jack, when he appears, is still capable of regarding a galactic crisis as an occasion for a dry remark.

The last impression is not of a universe completed but of work still available. That is faithful to the central invention. A Stargate is a machine whose greatest narrative property is not where it sends people. It is that it gives them another choice of where to go.

And if you have spent enough evenings with these characters, the sound of an address being dialed does not need to announce the end of a story. It can simply sound like the beginning of another good one.
