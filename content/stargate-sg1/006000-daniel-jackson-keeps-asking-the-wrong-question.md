# 006000 — Daniel Jackson Keeps Asking the Wrong Question

**Track:** Stargate SG-1: Through the Gate. **Audience:** Fans of *Stargate SG-1*. **Prerequisites:** Familiarity with the original SG-1 team and the Goa'uld premise. **Scope:** Daniel as translator, moral irritant, grieving husband, explorer, ascended being, and teammate. **Spoilers:** Major spoilers through season ten, including Sha're's fate, Daniel's ascension and return, Abydos, and the Ori storyline.

# Daniel Jackson Keeps Asking the Wrong Question

There is a ship crawling across a green world, and behind it the green world is disappearing.

The machine is not malicious. It is enormous, patient, and almost perfectly committed to its purpose. It carries the genetic inheritance, art, memories, and hope of a civilization that no longer exists. It has spent an immense span of time searching for conditions under which those people might live again. Now it is remaking a planet to suit them.

There are already people on that planet. SG-1 helped bring them there. If the machine proceeds, the new settlers will die. If Jack O'Neill destroys the machine, the vanished civilization aboard it may lose its last chance to return.

The military problem is horrible but legible: protect the people you have promised to protect. Jack prepares a weapon. Samantha Carter helps make it. Daniel Jackson, who also wants the settlers saved, refuses to accept that the only available question is which civilization gets to survive.

He goes to talk to the machine.

This is *Scorched Earth*, and it gives us Daniel Jackson in miniature. His superpower is not that he knows the right answer. It is that he has a habit of asking a question that the present crisis has made inconvenient. Who built this? What do they believe they are doing? Are their instructions the same as their interests? Have we mistaken a practical problem for an unavoidable moral choice?

The series occasionally treats him as the man who is holding everybody up. Sometimes he really is. But if Jack is the person who decides when to act, and Carter the person who discovers what can be done, Daniel is frequently the one who asks whether they have understood what they are acting upon.

It is a job with no guarantee of making you popular.

## A scholar the universe proved right

In the 1994 film *Stargate*, Daniel is introduced as an outsider to the respectable conversation. His ideas about ancient Egypt have made him easy to dismiss. He arrives at a military project whose employees possess an artifact they do not know how to use, and recognizes something in its inscriptions that changes everything. A map that has been treated as an archaeological puzzle becomes a set of coordinates. The great stone ring is a means of travel.

This is a fantasy that flatters scholarship. A specialist who has been laughed out of polite company turns out to hold the key to the universe. Yet the film also gives him something more important than vindication. On Abydos he meets people, learns something about their language and history, and makes a life among them. He marries Sha're. By the time the television series begins, that life is not merely a reward for solving a riddle. It is what makes him vulnerable.

In *Children of the Gods*, the Goa'uld take Sha're as a host. Daniel goes through the gate again because he hopes to find her. That personal motive is complicated by his increasing understanding of the civilizations the Goa'uld dominate. A scholar in a war may be asked to produce translations, map ruins, and interpret a device that could kill the people standing beside him. Daniel does those things. But he has also lived as somebody's neighbor and husband on a world that the military initially knew as an objective.

Michael Shanks gives the television Daniel an appealing and sometimes exhausting combination of eagerness and certainty. He can become so excited about a fragment of language that he briefly loses sight of the armed people around him. He can also become indignant when the armed people regard the language as an obstacle between them and their mission. His face often tells us that he is already halfway through an explanation before he realizes everyone else has stopped listening.

That could have produced a very simple character: the clever civilian who translates a clue before the soldiers do the heroic work. Instead, Daniel's way of reading people slowly changes what counts as heroic work.

## An old room, four languages

Consider *The Torment of Tantalus*, an episode from the first season. SG-1 discovers that a young researcher, Ernest Littlefield, traveled through the gate in 1945 and never came home. Decades later, his former fiancée Catherine Langford learns that he may still be alive. The team goes to a ruined place where Ernest has lived alone among the remains of an extraordinary meeting hall.

Four great civilizations once exchanged knowledge there. Their languages surround a device that might have answered questions humanity has scarcely learned to formulate. Daniel is almost overwhelmed by the discovery. For him this is not a warehouse of gadgets. It is evidence that intelligence need not be a lonely achievement: beings separated by biology and history once thought it worthwhile to meet, speak, and learn from one another.

He also knows the place is falling apart. The way home may vanish. His refusal to leave is not a triumph of enlightened curiosity over ignorant soldiers. Jack has a good reason to force the issue. Catherine has just recovered somebody she thought she lost forever, and Daniel is risking the lives of others for information that may not survive the next few minutes.

The episode makes his devotion admirable and exasperating at the same time. He wants a little longer with a possibility that may never come again. Jack wants his friend alive. Both are looking at something precious and asking whether they can bear to lose it.

There is a useful symmetry with Ernest. The older scholar spent decades pursuing the meaning of what was before him. Daniel arrives hungry for the same treasure. Catherine's presence reminds them that knowledge is not the only kind of life a person can have. Stargate does not resolve that tension by declaring archaeology silly. It gets Daniel out of the collapsing chamber, and allows the promise of those four languages to remain with him.

It will come back later, with consequences far larger than anyone in the room can imagine.

## The monster who becomes a person

*The First Ones* supplies another beautiful test. Daniel is working on an archaeological site when an Unas takes him prisoner. The creature is large, frightening, and apparently determined to drag him off into the wild. A rescue team is sent to find him. The situation seems to have the shape of a monster adventure: helpless scientist, dangerous beast, soldiers coming to the rescue.

Daniel starts trying to talk.

Not because he has evidence that conversation will save his life. There is very little evidence of anything. He experiments with sounds, gestures, the sharing of water, and the observation of ritual. He begins to understand that his captor, whom he comes to call Chaka, belongs to a society whose customs have a logic, even though those customs put Daniel in danger.

The episode does not make Chaka harmless. Daniel recognizes that he is at risk of being killed in a rite of passage. He nevertheless notices the creature responding to him as someone rather than something. By the time Jack and Carter arrive with weapons, the central question has changed. Daniel is no longer merely an object to be rescued. He is a person negotiating, imperfectly, with another person. The rescue must take account of that relationship.

This is not just a lesson about being nice to aliens. It is a change in the information available to the team. A creature interpreted only as an attacker creates one set of possible actions. A creature understood as a member of a culture creates others. Daniel has not defeated the danger with a vocabulary list; he has made it possible to see a situation that firearms alone could not describe.

There is a limit worth stating. Daniel's quick interpretations of language and ritual are sometimes astonishingly convenient. Real field linguistics does not generally yield such fluent understanding in an afternoon under threat of sacrifice. The drama compresses inquiry because the story needs a conversation before the rescue arrives. But the emotional truth is persuasive: curiosity can be an ethical act when it refuses to decide, in advance, that the stranger is incapable of having reasons.

There is a later payoff that makes the friendship more than a one-episode lesson. In *Enemy Mine*, Earth wants naquadah from territory occupied by an Unas community. The mineral is needed for ships that could defend Earth. The military has a genuine strategic need, and the Unas have a genuine claim to the land and the places they treat as sacred. A commander at the mine looks at the indigenous inhabitants and sees creatures obstructing an essential project. Daniel sees people with whom a negotiation is possible.

He calls on Chaka to help. The new problem is no longer whether a human prisoner can befriend an Unas captor. It is whether a technologically powerful civilization can take the inhabitants of a planet seriously before it takes their resources. The eventual arrangement involves the Unas themselves doing the mining. The episode does not abolish unequal power or the pressures that created the conflict. It shows Daniel performing a useful diplomatic task precisely because he refused, years earlier, to accept the category of monster as an adequate description of an alien life.

The old conversation has become practical knowledge. This is how a small adventure can change the conduct of a much larger program.

## The limits of understanding

The danger in admiring Daniel is to turn him into the one character who is always morally correct. The series is more interesting when it resists that temptation.

His search for Sha're is a good example. Her body has been taken over by Amonet, a Goa'uld. Daniel believes he may be able to reach his wife, to find a way to separate host and parasite, to rescue the person he loves from a terrible captivity. That hope is neither a policy nor an abstract principle. It is a form of fidelity. It also makes every encounter with Amonet a risk to Daniel and to others.

In *Forever in a Day*, Teal'c kills Amonet when she attacks Daniel with a hand device. Sha're dies with her. The episode stretches Daniel's experience of those seconds into a series of visions in which grief, anger, forgiveness, and the possibility of a future move strangely around one another. He has to accept that Teal'c saved his life, even though the cost was the life he wanted to save.

There is no argument clever enough to restore Sha're. In a series filled with devices that reverse death, transport bodies across the galaxy, and eventually allow certain beings to rise above mortal existence, that fact is important. Daniel can be an interpreter of everything in front of him and still fail to recover the person he knows best.

He has to learn what to do with a love that no longer tells him where to go. Sha're leaves him the responsibility of finding the child she bore while under Goa'uld control, and the search gives his grief another direction. The story does not erase what he lost by finding him another task.

*Absolute Power* later turns Daniel's curiosity against itself. The child, Shifu, possesses dangerous inherited knowledge. Daniel wants it, because knowing how the Goa'uld rule might help bring them down. Shifu allows him to experience a vision of a future in which that knowledge has made him a frightening ruler, prepared to violate his friends and destroy a city in the name of protecting Earth.

The episode's device is overtly didactic, but the target is well chosen. Daniel is most vulnerable when he assumes that knowledge is safe because he desires it for good reasons. A person can ask an intelligent question for a deeply foolish purpose. He is not immune to the fantasy that, given enough understanding, he could finally make the world submit to a humane plan.

That is why his skepticism toward powerful others is credible. He has reason to be skeptical of himself.

## The civilization that must not return at someone else's expense

Now return to *Scorched Earth*. The Enkarans are a displaced human population who need a particular environment to survive. SG-1 helped relocate them, and the team has acquired obligations by doing so. The Gadmeer, meanwhile, have left behind a vessel designed to restore their civilization from stored biological and cultural material. Its automated plan is to transform this world. The vessel regards its task as a mission of preservation, not conquest.

Jack sees the settlers in danger and decides that the machine has to be stopped. He cannot ask the Enkarans to die merely because a computer has arrived carrying a more ancient claim. Carter, who can understand the engineering, prepares the means to disable or destroy the vessel when instructed. It is a command decision, and it is a terrible one.

Daniel does something that might be maddening from Jack's position. He boards the ship and talks to Lotan, the artificial representative it has made for communication with him. Lotan speaks with the authority of a system running out of options. The instructions say the terraforming must continue. The requirements for another world are precise. Starting over may be impossible.

Daniel is not persuaded that an instruction exhausts the meaning of the mission. The Gadmeer were a civilization, not an algorithm. Did they intend the survival of their people to require the destruction of another? What would the creators of this vessel have wanted their legacy to become? He takes Lotan down to see the Enkarans, rather than allowing their existence to remain an abstract variable in a calculation.

There is a real risk here. Jack's deadline has not gone away because Daniel has improved the conversation. The bomb is still being prepared. Daniel's trust could cost the settlers their lives. Yet the encounter changes Lotan's understanding of his purpose, and a further look through the ship's survey data reveals a possibility that neither side had considered: a world unsuitable for the Gadmeer because it already has native inhabitants is in fact the ancestral home of the Enkarans.

The solution is not the triumph of empathy alone. It requires accurate information about planetary conditions, a computer that can revise its decisions, and people willing to accept a new bargain. Daniel's contribution is to keep the search space open long enough for that information to acquire meaning.

This is also one of the series' more generous depictions of disagreement. Jack is not stupid for fearing delay. Daniel is not foolish for refusing a seemingly inevitable slaughter. Carter is not merely the technician who carries out orders. Their different responsibilities create a pressure that a single, perfectly correct protagonist would never have produced.

## The radiance and the restriction

Then *Meridian* takes Daniel into a different sort of crisis. On the world of Langara, the Kelownans are developing dangerous technology based on naquadria. An accident threatens the researchers. Daniel intervenes to prevent disaster and receives a lethal dose of radiation. The authorities initially have their own reasons for describing what happened in ways that protect their position. The man who exposed himself trying to help can be made a convenient culprit.

It is one of the sharpest reminders that being morally serious does not make you powerful. Daniel has made a brave choice and cannot argue his body out of the consequences. His friends come to understand that conventional medicine is not going to save him. Oma Desala offers another way: ascension, a passage to a higher plane of existence.

For a character fascinated by the oldest civilizations and the possibility that consciousness is stranger than the world he was taught, this has the feel of an extraordinary consummation. He has spent years looking for what lies beyond the familiar. Now he may pass beyond it himself.

But the series wisely makes ascension less like graduation and more like another argument about power and responsibility. Daniel can perceive more than he once could. He can travel and observe in ways that make the Stargate seem almost provincial. He is also bound by rules forbidding interference in the lives of lower beings. His compassion has survived the transformation; the freedom to act upon it has not.

In *Abyss*, Jack is tortured and repeatedly revived by Ba'al. Daniel visits him but insists that he cannot simply use his ascended abilities to free him. It is an excruciating scene for both friends. Jack sees somebody who appears to possess exactly the power they need and refuses to use it. Daniel sees the suffering of a friend and confronts the limits of what he is permitted to do. The conversation is not a debate club exercise. It occurs inside an ongoing ordeal.

Daniel's frustration with noninterference is understandable. Yet the series has already shown what happens when a person believes good intentions authorize unlimited power. The problem is not solved by deciding the Ancients are heartless or Daniel is naive. We are being asked how anyone possessing vast capacities could act without making everyone else subject to their judgments.

In *Full Circle*, the conflict reaches his adopted home of Abydos. Anubis threatens it. Daniel tries to protect its people, stretching against the prohibition until the other ascended beings stop him. The result is a painful defeat; the world he once chose as home is devastated. Daniel's relationship to the Ancients no longer resembles the discovery of wise elders waiting to provide an answer. Knowledge and power are separated from wisdom in ways his younger self would have found almost unbearable.

He returns to mortal life, with the memory of his ascended existence impaired. It is tempting to treat his return simply as a reset to the familiar four-person team. There is more to it. A man whose long search once took him beyond ordinary existence comes back to the work of interpreting people, artifacts, and difficult choices. He is still most useful when he is not omniscient.

## The performance of having too many selves

Michael Shanks has to make a difficult mixture look natural. Daniel is at once academically confident and physically vulnerable, generous and stubborn, capable of great indignation and absurdly pleased by a minor discovery. He often speaks faster when excited, which plays neatly against Jack's deliberate impatience. In a briefing, Daniel can make an extraordinary proposition sound like the beginning of a long seminar nobody asked to attend. In a crisis, the same earnestness can become tremendously moving.

*Lifeboat*, in the seventh season, gives Shanks an especially conspicuous performance challenge. The minds of people from a crashed ship have been placed within Daniel, and different personalities emerge through him. The setup allows for shifts in voice, physical bearing, fear, and authority. Yet its real subject is the cost of treating a living person as a container for valuable information. The displaced people have a genuine claim to survival, but Daniel has a claim to his own mind and body. Nobody can save everybody by pretending that claim disappears.

It is a situation fitted to this character: a translator whose work has always involved allowing other voices to be heard is now overwhelmed by voices that literally displace him.

The later Ori story takes the problem in another direction. In *The Shroud*, Daniel appears transformed into a Prior, an agent of the religious empire threatening the galaxy. His friends must decide whether the person behind that altered appearance is still Daniel, and whether his plan can be trusted. The man who spent years insisting that enemies might not be what they seem now needs others to exercise exactly that kind of discernment on his behalf.

The episode does not suggest that every stranger deserves unrestricted trust. It suggests that knowing somebody is a practice, not a label attached to a uniform or a changed face. SG-1 has survived long enough for its members to recognize one another by habits of judgment, courage, and infuriating persistence.

## The wrong question, asked again

Daniel Jackson is often described as the conscience of SG-1. That is broadly true, but it makes his part in the series sound easier than it is. A conscience does not automatically generate the right operational plan. Daniel asks people to delay, to investigate, to distinguish a threat from a misunderstood intention. Sometimes his colleagues are right that the danger will not wait. Sometimes his enthusiasm blinds him to what he might become if given exactly the information he wants.

He is especially compelling because he keeps going through these failures without surrendering the habit of trying to understand. His grief does not convince him that love was foolish. His mistakes do not convince him that inquiry is worthless. His journey among the ascended does not transform him into somebody too lofty to care about frightened people in an alien village.

An archaeologist's instinct is to approach a broken object and wonder what kind of life made it. A translator's instinct is to encounter an opaque message and assume there might be an intelligible thought on the other side. Neither instinct guarantees that every artifact is safe or every speaker honest. Both offer a form of resistance to the speed with which fear can turn a person into a target.

Back on that green world, the machine did have to be stopped. Jack was right about the approaching catastrophe. The question Daniel insisted on asking was whether stopping the machine had to mean destroying the people it carried. Because he asked it, the story acquired a solution that did not exist for the characters when the countdown began.

That is Daniel's gift to *Stargate SG-1*. He does not know everything. He keeps preventing everyone else from being satisfied with the things they think they know.
