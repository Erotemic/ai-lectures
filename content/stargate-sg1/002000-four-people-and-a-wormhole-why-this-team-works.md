# 002000 — Four People and a Wormhole: Why This Team Works

**Track:** Stargate SG-1: Through the Gate. **Audience:** Newcomers and fans. **Prerequisites:** The premise of the 1994 film and *Children of the Gods* is explained where needed. **Scope:** The original four, Hammond and the early SGC; how cooperation and disagreement generate the series. **Spoilers:** Selected first- and second-season adventures, including *The Enemy Within*, *The Nox*, *Cor-ai*, *The Torment of Tantalus*, and *The Fifth Race*. No late-series endings.

# Four People and a Wormhole: Why This Team Works

Four people walk through a circle of shimmering water. One of them is carrying a weapon and is already wondering where the enemy is. One is trying to read the inscriptions. One wants to know whether the machinery is doing something dangerous. The last knows more about the enemy than anyone else, because until recently he worked for them.

That is the basic arrangement of *Stargate SG-1*: a soldier, a scholar, a scientist, and a former servant of an alien god. It sounds like the beginning of a joke about a bar in a remote galaxy. Often, it practically is. But the combination is an exceptionally good way to tell adventure stories. A discovery that delights one person alarms another. A plan that looks humane to one looks suicidal to the person responsible for getting everybody home. An apparent miracle can be a machine, a threat, or a mistake in translation. Four people can look at the same phenomenon and see four different problems.

A television team needs more than contrasting occupations. Its members have to become people whose judgments the audience wants to hear, even when those judgments are wrong. A well-designed ensemble turns a plot into a conversation. That conversation can happen while the characters are running, fighting, searching, or arguing about why they are doing any of those things.

In SG-1, the most reliable source of suspense is not whether a Stargate will open. It is what these four people will decide once it does.

## The room where they become a team

The series begins in the shadow of the 1994 film. Earth possesses a monumental device found in Egypt, and the military has sent a small expedition through it to a distant world called Abydos. The expedition encountered Ra, an alien tyrant who sustained a claim to divinity with advanced technology. Ra was defeated, the travelers returned, and the device appeared to have served its purpose.

Then someone else comes through. The television pilot, *Children of the Gods*, introduces Apophis, another Goa'uld ruler. Earth realizes the ring is not an isolated passage to one planet, but part of a vast network. There is no sensible way to defend against a network one scarcely understands. The Air Force reopens the program and begins sending teams to explore it.

Colonel Jack O'Neill is recalled to lead the first team. Richard Dean Anderson plays him as a man with authority and a strong resistance to ceremony. Michael Shanks plays Daniel Jackson, whose command of languages and history helped open the original expedition's path. Amanda Tapping plays Captain Samantha Carter, a military scientist who can understand both the mission and the machine. Christopher Judge plays Teal'c, a Jaffa warrior who has served Apophis as his first prime and then chooses to help his prisoners escape.

That last decision is a greater risk than an ordinary change of employer. Teal'c leaves a military hierarchy whose masters demand worship. His family remains within the society he has betrayed. On Earth, the soldiers who meet him have good reason to be frightened. He is physically formidable, carries knowledge they cannot verify, and has been an officer of the power now threatening their world.

The pilot therefore does not assemble four people with identical values and mutual confidence. It brings together people with reasons to distrust one another, then requires them to collaborate under pressure. Jack is responsible for the consequences. Daniel wants to understand the unfamiliar. Carter has to convert partial evidence into useful action. Teal'c is the only one with direct experience of the enemy's institutions. Remove any one of those functions and the team becomes less capable, but also less interesting.

The most important difference between this arrangement and a squad of interchangeable action heroes is that no one can simply absorb everyone else's role. Jack cannot improvise an ancient translation by shouting at it. Daniel cannot repair a gate system by wishing the inscriptions made sense. Carter cannot learn the full politics of Jaffa society from an instrument reading. Teal'c cannot make Earth's leaders trust him by announcing that he is trustworthy. They must work across those limits.

## Jack is in command, not in possession of every answer

Jack's authority gives the conversations a shape. Someone must decide when to leave a dangerous planet, whether to fire on an enemy, and what to report to General Hammond afterward. Without that responsibility, disagreement would be free entertainment. With it, disagreement has a cost. Jack must listen, judge, and then act.

Anderson makes command look less like perfect confidence than like constant impatience with imperfect information. He is fond of jokes, especially when another character is becoming impressed with their own importance. A hostile alien ruler may demand reverence; Jack tends to respond as though a pompous bureaucrat has joined the meeting. The humor is useful against the Goa'uld precisely because their authority depends on people taking the performance seriously.

But the joke cannot resolve the danger. In *The Enemy Within*, Major Kawalsky, Jack's friend and fellow officer, has been taken over by a Goa'uld. A familiar person may no longer control his own actions. Jack's military training can identify the immediate threat, but it cannot supply an easy answer to a dreadful question: how do you save somebody whose body has become dangerous to everyone around him? The episode is an early reminder that Jack's decisions are not guaranteed to produce the ending he wants.

That experience helps explain why his authority becomes convincing. He has to live with what happens after an order. A character who never doubts and never loses anything might be an efficient commander in a simple adventure, but he would not have the kind of working relationship SG-1 requires. Jack's humor allows him to remain approachable without surrendering the duty to make difficult calls.

There is also an interesting limit to his skepticism. He is quick to dismiss supernatural language when a false god uses it to intimidate people. Yet he must repeatedly accept that unfamiliar civilizations know things the Air Force does not. His suspicion of grandeur is healthy; a suspicion of expertise can become a handicap. Some of the show's best conversations involve Carter or Daniel requiring him to endure an explanation he would rather skip.

Jack is not the hero because he can always tell everybody else what to do. He is the hero, at his best, because he can remain responsible while allowing the people beside him to know more.

## Daniel changes what counts as a problem

Daniel is often identified as the team's conscience, and that is part of his function. But it is too narrow. His more fundamental contribution is interpretive. He changes the description of the situation before the others have committed themselves to a solution.

Suppose an expedition reaches a settlement that treats an alien ruler as a god. A soldier may see a military occupation. A scientist may see evidence of advanced weaponry. Daniel sees stories, rituals, and institutions: the ways people have learned to explain their lives. He knows a people can obey a terrible ruler without being foolish, and that symbols may preserve information their users no longer fully understand.

That knowledge is practical. Language can tell the team where a gate leads, whether a warning concerns a machine or a burial site, and why a local community is refusing help. It can also slow them down. The scientific miracle or military opportunity that seems obvious to somebody else may appear to Daniel as an irreplaceable part of another civilization's life.

In *The Torment of Tantalus*, the team finds Ernest Littlefield, an explorer who passed through Earth's gate in 1945 and became stranded. The episode leads them to a place of extraordinary archaeological importance: a meeting site associated with several ancient races, where symbols might offer a way to understand the wider universe. It is exactly the kind of discovery Daniel has spent his life seeking.

And then they have to leave.

Daniel's reluctance is not mere absent-mindedness. He can see that knowledge may be lost forever if they depart. Jack can see the immediate lives endangered by remaining. The scene works because neither has been assigned a foolish position. Daniel would be a worse scholar if he felt nothing at the threatened loss. Jack would be a worse commander if he let that feeling govern everyone else's survival.

The argument reveals what teamwork can do for a person. It can provide an answer that is not a compromise between identical desires. Somebody is present who recognizes a risk that the other person's devotion has pushed out of view.

Daniel's generosity can also be a liability. He has a habit of assuming that understanding an adversary may be the beginning of saving them. Sometimes it is. Sometimes the adversary knows exactly what they are doing. The writing becomes strongest when it lets his compassion create problems as well as solve them.

## Carter turns wonder into something you can do

Captain Carter enters a story whose central machine is already operating. Her task is not to invent the Stargate or to perform a lecture on theoretical physics. It is to make an immensely complicated set of uncertainties manageable enough for a mission to proceed.

That creates a distinctive kind of action scene. Carter studies the evidence; something refuses to behave; she proposes a mechanism; and suddenly everyone must move before a calculation fails. Her expertise can be as urgent as a chase. The science-fiction physics is often invented, but her dramatic behavior follows the logic of engineering: identify a constraint, test an explanation, and find a workable solution under pressure.

In *Solitudes*, Jack and Carter seem to have been thrown off course during a Stargate journey. They are trapped in an icy environment, injured and struggling to survive, while their colleagues on Earth search for an explanation. Their apparent isolation changes meaning as evidence accumulates. The episode rewards attention to details that initially seem inconsequential. Carter's usefulness is inseparable from her willingness to reconsider the first explanation.

She is also a military officer. This dual identity is easy to describe and harder to write well. When Jack orders something she believes technically impossible, she must explain why without pretending that the hierarchy has disappeared. When an experiment might work but the team lacks time to be certain, she must live with decisions made on incomplete evidence. Her relationship to Jack is therefore not the simple opposition of intelligence and instinct. It is a working negotiation between different forms of responsibility.

The first season sometimes announces Carter's competence too loudly, as if the audience must be convinced that a woman belongs on an elite military team. The series improves whenever it stops making a speech about her qualifications and simply allows her to perform the work. Her curiosity, irritation, humor, resourcefulness and judgment are much better arguments than any introductory declaration.

A useful detail is that Carter can be amazed. Expertise has not made the galaxy ordinary to her. She recognizes a technical miracle as a miracle worth studying, while also considering whether it will explode. The two reactions belong together. Science in this series is neither a wet blanket laid over adventure nor a magical incantation that dispenses solutions. It is another way of being fascinated by the world.

## Teal'c knows the price of being wrong

Teal'c does not merely give SG-1 access to enemy tactics. He changes the moral status of what they are fighting. Through him, the Jaffa cease to be a uniform category of heavily armed opponents. They become people who have families, beliefs, obligations and reasons to doubt their masters.

His first great act is a choice made in defiance of the only political order he has known. Yet the series is careful, especially as it develops, not to treat that choice as an instant purification. In *Cor-ai*, Teal'c returns to a world where a man recognizes him as the Jaffa who killed his father. The accusation concerns something Teal'c did while serving Apophis. His present allegiance cannot simply erase it.

Jack initially wants to get him out. From the team's perspective, that is loyalty: rescue your friend from a system you do not understand. Daniel argues for respecting the local proceedings. Teal'c refuses to flee, because he recognizes the claim against him. The characters are not arguing about a hypothetical moral rule. Each has a defensible relation to the person at the center of the danger.

Teal'c's quietness gives these scenes their weight. He does not fill every uncomfortable silence with an explanation of why he has changed. Christopher Judge often makes him visibly listen while the others talk. A small movement of the eyes, a pause before accepting Jack's absurd suggestion, or a carefully delivered answer can be funnier than a page of dialogue. The comedy depends on his being fully aware of what is ridiculous, rather than failing to understand human behavior.

Teal'c's restraint is also the mark of someone who has learned to conceal feelings in a violent hierarchy. As we become familiar with him, that restraint can read as anger, loyalty, embarrassment, affection or determination. The audience learns to attend to a character who initially looks imposing and unapproachable.

He is not simply a redeemed villain in military uniform. He is a man trying to help dismantle an institution while living with his own participation in it. And because he is a member of SG-1, the story can keep returning to that difficult project without turning every week's adventure into a sermon.

## The fifth person at the table

The original team has a commanding officer on Earth: General George Hammond, played by Don S. Davis. He seldom gets to walk through the gate with them. His absence from the field is an important part of the design.

Hammond is responsible for people he cannot watch. A mission can go silent. A radio transmission can be deceptive. A scientific explanation can arrive too late to clarify whether his personnel are alive. He has to balance the recommendations of people he trusts against orders from authorities who are not standing in the gate room.

His presence stops the SGC from becoming a private club of four adventurous friends. Somebody has to approve missions, treat injuries, arrange resupply, and answer for the program's decisions. There are also technicians, guards, medical officers, and other teams who share the consequences. SG-1 may be the title, but the drama takes place inside an institution.

The show's generosity toward Hammond is that his authority is not automatically an obstacle. He can disagree with his field officers and still take them seriously. He can order them to return and understand why they are reluctant. The audience learns to trust him not because he gives the team permission to do anything, but because the permission sometimes has to be earned.

Consider the difference between a leader who says no because the plot needs obstruction and a leader who says no because dozens of people could die. SG-1 is capable of both kinds of television, but Hammond is at his best when the second kind governs the scene. He makes the gate room feel like a place where choices are recorded and carried forward.

## One episode, four incompatible instincts

*The Nox*, a first-season story, is a compact demonstration of why the ensemble works. SG-1 travels to a world in search of a potentially valuable capability. The expedition collides with Apophis's presence and discovers a group of people who initially appear unable or unwilling to defend themselves with violence.

From Jack's perspective, the Goa'uld present an immediate tactical danger. Teal'c knows just how dangerous Apophis can be. Carter wants to understand the abilities the team has witnessed. Daniel is inclined to ask what these people believe and why they are refusing the kind of help Earth would offer.

The Nox do not fit neatly into the roles the team has prepared for them. Their refusal to fight does not mean they are powerless. Their apparent vulnerability does not mean SG-1 understands how their society survives. The humans arrive eager to save people and slowly discover that they may be the least knowledgeable participants in the encounter.

A weaker adventure would give each character a designated clue and combine the clues into a solution. This episode does something richer. It makes the entire team's starting description suspect. Jack's protective urgency is sincere but can become arrogance. Daniel's instinct to listen is valuable but not an automatic answer. Carter's curiosity does not entitle Earth to another civilization's technology. Teal'c's knowledge of Apophis tells the team what they are facing, but not what the Nox need.

The episode is enjoyable because the team remains recognizably itself even while its assumptions fail. The same behavior that makes these people competent on one world makes them clumsy on another. The lesson does not require the four to become different people by the end. It requires them to retain the capacity to be surprised.

## Friendship does not require agreement

Television friendships often begin by sanding down differences. The abrasive soldier becomes kinder, the scholar more pragmatic, the scientist less formal, and the outsider more comfortable, until everyone reaches a pleasant middle. SG-1 does change its characters, but the relationships work precisely because those changes do not eliminate the differences.

Jack still finds Daniel exhausting after he has learned to respect him. Daniel still resists orders he thinks are morally inadequate. Carter still talks through a technical problem when Jack wants to know whether they will survive it. Teal'c still takes obligations seriously that the others would prefer to escape with an improvised plan. Their confidence in one another does not require constant approval.

There is an affectionate grammar to the scenes. Jack interrupts an explanation; Daniel protests; Carter supplies the important point in fewer words; Teal'c observes something neither of them has considered. The sequence can be funny even when no one says anything designed as a joke. Years of repeated disagreement make the timing pleasurable. We recognize not just the characters but the space between them.

Yet the team is not beyond criticism. Some early plots lean on convenient cultural stereotypes. *Emancipation*, for example, confronts Carter with an alien society defined largely by restrictive gender customs, but the scenario provides fewer interesting dimensions to its local characters than it could have. A story can announce the protagonists' virtues without doing much to understand the people they encounter. The same ensemble that can question its own assumptions is sometimes placed in writing that has already settled the argument for it.

That is a real limitation, and it helps us see what the best episodes accomplish differently. *The Nox* refuses to let SG-1's benevolence establish their superiority. *Cor-ai* makes the team's own friend responsible for a terrible act. *The Torment of Tantalus* shows the costs of a good scholar's devotion. These stories allow the characters to be admirable without making every one of their instincts correct.

The real reward of long-form storytelling is that a relationship can carry this history. Jack's next sarcastic remark lands differently after we have watched him protect Daniel. A small gesture of concern from Teal'c means more when we know what he has risked to be there. The team becomes familiar, and the familiarity continues to generate surprise because the people are not interchangeable.

## A decision nobody could make alone

By the time *The Fifth Race* arrives in the second season, the team's collaboration can support a story in which one member is losing the ability to communicate in his own language. Jack is overwhelmed by an ancient repository of knowledge. He becomes capable of extraordinary feats while becoming less able to explain what is happening to him. A skill acquired at ruinous personal cost begins to look less like an advantage than a crisis.

Daniel recognizes fragments of meaning. Carter tries to understand the mechanism and what can be done with it. Teal'c offers steady practical assistance. Hammond has to decide how far to trust a mission he cannot fully comprehend. Jack himself is both the source of the next clue and the person the others are trying to save.

It is an elegant reversal of the show's ordinary pattern. Usually the team divides a problem among its members. Here the problem is lodged in one of them, and no one can solve it merely by being the expert in their own field. Knowledge, empathy, engineering and judgment all have to remain in conversation.

The episode eventually enlarges the universe with an encounter that suggests humanity might someday be accepted among much older civilizations. It does not accomplish this by discovering that Jack secretly belongs to an exalted bloodline or that Earth was always guaranteed a special destiny. The possibility is earned through action, cooperation, curiosity and the ability to survive an encounter that should have been beyond the team's reach.

Those are qualities the four have been demonstrating, imperfectly, since the first trip.

## The people waiting at the door

There is a small ritual at the end of many SG-1 adventures. The travelers return through the event horizon. They come down the ramp into the familiar gate room. Somebody is waiting, and the astonishing discovery of the week becomes a report, a medical examination, an argument with the general, or a story that one member will tell badly and another will correct.

That ritual is why the series can send us to so many places without losing its center. We do not need to care about every stone ruin or every alien technology equally. We care about the people who come back changed by what they have seen, sometimes in ways they scarcely notice themselves.

The series could have been a weekly demonstration of a science-fiction prop. It could have been a chain of military rescues, or an archaeological mystery that reset at the end of each hour. The ensemble makes it more generous than any one of those formulas. The action hero needs the linguist. The linguist needs the officer who will end the mission. The engineer needs a team willing to trust a calculation. The former enemy needs companions who believe change is possible without pretending that his past has vanished.

Four people walk through a doorway. The destination is extraordinary; their first conversation on the other side is generally about something ordinary: where to go, whether to trust what they see, and how to bring everybody home. The galaxy supplies the question. The team makes the answer worth hearing.
