# 014000 — The Nox, The Other Side, and the Cost of Helping

**Track:** Stargate SG-1: Through the Gate. **Audience:** Fans and viewers interested in SG-1's ethical dilemmas. **Prerequisites:** The basic premise of SG-1 as a military exploration team. **Scope:** Pacifism, first contact, technological exchange, racial ideology, rescue, noninterference and conflicting civilizations. **Spoilers:** Complete plots of *The Nox*, *Learning Curve*, *The Other Side* and *Scorched Earth*, including a morally severe ending to *The Other Side*.

# The Nox, The Other Side, and the Cost of Helping

A new Stargate address appears on a computer screen. A team walks into a room with a metal ring at one end and equipment designed to keep everyone alive at the other. There is a burst of light, a shimmering surface, and four people step through to another world.

In the most comforting version of *Stargate SG-1*, the people on that world need help. They are threatened by the Goa'uld, or stranded, or ill, or unaware of some danger that the visitors from Earth can solve. SG-1 asks the right questions, argues amiably, improvises with a few pieces of technology, and leaves the world better off than it found it. We are invited to love the team for precisely that reason.

But what happens if the people asking for rescue are aggressors? What if the civilization refusing Earth's help understands the problem better than the soldiers who just arrived? What if two groups will die unless the visitors choose which one to save? What if the act of teaching someone a humane value comes with an injured child whom the lesson cannot repair?

The series asks these questions most convincingly not through elaborate speeches about foreign policy, but through episodes that begin as ordinary missions. A promising technology appears. A friendly voice comes through the gate. A person is in danger. The team's first response is morally attractive. Then a detail makes it difficult to continue acting on that first response without further thought.

Four stories in particular reward comparison: *The Nox*, *Learning Curve*, *The Other Side*, and *Scorched Earth*. They are not a single coherent philosophy of intervention. They do, however, supply four different ways that a confident rescue mission can encounter another society's right to refuse, another society's culpability, and the limits of SG-1's knowledge.

## The people who refuse to play the game

In the first-season episode *The Nox*, Earth's government wants the Stargate program to obtain useful technology. A report of a strange creature suggests that a world may contain some means of becoming invisible. The attraction is obvious: an enemy who can detect you has weapons and ships far beyond Earth's ability to defend against. An invisibility technology might save lives.

SG-1 travels there and encounters Apophis, who is also hunting. Jack plans to ambush him. For a moment, the episode looks like a straightforward opportunity to defeat an established villain and bring home something valuable. Instead, the team is killed and restored by the Nox, an apparently gentle people who live in the forest and refuse violence.

The Nox appear almost offensively defenseless to visitors trained to think in terms of tactical advantage. They can conceal living beings and bring back people who have been killed. They do not use those powers to drive out Apophis. When SG-1 proposes to defend them, their elder, Anteaus, rejects the offer.

The tension here is genuine because Jack's fear is not imaginary. Apophis does kill. He brings armed servants into the forest. The Nox's refusal to fight might seem admirable only until someone else pays for it. The visitors see a vulnerable family, including a child, and cannot understand why people with so much power will not use it in a way Jack recognizes as protection.

The episode's great reversal comes when the Nox reveal that their small settlement is not the measure of their civilization. Their astonishing city, hidden above the forest, discloses technological and perhaps moral experience well beyond what SG-1 imagined. The people whom Jack regarded as naïve are the ones who have been gently, persistently educating him.

That last revelation is delightful, but it should not be allowed to solve every question automatically. If the Nox are very powerful, their pacifism becomes easier to maintain. They are not a village with no means of defending itself against a conquering army. One might ask what their principles would demand if the cost of nonviolence fell on neighbors who did not possess such extraordinary protection. The story does not fully answer that question.

It does make a strong objection to a familiar imperial habit of mind. The person who arrives with weapons and assumes the natives need protection may know less about their situation than they do. Jack's desire to help is sincere. His premise—that these strangers need help on the terms he understands—is unsupported. The Nox are not grateful pupils waiting for a lecture about civilization. They are capable of disagreeing and of requiring the visitor to leave.

The scene changes the meaning of power. A culture can possess immense technological capacity and decline to use it to impose its conclusions. SG-1 normally measures progress by what a device permits the team to do. The Nox ask whether possessing a capability also creates a duty to deploy it. Even an action undertaken for someone else's protection may become a form of domination if the people being protected have not asked for it.

## A child who carries a civilization

*Learning Curve* provides an uncomfortable variation on the idea that advanced science reveals advanced morality. The Orbanians have developed a system for gathering knowledge with extraordinary speed. They implant children with tiny machines that accumulate experience. At a designated age, the children undergo the Averium, a procedure that transfers the acquired knowledge to the population, leaving the young person severely impaired.

SG-1 meets Merrin, a bright child helping Carter understand naquadah reactor technology. The visit is a scientific opportunity. Earth may gain the ability to generate power on a scale it badly needs. Yet Merrin's society treats the destruction of her accumulated selfhood as an acceptable mechanism for collective education.

We are meant to find this disturbing, and the episode earns that response. The child is not an abstract unit in a calculation. She can speak, learn and be curious. A society that says her sacrifice is for the good of everyone is also saying that her future as an individual does not carry enough weight to change the procedure.

Jack's response is characteristically personal. He takes Merrin out of the base and into an ordinary school so that she can paint and play. This is not a technical intervention. It is an attempt to give her a part of childhood that the Orbanians have not recognized as valuable. Her new experiences eventually spread through the knowledge-transfer procedure, changing the society's attitude toward education and play.

There is warmth in the ending. There is also a difficulty a sentimental reading can obscure. Merrin still undergoes the procedure. The transformation of the Orbanian social order is purchased, in the episode as told, with the loss of the child whose experiences produced it. The new society may treat later children differently; Merrin herself cannot be restored to the person she was before the Averium.

The story wants Jack's defiance to be an act of kindness, and in one respect it is. The day at the school is real, not merely an educational instrument. Yet the result also demonstrates how easily a generous gesture can become part of an experiment in changing a civilization. Jack gives Merrin a chance to have fun, but the audience is rewarded when her experience changes the whole planet. We should notice the person whose loss enables that happy outcome.

This is one of the reasons *SG-1* is more interesting when its central team is allowed to be imperfect. The episode does not have to be condemned because it ends hopefully. We can appreciate the humane impulse, understand why the writers want the Orbanians to learn, and still feel the unresolved pain of the child left behind. The desire to help can be better than indifference without thereby becoming morally complete.

## The voice that asks Earth to save it

*The Other Side* begins with one of the simplest and most effective moral hooks in the series. A voice has been calling Earth through the Stargate. Before reliable communication is established, several people sent through the gate have died against Earth's protective iris. When the SGC finally speaks with the caller, he is a human-looking representative of Euronda, a society under siege.

A person at the end of a desperate telephone call knows how to tell the story of their suffering. Alar speaks as a leader whose people are being bombed, whose supplies are failing, and whose technology may help Earth in return for humanitarian assistance. The offer answers Earth's tactical anxiety and its charitable impulse simultaneously. The program needs new weapons and defenses. These people need food, supplies and a way to continue resisting.

It is a credible temptation. General Hammond cannot disregard the possibility of a trap, but the first impulse to send help is not foolish. If somebody contacts you because they are about to be destroyed, refusing to listen is itself an act with consequences. The SGC begins with humanitarian concerns and then finds itself entangled in a military bargain.

The difficulty is that Alar's account has a missing half. Who are the Eurondans fighting? How did the war begin? What do the words he uses for his enemies mean? Daniel keeps asking questions that threaten to spoil the arrangement. Jack is more willing to accept the immediate evidence of a society being bombed. His impatience is understandable and hazardous in exactly the same way.

The reveal is severe. The Eurondans have pursued a program of genetic purity. They regard their opponents as inferior because of the way they reproduce and the diversity of their population. The Eurondans built an underground refuge and poisoned the sky in a campaign of extermination. They are not innocent victims who have been misunderstood by a ruthless enemy. They have been trying to destroy the people they call their enemies.

The episode allows information to arrive through more than an explanatory speech. There are the people who have been preserved in stasis, the way Alar discusses Teal'c, the names attached to the enemy, the design of the underground complex. The clues are embedded in social behavior and infrastructure. The truth is visible before the leader is willing to present it as a confession.

The ending is among the most disturbing in the show's early run. Jack turns the Eurondans' remote fighters against their defenses, helping the opposing force destroy the base. When Alar tries to escape through the Stargate, Jack warns him not to follow. Once back on Earth, Jack orders the iris closed. A subsequent impact strongly implies that Alar has been killed by that barrier.

We can understand why Jack refuses to make an alliance with an openly exterminationist regime. We can also distinguish that refusal from the decision to let its leader die after the immediate firefight. The show places that decision onscreen and does not spend long discussing whether the punishment was necessary or authorized. Carter's reaction helps keep the moment from feeling simply triumphant.

There are two moral reversals, not one. First the alleged victims are revealed as perpetrators. Then the protagonist, appalled by the perpetrators, exercises the kind of unreviewed lethal discretion that the series often reserves for villains. The episode is strongest when we resist smoothing away that second reversal. Jack can correctly reject the Eurondans' ideology and still make a decision that warrants scrutiny.

## How much information is enough?

*The Other Side* exposes the weakness of a mission designed around acquiring technology. Knowledge appears to be the means of surviving the Goa'uld threat. That is true. But acquiring a weapon from a society is not ethically neutral if the price is helping that society kill people whose identities the buyers have never investigated.

The show's premises make the pressure real. Earth is not a superpower in the galactic context of the early seasons. Its defenses are inadequate against enemy motherships. SG-1 has seen planets destroyed and friends enslaved. A remote-controlled fighter fleet and advanced power system are not trivial prizes. The temptation to accept a bargaining partner's story is greater when the prize might protect millions at home.

That is why the episode's criticism should not be simplified into a claim that scientists or soldiers simply ought to have been nicer. The practical problem is how an institution verifies what an interested party tells it when it lacks independent intelligence and faces a deadline. A rescue request and a procurement opportunity are liable to distort each other. The SGC does not yet have the evidence to justify choosing a side in a civil war, and still moves toward supplying the side that contacted it first.

Daniel's insistence on learning the history is essential, even though he cannot produce perfect information either. Jack's later reversal is a reminder that a leader who neglects the first stage of investigation may have to take far more drastic action once the missing facts become undeniable. The episode makes the cost of haste visceral.

There is a broader lesson about first contact here. Human resemblance does not establish shared values. The Eurondans look familiar, speak familiarly, and make a familiar appeal for help. Their institutions are not therefore humane. Conversely, a people who look strange or refuse familiar forms of government need not be in need of rescue. Put *The Other Side* beside *The Nox* and the danger becomes clear: the team is tempted to trust the wrong people because they appear legible, and to underestimate the right people because they do not.

## Two worlds and one machine

The fourth-season episode *Scorched Earth* offers a dilemma that is hard for a different reason. SG-1 has helped the Enkarans relocate to a world where their biology permits them to survive. A massive vessel arrives and begins changing the planet's atmosphere and ecosystem. The process will destroy the new settlement. It is easy to decide that the machine is an enemy and ought to be stopped.

Then the team learns what the machine is carrying. The Gadmeer, a sophisticated civilization destroyed by war, have preserved the materials and knowledge needed to reconstruct their species. The vessel has searched for a planet capable of supporting that project. It chose the Enkarans' world before they were there and began transforming it without recognizing the claims of the recent arrivals.

Now the straightforward rescue has become a conflict between two populations at risk of extinction. The Enkarans cannot survive on most worlds. The Gadmeer cannot easily restart the reconstruction project elsewhere. SG-1 helped move the Enkarans onto this planet and has acquired responsibility for their safety. The machine embodies the last known hope of a different civilization that has already suffered catastrophe.

Jack reaches for the option a field commander can execute: destroy the vessel before it destroys the people he promised to protect. Daniel refuses to regard that as the only possible choice. He talks to Lotan, the ship's representative, a bio-mechanical being made in the Enkarans' image. Jack's proposed attack may end one civilization in order to save the other; Daniel's effort may leave the Enkarans in the path of a transformation that cannot simply be halted.

Their disagreement is not a clash between a brave humanitarian and a cruel soldier. Jack recognizes a duty to the people the team has relocated. If no solution appears, he must decide whether to let those people die while defending a machine's claim to begin a civilization. Daniel recognizes a duty not to destroy an entire possible future merely because its immediate victims are easier to recognize than the people encoded in the vessel.

The resolution depends on a new fact. During the ship's search, it rejected a planet partly because intelligent Enkaran life already lived there. That planet is the Enkarans' original homeworld. Lotan can take them home, and the Gadmeer project can continue on the present planet. The supposedly insoluble moral conflict was being held in place by incomplete information and a mission system unable to imagine a different arrangement.

This is a satisfying science-fiction answer, and also a convenient one. In the real world, the third option may not exist. The strong lesson is not that every dilemma has a secretly perfect resolution if a sympathetic archaeologist keeps talking. It is that a team ought to be cautious before declaring that the options it can presently see are the only options available.

There is a quieter point about representation. Lotan looks like an Enkaran because the vessel built him to communicate with the people on the planet. This makes dialogue possible. Daniel's persuasion does not succeed because he has discovered a magic phrase for overriding a computer. It succeeds because he helps the representative perceive the affected lives and reinterpret the mission. A machine built to preserve civilization begins to understand that the means of doing so can betray the values the preservation was meant to protect.

## Does the gate create an obligation?

These episodes are effective in part because the Stargate itself makes neutrality difficult. A wormhole is not a view through a telescope. It is an accessible route between communities. If an ambassador calls for help, Earth may have to decide whether to send supplies. If a team moves refugees, it becomes implicated in their safety. If the visitors reveal a weakness in a ruler's authority, they may change the political future of a planet simply by returning home.

The SGC's stated mission combines exploration, protection and technology acquisition. Those goals are not always compatible. A scientist wants to understand a reactor; a commander wants a weapon; a physician wants to treat a patient; a displaced person wants a safe place to live. The institution cannot know beforehand which objective will become urgent on a given world.

*The Nox* warns against assuming authority because one has arrived with a gun. *Learning Curve* warns that a society's prosperity can conceal the exploitation of a child, and also shows that a well-intentioned intervention may leave a particular child irreversibly harmed. *The Other Side* warns that victims' stories can be selective and that the discovery of guilt does not give the rescuer an automatic exemption from moral scrutiny. *Scorched Earth* warns that one population's emergency may be inseparable from another population's survival.

It is tempting to convert those four stories into a neat policy: always intervene, never intervene, always investigate, or follow the orders of the locals. None is adequate. Investigation can take time that a dying person does not have. The locals may be divided. Refusal to intervene may preserve one's innocence at the expense of someone else's life. Decisive action may prevent an atrocity or become part of one.

The characters are attractive precisely because they disagree. Jack is usually quicker to accept the responsibility of an immediate decision. Daniel is quicker to suspect that the framing is incomplete. Carter often discovers the physical conditions that make an alternative possible. Teal'c can recognize the experience of someone whose apparent cooperation has been shaped by a coercive order. Hammond must determine what his people can lawfully and practically commit the Earth to doing.

Those distinctions do not divide into good conscience and bad conscience. A commander who will not decide can be as dangerous as one who decides too quickly. A translator who asks another question can prevent a disaster, but can also be accused of asking it when nobody has time to answer. The team's conversations are its ethical equipment, just as much as the iris and the dialing computer are its technical equipment.

## Leaving by the same gate

There is a visual rhythm to *SG-1* that encourages a comforting fiction. The team leaves through a gate, confronts a problem, and returns. The ring shuts down behind them. The mission seems finished, and the next episode can begin on another morning.

The best ethical stories resist that closure. The Nox remain on their world with principles the team has not adopted. Merrin remains altered after teaching her society to value play. The Eurondans' base is destroyed, and Jack carries the knowledge of what he ordered at the iris. The Enkarans can go home because Daniel and Lotan found an overlooked possibility, but there is no guarantee that every future conflict will permit such a solution.

These stories also defend the show's optimism more strongly than an endless sequence of clean victories would. To help someone is not always to know best. To ask questions is not always to avoid responsibility. To refuse a weapon may require courage, and to accept one may create obligations that cannot be discharged by winning a battle.

What makes the Stargate such an effective narrative invention is that it does not grant mastery over the place to which it leads. It offers access. A new address promises another story, but not the right to define that story for the people already living there.

The team can walk through the gate with training, courage, and excellent intentions. Then the strangers on the other side may tell them something they do not want to hear. They may need to be saved. They may refuse to be saved. They may be lying. They may have an answer nobody on Earth could have imagined.

A good *Stargate* adventure lets the people who step through the ring be changed by the encounter as well. It makes rescue a difficult activity rather than a flattering identity. Sometimes SG-1 does the right thing. Sometimes it finds the right thing only after nearly doing the wrong one. And sometimes the gate closes before anyone has earned the satisfaction of a clean answer.

## Source and interpretation notes (not spoken)

Primary story anchors are the episodes *The Nox* (1997; Hart Hanson; Charles Correll), *Learning Curve* (1999; Heather E. Ash; Martin Wood), *The Other Side* (2000; Brad Wright; Peter DeLuise), and *Scorched Earth* (2000; Joseph Mallozzi and Paul Mullie; Martin Wood). The Nox rescue SG-1 and later reveal their advanced city. Merrin undergoes the Averium and the Orbanians receive her experiences, but she is not simply restored. Alar's ideology, Euronda's attack on its opponents, Jack's redirection of drone aircraft and his order to close the iris are all depicted in *The Other Side*; the impact after the closure strongly implies but does not explicitly show Alar's death. Lotan discovers that the Enkarans' homeworld is the planet previously rejected by the Gadmeer vessel, permitting the two groups to survive. The broader claims about intervention, consent, competing institutions and military decision-making are the essay's critical interpretations, not credited statements of writer intent or references to actual international law.
