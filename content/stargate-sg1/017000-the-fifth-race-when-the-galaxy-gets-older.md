# 017000 — The Fifth Race: When the Galaxy Gets Older

**Track:** Stargate SG-1: Through the Gate. **Audience:** Fans and curious newer viewers who know the original team. **Prerequisites:** The Stargate premise and, ideally, *The Torment of Tantalus*. **Scope:** A close reading of the season-two episode *The Fifth Race*, its character construction, production history, and the mythology it opens. **Spoilers:** Full spoilers for *The Fifth Race*, with brief references to later Ancient and Asgard stories through the finale.

# The Fifth Race: When the Galaxy Gets Older

Seven chevrons. This is how an ordinary journey begins. The address is entered, the ring turns, the machine locks its symbols, and a circle of light gives way to water. People have been doing this on *Stargate SG-1* for a season and a half. The device is astonishing, but the audience knows its grammar.

Then the eighth chevron engages.

The Stargate does not grow larger. Nobody wheels in a more impressive ring. There is no new spaceship unveiling its engines. The familiar machine performs one operation that it has never performed before, and suddenly the scale of the universe changes.

That is one of the achievements of *The Fifth Race*, a second-season episode written by Robert C. Cooper and directed by David Warry-Smith. The story is about Jack O'Neill suffering an involuntary flood of alien knowledge. Yet its greatest revelation arrives when something we have learned to take for granted shows us that we have barely begun to understand it.

An extra chevron is a splendidly economical way to make a science-fiction universe feel bigger. It does not replace the earlier rules. It tells us the earlier rules were incomplete.

And because this is *Stargate SG-1*, the revelation is inseparable from a man who does not want to be a scholar and is becoming one against his will.

## A clue that survived a collapse

The episode begins by reaching backward. In the first-season story *The Torment of Tantalus*, SG-1 finds a ruined meeting hall on a remote planet, where four ancient civilizations once exchanged knowledge. Daniel Jackson is enthralled by the possibility. On the walls are writing systems that belong to different alien peoples; the room implies cooperation across distances and lifetimes that the young Stargate program can scarcely imagine.

The hall is collapsing. Its secrets cannot simply be packed in a crate and taken home. The team survives, but the object Daniel most wants to preserve is lost.

At first this might have looked like a typical television adventure's bittersweet ending: the characters glimpse an extraordinary thing, then the set is destroyed and the next week's story begins. *The Fifth Race* refuses that kind of forgetfulness. Records from the old expedition remain. An image returned by a probe shows symbols related to one of the languages in the destroyed room. Daniel recognizes them. An earlier disappointment has become the reason for a new journey.

This is a major pleasure of serialized adventure. The world does not have to wait until an episode announces a mythological arc before it begins accumulating history. Something seen in passing can acquire significance later, because the characters carried it home in a notebook, a memory, or a question. A collapse does not destroy the value of having looked.

Daniel has an idea about who the writers of those inscriptions might have been. The Asgard, who have represented themselves to some human societies through the imagery of Norse mythology, may have belonged to the ancient alliance. The other languages could belong to their peers. It is a tempting theory: the galaxy may have had an intellectual history much richer than the reigns and rivalries of the Goa'uld.

Notice how radically that shifts the apparent past. The Goa'uld surround themselves with pyramids, titles, ceremonial weapons, and the grandeur of old gods. They insist upon a story in which their authority is primordial. Here is evidence of a civilization of civilizations, one that does not appear to have been organized around one species demanding worship from all the others.

The clue does not tell the team what that history was. It tells them there is something worth finding out.

## The emptiest room in the galaxy

SG-1 reaches the planet designated P3R-272. The destination is not particularly spectacular. The team finds a small room, a few markings, and an apparent device that seems to offer no straightforward way of operating it. Jack quickly loses patience. Daniel would like to stay and make sense of the place. They have been arguing about how long a mystery deserves their time since the beginning of the series.

Then Jack looks into a viewer in the wall, and it seizes him.

What follows is closer to a medical emergency than an archaeological discovery. A mechanism reaches out, floods his mind with information, and leaves him unconscious. The team brings him home. Dr. Janet Fraiser examines him. Initially he seems to have escaped serious injury, but something has changed.

The spareness of the location is important. A huge decorated temple would announce that the audience should expect an ancient revelation. A nearly empty room does not give the eye much to celebrate. The technology acts first, and understanding must follow. We are not invited to admire the architecture for several minutes before a wise hologram explains its purpose. We watch a familiar person suffer the consequences of using an object nobody could identify.

The scene also inverts the usual Stargate premise. Normally Daniel reads a text, Carter experiments on a machine, and Jack decides whether the results are worth the danger. Here, the machine reads Jack. It bypasses his preferences and places the results somewhere that his teammates cannot simply examine.

We are used to the science-fiction fantasy of downloading knowledge. It often functions as a shortcut: put a device on somebody's head and they will know a lost language, a secret martial art, or the location of a cosmic treasure. *The Fifth Race* asks a less convenient question. What if knowledge arrives without understanding? What if it exceeds the capacity of the mind asked to carry it? What if learning something is not an addition to the person you were, but a displacement of that person?

Jack's accident is, in that sense, a kind of possession without a possessor. No villain is deliberately steering him. Yet he is not entirely free.

## The joke turns frightening

At first, the change is funny. Jack begins replacing familiar English words with words nobody else knows. He insists he did not say what Daniel heard him say. The disagreement has the structure of an ordinary Jack-and-Daniel quarrel: Daniel is amused and inquisitive; Jack is increasingly irritated that a scholarly discussion is happening to him.

Richard Dean Anderson is particularly good at playing the indignity of being the only person in the room who does not understand his own sentence. The physical comedy and the verbal comedy pull against each other. Jack sounds like Jack. His impatience sounds like Jack. But the words he intended to say are slipping away before they reach his mouth.

There is a clever dramatic reason to assign this condition to O'Neill. If Daniel acquired a new language involuntarily, he might be fascinated even while terrified. If Carter suddenly became able to manipulate machines far beyond human understanding, she might initially see possibilities for research. Jack resists the entire premise. He wants to be understood by the people he trusts. The new knowledge does not delight him, because it is taking away the thing he uses to make himself known.

In the gym, in the infirmary, and in the briefing room, the joke repeats with a different pressure each time. The characters attempt translation. Daniel hears affinities between the strange language and Latin. Carter and the technicians confront effects in computers that nobody has been authorized to produce. Fraiser becomes increasingly concerned that Jack's brain is being altered in ways a normal body cannot sustain.

The funniest scenes are funny because the danger has not yet swallowed the character. He is still capable of irritation, mischief, and the dry timing that makes so many SG-1 conversations enjoyable. But as the episode progresses, his inability to communicate ceases to feel like a temporary comic inconvenience.

There is something quietly horrifying about losing a language you have been speaking since childhood. The people who love you can recognize your face. They can see that you are frightened. They cannot know exactly what you are asking them to do. Jack becomes increasingly brilliant at things nobody expected him to know and increasingly unable to tell anyone what he wants.

The episode's science-fiction joke contains the possibility of a very ordinary kind of isolation.

## A human body, not a library shelf

The explanation gradually emerges. Jack has encountered a repository of Ancient knowledge, a storehouse created by the civilization that built the Stargates. The information has entered him wholesale. The episode does not pretend that possessing data and being capable of using it are equivalent. Instead it depicts two contradictory processes: an expansion of capacity in some areas and a growing loss of ordinary function in others.

Jack can enter vast numbers of gate addresses into the base computers, apparently adding destinations unknown to the SGC. He can manipulate unfamiliar code with ease. Yet the act feels almost automatic, as if a part of him recognizes a system the rest of him has never learned. He draws and builds devices while struggling to explain what those devices are for.

Here is a smart distinction: the episode does not transform him into a confident lecturer on Ancient civilization. It makes him a carrier of knowledge that is only partially accessible to the personality we know. He has not completed ten million years of graduate school. He has been made into an unsuitable storage device.

Fraiser's concern grounds the premise. An enormous archive may be precious to the military, to scientists, and to historians. None of those interests makes Jack's impending death acceptable. People around him can admire the information and still need to remove it. The fact that the repository contains wonders is not an argument for allowing it to destroy the person holding them.

There is also a gentler version of the show's recurring critique of the Goa'uld here. False gods demand submission to superior knowledge and power. The Ancients' device does not appear to demand worship. It does, however, demonstrate that a technology can be indifferent to the person it damages. We should not confuse an ancient artifact with a benevolent intention simply because it belongs to a civilization more sophisticated than our own.

Daniel wants to decode the situation. Carter wants to keep the machines under control. Fraiser wants her patient alive. Hammond must consider whether the growing changes make Jack a danger to himself and to the installation. Nobody can simply accept the archive as a gift without accepting its consequences.

## A second mission becomes a rescue

The episode complicates matters with another team expedition. Carter and Teal'c travel through the gate and find themselves stranded when their destination's dialing device malfunctions. There is a further hazard: the planet's environment will soon become dangerously hot. What looked like a routine attempt to explore another lead turns into a countdown.

The structure is good television engineering. Jack's condition is the central mystery, but it could easily become repetitive if every scene consisted of somebody asking him what he knows. The stranded team gives his new knowledge an immediate purpose. It also divides the original ensemble so that the people trying to save Jack and the people who need Jack's help cannot simply stand around the same conference table.

As ordinary communication fails, Jack manages to provide information that helps solve the dialing problem. The insight has not restored him. A man losing access to his own thoughts can still save his friends. The operation is both a reminder of what his new knowledge can accomplish and evidence that it is not knowledge he knows how to live with.

Carter and Teal'c are essential here. Teal'c provides competence grounded in experience with alien equipment and hostile environments; Carter supplies the scientific interpretation and the work needed to turn instructions into action. Jack's alien data does not make their skills obsolete. It becomes useful only because other people can receive, question, and apply it.

This is an especially satisfying way to avoid the lone superhuman savior problem. Jack has acquired an astonishing capacity by accident. His friends have developed their capacities through years of ordinary, recognizable labor. The episode needs both.

## The machine beyond seven chevrons

Soon Jack builds a strange power unit using materials available at the SGC, including components from Teal'c's equipment. He attaches it to the gate system. The computers are no longer behaving according to the familiar pattern. The dialing sequence runs beyond the usual address.

The eighth chevron locks.

We have now reached the episode's second great shift. The first was internal: Jack knows more than a person can bear. This one is spatial: the gate knows more than the Stargate program understands. Carter suggests that the additional symbol may permit travel beyond the established network, to another galaxy.

An extraordinary amount of science-fiction writing is devoted to making a universe larger through expensive new images. The simplest method is to show another solar system or a bigger fleet. *The Fifth Race* enlarges the map by changing the use of a piece of machinery already built into the premise. The visual effect is a familiar circle of light. The conceptual effect is an entire new category of distance.

This is an earned development because the earlier episode supplied the mystery, the team's previous journeys supplied encounters with advanced peoples, and Jack's crisis supplies the immediate need. A random new transport system would have felt like a way for the writers to skip past established obstacles. An unfamiliar feature of the Stargate itself feels like an answer hidden inside the premise all along.

Hammond has to decide what to do. Jack's strange device has taken command of the dialing system. A man whose mind is failing is about to step through a gate leading somewhere that the SGC cannot identify and may not be able to reach again. In a show that regularly treats the gate as an instrument of hope, here it is also the only possible way out of a medical emergency.

Jack goes.

## The Asgard are not gods either

He arrives on Othala, an Asgard world in the galaxy of Ida. The beings he encounters are familiar in appearance to viewers of the show's earlier Norse-mythology stories: small, physically delicate-looking aliens who possess technical capacities far beyond those of the humans they have been observing.

Their presence changes the tone. Much of the episode has been about a person's mind breaking under the strain of an alien archive. The Asgard can recognize the problem. They explain that the repository was not built for human physiology and remove its contents, allowing Jack to recover.

Now the encounter can become a conversation rather than an ordeal.

The Asgard confirm the old alliance Daniel glimpsed at Heliopolis. Its members were the Asgard, the Nox, the Furlings, and the Ancients. These names do not function as a list of monsters scheduled to be fought. They imply an older diplomatic and intellectual order, one in which even powerful races did not exist as isolated masters of all creation.

There is something refreshing about the Asgard's combination of condescension and generosity. They are not interested in pretending that humans already know enough to sit as equals in the alliance. They also do not demand that Jack bow before them. They treat his predicament as a problem that can be fixed. They can admit humanity is still developing while regarding its development as promising.

That makes the conversation a pointed contrast with the Goa'uld. The Goa'uld maintain authority by announcing that a gap in power is a gap in fundamental worth: because they possess advanced technology, other beings must serve them. The Asgard acknowledge a gap without treating it as justification for enslavement. They recognize potential, and their response to a human who needs help is to help him.

It is important not to turn them into perfect elders. They have been observing Earth without entering an equal relationship with it; their understanding of humanity is incomplete; later stories reveal deep limitations to their own power. This first significant meeting is valuable precisely because they offer the possibility of a different relationship, not proof that the universe has benevolent parents who will solve Earth's troubles forever.

## Why Jack is the right ambassador

Jack's response to his rescuers has a pleasing lack of ceremony. He is grateful. He is also entirely himself again, capable of jokes and of the plain insistence that people like his own are doing their best. He does not deliver an elaborate account of human destiny. He tells extraordinarily advanced beings that Earth is curious, inexperienced, and now out there taking risks.

That is a modest defense of humanity. It is also a more believable defense than a speech listing the species' glorious achievements. The Asgard have seen enough civilizations to know that one impressive machine or battlefield victory is not a moral qualification. Jack's case for Earth is a description of activity and attitude, not a claim to supremacy.

We have just watched his friends refuse to abandon him because he was no longer useful as his old self. We have watched him struggle to save colleagues while losing the means to explain himself. Those are better arguments for humanity's potential than a stolen database of impressive facts.

When the Asgard say that humans have begun to take the steps toward becoming a fifth race, the words should be heard as recognition of a possibility. They are not awarding a diploma. They do not declare that Earth has become the equal of the Ancients or the Nox overnight. They see promise, and the episode has given us reasons to think that promise is something other than raw technological power.

It is also funny that this expansive mythology is being introduced to the member of SG-1 least inclined to treat the revelation with academic reverence. Daniel would want to ask questions for a week. Carter would want a tour of the equipment. Jack is mostly delighted to be alive and able to speak normally. The show uses his relief to keep the scene humane.

## The writer's wager

Cooper later described *The Fifth Race* as an effort to write an O'Neill-centered story and work more closely with Richard Dean Anderson. He also recalled wanting to return to the loose thread of the four-race meeting place and develop the Asgard's role in the series. Those recollections, offered years after the episode aired, are useful evidence of creative intention, though they are not a substitute for the finished episode itself.

What is striking is how thoroughly the choices serve one another. A writer interested only in mythology could have used Daniel to deliver an extended lecture about lost civilizations. A writer interested only in Jack could have given him a conventional rescue or military command crisis. Combining the two forces O'Neill into a problem that exposes what he values: the ability to communicate with his team, to make a useful decision, and to return home as himself.

The directing and performance preserve a tonal range that became a hallmark of the series. A language joke can become painful without the episode announcing a genre change. A machine-room mystery can become a cosmic revelation while the familiar operations of the SGC remain visible. The team can argue over a man's medical condition in one scene and carry out an interstellar rescue in another without treating either as a lesser kind of science fiction.

The production does not have to render ten million years of Ancient history. It needs a wall device, a computer screen filling with new addresses, a familiar gate behaving differently, and a few Asgard whose quiet composure makes the humans look wonderfully young. The scale is supplied by connections among the ideas.

There is a tradeoff. *The Fifth Race* gives us almost no extended encounter with the Ancient civilization itself. The Furlings remain a name. The Asgard's welcome lasts only long enough to establish a possibility. A viewer who wanted a thorough explanation of galactic history could reasonably feel that the episode is all doorways and no completed exploration.

But that is precisely what makes it a useful turning point. The episode is not a reference encyclopedia. It is an invitation to imagine that the story can now go somewhere even its protagonists could not previously conceive.

## What the promise eventually becomes

Later seasons return again and again to Ancient knowledge, and the danger it poses when the human mind cannot contain it. *Lost City* places Jack in a related ordeal, this time with the defense of Earth at stake. Other stories show how inheriting the work of an older civilization can transform a young society's political and military position. By the series finale, the Asgard's legacy has become an inheritance Earth must learn to carry.

Those developments illuminate both the strength and the danger of the promise made in *The Fifth Race*. In the hopeful reading, humanity's curiosity and cooperation lead to a larger fellowship, and the old races recognize a worthy successor. In a more critical reading, the history of the galaxy becomes a ladder leading toward Earth, with other civilizations functioning chiefly as obstacles, benefactors, or extinct predecessors.

The early episode gives us a way to resist the narrower reading. Its four great races were an alliance, not a single empire. Their greatness was not described only by the weapons they could build. The premise of their meeting is that even beings with extraordinary knowledge found reasons to learn from one another. A genuine fifth race would have to be capable of that as well.

Jack did not reach the Asgard because Earth had conquered enough worlds to earn an audience. He reached them because an accident threatened his life and an inherited machine carried him farther than anyone at the SGC knew a gate could go. The Asgard helped him before asking for proof that he deserved to survive. The title speaks of a future relationship; the episode's most convincing evidence for it is a sequence of acts of care.

And when Jack comes home, he has not brought the Ancient archive back in his head. The miraculous information is gone. Daniel still has questions. Carter still has machines to investigate. Teal'c still has his own people to free. Hammond still commands a dangerous program from under a mountain.

The series could have ended this adventure by allowing SG-1 to keep a device that made the Goa'uld suddenly irrelevant. Instead it returns the team to familiar work, with one difference. Their maps no longer describe the limits of the possible.

## The eighth symbol

Think again about that extra chevron. It is almost comically small: one additional step in a sequence of symbols viewers have watched hundreds of times. Yet it is enough to change our sense of where the Stargate leads. A familiar machine has always contained an undiscovered world of options.

Good science fiction can do this with an alien landscape. *The Fifth Race* does it with an extra movement of a ring, and with the return of a colleague who can speak plainly again.

The point is not that humanity was secretly destined to become the most powerful species in the universe. The point is that the universe is older, more inhabited, and more intellectually connected than the people of Earth imagined. Those people have entered it with incomplete knowledge, imperfect motives, and surprising capacities for friendship.

They are not ready for everything they will find. They have begun finding it anyway.

## Source and interpretation notes (not spoken)

*The Fifth Race* first aired January 22, 1999, written by Robert C. Cooper and directed by David Warry-Smith. The prior four-race meeting-place discovery is from *The Torment of Tantalus*. The eighth-chevron journey takes Jack to Othala in the Ida galaxy; it is not an ordinary seven-chevron Milky Way address. This reading treats the words “fifth race” as recognition of promise, not completed parity, consistent with the episode's dialogue. Cooper's retrospective production recollection is attributed separately in the metadata.
