# 025000 — Ascension and the Price of Refusing to Intervene

**Track:** Stargate SG-1: Through the Gate. **Audience:** Fans; philosophically oriented character and story analysis. **Prerequisites:** Basic knowledge of Daniel Jackson and the Ancients. **Scope:** Oma Desala, Shifu, Orlin, Daniel's ascension, Anubis and the Ancients' rules, with later Ori-era complications. **Spoilers:** Major events in *Maternal Instinct*, *Absolute Power*, *Ascension*, *Meridian*, *Abyss*, *Full Circle*, *Threads* and the later Ori story.

# Ascension and the Price of Refusing to Intervene

There is a diner somewhere beyond the ordinary universe. A man sits at a counter, a waitress is serving, and another customer makes conversation as though there were nothing remarkable about any of this. The man is Daniel Jackson. He has died, or come so close to death that the distinction is suddenly in doubt. The waitress is Oma Desala. The friendly stranger has an interest in what Daniel decides next.

It is one of the stranger images in *Stargate SG-1*: the mystery of a higher plane of existence expressed through a room in which someone might order a cup of coffee. The series has shown us giant alien ships, ancient cities and gods armed with impossible technology. When it comes time to represent existence beyond physical life, it chooses a place built for conversation.

That choice is fitting. The most difficult question raised by ascension is not what an ascended being looks like. It is what that being owes to the people who remain below.

These entities possess powers that make the Goa'uld's theatrics look small. They can appear, disappear, survive without ordinary bodies, and in some circumstances affect physical events on an immense scale. They know more than Earth knows about the universe's history. Yet the Ancients, or the community of ascended beings often referred to as the Others, enforce a doctrine of noninterference. The lower plane must be allowed to develop without their intervention.

For a series built on answering distress calls from other planets, this is a provocation. Helping people is what SG-1 does, even when it causes arguments about sovereignty, consent or unforeseen consequences. The ascended have a reason for refusing. It is not a foolish reason. But as the show develops their story, it becomes increasingly difficult to tell where restraint ends and abandonment begins.

The diner is where this disagreement finally becomes impossible to ignore.

## A promise made at Kheb

Before Daniel is offered ascension himself, he encounters the idea through a child. In the third-season episode *Maternal Instinct*, SG-1 searches for the Harcesis, the child born of Sha're while she was possessed by the Goa'uld Amonet. The child carries the inherited knowledge of the Goa'uld, information that could become a military advantage or a catastrophic weapon in the wrong hands. Daniel has also promised Sha're that he will protect her son.

The search leads to Kheb, a place surrounded by Jaffa legends and religious stories. Daniel meets a monk whose teachings demand patience. He wants practical answers: Where is the child? How can they get him away before Apophis arrives? Instead he is asked to attend to himself, his anger and his assumptions about what strength looks like.

Oma Desala appears as a being who can act through forces the team scarcely comprehends. Daniel eventually reaches the child, but his apparent success creates another decision. He could take the Harcesis to Earth, giving his allies access to extraordinary knowledge and bringing the child into the same secret military apparatus that has rescued so many others. Or he could keep his promise in a different way, allowing Oma to protect the child beyond the reach of those who want to possess him.

The episode's spiritual vocabulary is not simply a declaration that knowledge is bad. Daniel is an archaeologist who has spent his life trying to learn things others dismiss. The story asks him whether another person's safety is subordinate to his desire to know. That is a sharper question, especially because the child cannot make an informed decision about becoming a weapon for Earth.

Oma also establishes an apparent model of power exercised with restraint. She does not furnish SG-1 with every answer. She leads Daniel to a choice. She intervenes in the immediate danger when the temple comes under attack, but her purpose is not to install Daniel as the ruler of a newly enlightened world. Her conduct suggests the difference between controlling someone else's future and helping that person find a safer path.

Later episodes will complicate this benevolent impression. They will reveal that Oma has been breaking the rules of her own order, and that she has made a mistake with consequences that threaten an entire galaxy. But the encounter at Kheb has to be taken seriously first. The appeal of her position is genuine. Infinite power wielded by a well-meaning rescuer could become another form of conquest.

## Shifu's lesson about being right

In *Absolute Power*, the Harcesis returns as Shifu, now outwardly older and capable of imparting something about the knowledge carried within him. The SGC sees a chance to acquire information that might transform the war against the Goa'uld. Daniel, who loves knowledge more than most people, is tempted by the prospect.

Shifu gives him a dream in which the temptation is followed to its conclusion. Daniel uses the Goa'uld's knowledge, gains immense technological power, becomes increasingly distrustful and authoritarian, and ultimately causes appalling destruction. The dream is a warning rather than a historical account of events that have occurred. Its function is to expose a possibility within Daniel, not to prove that Daniel was destined to become a villain.

The dream is disturbing because it begins with motives Daniel can defend. He wants to protect the innocent, end the Goa'uld's abuses, and prevent further deaths. But once he has nearly unbeatable power, the people who question him begin to look like obstacles to safety. When a person becomes convinced that only he understands the threat, disagreement can be relabeled disloyalty.

The danger is close to the familiar temptations of the Goa'uld themselves. A ruler claims that obedience is necessary because only a ruler with superior knowledge can keep people alive. The difference between protecting a world and owning it is gradually erased.

Shifu's lesson therefore provides one of the strongest arguments in favor of the Ancients' caution. It is easy to want a more powerful friend to descend from heaven and solve a problem. It is harder to predict what that solution will do to everyone who must live with it. The fact that Daniel is basically decent does not give him immunity from the habits that extraordinary power can encourage.

But the dream cannot answer every question. It shows that intervention can corrupt the helper and injure the helped. It does not demonstrate that every refusal to intervene is good. The series has more than one kind of danger to consider, and eventually it places those dangers in direct conflict.

## Orlin and the planet that was saved

The fifth-season episode *Ascension* introduces Orlin, a being who once broke the rules for a reason that seems difficult to condemn. The people of Velona were under attack by the Goa'uld. He could see that they might be destroyed. Against the prohibition on interference, he gave them knowledge enabling the construction of a powerful weapon. They drove the attackers away.

At first, the story seems to have an obvious moral. Orlin was right to help. Someone with the ability to stop an invasion should not stand aside simply to preserve the purity of a rule. Samantha Carter understands the force of that argument. So do most viewers who have watched SG-1 repeatedly intervene against Goa'uld domination.

Then Orlin tells her what happened next. The Velonans planned to use the same technology to conquer other worlds. The ascended collective responded by destroying the civilization and punishing Orlin, leaving him on the ruined planet. He had saved them from one form of domination while enabling them to contemplate another. The supposed cure was more powerful than the disease.

It is a grim answer, but not a clean one. The Velonans' aggressive turn helps explain why the Ancients fear giving lesser civilizations overwhelming weapons. It does not automatically justify wiping out an entire population. And the very beings who prohibit interference have just interfered with devastating force. The difference, Orlin explains, is that the collective decided such action was necessary to correct his mistake.

Notice what this reveals. Noninterference is not an absolute incapacity. It is an institutional rule, administered by powerful beings who reserve exceptions for themselves. There is a form of governance on the higher plane, with decisions about permissions, sanctions and acceptable consequences. The Ancients are not distant natural laws. They are participants in an order they have constructed.

The contradiction is fertile ground for drama. If a lone being helps a threatened population, that is a forbidden intervention. If the collective destroys the population to prevent later misuse, that is authorized correction. Perhaps the distinction is defensible under some elaborate theory of collective responsibility. The episode does not supply enough to make it obviously just. It gives us Orlin's guilt, Carter's objections, and a world reduced to ruins.

Orlin later acts to keep the SGC from reactivating the Velonan weapon. His story is not merely a warning that helping has consequences; it is an account of a person who must try to prevent the same mistake from being repeated. The technology has outlived the people it was supposed to save. Knowledge can be transmitted in an instant, while political judgment takes years to develop.

## Daniel's passage through death

In *Meridian*, Daniel is exposed to a lethal dose of radiation while attempting to avert disaster on Langara, then known to the team through the nation of Kelowna. He does not die because he has been reckless in pursuit of personal power. He acts to prevent harm and pays a terrible bodily price. In a hospital bed, with his friends unable to repair the damage, Oma Desala offers him another way forward.

There is no purely mechanical explanation for why this should be possible, and the show does not need one. What gives the scene force is how intimate it is. The person who spent years interpreting ancient fragments is being asked to accept something beyond his ability to measure. His friends can offer love, grief and presence. They cannot follow him.

Ascension appears here as mercy, perhaps even fulfillment. Daniel has dedicated his life to understanding other cultures and protecting people whose histories he could have ignored. A being who once led him toward the safety of Shifu now offers him a form of existence in which his pain need not be final.

The change also creates a problem for television storytelling. If one central character can survive death by joining a higher plane, will every future mortal danger feel trivial? *SG-1* addresses that possibility by showing that ascension is not simply a superpower attached to the same old person. The new state comes with prohibitions, distances and the loss of some kinds of relationship. Daniel may have gained powers, but he has also entered a community that limits what he can do with them.

His individual essay could be told as a story of leaving, returning and eventually choosing ordinary life. But through the lens of ascension's politics, something else becomes clear: Daniel's death makes the doctrine personal. He has joined the people who refuse to intervene. Now he must watch the suffering of those he loves from a position that seems to allow help but forbids it.

## Abyss and the cruelty of being nearby

In *Abyss*, Jack is imprisoned and tortured by Ba'al. Daniel appears to him as an ascended being. To Jack, this is at once a comfort and an infuriating fact. His friend is here. His friend is somehow beyond the physical restraints that bind Jack. Why, then, can Daniel not simply free him?

The answer is the prohibition on direct intervention. Daniel can offer an extraordinary escape through ascension, but that is not the same as rescuing Jack from the room and returning him to his team. Jack resists Daniel's proposed solution. He wants to live, to escape, and to remain the person he is. He is not interested in an enlightenment that requires him to give up ordinary existence as the price of relief.

The scenes work because two forms of loyalty collide. Daniel is trying to help within rules he has accepted or is forced to obey. Jack asks for the kind of help a friend would normally provide: get me out of this place. Ascension has made Daniel powerful enough to reach him and strangely powerless to do the one thing Jack wants.

For someone watching through Jack's suffering, the doctrine can feel obscene. Why should rules be preserved at the expense of a prisoner undergoing torture? For someone remembering Shifu's warning, the danger of treating every beloved person as an exception is also visible. Once Daniel is permitted to save Jack, should he save every other prisoner in the galaxy? If so, who chooses which lives are rescued and which tyrants are removed? A single rescue can be easy to justify without yielding an entire theory of legitimate divine government.

The series never resolves the tension simply by naming it. It allows friendship to make an abstract principle hurt. That is enough to turn a policy debate into character drama.

## Full Circle: the promise broken at Abydos

The conflict reaches one of its most painful points in *Full Circle*. The endangered world is Abydos, where Daniel lived, where the original film began, and where the consequences of Goa'uld violence have already reached people he knows by name. Anubis comes searching for the Eye of Ra. Daniel, now ascended, tries to guide his old colleagues toward an advantage that might protect Earth as well as Abydos.

Again he has limits. He can appear, warn and encourage, but his ability to act directly against Anubis is constrained. Eventually he confronts Anubis himself, ready to use the power of his new existence to stop the catastrophe. He is pulled away before he can do it. The attack proceeds. Abydos is devastated.

There is a cruel economy to this scene. Daniel has learned that the Ancients built the Stargate system; that an ancient city holds knowledge capable of resisting Anubis; that a higher plane of existence is real. He possesses answers that would have seemed miraculous to the young archaeologist who first opened the gate. Yet the people of Abydos are not saved from the attack through his command of that knowledge.

Oma later helps the Abydonians ascend, and SG-1 encounters their apparent return before the true nature of the experience becomes clear. It is a consoling ending of a kind. The people have not been abandoned to simple oblivion. But the settlement and the lives they knew are gone. Ascension has preserved them in a different state, not restored the world that was destroyed.

That distinction is important. It keeps the show from treating transcendence as a cheap undo button. The harm done by Anubis remains a harm, even if the survivors exist in a form the team cannot fully understand. Daniel's failure to stop it remains meaningful. The Ancients' rule is now associated with a concrete loss rather than a hypothetical risk.

And the underlying puzzle deepens: if the rule protects lower beings from the dangers of intervention, how did a partially ascended monster like Anubis come to exist at all?

## The Anubis problem

*Threads* returns Daniel to the higher plane and finally exposes much of the history concealed behind that question. The setting is the diner. Oma is there, offering guidance. Another patron, Jim, is talkative, sardonic and increasingly interested in explaining the rules. He is not what he first appears.

Oma once helped Anubis ascend, having been deceived about what kind of being she was helping. The collective imposed consequences but did not simply erase him. His ascension was only partly reversed. He continued to act upon the physical world, constrained in certain respects to what a mortal Goa'uld could have done, while enjoying forms of existence and knowledge that made him extraordinarily difficult to defeat.

The being who lectures Daniel about the system is Anubis himself. His disguise is more than a mystery twist. It is an indictment of the system he understands well enough to exploit. The Others have rules, he tells Daniel, and he knows their edges. The collective has not prevented him from menacing civilizations. It has created a category of tolerated action in which he can continue his ambitions.

Oma's punishment has been to live with the consequences of helping him. She has watched the suffering he causes, unable to stop it by the ordinary forms of intervention she once practiced. Her remorse is genuine, yet remorse is not much use to people whose planets he threatens.

At last she chooses to engage Anubis in a struggle that will occupy them both, perhaps indefinitely. It is a sacrifice without the satisfying neatness of destroying a villain in combat. She does not wipe the mistake from history. She commits her own existence to containing the damage.

The action is powerful because it is not merely a declaration that the rule was wrong. Oma's first intervention was a terrible error. The collective's response to that error also allowed terrible consequences. Her final choice admits both things. She can neither pretend that help is always good nor use the risk of helping to justify endless inaction.

There is a miniature theory of responsibility here. You may not have intended the outcome, and you may have acted under sincere convictions. If your action empowered a disaster, discovering the disaster does not end your obligation. Nor does the fact that a rule forbids further action necessarily settle what you should do.

## The Ori and an older argument

The later arrival of the Ori makes the Ancients' restraint look different again. Here is a group of ascended beings who are willing to intervene aggressively in mortal affairs, using religious devotion and the power it yields to sustain a regime of domination. They exploit the willingness of vulnerable civilizations to treat superior power as moral authority.

The Ori make a forceful argument against the simple fantasy of benevolent cosmic rulers. Their ability to perform miracles does not make their demands worthy of obedience. They offer salvation under conditions designed to preserve their own power. In that respect, they are a monstrous answer to a question the Goa'uld already posed: what if someone truly powerful insists that power proves the right to rule?

But the Ori are not a vindication of every Ancient refusal. The presence of evil intervention does not prove that good intervention is impossible. Nor does it establish that people on the lower planes should be left defenseless while ascended tyrants build an empire. The Ancients' reluctance to become participants in mortal politics protects one value while exposing another.

The story of Merlin and Morgan le Fay shows that individual members of the old order continue to struggle with that conflict. Their actions are complicated and not always plainly authorized. It is possible to admire the Ancients' fear of overwhelming lesser civilizations while still judging their institutional silence harshly.

The distinction is essential. An ethical principle is not a guarantee that everyone who invokes it behaves ethically. Restraint can protect freedom. It can also become a respectable name for declining responsibility. SG-1 is at its best when it refuses to make that dilemma comfortable.

## The people who still have to choose

What does all this do to the human characters? It prevents the series from resolving its problems by outsourcing every decision to a wiser civilization. No ascended council is going to provide the perfect policy for exploration, intervention or war. Even when the Ancients possess the information humans need, human beings still have to decide what to do with it.

That limitation preserves the original team's different virtues. Jack is suspicious of authority that cannot explain itself. Carter wants evidence and practical alternatives. Teal'c understands the political cost of obedience to beings who claim a right to rule. Daniel wants to save people and remains willing to ask uncomfortable questions of his own allies. None of them has a flawless system. Together, they are often more humane than any one of them could be alone.

There is an appealing irony in a show about attaining higher planes of existence choosing, again and again, to find its moral center in arguments among people who are still vulnerable. They can be injured. They can misunderstand each other. They may arrive too late. They need consent, institutions, repairs, medicines and friends. Those limitations frustrate them, but they also force them to confront the consequences of acting.

The ascended are valuable to the story because they expose the dangers that the heroes might someday face if they become too powerful to hear disagreement. They also expose the cruelty of using perfect restraint as an answer to every emergency. The question is not whether intervention is always justified. It is whether the existence of a real risk absolves someone from making a difficult judgment.

We return, finally, to the diner. Daniel is surrounded by possibilities that exceed anything his old colleagues could have imagined. He has a chance to remain on a higher plane, but he has also seen the price of that plane's rules and the weakness of those who administer them. The place looks ordinary because the moral question is ordinary, even when the powers involved are divine: if you can help, what do you owe, and how do you keep your help from becoming another kind of harm?

There is no single answer that would make ten seasons of *Stargate* unnecessary. There is only the practice of choosing under conditions of ignorance, with people who can challenge you, and accepting that what you do may leave responsibilities behind.

The great temptation of ascension is to imagine a position above those responsibilities. The strongest moment in the story is Oma's recognition that no such position is available. She has to act, knowing that she cannot undo everything she once did.

And on Earth, the people who have never ascended continue the less magnificent work of opening the gate, crossing into danger, arguing about what is right, and trying to bring one another home.
