# 010000 — New Faces at the Same Table: Jonas, Mitchell, and Vala

**Track:** Stargate SG-1: Through the Gate. **Audience:** Returning viewers and fans. **Prerequisites:** Familiarity with the original SG-1 team. **Scope:** The changed ensemble from seasons six through ten; Jonas Quinn, Cameron Mitchell, Vala Mal Doran, and the structure of belonging. **Spoilers:** Daniel's departure and return, the battle in Antarctica, the Ori storyline, Vala's family, and the series finale.

# New Faces at the Same Table: Jonas, Mitchell, and Vala

Imagine the conference room at Stargate Command. There is a familiar arrangement of chairs, a view of the gate through the glass, and a mission that will probably sound impossible before the meeting is over. General Hammond might be at the head of the table. Jack will interrupt. Carter will explain a technical obstacle. Daniel will question the plan. Teal'c will wait until a useful moment to say something that ends an argument.

Now remove one of the people from that arrangement. The table is exactly the same size. The work still needs doing. Somebody else must sit down.

That sounds like a routine problem for a long-running television program. Actors leave; new actors arrive; the producers provide a reason and hope the audience accepts the change. But *Stargate SG-1* is a series in which the relationship among four people is at least as important as the planets they visit. A replacement therefore tests what the show thinks its own identity is. Is SG-1 a particular group of friends? A military designation attached to whichever personnel are available? An institution whose purpose outlives everybody who serves in it? A kind of argument, repeated by people who trust one another enough to disagree?

From season six onward, the show gets to explore those possibilities not once but several times. Jonas Quinn arrives after Daniel's death and ascension. Cameron Mitchell arrives after the original team has come apart. Vala Mal Doran arrives without the intention of belonging to a team at all. They do not perform the same dramatic job. In fact, the strongest way to understand the late ensemble is to stop treating all three as replacements.

## Jonas arrives carrying a debt

Jonas is introduced on Kelowna in the fifth-season episode *Meridian*. He works for a government developing naquadria, a valuable and dangerously unstable material. The experiment goes wrong. Daniel Jackson acts to prevent a catastrophe and receives a fatal dose of radiation. The political response in Kelowna is not a solemn admission of responsibility. Officials are concerned with maintaining their position and controlling the story. Jonas sees the gap between what happened and what his government intends to say happened.

His first important action is not solving a puzzle or speaking an ancient language. It is refusing to accept that official version. He defects to Earth, bringing naquadria and information that might help the people Daniel died protecting. By the time season six begins, Jonas has lived for months inside Stargate Command, a guest who is also something like a prisoner of his own uncertainty. He is not under arrest in the ordinary sense. Yet he has nowhere he can safely call home.

So he reads. In *Redemption*, the series establishes that he has worked his way through an astonishing amount of Daniel's material, learning languages, history, mythology, and the particulars of Earth's fight against the Goa'uld. His memory is remarkably strong. This is, on one level, an efficient television device. The new teammate needs to keep up with experts who have already spent five years discovering the galaxy. Give him extraordinary retention, and the writers can plausibly put him in the briefing room without six seasons of apprenticeship.

But that solution is more interesting when we look at how Jonas behaves. He has studied Daniel's books; he has not acquired Daniel's personality. He is less combative, more openly delighted, and quick to notice details that the veterans have stopped considering. Daniel's curiosity often becomes an ethical challenge. Jonas's curiosity arrives with the eagerness of somebody who has discovered that all the stories in the library describe real places and that he may get to see them.

Jack does not welcome him. There is a practical question of trust: Jonas recently worked for a government that concealed a grave mistake. More deeply, Jack has lost a friend. To accept the newcomer immediately would feel like admitting that a chair can be filled and a person replaced. Teal'c is more ready to see Jonas on his own terms. Carter, too, must move from hurt and reservation toward partnership. The new man cannot earn his place by impersonating the dead one, and the show is sensible enough to let that take time.

## A different source of wonder

In *Nightwalkers*, Jonas joins Carter and Teal'c on an investigation in a small American town. The atmosphere of the episode is closer to an eerie conspiracy story than an archaeological expedition. People behave strangely. A local business is implicated in something hidden. Jonas's attentiveness becomes useful. He does not simply announce answers that Carter could have supplied. He sees patterns in mundane behavior, and the details begin to disturb him.

An alien recruited into a secret American military program is well placed to notice how bizarre ordinary American life can be. This is one of Jonas's quiet advantages as a character. Daniel looks at alien worlds and asks what their inhabitants know about themselves. Jonas looks at Earth with a similar freshness. The series does not exploit that reversal as often as it might, but Corin Nemec gives Jonas a pleasant air of someone whose astonishment has not worn off.

He can also be wrong. His intelligence does not make him Daniel's intellectual successor in every field. Reading an archive does not reproduce an archaeologist's experience of fieldwork, interpretation, and the cost of making a moral choice. Jonas learns how this particular team operates: the value of an observation, the limits of certainty, the difference between being useful and being trusted. He has a talent for the first and needs time for the last.

The distinction is especially important because the show itself is tempted by convenience. Daniel's absence creates a hole in exposition. When a script needs a character to decode an inscription, one can hear the machinery of replacement creak. Jonas is most engaging when the script gives him his own emotional or political stake. He understands what it is to be associated with a decision he could not stop, and he knows that home can be a place whose officials would prefer he kept quiet.

His departure in *Homecoming* is therefore a resolution rather than a simple eviction when Daniel returns. Kelowna is threatened by Anubis, and Jonas helps save a world whose leaders have repeatedly disappointed him. Then representatives of the planet's rival nations want him to serve in a new political arrangement. It is a lovely return of the problem from *Meridian*. He left because his country would not tell the truth. He returns because it may finally need somebody prepared to speak truthfully and work with its enemies.

Jonas does not fail at being SG-1's fourth member. He succeeds at becoming the sort of person who can leave it. His later return in *Fallout*, when his world is endangered by unstable naquadria, reinforces that possibility. His story continues beyond the chair in Colorado.

## A hero arrives to find the team missing

Cameron Mitchell has the opposite introductory problem. Jonas had been an outsider seeking admission to a functioning team. Mitchell arrives expecting admission to a famous team and discovers that it no longer functions.

In the ninth-season opening *Avalon*, Cameron enters the SGC with the enthusiasm of a soldier who has finally reached the assignment he has wanted. He is no inexperienced recruit. During the battle above Antarctica, when Anubis attacked Earth, he flew an F-302 in defense of the people whose mission would save the planet. His fighter was lost, his copilot died, and he endured a painful recovery. His conduct earned recognition and a promise from Jack O'Neill that he could choose an assignment. Naturally, he chose SG-1.

Only now Jack is working at a higher level, Daniel intends to go to Atlantis, Teal'c is occupied with Jaffa politics, and Carter is pursuing research elsewhere. A team name has survived its team. Mitchell has been given the command he asked for, but not the thing he thought he was asking to command.

This is an exceptionally good premise for a new lead. Instead of announcing that the next hero is cooler, tougher, or brighter than the previous one, the series lets him be impressed by the characters we already love. His energy is that of a fan who has somehow wandered into the actual control room. He knows the stories and wants to take part in them. The problem is that his heroes have lives and responsibilities beyond his wish to recreate their best days.

Mitchell's work in the opening episodes is largely social. He visits people and tries to persuade them to come back. He is persistent without being fully aware of how exhausting persistence can be. Ben Browder plays him as resourceful, stubborn, and delighted by danger, with a slightly different rhythm from Richard Dean Anderson's Jack. Jack usually makes fun of a situation because he knows its danger. Mitchell sometimes seems excited by the possibility that it will become dangerous. Their humor has different temperatures.

The invitation to compare them is unavoidable. Both are American officers comfortable with jokes, command, and improvisation. Yet an exact comparison misses Mitchell's real dramatic function. Jack often resisted the institution that wanted him to act like a model officer. Mitchell is eager to belong to an institution he admires. Jack has to be pulled toward duty; Mitchell must learn that duty is more complicated than the heroic stories told about it.

## What does command mean when Carter is already there?

There is an awkwardness the show never entirely escapes. Samantha Carter has earned command of SG-1 by experience, rank, and a long record of leading missions. Why should the newcomer get the team's leadership instead? The plot's answer is that the group had disbanded before Mitchell received the assignment. That is coherent in the paperwork and less satisfying as a statement about the people involved.

This was not merely an invented retrospective complaint. Writer and producer Joseph Mallozzi later described disagreements in the writers' room over Mitchell's introduction, including concern that his position would diminish Carter's established authority. His recollection describes an attempt to share command, although the way that arrangement played on screen did not remove all the friction. That production testimony is illuminating because the audience's discomfort had a precise dramatic basis. It was not hostility to an unfamiliar face. It was the sense that continuity should mean something for the characters' professional achievements.

It is possible to enjoy Mitchell and still think his arrival exposes a weak decision. That combination is better criticism than either reflexively resenting him or pretending that the issue does not exist.

The best late episodes often reduce the problem by allowing leadership to become a set of practical relationships. Carter need not surrender her judgment. Teal'c has independent commitments among the free Jaffa. Daniel does not stop asking dangerous questions. Mitchell provides momentum and coordination, but he is not an all-purpose answer machine. The team works when he earns the right to join its conversation, not when every other character suddenly waits for his orders.

His ordinary friendships are part of that process. He is capable of appreciating a colleague's skill without feeling obliged to defeat that colleague at it. He has confidence, but the confidence is tested by failures that cannot be laughed away. In *Collateral Damage*, a crime and manipulated memories place him in an intimate, terrifying position: he cannot trust the evidence in his own mind. The show lets its enthusiastic new leader become uncertain without abandoning the characteristics that made him recognizable.

## Vala enters by stealing the vehicle

Then there is Vala Mal Doran, who makes the very idea of a replacement seem politely inadequate.

She first appears in *Prometheus Unbound*, dressed as a fearsome armored attacker, taking control of Earth's starship while Daniel is aboard. She is not there to join SG-1, serve a noble cause, or make a good impression. She wants the ship. She is persuasive when deception suits her, flirtatious when it distracts a target, and capable of switching plans almost before anyone has understood the previous one.

Claudia Black and Michael Shanks turn the episode into a two-person contest, an alternation of demands, challenges, performance, and reluctant cooperation. Daniel's seriousness is a gift to Vala's improvisation. Her evasions are an irritation that he cannot explain away by finding the right translation. Each can see how the other operates. Neither can make the other stop.

This new character is important for what she lacks. The old team members enter the program with recognizably honorable commitments. Even when Jack's motives are complicated, he serves as an officer. Daniel searches for truth and the people he has lost. Teal'c seeks freedom. Carter wants to explore and protect. Vala has learned to survive by making every commitment temporary.

Her history gives that survival strategy weight. She was once the host of the Goa'uld Qetesh. After the symbiote's removal, her body still carried the consequences of Qetesh's rule. In *The Powers That Be*, people who had been exploited by the Goa'uld recognize Vala as the figure they worshiped. She is tempted to use the situation for gain, then confronted with suffering and anger that do not vanish because she herself was enslaved. There is no convenient accounting system that separates every harm done by Qetesh from every deception Vala committed afterward.

Her humor is often a preemptive strike. Before anyone can expose her vulnerability, she supplies a more outrageous story. Before anybody can demand an honest account, she performs a person who has no interest in honesty. Sometimes this is funny because the others are wonderfully ill equipped to handle it. Sometimes it is exhausting because the evasions postpone the connection she wants.

## The former host and the would-be family

Vala's development through the Ori conflict makes her more than the character who annoys Daniel. In *Crusade*, she describes life in the Ori home galaxy, where she has survived by assuming a role within a community of believers. Tomin offers her care. He is also drawn into a religious and military system that is preparing to attack worlds he has never seen. Vala's situation requires her to manipulate appearances, yet the emotional consequences of those appearances become real. She grows concerned for him, and the possibility of being loved becomes entangled with fear of what the Ori will do.

Her pregnancy and the birth of Adria are not merely another supernatural twist. The Ori have made Vala's body part of their strategy. She is a former host who has already experienced one system's use of her body, and now she becomes the mother of the being intended to lead another system's conquest. Adria's terrifying growth and certainty give Vala a relationship that cannot be managed by a well-timed joke. Love, revulsion, guilt, protectiveness, and helplessness are all available to her at once.

The series has a habit of putting its most difficult moral work in absurd circumstances. Vala can be insufferable and frightened in the same scene. She can care about Tomin and still lie to him. She can want to rescue Adria without pretending Adria's actions are harmless. Claudia Black keeps the performance quick and buoyant enough for the weekly adventures, but the weight is there when a scene allows it to surface.

Her father in *Family Ties*, played by Fred Willard, helps explain why she finds promises difficult to trust. He is charming, evasive, and perpetually ready to exploit affection. Vala recognizes the tricks because they are close to her own. The episode is broad comedy, but the situation is not trivial: how does a person learn to belong when the version of intimacy they grew up with was another opportunity to be deceived?

For Jonas, SG-1 was a place where he could repay a debt and find the courage to return home. For Mitchell, it was a community whose achievements he wanted to share. For Vala, the team becomes the first place where she might not have to create a different story about herself every morning.

## Why the old team has to change

The changing cast is sometimes discussed as though a television series ought to freeze its ideal arrangement the moment the audience becomes attached to it. That desire is understandable. SG-1's original chemistry is excellent, and an admired character's departure can feel like a breach of trust between a show and its viewers. The sixth season really does sound different without Daniel. The ninth season really does sound different without Jack at the center. There are jokes that another actor cannot deliver with Anderson's timing, and arguments that do not have the same force without Michael Shanks's Daniel at the table.

But sameness would be its own failure. A military program that goes on for a decade cannot plausibly preserve every posting, friendship, and command relationship unchanged. A galaxy that slowly becomes known will alter the people doing the discovering. The Jaffa acquire political work that Teal'c cannot solve by joining another mission. Carter earns authority that ought to reshape the institution around her. Daniel finds new reasons to leave and return. A successful organization must learn to outlast its founding personalities, even if the show itself never finds a painless way to dramatize that fact.

This is where late *SG-1* sometimes earns more affection than it receives credit for. Jonas, Mitchell, and Vala each expose a different weakness in the idea of a perfect team. Jonas tests whether love for an absent friend can coexist with welcoming somebody new. Mitchell tests whether heroism can be inherited without erasing the achievements of the people already there. Vala tests whether an institution built around orders, clearances, and expertise can make room for somebody whose chief talent is refusing to be pinned down.

They also bring pleasures of their own. Jonas's bright attention to the everyday. Mitchell's slightly embarrassing enthusiasm for the adventure everyone else has learned to endure. Vala's ability to make Daniel Jackson look as though grammar itself has betrayed him. These are not substitute servings of the same flavor. They change the conversation.

## The most revealing seat is the one nobody assigns

Near the end of the series, *Unending* traps the team aboard the Odyssey in a situation where time becomes an unbearable resource. Without spoiling each maneuver of their escape, the episode makes an unexpectedly domestic choice: it stays with the people. Their lives continue inside an impossible enclosure. They age, disagree, work, rest, and seek each other's company. They are no longer merely a team waiting for the next mission briefing. They are the people with whom the rest of their lives may have to be spent.

Vala's place among them is central to that emotional effect. She once treated every relationship as a negotiation she could abandon before the terms turned against her. Here, abandoning the relationship is no longer the sensible escape. She has something to lose because she has something worth keeping. The character who arrived intending to steal a ship has found a home aboard one.

The finale is not proof that every cast decision along the way was wise. It does not settle the problem of Carter's command, restore all the missing scenes with Jonas, or substitute for the particular magic of the original quartet. It does something more persuasive. It shows that the newcomers have acquired histories with one another, and that those histories are worth feeling.

Go back to the conference table. A chair is empty. There is a reason to grieve the person who used to sit there, and a reason not to ask the next person to imitate them. Eventually the table will change again. What survives is not the seating chart. It is the practice of taking one another seriously when the galaxy gives them every reason not to, and sometimes of refusing to take themselves too seriously when the work becomes almost impossible to bear.

That is why SG-1 can be an institution without becoming impersonal, and a group of friends without requiring every friend to stay forever. The gate opens. Someone has to decide to go through. Different people bring different reasons for saying yes.
