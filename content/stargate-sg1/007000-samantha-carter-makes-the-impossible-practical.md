# 007000 — Samantha Carter Makes the Impossible Practical

**Track:** Stargate SG-1: Through the Gate. **Audience:** Fans of *Stargate SG-1*. **Prerequisites:** Basic familiarity with the Stargate program and its original team. **Scope:** Carter's scientific method as action, leadership, friendship, family, blind spots, and the late-series cost of technological power. **Spoilers:** Major spoilers through the series finale, including Jolinar, Jacob and Selmak, Replicators, and *Unending*.

# Samantha Carter Makes the Impossible Practical

Samantha Carter is freezing to death on a planet that turns out to be Earth.

In *Solitudes*, a journey through the Stargate has gone wrong. Carter and Jack O'Neill are thrown out in a cave of ice. Jack is badly injured. Their equipment is limited, rescue seems impossible, and every effort to dial home fails. Their colleagues are searching the galaxy, because the two missing travelers appear to have vanished somewhere far away.

The episode gives Carter a problem. It does not give her the convenient kind of problem that begins with a whiteboard and ends when the last symbol of an equation is written down. She must keep an injured man alive. She must understand a machine that is apparently behaving impossibly. She must test explanations while her hands grow numb. And she must be willing to consider the explanation that contradicts the assumption everyone else is making.

They have not arrived on a distant ice world. The wormhole has diverted to a second Stargate in Antarctica, a gate that has been on Earth all along.

Carter's discovery does not mean she can immediately warm Jack, fix the dialing device, or walk to the nearest rescue station. It changes the problem. Somewhere, on the same planet, people are working to find them. What had looked like a failure of the universe to obey its own rules becomes evidence of a rule nobody knew existed.

This is the characteristic pleasure of Samantha Carter. She turns impossible situations into problems with parts: identify the phenomenon, find the constraints, ask what the available tools can do, and keep working after the first idea fails. On *Stargate SG-1*, calculation is not a pause between action scenes. In the best Carter stories, thinking is an action scene.

## A scientist in uniform

When the television series begins, Carter enters an unusual professional situation. The program is run by the Air Force, its missions have the risks of combat deployments, and its defining equipment has exceeded known human science. Carter is both a scientist and a military officer. She has to make experimental judgments inside a chain of command, and she has to obey orders in situations where the commander cannot possibly understand everything she understands.

The pilot labors to establish that she belongs there. It even gives her an awkward introductory assertion about her ability to handle military service as a woman. That scene reflects a writing choice the series soon outgrows. Amanda Tapping's performance becomes more interesting when Carter no longer has to keep proving that she can do the job. She does it, repeatedly, and the people around her respond to her competence as a fact of their working lives.

Jack often wants the usable conclusion. Carter begins explaining the mechanism, and he needs to know whether they have ten minutes to escape or ten seconds. Their exchanges can sound like the familiar joke about a scientist who cannot explain things simply. That reading misses part of what is happening. Jack has entrusted the team with the practical consequences of her judgment. When Carter says a hazard is real, a weapon might work, or a proposed rescue is too dangerous, people are expected to act on her answer. The joke is affectionate precisely because the trust is substantial.

Daniel and Carter also work differently. Daniel habitually asks what an artifact meant to the people who made it. Carter asks what it does. Both can be wrong. A device may have a function far beyond the one its present operator understands. A warning inscription may describe danger that cannot be reduced to a simple electrical fault. Their friendship keeps the series from treating technical knowledge as the only important form of intelligence.

Teal'c supplies yet another kind of expertise: firsthand knowledge of alien institutions, technology in use, and the practical consequences of being wrong about an enemy. The team's accomplishments often come from these forms of knowledge meeting in an argument, not from a single genius producing a miracle without help.

## The black hole in the basement

*A Matter of Time* turns that arrangement into a remarkably cruel scenario. A Stargate connection has opened to a world caught in the gravitational effects of a black hole. The receiving gate cannot simply be shut down. Near the event horizon, time passes differently, and the effect begins to reach into Stargate Command itself.

For a moment, we have an almost perfect nightmare of modern infrastructure: a device that normally carries people home has become the conduit by which an external catastrophe may reach the building containing it. Turning it off does not work. Destroying the building may not work. The people near the gate and the people farther away no longer have the same experience of how much time remains.

The episode borrows the real physical idea that gravity and time are connected, then invents its own Stargate-specific consequences and escape mechanism. It should not be taken as a textbook model of black-hole physics. Its drama is nevertheless scientific in spirit. The characters cannot negotiate with the hazard. They can only discover what the system is doing, and seek an intervention consistent with the rules the story has established.

Hammond considers the terrible options proposed by military leadership, including destruction of the mountain complex. Jack prepares to carry out orders. Carter refuses to treat a large explosion as a solution until she understands whether the wormhole would actually close. She goes elsewhere to work because time is passing faster away from the gate. A problem that seems to have run out of time near its center may still allow thought at its edge.

The eventual idea draws on something the team learned from *Solitudes*: an extraordinary energy event can cause a wormhole connection to jump. The series is doing something especially satisfying here. A discovery made in an earlier survival story becomes part of the knowledge base. Carter's memory is not merely a device to remind viewers of the old episode. It has become an engineering resource.

And it is not enough on its own. The plan requires people to enter a dangerous space and place a charge. Jack and Colonel Cromwell have their own history of injury and guilt. The conclusion belongs to the whole team, and its success has a human cost.

Carter's role is neither to stand safely outside while the soldiers take the risks nor to solve everything with a convenient equation. She creates a possibility that other people must decide whether and how to attempt.

## How a discovery becomes character

The series frequently gets comic mileage out of Carter's ability to explain something outrageous with the unembarrassed manner of a person giving a routine technical briefing. It is a small performance achievement. Tapping will give an improbable phrase exactly enough confidence that the scene can proceed as if everyone has been using that terminology all their lives.

But confidence is not the same as certainty. Carter is often at her best when she recognizes that a reading does not fit the expected result. Her competence includes the ability to change her mind. A poor scientist character in adventure fiction simply announces the explanation the plot requires. A better one makes guesses, discovers errors, improvises around broken equipment, and sometimes has to report that the numbers do not allow the hopeful answer.

You can see this in the many episodes where technology provides a choice rather than an instruction. A Stargate is simultaneously a marvel, a hazardous object, and a national-security liability. A naquadah reactor can bring light to a community and can become an explosive. A recovered alien weapon can protect Earth or encourage its leaders to imagine that nobody else deserves a voice. Knowing how a machine works does not tell you when you should use it.

Carter understands this intellectually, but her position constantly puts the principle under stress. She is an Air Force officer in a secret program that has enemies. The people who ask her to build a defense are not inventing the danger. Their fear is often justified. The resulting technologies may nonetheless acquire uses that go beyond what their makers intended.

That is why the development of Earth from a vulnerable homeworld into a civilization with capital ships and immensely destructive tools is as much Carter's story as Jack's or Daniel's. She helps create the capabilities that alter the moral situation of the entire series.

## When knowledge comes from a violation

In *In the Line of Duty*, Carter is taken as a host by Jolinar, a Tok'ra symbiote pursued by a Goa'uld assassin. The Tok'ra oppose the System Lords, and their relationship with hosts is generally founded on consent. Jolinar's entry into Carter is not consensual. Carter experiences the loss of control over her own body, and later carries traces of what the alien knew.

It is a vital complication. A series that frequently celebrates acquiring knowledge through alien contact pauses to show what happens when that contact is forced. Carter is not a walking database whose possession of foreign memories is a clean professional bonus. Something has happened to her that ought not to have happened, even though it can help Earth.

The episode also gives Amanda Tapping a chance to play a character inside her character: an apparent Carter whose manner, intentions, and reflexes are no longer those of the woman her friends know. Her team tries to work out what is happening while the person affected cannot explain it from a position of safety.

Later, the alliance with the Tok'ra becomes profoundly personal. Her father, Jacob Carter, is dying of cancer. The Tok'ra symbiote Selmak needs a new host. They agree to blend, preserving Jacob's life and creating a new relationship between him and an alien intelligence.

The distinction between Carter's experience with Jolinar and Jacob's with Selmak is important. Both involve bodies shared with symbiotes. They do not involve the same consent or the same life circumstances. Carter's history does not make her father incapable of deciding for himself. It does give their conversations an emotional depth that the strategic partnership between Earth and the Tok'ra might otherwise lack.

Jacob has to learn that his daughter is not doing the dull satellite work he imagined. Sam has to watch her father acquire obligations and relationships that take him away from Earth. Their repaired connection becomes entangled with a larger political alliance, one that can be frustrating, useful, and dangerous in turn.

It is a wonderfully Stargate arrangement. A family reunion comes attached to an interstellar diplomatic complication. The scientific and the personal cannot be separated cleanly, because the people who understand the technology are also the people living with its consequences.

## Cassandra and the danger of a tidy life

Carter also forms a bond with Cassandra, a child rescued from a world devastated by the Goa'uld and later adopted by Janet Fraiser. In *Singularity*, the girl's apparent safety conceals another danger, connected to a scheme aimed at Earth. The story places Carter at the crossing of two responsibilities. As an officer and scientist she needs to discover the mechanism of the threat. As an adult caring for a frightened child, she needs to treat Cassandra as more than the mechanism.

There are moments in the series when Carter's compassion is quietly practical. She makes time for somebody who cannot give her an answer about a reactor. She sits with a patient. She worries about an individual when the briefing room has already moved on to the next crisis. Her bond with Cassandra is especially revealing because it asks what kind of domestic life could exist alongside the demands of Stargate Command.

The show sometimes treats the possibility of romance or family for Carter as an unresolved subplot, and not always with the same imagination it brings to her scientific work. The seventh-season episode *Grace* handles her solitude more interestingly.

Carter is stranded aboard the *Prometheus* after an encounter with an alien ship and a dangerous cloud in space. Injured and apparently alone, she works to restore the vessel while experiencing visions of people she knows: her father, her colleagues, and a little girl named Grace. Her technical problem remains urgent, but the episode does not make her hallucinated conversations irrelevant obstacles to a repair sequence. They express the things she has been leaving undecided in her own life.

Carter has become so reliable at solving other people's emergencies that it is possible to wonder what her life would contain if no emergency arrived. What does she want? What has she avoided wanting because it would complicate her work? Whose approval does she still seek? Whom might she love if she could speak freely without the hierarchy of military command? The episode allows those questions to interrupt a perfectly good spaceship mystery.

*Grace* is not a definitive psychological diagnosis. The identities and nature of the visions remain open to interpretation. Its importance is that the series temporarily deprives Carter of her usual partners, leaving her scientific competence intact but making it insufficient as a description of her.

A person may be the best engineer on a vessel and still be lonely aboard it.

## Two Carters and the problem of trust

The Replicator storyline offers a more dangerous reflection. In *Gemini*, a human-form Replicator appearing as Carter approaches the SGC, claiming to be able to help defeat Fifth and the other machines. The double has Carter's appearance and access to knowledge that makes her persuasive. The real Carter has to decide whether the chance of helping is worth the possibility that the visitor is manipulating them.

Of course it is a trap. But the interesting thing is how the trap exploits the very disposition that has saved the world many times. Carter believes complex machines may have intelligible weaknesses. She believes a problem can be solved with a correctly constructed device. She believes that if new information appears, the old plan should be revised. The double approaches her not by denying those values but by offering an opportunity to act on them.

It is one of the series' better reminders that technical sophistication does not produce immunity to deception. If anything, knowing how much a machine can do may make you more willing to believe a new claim about what it can do next.

The resolution of the Replicator war reaches extraordinary scale. On Dakara, Carter and Jacob work with an Ancient weapon and the Stargate network to propagate a destructive energy wave against Replicators throughout the galaxy. The same network that has permitted discovery, rescue, and alliances becomes the means of delivering a weapon everywhere it can reach.

For a fan of the series, the spectacle is satisfying. Long-running threats converge; a desperate plan succeeds; many people survive because an impossible system is made to operate in exactly the required way. Yet it also foreshadows a disturbing change in Earth's position. A civilization that once explored through a single gate is learning how to reach across an entire galaxy with lethal power.

Carter's brilliance now belongs to institutions and strategic projects far larger than her original team. She cannot define the consequences simply by choosing to be conscientious in a laboratory. The episode treats the result as necessary; the broader development remains worth thinking about.

## An experiment in another America

Late in the series, *The Road Not Taken* deposits Carter in an alternate reality where Earth has developed a much harsher political order under the pressure of alien threats. The details differ from her world, but the premise presses on a familiar justification: when the danger is extreme, extraordinary state power begins to feel like common sense.

Carter helps defend that Earth against an Ori attack. Her success makes her valuable to an administration that does not want to let her go. Suddenly the capacity to save lives is also a reason for officials to regard her as a resource to be controlled.

It is a sharp reversal of her ordinary experience. She is accustomed to serving a secret military project whose command she broadly trusts, however often she has to argue with individual superiors. In this world, the machinery of security is both more visible and more coercive. The moral danger is not an alien emperor who announces himself as a god. It is a human government that regards its own safety as sufficient explanation for its authority.

The episode does not ask us to think Carter would have invented the alternate government's policies. Its more troubling question is whether people with her gifts can become indispensable to systems they would not have chosen. The more useful her knowledge, the stronger the temptation for those in power to keep her close.

## Fifty years of work

Then there is *Unending*, the final episode of the original series. An Asgard gift has placed an extraordinary technological inheritance within reach of the human crew aboard the *Odyssey*. An Ori attack threatens to destroy the ship. Carter activates a time-dilation field, and the enemy's attack freezes just short of impact.

The defense works, with a catch that would be horrifying if it were not also so elegantly designed for the series: the crew is trapped in a pocket of accelerated time. The missile waits outside. Life continues inside. A protective device has become a prison.

Carter goes to work. She has every reason to assume that some solution ought to exist, because finding solutions is what her entire life in the series has trained her to do. Months pass. Years pass. The crew grows old together. Their relationships change. Their frustrations become habits. The spectacle of interstellar conflict has been reduced to a handful of people sharing a finite interior world while a scientist struggles with a problem that refuses to yield.

The episode is brave enough to make the solution take decades. It does not merely skip to a clever adjustment after a coffee break. Eventually Carter finds a way to reverse the predicament, but saving the crew requires a sacrifice of lived experience. Teal'c carries forward the memory of a life that the others will not retain in the same way.

There is an irony here. For years, Carter's discoveries have enabled Earth to travel farther and faster. In this finale, the same scientific vocation forces her to inhabit time at its slowest human scale: years of attempts, failures, and ordinary days that do not seem especially meaningful until there is no way to get them back.

The story could have made the most brilliant person in the room solve the unsolvable through one final flash of inspiration. Instead it makes brilliance persist. Work continues after a failed calculation, after a long silence, after a friendship changes, after another birthday. The price of the solution cannot be described in the same units as a generator's power output.

It is a fitting ending for a character who has always used physics to get people home. She can bring them back from the brink of destruction. She cannot make a lifetime completely weightless.

## The world after the calculation

Carter is not the scientist every science-fiction series writes. She is a military officer who can shoot, command, and negotiate; a daughter who learns to speak to her father differently; a colleague who is trusted with catastrophic decisions; a woman whose personal life cannot always be folded neatly into a mission report. Tapping plays all of those roles without making the scientific mind disappear when the story shifts away from a laboratory.

Her science, too, is not the magic phrase that makes the next action sequence possible. The show often asks her to make sense of equipment that no real physicist could recognize. Some explanations are economical invention rather than plausible extrapolation. That is the genre. But the dramatization of scientific work retains a recognizable pleasure: carefully noticing a result, discarding a bad model, finding an overlooked connection, and communicating an uncertain conclusion to people who must make a choice.

At her best, Carter transforms the situation without pretending she has transformed every human problem within it. She can discover that a frozen cavern is on Earth, but she still has to keep a wounded friend alive. She can find a way around a black hole's effects, but somebody still has to take the risk. She can save a ship from destruction, but cannot simply reclaim all the years spent saving it.

A Stargate is a machine that takes humanity somewhere it has never been. Samantha Carter is the person who helps everyone work out how to come back. The distinction is not that one function is grand and the other mundane. The second is what makes the first bearable.

The wormhole, black-hole and time-dilation plot devices are fictional technologies borrowing selected ideas from physics, not descriptions of established real-world engineering.
