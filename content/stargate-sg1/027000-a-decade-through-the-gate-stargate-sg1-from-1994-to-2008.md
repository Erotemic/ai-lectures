# 027000 — A Decade Through the Gate: Stargate SG-1 from 1994 to 2008

**Track:** Stargate SG-1: Through the Gate. **Audience:** Established fans and listeners who want a substantial history of the series. **Prerequisites:** None beyond the premise that the Stargate opens paths to other worlds, though this retrospective reveals the fate of many major characters. **Scope:** The 1994 film, the 1997–2007 television series, the two 2008 follow-up films, and the changing character, production and moral ambitions of SG-1. **Spoilers:** The entire television series, *The Ark of Truth* and *Continuum*. This is the track's concluding essay.

# A Decade Through the Gate: Stargate SG-1 from 1994 to 2008

At the center of *Stargate SG-1* is a room that hardly looks like a place from which humanity might remake its understanding of the universe.

There are concrete walls, metal railings, a control booth, a ramp, an enormous ring, and enough military personnel to make even a miraculous discovery feel like a workplace. The people in the room wear practical clothes. They carry radios. Somebody has to check the dialing computer. Somebody has to decide whether to open the iris. Somebody has to inform the general that an off-world team is overdue.

It is difficult to think of a better setting for the particular kind of science fiction the series became. It does not ask what if one extraordinary person were transported to the center of the cosmos. It asks what happens when human beings build a standing organization around a miracle and keep showing up for work.

Across ten television seasons and two follow-up films, that question becomes a history. Four people cross a threshold. They make friends and enemies. Their discoveries turn into procedures, then alliances, then ships and institutions. The galaxy first threatens them, then negotiates with them, then sometimes depends on them. The team changes. Actors leave and return. The tone moves between sincere grief and gleeful parody. The same ring stands in the same room long enough for it to gather emotional weight.

And all the while the show tries to preserve something simple: there is another address, and someone has to find out what is on the other side.

This is a story about how a television series managed to keep that promise for a decade, where it strained under the weight of its own achievements, and why the prospect of another mission remained appealing even after the heroes had become some of the most powerful people in their fictional galaxy.

## The most productive change from a film

The beginning was a film built around a single extraordinary expedition. Roland Emmerich and Dean Devlin's *Stargate*, released in 1994, turns a strange artifact found in Egypt into a journey through space. Daniel Jackson deciphers the symbols. A military team under Colonel Jack O'Neil crosses to Abydos. They encounter a society ruled by Ra, whose appearance as an Egyptian god conceals a technologically sophisticated extraterrestrial tyrant.

The film's pleasures are immediate: the stone ring, the moving chevrons, the first passage through the shimmering surface, a desert planet, a liberation story. It has a destination and a climax. The expedition can return home with a story to tell, and the door can be closed again.

The television series took the one piece of that ending that begged to be challenged. Why should the door stay closed?

Brad Wright and Jonathan Glassner, then working on *The Outer Limits*, independently saw the potential for a series and were brought together by MGM. Wright later recalled that they could imagine a continuing organization of small teams exploring newly reachable worlds. The idea is almost obvious in hindsight, which is part of its brilliance. A science-fiction premise that works for one film may become more interesting when treated as an ordinary, repeated activity.

The premiere in 1997 made the decisive adjustments. Ra had not been a solitary monster. He belonged to an imperial class of Goa'uld who had scattered humans across planets and claimed the identities of gods. Abydos was not the only destination. The Stargate was part of a network. The door did not lead to an ending; it led to a map.

There was a casting and tonal change too. The film's Jack, played by Kurt Russell, was a grim officer living with unbearable grief. Richard Dean Anderson's Jack O'Neill, with a second L, could still carry that grief, but had another way of meeting the universe. He could make a joke while looking directly at something terrifying. Anderson brought the self-awareness, skepticism and comic timing that made a bureaucratic workplace in a mountain a place where viewers wanted to spend hundreds of hours.

Daniel returned, now played by Michael Shanks. Amanda Tapping's Samantha Carter became a second way of approaching the unknown: a scientist and military officer who could think through a deadly technical problem while everyone else was still deciding whether to panic. Christopher Judge's Teal'c arrived as a former servant of the enemy who had decided that service must end. Don S. Davis's General Hammond gave the work an authority that was stern but, at its best, answerable to conscience.

The original film has wonder. The series adds continuity. You discover another planet on Monday; by Friday you may need to explain to your commander why helping its inhabitants has endangered someone else. Tomorrow, the same people must pass through the same door again.

That change made room for a form of adventure in which consequences could accumulate even when individual episodes reached satisfying conclusions.

## The weekly ritual, and why viewers learned to trust it

An SG-1 episode often begins in a room the audience knows. A mission briefing establishes an address, a mystery or a threat. The team approaches the ring, watches the unstable vortex collapse into a flat surface, and passes through. The other side presents a problem that the briefing did not anticipate. Someone has to make a choice. The team might return by the end, carrying something more complicated than its original orders.

The format is easy to imitate and hard to sustain. A television series can fill an hour with alien plants, a frightened village, a malfunctioning computer, a hostile official or a strange disease. But for those stories to mean more than the week's puzzle, the audience must believe that the same people who lived through the last one are still present in this one.

One of SG-1's most reliable devices is disagreement. Jack wants to protect his team and finish the mission; Daniel wants to understand the strangers in front of him; Carter wants reliable evidence; Teal'c knows things about Goa'uld society that none of the others can see. A line delivered as a joke may carry a serious objection. A briefing-room argument can become an adventure's real climax. Often the critical discovery is not a weapon or a set of coordinates. It is the recognition that the team misunderstood the situation.

Consider *The Nox*. The team arrives hoping to find a way to defend Earth from the Goa'uld. Instead it encounters a people whose apparent fragility exposes the visitors' assumptions. The Nox refuse to equate possessing power with the obligation to wield it as Earth expects. Jack's urgency is comprehensible; Earth is in danger. So is the Nox refusal to make themselves an answer to a stranger's military problem. The episode is richer because neither side exists merely to be the other's lecture.

Or consider *Cor-ai*, when Teal'c is judged for an act committed while serving Apophis. The viewer trusts Teal'c now. That does not abolish the history of people who suffered because of what he once did. His teammates have to choose how to stand beside someone whose liberation did not erase his responsibility. This is the kind of problem that a recurring cast can sustain more effectively than a one-off adventure. The people have pasts, and their pasts do not always cooperate with the plot.

The best weekly stories also knew when to abandon a grand cosmic mystery for something intensely ordinary. *Solitudes* traps Jack and Carter in what appears to be an alien ice world, while an enormous effort to find them unfolds elsewhere. The solution depends on understanding the Stargate's system rather than simply surviving a villain. *A Matter of Time* makes a black hole into a threat to the base itself, turning the familiar room into a place where the laws of space and time cannot be taken for granted. The gate can deliver the universe's wonders, but its rules are also a source of suspense.

What makes these episodes memorable is not simply the premise. It is that the viewer knows who will be impatient, who will calculate, who will sympathize, and who will say something unexpectedly funny when the situation grows intolerable.

## A galaxy that keeps getting older

The early Goa'uld are magnificent antagonists for this particular format. They wear the authority of gods, rule by violence, and possess technology they conceal behind ritual. The revelation that a divine claim can be a political performance is exhilarating. It gives the heroes a way to challenge tyranny through knowledge as well as force.

It also gives the show a problem it will return to for years. If a society's mythology has been used to justify enslavement, what happens when outsiders expose the fraud? Teal'c cannot liberate every Jaffa by declaring that Apophis is false. His decision to defect begins a political process that must survive fear, habits of obedience, family conflict and rival ideas about the future. The show grows more compelling when it acknowledges that overthrowing rulers is not the same as building a stable society.

And then it adds layers of history beyond the Goa'uld. The Asgard arrive as powerful allies, but not invulnerable rescuers. The Tok'ra can oppose the same enemy while disagreeing profoundly with Earth's methods. The Nox remain morally and technologically beyond what the visitors can comfortably categorize. The Ancients are gradually revealed as people who built enormous systems, left astonishing technology behind, and may have failed to accept responsibility for what followed.

An especially lovely moment comes in *The Fifth Race*. Jack's mind is filled with Ancient knowledge he cannot safely contain. Language begins to slip away from him. The team's most instinctively skeptical member becomes a temporary conduit for information too large for his life. The solution leads toward an encounter with the Asgard and the possibility that humanity might someday become a peer among the galaxy's oldest civilizations.

That promise changes the emotional direction of the series. In the beginning, the heroes hope they can survive long enough to learn where the next threat comes from. Now the universe seems to contain a place for them at a table they had barely known existed. The Asgard's interest is not a declaration that Earth has earned unlimited authority; it is recognition that humanity might be ready for a long apprenticeship.

The wonder of discovery survives the expansion of mythology because each new layer rearranges the older ones. A Goa'uld weapon that once looked unbeatable may prove primitive beside Asgard engineering. An Ancient system may solve a problem and create another. A peaceful civilization may possess more power than an imperial fleet and choose not to display it. The galaxy is not simply a ladder of stronger enemies. At its best, it is a place where technical sophistication, wisdom, political legitimacy and moral courage are separate qualities.

The show occasionally loses that distinction. New threats can demand ever-larger weapons, and the growing inventory of advanced technology can turn a mystery into a hardware problem. Yet the attempt to keep moral and technological authority separate gives SG-1 a deeper identity than a sequence of battles alone could provide.

## Four temperaments, and the institution around them

The central quartet could have been a collection of specialties: officer, archaeologist, scientist, alien warrior. Over time they become a set of relationships in which each person can recognize the others' limitations without reducing anyone to them.

Jack's humor is the most visible example. He punctures self-importance in a universe full of rulers who speak as though obedience is a law of physics. The comedy is not always kindness. Jack can be impatient, suspicious and dismissive. Daniel sometimes has to insist that the person across the room is not a problem to be solved by force. Yet Anderson's performance often makes his resistance to grand speech a source of trust. He can recognize a bluff, make an impossible demand sound ridiculous, and give his frightened team permission to remain human.

Daniel's gift is not merely that he reads ancient languages. He insists that translation has consequences. If you understand what a person says, you may discover reasons that complicate your instructions. In *Scorched Earth*, the rescue of one population collides with the survival requirements of another. The plot is not resolved by naming one group innocent and the other expendable. Daniel makes himself inconvenient because he refuses to accept the initial terms of the conflict.

Carter turns inquiry into visible labor. Her extraordinary competence makes science an action rather than a lecture. She thinks through the operation of a gate, the behavior of a field, or the limitations of an alien device while colleagues are depending on the answer. Her finest scenes make both the excitement and the exhaustion of that work legible. Competence does not exempt her from impossible choices. It puts her in the position of having to make them under pressure.

Teal'c brings a past none of the others can reproduce. He understands the machinery of obedience because he helped operate it. His quiet formality can make a small gesture or dry response funnier than a roomful of jokes. His physical courage is obvious; the harder courage is returning to people who regard him as a traitor, acknowledging the suffering caused by his old loyalty, and persisting in the Jaffa struggle when the rebellion becomes politics rather than heroism.

The SGC gives all four a place in which their differences can produce more than conflict. Hammond must balance the demands of military command, political oversight and the people he sends into danger. Janet Fraiser receives the bodies that adventurous television can be tempted to treat as disposable. Walter, Siler and the other familiar staff make the mountain feel occupied by professionals whose work continues between the spectacular events. The institution is at its most credible when its procedures exist to care for human beings rather than merely to restrict the heroes.

But the institution's legitimacy is not secure. The NID's clandestine thefts reveal how the rhetoric of security can justify conduct that endangers both other worlds and Earth's own principles. Kinsey can be arrogant and opportunistic without every question he asks about oversight being illegitimate. International disclosure introduces the possibility that a discovery with consequences for everyone cannot remain the exclusive property of a single country indefinitely.

This is the show's political imagination at its most interesting: not a program that should be disbanded, and not a program whose noble protagonists should be trusted with everything, but an organization whose very successes make accountability more necessary.

## The joke is evidence that the people are alive

Few science-fiction adventures could ask audiences to accept a galactic war, a semi-sentient race of machine predators, the horrors of parasitic enslavement, and a prolonged sketch about a man learning to juggle during an endless time loop. *SG-1* can, because its comedy is not a separate program pasted over the serious one.

*Window of Opportunity* is a model of the method. Jack and Teal'c endure the same day repeatedly while Daniel and Carter search for a solution. The recurrence turns routine into an opportunity for rebellion. The brief golf game through the Stargate works because the team has spent years treating that ring as a gateway to consequences too serious for leisure. For one absurd moment it becomes a way to hit a ball unimaginably far.

Yet the episode's ending does not dismiss pain. Malikai is trapped by grief; he wants time to return to a place where a loved one is still alive. Jack knows enough about losing a child to recognize a temptation that no amount of repetition can satisfy. The comedy makes the grief sharper because we have been laughing with a man who has learned to keep moving around an old wound.

That range is vital to the show's endurance. Humor prevents an enormous mythology from becoming oppressive. It also keeps the team from sounding as though it believes its own publicity. Many of SG-1's enemies demand awe. The protagonists are reluctant to provide it. That reluctance is funny; it is also politically resonant in a galaxy full of people who claim absolute authority.

Eventually the show becomes comfortable making itself the object of the joke. *Wormhole X-Treme!* invents a melodramatic television adaptation of the Stargate program inside the Stargate universe. *200* lets the writers and performers play with casting, genre, cliché, budget-saving tricks and the questionable decisions television productions sometimes make. These episodes work best when the self-parody feels earned by the audience's familiarity with the ordinary show. They are a reward for knowing the language of SG-1 well enough to recognize it when exaggerated.

The humor is not infallible. Some jokes rely on broad caricature. Repeated character quirks can become a shorthand for actual characterization. And self-reference loses its charm if it substitutes for telling another good story. But at its strongest, the comedy keeps a long-lived fictional world from being embalmed in its own importance.

## Ten years of television means ten years of changing circumstances

The show's production history helps explain the texture of its storytelling. The series premiered on Showtime in July 1997 and spent five seasons there before moving to the Sci Fi Channel in 2002. It was produced in and around Vancouver, British Columbia, where the same forests, industrial locations, gravel pits, large institutional buildings and custom-built sets could become many different destinations.

The repetition is sometimes visible. An alien planet may look like a wooded corner of coastal Canada. One society seems to live in a village a short walk from the gate, while another inhabits a city represented by a few corridors and a well-chosen establishing image. Costumes may carry the weight of worldbuilding that a larger production would distribute across hundreds of extras and elaborate streets.

Yet this economy produces some of the show's most durable images. The gate room is a real, navigable set, not an abstract background. Viewers learn the positions of the ramp, control room, blast doors and adjacent corridors. If someone fails to come home, the emptiness of the room can register without a speech. An entire fictional workplace has been built through the patient reuse of physical space.

Contemporary accounts from the art and effects departments describe the challenges of constructing the gate itself and of making it operate again and again. Such work is easy to overlook because an effective prop eventually stops looking like a prop. The iris becomes something the audience understands with the same automatic confidence as a door at home. You know what it means when it closes. You know what it means when it does not.

The show's style changes across its run. Early seasons are often closer to isolated adventure and direct confrontation with the Goa'uld. Middle seasons build the mythology outward, give recurring allies and enemies a longer memory, and experiment with increasingly ambitious stories. The later period uses larger interstellar conflicts, Earth ships and the Ori to sustain a setting in which the original heroes are no longer novices.

Some of these changes respond to storytelling opportunity. Others arise because television is made by people whose careers, interests and circumstances change. Writers and producers adjust the balance of serialized and self-contained stories. Performers step away, return or assume different responsibilities. Channels, budgets, the prospect of a film and the possibility that the current season might be the last all influence the kinds of finales a production can attempt.

It is important not to invent a single perfect master plan after the fact. The long continuity is impressive partly because it grew under changing conditions. Characters who were not meant to carry the entire decade become essential. Ideas that might have sustained a few episodes become the foundation for years of conflict. A finale imagined as a possible ending becomes the doorway to another year.

This kind of television gives audiences something that a perfectly plotted, finite adventure cannot offer in the same way: the sense of a shared place that has changed with them over time.

## The endings that kept moving

There are several moments in the show's middle years that feel like endings. The seventh-season finale *Lost City* is perhaps the grandest. Years of fragments about the Ancients, their language and technology, and the growing menace of Anubis converge in a story that threatens Earth itself. Jack gains access to Ancient knowledge, again at risk to his own life. The team searches for a way to defend the planet. The climax in Antarctica gives the series the scale of a theatrical event, with a battle that transforms Earth's position in the galaxy.

It is satisfying in the way a successful long adventure can be satisfying: earlier details finally become relevant to an enormous payoff. It also shows how far the series has traveled. A program whose initial equipment consisted of a salvaged ring, an iris and a few teams has become the defender of a world with extraordinary ancient weapons at its disposal.

The success creates a challenge. If Earth can defeat a System Lord's fleet with an Ancient installation, how much danger can the next enemy pose without requiring the writers to introduce an even larger power? If every discovery ultimately becomes a stronger weapon, will the original thrill of entering an unknown village survive?

One answer was to change who was doing the exploring. *Stargate Atlantis* began in 2004, during SG-1's eighth season, sending an expedition to the Pegasus galaxy and reestablishing the vulnerabilities of a remote human presence. Another answer was to change the people at the center of SG-1 itself.

Daniel had already departed through ascension after *Meridian*, and Jonas Quinn briefly took his place. This was not a straightforward substitution of one clever translator for another. Jonas came from a world whose political decisions had led to catastrophe, and his eagerness carried both admiration for the team and a desire to earn his place. Daniel's eventual return rearranged the balance again. Later, Jack moved toward command, and Cameron Mitchell took on a role that had to reconcile his enthusiasm for SG-1 with the experience and authority of people already on the team. Vala arrived as someone whose evasions, jokes and apparent selfishness forced the others to decide whether their community could make room for another difficult person.

The transitions were not always seamless. Viewers who cared about the original quartet had good reason to feel that the loss of daily Jack-and-Daniel exchanges changed the show's character. Mitchell's formal leadership could feel peculiar alongside Carter's experience. Vala's humor had to be balanced against the gravity of the Ori conflict. But it would be a mistake to treat these additions only as disruptions. They explore different ways of belonging to a team whose earlier history has become almost legendary inside its own universe.

That problem belongs to long-running television as much as to Stargate. Once characters have become beloved, a replacement is measured not only against the job description but against years of small familiar gestures. A new performer cannot manufacture that history in one scene. The show has to let new relationships develop, sometimes in the presence of an audience that would prefer the old arrangement to remain intact.

## A more powerful Earth is not a simpler Earth

The change in SG-1's scale is not only about weapons. It changes the question the series asks of its heroes.

At first, Earth is a vulnerable outsider looking for protection. The Goa'uld know more, travel farther and command terrifying resources. The Asgard and other advanced allies appear almost incomprehensibly capable. The human protagonists must improvise and negotiate from weakness. Their limitations are the dramatic engine.

After years of salvage, study, alliances, diplomacy and combat, Earth possesses ships capable of interstellar travel, sophisticated defenses, and knowledge that other civilizations covet or fear. The Asgard eventually entrust humanity with the legacy of their technology. In *Unending*, this inheritance arrives alongside their own extinction. The gift is immense, and the circumstances make celebration feel inadequate.

There is a quietly radical shift here. The people who once knocked on another civilization's door asking for help now have the capacity to become the powerful outsiders who arrive with an offer that carries hidden demands. Their decisions can affect entire regions of the galaxy. Other societies must decide whether Earth is a trustworthy ally, an overconfident newcomer, or a threat that does not yet understand itself.

The series does not always explore this transition as critically as it could. Earth sometimes benefits from a generous presumption that its military command will use extraordinary technology responsibly. People on other planets are occasionally written as politically naive, so that the visiting Americans can recognize the obvious solution. The show's insistence on the freedom of others can coexist awkwardly with stories in which outsiders liberate those others in a single episode, then leave without seeing the institutional aftermath.

These are not minor questions, because SG-1 explicitly builds its drama around the misuse of power. The Goa'uld think power proves entitlement. The Ancients sometimes use noninterference to avoid the consequences of their own history. The Ori turn literal miracles into mechanisms of obedience. Earth cannot oppose those regimes consistently without asking how its own growing power should be constrained.

The best episodes make room for that discomfort. *The Other Side* begins as a seemingly straightforward opportunity to aid a population at war, then reveals information that makes the visitors' first assumptions intolerable. *Learning Curve* asks whether rescuing a child from an exploitative custom is enough, or whether the whole relationship between the child's society and its knowledge must be understood. *Scorched Earth* turns a humanitarian rescue into a collision between the survival requirements of two populations. The show is more persuasive when it allows choices to remain difficult after the villain has been identified.

It is also worth acknowledging the setting's cultural shortcuts. Drawing alien civilizations from a stock of Egyptian, Norse, Arthurian and other historical references makes the universe accessible and gives archaeologists something interesting to do. It can also flatten distinct human traditions into interchangeable evidence that aliens were there first. The most interesting applications of the conceit treat history as a living field of inquiry, not as a collection of decorative names for spacecraft.

An affectionate history of SG-1 has room for both judgments. Its imagination could be broad and generous. Its shortcuts could be reductive. Admiration is not improved by pretending the second fact never happened.

## The great stories in the margins of the war

When people remember a ten-season science-fiction series, they often describe its main mythology: the defeat of the Goa'uld, the rise of Anubis, the Replicators, the Ori. But SG-1's identity is also preserved in episodes that interrupt the grand sequence.

*The Torment of Tantalus* is one of the clearest. The team discovers that a traveler passed through Earth's gate decades before the modern program and was stranded on another world. Daniel's delight in ancient knowledge is balanced against the tragedy of a lifetime spent waiting, and the immediate need to rescue a living person. A discovery can be historically enormous and still fail to justify abandoning someone to die for it. The story makes room for both wonder and judgment.

*Heroes* uses a documentary crew to make the invisible labor of the SGC visible, then subjects that act of recording to an ethical test. Janet Fraiser's death is devastating because the show has made her a presence in the recurring rooms and routines of the base. The emotional effect depends on years of continuity that were not written solely to prepare the audience for a tragedy. Television can do this: make a supporting character an indispensable part of the world's texture before a single story asks you to see how much that work meant.

*Window of Opportunity* shows another use for accumulated familiarity. The routine of the base has become so legible that the audience can enjoy its disruption. The spectacle of Jack and Teal'c misbehaving is funnier because we have watched them obey, argue about, or work around those same procedures for years. When grief enters the ending, the episode reminds us why the laughter has not canceled the reality of their losses.

And *The Fifth Race* offers the most optimistic kind of payoff. The universe grows rather than contracts around the characters. Humanity's future is not fixed by one battle. The Asgard see possibilities, and the team is still young enough as a civilization to deserve instruction. The episode helps define the show's confidence that learning and cooperation can make a society more capable without necessarily making it less humane.

Notice how different these four stories are. One is an archaeological tragedy. One is a documentary about sacrifice. One is a comedy. One is an invitation into deep galactic time. They all feel like SG-1, not because they use identical plots, but because they make curiosity and responsibility collide in a familiar group of people.

That range is why reducing the series to a greatest-hits list of battles and alien races misses so much of its appeal.

## The Ori, the last war, and an argument about belief

The last two seasons introduced a threat designed to challenge the old formula. The Goa'uld are pretenders to divinity: technologically powerful parasites who rely on people mistaking spectacle for godhood. The Ori are different. They are ascended beings capable of genuine feats that lower-plane people cannot duplicate. Their authority remains coercive, but the easy argument that the gods are merely frauds no longer works.

The distinction expands the show's concerns. A society can be manipulated not only by false miracles but by real power presented as a demand for obedience. The Ori use faith, fear, military force and a structured church to make submission a condition of safety. The Ancients, meanwhile, refuse most intervention in the lower planes even when their estranged relatives are exploiting them. Their restraint can be understandable without being satisfactory.

This mythology also gives Daniel, Vala and Teal'c personal reasons to resist. Daniel has spent years opposing those who use knowledge as a claim to absolute authority. Vala has lived as the unwilling host of a Goa'uld and then seen the Ori claim her family and her child. Teal'c knows the painful work of leaving a system that has organized not only one's public obedience but one's most intimate sense of identity.

The resulting story is ambitious, sometimes uneven, and at its best morally sharper than another contest over an increasingly powerful starship. It asks what liberation would mean for someone who sincerely believes in the system that has harmed them. It also asks whether a superior power's refusal to help is an ethical principle or an evasion of responsibility.

*The Ark of Truth* concludes the war through an ancient device that forces recognition of the Ori's deception. It gives the series a formal victory, while leaving an awkward question: can one protect freedom by compelling belief, even if the belief is true? Robert C. Cooper, the film's writer and director, acknowledged this tension in a contemporary interview rather than presenting the Ark as morally unproblematic.

Perhaps that makes the ending more revealing than a cleaner triumph would have been. For years SG-1 has relied on technical discoveries to resolve impossible threats. The final giant threat is ended with a technical discovery that trespasses on the very freedom the team has been defending. The story wins its war but cannot quite release itself from the danger of superior technology.

## A television ending, and two movie endings

The regular series finally concluded in 2007 with *Unending*. It is not the giant Ori battle a viewer might expect. Instead the crew of the Odyssey spend years inside a time-dilation field while a fraction of a moment passes outside. They age, argue, love, work, grieve and learn to live in an environment from which there seems to be no escape. Carter eventually finds a solution, but Teal'c alone retains the memory of the long life the others have lost.

It is a moving choice because the story is about time spent together. The central question of a ten-season series is not merely whether the galaxy can be saved. It is what the people have meant to one another during the attempt. The extended life aboard the Odyssey stages that question explicitly, and the lost memories make it hurt. Some experiences form the people we know, even when the rest of the world cannot see the experience at all.

The two films released in 2008 handle the unfinished business in different ways. *The Ark of Truth* resolves the Ori conflict. *Continuum* uses Ba'al's interference in the past to erase the Stargate program from history and place familiar characters in a world that never learned to expect them. The former seeks finality for the late-series mythology. The latter returns to the delight of an old-fashioned Stargate predicament: strange circumstances, incomplete information, a clever enemy and a race to get home.

*Continuum* also has a touching way of honoring the show's history. The protagonists' achievements have become so extensive that erasing them produces a different world. Their ordinary existence as a team is itself the miracle being protected. Mitchell's lonely intervention in the past saves a future he cannot remember alongside his friends, and the evidence survives as a photograph.

Neither film is required to act as a ceremonial funeral. The ring is still available. The team may go to another world tomorrow. SG-1 was a story about work that could continue, so its endings honor the people without pretending the work must cease.

## Why the room still feels occupied

By the time the full series is over, a viewer has spent a remarkable amount of time in the rooms beneath Cheyenne Mountain. It can be easy to forget how much of the pleasure comes from doing the same thing repeatedly, with small variations.

A familiar voice calls from the control room. A warning light appears. The chevrons lock. A gate opens somewhere it should not, or refuses to open somewhere the team desperately needs to go. An officer looks toward the ramp. A medic rushes past. Somebody offers a plan that sounds unreasonable until Carter begins to explain why it might work. Jack makes a remark that earns an exasperated look from Daniel. Teal'c pauses just long enough for the room to wonder what he is thinking.

These gestures are not grand mythology. They are the grammar of people who have learned to share a dangerous occupation. Fiction that lasts this long can make the audience feel it knows the workplace, even as the workplace becomes progressively stranger. Returning there is a pleasure in itself.

This helps explain why the show can survive shifting tones. It can present political argument, a monster attack, a temporal paradox or a parody of its own writing because viewers have confidence in the people holding those forms together. When one character changes, the others supply a memory of what came before. When the universe grows too large, the gate room restores a human scale.

It also explains why its finest emotions rarely require enormous speeches. Hammond's steadiness is meaningful because we have seen him make a thousand smaller decisions. Fraiser's loss is unbearable because she has spent so much time quietly saving people. Daniel's excitement over an inscription is endearing because we know how rarely the rest of his team shares his first priorities. Teal'c's jokes are funny because years of restraint have taught us to hear the humor in half a sentence. The audience supplies a history the script no longer has to recite.

No amount of contemporary visual spectacle can reproduce that accumulation by itself. It has to be earned with scenes, returns, consequences and the willingness to let characters stay in a room together long enough to become familiar.

## An optimistic show that knows its heroes can be wrong

It is tempting to call SG-1 simply optimistic, and that is partly true. Its central assumption is that the universe can be explored by people who are curious, resourceful and basically willing to help. Enemies can be defeated. Ancient mysteries can be studied. Former servants of tyrants can become allies and leaders. Even the most forbidding technology may turn out to be comprehensible with enough effort.

But the optimism is stronger when it is not mistaken for certainty. The team is often wrong. Its members disagree. Their discoveries create dangers they did not anticipate. Some people refuse Earth's help for good reasons. Some allies prove difficult. An institution founded to protect the planet can hide conduct its own citizens might condemn. Knowledge can be misused, and rescue can become domination if the rescuers stop listening.

The series sometimes resolves these problems too easily. It can put too much faith in its protagonists or forgive destructive choices because the next episode has begun. Yet it repeatedly returns to a more demanding idea: knowing more does not relieve you of the obligation to choose well. Sometimes it makes the choice harder.

This is why the invention of the gate remains so elegant after all the ships and ancient devices have appeared. It is a machine that creates contact. Contact is not the same thing as understanding. A door makes a conversation possible; it cannot guarantee that the conversation will be wise, honest or kind.

The heroes' greatest achievements come when they treat the other side as inhabited by people rather than as a supply of technology or a field of targets. The series' failures become more visible when it forgets that principle. The difference supplies an honest standard by which to admire the show without worshipping it.

## One more address

Imagine returning to the SGC before the first episode, before Carter has made the control room a familiar place, before Teal'c has chosen to betray Apophis, before Daniel has crossed a second gate, before anyone has met the Asgard, built an interstellar ship or learned what the Ancients left behind.

The room is waiting. No one knows what the program will become. The machinery is enormous and slightly absurd. The people who will operate it are capable but deeply limited. They have too little information, too many possible dangers, and a reason to be afraid of the next address.

Now imagine the same room ten years later. The walls have barely changed. The institution has changed beyond recognition. The galaxy has filled with friends, enemies, histories and unfinished responsibilities. So have the people who keep coming back through the ring.

The distance between those two images is the achievement of *Stargate SG-1*. It turned an exciting film premise into a sustained account of what exploration does to explorers. It found an adventurous, companionable rhythm without becoming entirely comfortable. It let knowledge produce power, and power produce obligations. It made room for grief, bureaucracy, slapstick, cosmic horror and the stubborn hope that the next stranger might become a friend.

There were weak episodes. There were shortcuts, abandoned possibilities and stretches when the growing machinery of the mythology threatened to bury the original simplicity. The series is long enough that there is no reason to pretend every season or reinvention succeeded equally well. Yet the central experience survives those uneven passages.

A small group of people has to decide whether to go through a door. They know they may find something wonderful. They know they may make things worse. They know the people beside them well enough to argue, and well enough to trust that the argument is part of the work.

The gate opens. Someone asks whether everybody is ready. A last piece of equipment is checked. A member of the team says something foolish or profound, sometimes both. Then they step forward into a place they cannot fully imagine.

The best ending for a series about such a door is not to explain every destination. It is to leave us glad that someone is willing to keep opening it.
