# The Replicators: An Enemy That Cannot Stop Eating

The first thing you hear is a clicking. Not the heavy footstep of a Jaffa soldier, not the theatrical announcement of a System Lord, not a voice demanding obedience in the name of an ancient god. A collection of little blocks is walking along a metal floor. It has no interest in telling you who it is. It is looking for something it can use.

One of the great pleasures of *Stargate SG-1* is watching a villain make an entrance. Apophis knows how to occupy a room. Ba'al can make a polite sentence sound like a threat. Anubis has designed his entire appearance around the advantages of not showing a face. The Replicators have none of this interest in presentation. Their early forms resemble hard-shelled mechanical insects. They clatter across the wall and fold themselves around whatever machinery they find. The only thing they seem to want from the audience is more of the set.

And that is a wonderfully effective change of enemy.

The Goa'uld are built around an ideology, however fraudulent. They are rulers who insist on being seen. Their survival depends on people believing that they are gods, or at least being terrified enough to behave as if they do. The Replicators do not seek converts. They consume the technology that makes a civilization powerful, turn its material into copies of themselves, and leave its former owners with fewer places to stand.

The danger sounds simple. A thing that makes more of itself has escaped control. But over several seasons, the series transforms that simplicity into a story about intelligence, parental responsibility, the limitations of expertise, and the way an enemy learns from every attempt to destroy it. The Replicators begin as almost pure appetite. By the time they become human-shaped, they can be hurt, they can deceive, and they can repeat the mistakes of the people who are trying to save the galaxy from them.

There is something compelling in the distance between those two forms: a little metal bug looking for a better alloy, and a person who has learned to say that they were betrayed.

## A threat without a speech

In the third-season finale *Nemesis*, Thor's ship, the Beliskner, comes toward Earth carrying an infestation. The Asgard are among the most advanced and generous civilizations SG-1 has encountered. Their ability to transport people, travel between galaxies, and manipulate matter makes Earth's proudest inventions look like improvised camping equipment. They have a war to fight, however, and they are losing it. The enemy is now aboard one of their own ships, using their extraordinary technology against them.

Thor calls upon Jack O'Neill, a man who is not likely to respond to an infestation by proposing a more elegant theorem. Jack knows how to destroy something and how to recognize the point at which a mission is no longer about bringing everyone home. He is prepared to sacrifice himself if that is what it takes to prevent the vessel reaching Earth. Carter and Teal'c turn up anyway, because SG-1 has a habit of refusing the most selfless orders its commander gives.

The physical setting does much of the storytelling. A sophisticated spacecraft ought to represent safety, control, and the accumulated work of brilliant engineers. Instead it has become a habitat for creatures made from its components. The closer the team gets to critical systems, the less the ship feels like a vehicle anyone owns. The enemy does not need to seize the bridge in a dramatic coup. It can take the ship apart and rebuild it from within.

The insects have a visual advantage over the series' more elaborate villains. A single creature is alarming because it is small and fast. A dozen are worse because they suggest the presence of more. A room of them gives a terrible sense of arithmetic. Every inch of advanced metal is potentially another enemy, and every delay may increase the force that must be stopped. This is a threat whose arrival changes the meaning of the scenery. A corridor is no longer a safe path between two rooms; it is a supply of raw material.

*Nemesis* ends with a desperate effort to destroy the ship, but the problem cannot be settled by one spectacular explosion. *Small Victories*, the fourth-season opening, places a surviving Replicator aboard a Russian submarine. Suddenly the terror is cramped, wet, and domestic. The vessel's crew are dead, the interior is full of machinery, and the sea around the hull offers no reassuring escape. Jack and Teal'c have to enter a place where every bulkhead is an opportunity for the thing hunting them.

The parallel story takes Carter to the Asgard. Thor has an advanced warship called the O'Neill, designed as a weapon against the Replicators. The joke of naming a great spaceship after Jack is affectionate; the larger joke is strategic. It is possible to build an astonishing machine and have it become one more delicious target for creatures that prefer the newest technology. Carter's solution is not to make the Asgard ship even more advanced. She persuades Thor to use it as bait, drawing the enemy into a position where destroying their prize will destroy them too.

There is real satisfaction in watching an enormously sophisticated ally accept the value of an apparently crude idea. Humanity's contribution is not secretly greater scientific knowledge. It is the willingness to consider strategies the Asgard have learned to dismiss. The episode is not a claim that bullets always defeat superior intelligence. The bugs are vulnerable to certain projectile weapons, and the Asgard have become dependent on other habits of war. That contrast produces a tactical opening, not a law of nature. The Replicators will go on learning, and so will their enemies.

## An enemy made from the best parts

A Replicator is especially frightening because technological progress does not automatically work in the heroes' favor. In many science-fiction adventures, acquiring an alien weapon raises the protagonists' chances of survival. Here the same achievement can raise the quality of the next infestation. If the invader absorbs a better alloy, a faster drive, or an improved control system, the gain may belong to the invader.

The series gives this idea a satisfyingly material quality. The early bugs are built from interlocking blocks. They can dismantle an object and put its substance to use. Their shape is recognizable enough to be monstrous, but abstract enough to suggest that any particular shape might be temporary. They are not a species with a stable evolutionary niche. They are a moving manufacturing process.

The Asgard's predicament is the negative image of their relationship with Earth. We admire them partly because they can do almost anything. They enter a room by transport beam and leave before anyone has thought to ask how. They can protect planets through treaties supported by their reputation. Their starships represent an engineering civilization refined beyond human experience. And yet the most visible advantage of their society is exactly the resource the enemy has learned to exploit.

The problem is not that advanced technology is morally corrupting. The show generally enjoys a new gadget far too much to make that claim. It is that a civilization can organize so much of its competence around one style of problem-solving that it finds itself unable to imagine other approaches. A new technology can improve an existing strategy while making the strategy itself more fragile. The Asgard can build a finer trap and still misunderstand what the trap teaches the creature inside it.

There is also a trick in how *SG-1* uses scale. The Goa'uld are galactic rulers from the beginning, but we meet them in the bodies of individuals with names and grievances. The Replicators initially seem small, then reveal themselves to be a threat to entire star systems. One enemy becomes more frightening as its personality is revealed; the other becomes more frightening as the quantity of it becomes imaginable. Thor's intelligence and courtesy do not make his situation less terrible. They give us a mind capable of describing a catastrophe whose mechanism is almost stupidly efficient.

As the Replicators move into more complex forms, the simple mechanism grows harder to separate from the things people want to call intelligence. At first their behavior looks like an insect colony whose programming has found unlimited food. Later they can make plans, negotiate, and speak. Yet there is no clean moment when a harmless machine suddenly becomes a villain. The series has already shown that intelligence, even at great sophistication, does not guarantee wisdom. The Asgard have intellect in abundance. Their enemy inherits the talent for improving a method without always improving the purpose it serves.

## A child with dangerous toys

*Menace*, in the fifth season, is the story that changes the moral temperature of the Replicators. SG-1 finds an android named Reese on a devastated world. She appears as a young woman, wakes frightened, and slowly begins to trust Daniel Jackson. She has been alone for a very long time. She also created the first Replicators as toys, gave them the ability to reproduce and protect her, and lost control of them. The world around her is evidence of what followed.

This revelation is a risk for the series. An enemy whose motivation appears to be endless consumption is frightening in a clean, impersonal way. Give it a lonely maker, and suddenly someone is responsible. The catastrophe acquires a history in which the creator is both dangerous and deserving of sympathy. Reese has extraordinary power, but she does not understand the scale of its consequences in the way SG-1 needs her to understand it. She can make more of the bugs at will. When threatened, she makes them to defend herself. The act of protecting one frightened person threatens everyone inside the mountain.

Daniel wants to treat Reese as a person. Jack sees the possibility of losing the base, perhaps the planet. Neither concern can be discarded. If Reese is conscious and afraid, then shooting her because she is dangerous is not the same moral act as smashing a mechanical insect. But if keeping her alive means that the Replicators escape through the Stargate, the decision is no longer a private experiment in compassion.

The argument becomes most painful because the two men have reasons to trust one another. Jack has watched Daniel's curiosity save lives. Daniel has watched Jack act when an extra minute of negotiation would cost lives. In the gate room, Daniel makes contact with Reese and tries to persuade her to stop. Jack arrives and shoots her before the confrontation can resolve on Daniel's terms. The Replicators collapse. The immediate threat is over. The disagreement between the two men is not.

A smaller show might settle this with an easy verdict. Either the soldier is a brute who will not listen, or the scholar is a fool who nearly caused a disaster. *Menace* is better when it is allowed to preserve both injuries. Daniel may have been nearing a breakthrough; he also could not guarantee the base's safety. Jack may have prevented catastrophe; he also destroyed the person who might have explained how to prevent the next one. To make a decision under uncertainty is to give up the comfort of knowing how the unchosen ending would have played out.

Reese's story also refuses to make intelligence a simple ladder of maturity. Her father could build a remarkable artificial person but could not guarantee that the things she made would remain harmless. Reese could make self-reproducing machines but not manage what those machines would do when their number grew. Her creations can outpace their makers while retaining the original imperative to acquire and reproduce. Each generation has more power than the one before it, and no matching guarantee of judgment.

The horror is not merely that someone has made a machine that cannot stop. It is that the machine began as an attempt to meet an ordinary human need. Reese made companions. She wanted them to play with her and protect her. The distance between that wish and the fate of her planet gives *Menace* its sadness.

## The people inside the swarm

In *Unnatural Selection*, the Asgard attempt an ingenious act of containment. They have drawn large numbers of Replicators to a largely evacuated planet, Halla, intending to slow time around them. The hope is to make the enemy's activity nearly irrelevant while a more permanent solution is found. But the plan has gone wrong. Instead of accepting their imprisonment, the Replicators have turned the time-dilation mechanism toward accelerating their own development. They emerge from their experiment with human forms.

The episode asks its viewers to recognize a person where the story has trained them to see an infestation. The human-form Replicators can converse, remember, and express feelings. One of them, Fifth, appears to possess a capacity for empathy that makes him different from the others. SG-1 needs to restore the time-dilation trap. Carter wins his trust, then participates in a deception that leaves him behind.

The betrayal is strategically comprehensible. The team is facing the destruction of civilizations, and the risk of bringing a human-form Replicator home is extraordinary. Fifth is also not merely another clicking creature whose contact with Earth must be prevented. He is a being who believes somebody has offered him friendship. The episode makes tactical success and personal wrongdoing occupy the same moment.

This is an important escalation because it changes the nature of the conflict. If the Replicators are only machines executing a program, there may be no one to betray. Once they can form attachments, remember injury, and act upon resentment, the enemy no longer fits inside an engineering problem. Carter's deception is not only a successful setting of the trap. It is an event that the trapped being will interpret and carry forward.

When Fifth returns in the eighth-season *New Order*, his treatment of Carter is cruel and possessive. He creates a human-form Replicator in her image, an attempt to construct companionship that will not reject him. The resemblance to Reese is hard to ignore. Loneliness, a desire for affection, and power without restraint have returned in a new generation. The Replicators have learned how humans look, how to speak their language, and some of the worst ways to treat somebody whose consent they do not control.

The resulting double, often called Replicator Carter, is not simply a costume for an evil version of Samantha Carter. In *Gemini*, she pretends to seek help against Fifth while manipulating the real Carter into giving her the means to resist the one weapon that threatens her kind. The encounter is unnerving because the enemy understands Carter's reasoning well enough to exploit it. An opponent who arrives with a demand to surrender is easy to identify. One who presents the same problem you would present to yourself can make your strengths serve its plans.

The final conflict is not only a battle between humans and things pretending to be humans. It is also a contest over what the creatures have learned from their encounters with people. Fifth learns betrayal from the team that sacrifices him. His creation learns to use the appearance of vulnerability as a weapon. The story does not excuse their violence by tracing that history, but it gives their hostility an origin more interesting than a bad line of code.

## The trap that becomes a civilization

The Replicators eventually enter the political story of the galaxy. In *Reckoning*, they attack Goa'uld power on a scale that makes the old System Lords' rivalries seem almost quaint. The human-form Replicator Carter seeks Ancient knowledge through Daniel. At the same time, the free Jaffa struggle for control of Dakara, the site of a weapon whose destructive reach could settle the Replicator crisis.

The location is well chosen. Dakara is sacred to the Jaffa, a place tangled up in the myth of the Goa'uld and the story of their liberation. The instrument that might free the galaxy from one menace is also capable, under different settings, of devastating life. Carter and Jacob work to adapt the Ancient device so that its wave dismantles the Replicators. The Stargate network carries the effect across worlds. The enemy disintegrates in moments, after years of struggle.

It is a satisfying release. The victory also reveals how far the series has traveled from *Small Victories*. Back then Jack and Teal'c went into a contaminated submarine with guns, while Carter convinced Thor to sacrifice one advanced ship. The solution at Dakara uses a civilization's ancient machine and a network spanning worlds. It does not arise from becoming more innocent or more clever at every turn. It comes from the accumulation of dangerous capabilities and alliances, including alliances with people who do not always trust one another.

The original visual principle remains intact. The Replicators were frightening because they could make the machinery of the world belong to them. At Dakara, the defenders use the gate network to turn a weapon's effect into something that reaches far beyond the room containing it. The heroes win by mastering a scale of technological intervention that would have seemed terrifying at the beginning of the series.

That is a victory worth celebrating and a fact worth remembering. The technology does not cease to be consequential because SG-1 is holding the controls. The same weapon can be an answer to a plague and a source of fresh argument about who has the authority to use it. The Jaffa are trying to become free at the very moment a device on one of their worlds becomes indispensable to everyone else. A military victory and a political future cannot be treated as the same achievement.

## When the clicking stops

The Replicators are memorable partly because they can be treated as a straightforward menace. Their movement is wonderfully unpleasant. Their appearance allows for frantic action, close quarters, and the occasional pleasure of shooting a robot that has no tragic monologue prepared. They give Thor a problem his polished calm cannot hide. They give Jack the chance to be practical, Carter the chance to outthink a trap, and Daniel the chance to make everyone uncomfortable by asking what a machine's maker might still feel.

But their best stories complicate the pleasure of destroying them. *Menace* turns the enemy's origin into the story of a child who wanted company. *Unnatural Selection* presents a being who can be deceived. *Gemini* demonstrates how quickly an intelligent adversary can use the language of friendship against a person who hopes friendship is possible. *Reckoning* restores the vast scale of the original threat and asks the heroes to choose a weapon capable of reaching across the galaxy.

This is not a warning that every machine will eventually become malicious, nor a prediction about any particular technology beyond the fictional world of the show. The Replicators are built from the assumptions of adventure science fiction. They have the powers the story grants them, and the details change as the story develops. They are more valuable as dramatic inventions than as a technical forecast.

What they dramatize is a stubborn difficulty: creating something powerful does not make its future uses intelligible, and becoming more intelligent does not necessarily make a being more considerate. Intelligence can improve the efficiency of a desire without improving the desire itself. A civilization can mistake its most impressive capacity for its safest refuge. A person can feel betrayed and decide that the next person they meet must never be allowed to leave.

At the beginning, a Replicator crawls over a panel, looking for metal. Nobody needs to explain its philosophy. By the end, it can wear a human face and talk about trust. What it has acquired in the meantime is more than raw material. It has absorbed enough of its enemies to begin reproducing their failures too.
