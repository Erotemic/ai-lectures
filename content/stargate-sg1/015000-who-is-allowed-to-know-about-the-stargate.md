# 015000 — Who Is Allowed to Know About the Stargate?

**Track:** Stargate SG-1: Through the Gate. **Audience:** Fans and viewers interested in the politics of the SGC. **Prerequisites:** The basic Stargate premise. **Scope:** Secrecy, oversight, rogue technology acquisition, competing governments, political accountability and disclosure. **Spoilers:** Events through seasons seven and eight, especially *Politics*, *Touchstone*, *Shades of Grey*, *Chain Reaction*, *Disclosure*, *Inauguration*, and *New Order*.

# Who Is Allowed to Know About the Stargate?

A president enters the Oval Office. There will be meetings, appointments, arguments about legislation and the thousand small demands of an administration. Then a senior military officer hands over a folder and explains that for seven years the United States Air Force has been sending people to other planets through an alien device hidden inside a mountain in Colorado. There are hostile empires, interstellar treaties, spacecraft, and technologies that could change life on Earth. The new president has not been told.

In *Inauguration*, Henry Hayes responds as a person might reasonably respond to news that the biggest fact in human history has been kept out of the briefing. What makes the moment funny is that the viewer has spent years taking the existence of the program for granted. To us, Cheyenne Mountain is an office. We know where the gate room is, we like the personnel, and we have learned that Walter's matter-of-fact announcements often precede something absolutely preposterous. For the new president, the secret is a constitutional and personal shock. The government he has just been elected to lead has another government-sized activity already running inside it.

The premise of *Stargate SG-1* contains a political arrangement that would sound alarming if we described it without mentioning the characters we love. A small group of officials has access to other inhabited worlds. It controls the principal route by which ordinary human beings might travel there. It acquires weapons and knowledge that have not been subjected to normal public argument. It fights wars without informing the people on whose behalf those wars are fought. Sometimes it makes decisions that could destroy the planet. Its best defense is that most of the people doing the work are remarkably decent.

We know Jack, Carter, Daniel, Teal'c and Hammond. We have watched them refuse cruelty and risk themselves for strangers. The series gives us reasons to trust them. Yet trust in people is not the same as a satisfactory account of who authorizes what they do.

The show's secret program is an ingenious device for adventure. The gate can transport a team between worlds, while Earth remains the recognizable Earth of traffic, schools, elections and expensive congressional arguments. But over ten seasons, the secret becomes more than a device that keeps the status quo intact. It becomes an unresolved question about citizenship, foreign policy, military necessity and the difficulty of telling the truth once keeping a secret has become a way of life.

## The price of not knowing

The first defense of secrecy is easy to understand. In the early series, Earth is vulnerable. The Goa'uld have ships that can overwhelm ordinary defenses. Knowing the address of Earth is already valuable to them. The SGC has an iris on its Stargate because travelers and weapons can arrive through the wormhole at frightening speed. A government confronting that danger might reasonably restrict information about the gate's location, its operating procedures and its plans to prevent invasion.

But there is a difference between protecting operational details and concealing the existence of the undertaking itself. The first can be argued as military necessity. The second means that the people who supply the soldiers, pay for the equipment and would suffer the consequences are unable even to ask what risks are being taken in their name. The ordinary citizen cannot approve a mission, object to a treaty, complain about an off-world injury, or ask why a new weapon is being brought home. The citizen is not merely outside a meeting; they do not know the meeting is taking place.

*SG-1* handles this arrangement partly by surrounding the secret with sympathetic people. Hammond consults superiors. He requires reports. Jack knows he can be relieved of command. The program has a chain of authority, a budget, and consequences for disobeying orders. The series is not imagining four independent treasure hunters who have acquired a wormhole and decided to keep it for themselves.

Yet the extent of official oversight is inconsistent, and the show notices. Senator Robert Kinsey arrives in the first-season episode *Politics* to question whether the program's costs and dangers can be justified. The audience is invited to dislike him. He is dismissive, self-righteous, and spectacularly poorly timed. SG-1 is trying to explain an imminent Goa'uld attack, and Kinsey is preparing to stop the very program that may be able to prevent it.

The framing makes his defeat satisfying. Still, remove the unpleasant delivery and one of his premises remains perfectly serious. An extraordinarily expensive, secret military program ought to face scrutiny. The fact that a program has saved lives does not exempt it from questions about the next mission. A senator can be mistaken about the threat and correct that a system without effective oversight is dangerous.

The series often gives Kinsey bad motives or corrupted connections, so that he can occupy the role of obstacle rather than auditor. Ronny Cox is excellent at making his certainties exhausting. The dramaturgy is understandable: a procedural fight about committee powers is not always what an audience wants when Apophis is sending an armada. But making the loudest advocate for scrutiny into a villain has a side effect. It encourages the viewer to confuse opposition to Kinsey with a defense of secrecy itself.

The best episodes keep that distinction visible. A question can be legitimate even when an awful person is asking it. A good commander can be honest without the arrangements around that commander being adequate.

## The gate has two sides, and so does the state

The corruption of the program does not arrive only through elected politicians. In *Touchstone*, SG-1 learns that someone has stolen a weather-control device from a peaceful world. The theft is committed by people who appear to be working for Earth. Madrona is suffering because a technology that maintained its climate has been taken away. The thieves have used a second Stargate and a secret route that the ordinary SGC command structure did not authorize.

The accusation cuts through a comfortable assumption. We often distinguish the heroes from the Goa'uld by saying that the heroes seek knowledge while the Goa'uld steal and dominate. Now people from Earth have behaved like the pirates the heroes condemn. They have used the same advantages of surprise, superior information and access to take something another society depends upon.

A gate is an extraordinary communications and transportation system. It is also a device whose control can determine whether a people receive help or lose a vital resource. That power creates temptations well beyond any one person's character. An agency can tell itself that a stolen device is necessary to defend Earth. A frightened official can decide that the future will forgive a theft if it produces a weapon. The planet being robbed cannot vote on the question.

*Shades of Grey* makes the problem personal. Jack appears to have turned against the SGC's diplomatic restraint, stealing technology from the Tollan and accepting a place among a rogue operation associated with the NID. The team believes he has betrayed its principles. In the end, we learn that his disgrace was a covert attempt to expose the thieves and their contact inside the legitimate command structure.

The resolution restores Jack to the team, but it does not erase what the episode has demonstrated. The secret program can contain another secret program, one that exploits the first program's own files and mission reports. The existence of classified channels makes it difficult to establish who is acting with authority and who is using the appearance of authority as cover. If even Hammond does not know who has been operating under the flag of his organization, the public has little hope of discovering it.

The story also performs a neat trick with the audience's feelings. We know Jack well enough to find his apparent conversion distressing, and that makes the exposure of the conspiracy gratifying. But consider the other people who have been affected by the rogue teams. The Nox, the Asgard and the Tollan have seen people from Earth take what they had no right to take. They cannot be expected to distinguish a respectable military uniform from an illegitimate one simply because our camera has spent more time with the respectable officers.

This is the diplomatic cost of a secret institution. When somebody misuses its name, the whole institution may lose the trust that took years to earn. An alien civilization does not see all the fine distinctions between the SGC, the NID and a covert contractor's chain of command. It sees human visitors taking things.

## The general who can be threatened

In *Chain Reaction*, Hammond is pressured out of command after people connected to the NID threaten his granddaughters. The replacement commander, Major General Bauer, treats the scientific and diplomatic work of SG-1 primarily as a source of powerful weapons. He divides the team and pursues experiments with a naquadah-enhanced explosive. The contrast is not subtle, and it need not be. The series is showing what can happen when an institution that has learned caution is placed under an authority with different priorities.

The pressure on Hammond is especially revealing. A good chain of command protects people from having to decide national policy according to threats against their families. Here personal courage cannot solve the structural problem. Hammond can refuse an order, but the people issuing the threat know where his family lives. His virtue is being used as a lever against him.

Jack's response involves his old adversary Harry Maybourne and a risky confrontation with Kinsey. It's an entertaining piece of political mischief. It also depends on unofficial maneuvers to remedy an unofficial abuse of power. The audience cheers because the people we trust win. The method is not a model of transparent civilian control. It is another operation in which a small number of insiders must improvise because the official mechanisms are unreliable.

*SG-1* is unusually willing to acknowledge that there can be dangers inside a protective institution. Not all threats arrive through the event horizon. Some come with credentials, committee appointments and a legal theory of why they should be in charge. This adds variety to the series; it also prevents the mountain from becoming an uncomplicated refuge.

Hammond's authority is persuasive because he has limitations. He cannot simply order a planet to cooperate. He cannot decide that the lives of his own people outweigh every obligation to strangers. He knows the difference between being able to seize a technology and having a right to take it. The pressure to replace him with someone more accommodating shows that command can be an ethical practice—but also that the ethical qualities of a commander can be overridden by people the commander does not control.

A democracy cannot plausibly rely forever on the personal integrity of a single general. Yet the series often does precisely that, because George Hammond is more dramatically satisfying than a system of well-written, publicly accountable rules. Don S. Davis makes the arrangement feel safe. It would be harder to defend if a less trustworthy officer sat in his chair.

## The meeting nobody outside can attend

*Disclosure*, in season six, finally takes the political argument beyond the United States. Officials from China, France and the United Kingdom learn that the Americans and Russians already know about the Stargate. They are understandably angry. Earth has been put at risk, and Earth has received valuable benefits, while most of its governments have been excluded from the conversation.

This is not a public announcement. It is an expansion of the secret's membership. The circle of people who know the truth becomes larger, and the new members are powerful nations. We can sympathize with that step and still notice who has not been invited: the citizens of those nations, representatives from less powerful countries, and anyone whose home has been made more vulnerable by decisions taken under the mountain.

The episode is a clip show, and the form has an unexpected advantage. A diplomatic disagreement needs evidence. We know the characters have fought extraordinary battles, but the new officials have not shared those experiences. The episode presents missions and crises as the case for continued American operation of the program. A television device often used to save production resources becomes a way to ask how a vast secret history might be presented to people who are not in the room when it happens.

The international representatives want access, influence and benefits. Those demands are not identical to a principled wish for world democracy. Great powers can object to exclusion because they want a larger share of the power. Russia's position is especially interesting: its officials know about the program and have participated in it, but remain dissatisfied with a subordinate role. The argument is partly about accountability and partly about who gets to enjoy the advantages of technological discovery.

Kinsey proposes transferring control to the NID. The suggestion is alarming to viewers who have seen the agency's rogues stealing from allies and threatening Hammond. Then Thor appears, a small Asgard with a considerable degree of military and diplomatic influence, and endorses the existing SGC leadership. The room's doubts are settled by the arrival of a powerful alien friend who trusts Hammond and his people.

The moment is delightful television. The dignitaries have been discussing a claim so enormous that it might still seem unreal to them; suddenly the claim has a courteous gray face and an authoritative voice. Thor's approval also pays off years of SG-1's patient alliance with the Asgard. Their reputation has become a defense of the program as surely as any weapon.

Yet the resolution has an irony worth enjoying. The political argument over whether one country's military should control humanity's main gate is settled in part by the preference of another civilization's military leader. Thor's judgment is informed and benevolent. It is not a vote by Earth's people. The SGC survives the challenge because the series has made its moral credibility convincing to the one visitor whose approval the assembled officials cannot ignore.

As a dramatic payoff, this works beautifully. As a constitutional settlement, it is provisional at best.

## The man who discovers his own government

*Inauguration*, near the end of season seven, is a second clip-show experiment in putting the show's accumulated history on trial. President Hayes receives the full briefing. Kinsey, now vice president, intends to use evidence of failures and questionable decisions to remove Hammond and SG-1. Richard Woolsey investigates, and the president must decide whom to believe.

Hayes is not simply another version of Kinsey. He has reason to be alarmed by what he learns, and he refuses to assume that the people handing him a folder have told him everything he needs to know. The series is at its best when it lets the president behave as a person with responsibilities, not as a machine that stamps approval on whatever the heroes already want.

The comedy of his initiation is also a reminder of scale. The viewers have spent years inside an exceptional world where transport beams are almost routine. Now someone with considerable authority encounters those facts for the first time. The room he occupies looks familiar; the country he governs turns out to be part of an interstellar conflict that ordinary public life has barely registered.

The secret is not only withheld from voters. It can be withheld from people whose formal offices seem to require knowledge of it. Kinsey has known enough to exploit the program, while Hayes arrives at the presidency without the full story. That imbalance is useful to a political operator. Information itself has become a resource that officials can hoard and spend.

Woolsey is a good addition to this landscape because he is not reducible to a criminal antagonist. His suspicion can feel officious, but it is possible for a skeptical investigator to care about lawful government and still misread the character of the people under investigation. He can also learn. *SG-1* gains something by allowing an auditor to remain a person rather than treating every demand for records as evidence of disloyalty.

The episode ends in a way that protects the characters and permits the series to continue. The program remains operational; the people we trust retain important influence. Yet the structural question survives the president's decision. If a competent president must find out about a planet-sized secret only after entering office, how many other forms of accountability depend on the good judgment of insiders? How long can a society treat the existence of other civilizations as classified operational information rather than a fact about its place in the universe?

## Secrecy begins to change the people it protects

The secrecy device is often most effective when the audience has forgotten it is there. Jack can visit a diner. Carter can talk to someone in an ordinary laboratory. A stranger can make a joke about alien abductions without realizing that the person across the table has spoken to an alien that morning. The contrast is a quiet comedy the series can use almost indefinitely.

It can also create loneliness. Personnel who have faced the death of a friend on another planet may have no ordinary way to explain the loss. A scientist cannot present a discovery to colleagues without disclosing its source. People whose lives have been changed by a classified incident must continue to live among people for whom that incident officially never happened. The private burden of knowledge grows alongside the operational burden of defending Earth.

This is one reason the documentary story *Heroes* is so powerful. The military filmmakers seek access to the SGC's work, and the personnel resist the idea that their experiences can be turned into an official account. The issue is not simply whether the public will see the footage. It is whether people who have lived inside a concealed institution can consent to being represented, and whether an institution can honestly remember loss without giving somebody outside its immediate circle the right to ask questions.

As Earth develops ships and establishes more agreements, keeping the entire program concealed becomes harder to justify by reference to one guarded room. The work is increasingly a foreign policy and a defense policy involving many worlds. It requires international cooperation and a growing industrial base. The story still needs the mountain to remain secret, but the fictional world's practical demands are beginning to outgrow that original dramatic convenience.

There is no simple switch that would solve everything. A sudden worldwide disclosure might create public fear, diplomatic crises and opportunities for enemies. A carefully staged disclosure might give some states or corporations an unfair advantage. Continuing complete secrecy leaves people unable to evaluate risks being taken on their behalf. The alternatives are difficult precisely because the stakes are real inside the story.

A mature version of the SGC's problem would have several layers. Operational security is necessary for missions and defenses. Independent oversight must be capable of investigating commanders and agencies without being dependent on their goodwill. Other nations and affected off-world peoples need legitimate ways to participate in decisions that alter their lives. Public knowledge of the universe need not reveal every address, weapon design or tactical plan. These are distinct questions even if the show often compresses them into one argument over who gets to turn the gate on.

## The secret as a second Stargate

The physical Stargate separates two places. The secret separates two kinds of knowledge on the same planet. Inside the mountain, the galaxy is inhabited, dangerous, politically complex and filled with people whose choices have changed human history. Outside, the night sky continues to mean what it meant before the program opened. People have not been given the chance to revise their understanding of their own world.

The separation is sometimes merciful. It allows an ordinary life to continue while specialists confront threats few citizens could do anything to stop. The audience can come home with the team and find familiar routines waiting. Yet those same routines depend on other people maintaining a fiction, and the people maintaining it have acquired power that cannot be made harmless simply by choosing good officers.

This is the underlying tension in the show's secret war. The SGC is at its best when it behaves as if all the people it encounters have dignity and agency. Its own home population is the largest group of people it does not invite into that conversation. The discrepancy is understandable as television structure. Within the story, it is something a developing interstellar society eventually has to confront.

The final question is not whether everyone should know the location of a dangerous artifact or the details of the next mission. It is whether humanity can claim to be entering a galactic community while most humans are denied the knowledge that such a community exists.

Thor can endorse Hammond. Hayes can resist Kinsey. Jack can expose a rogue operation, and the personnel at Cheyenne Mountain can continue earning trust through their decisions. Those are achievements by good people. None of them supplies a permanent substitute for legitimate government.

At the start of *SG-1*, secrecy is the price of keeping a small and vulnerable Earth alive. By the time the SGC has ships and diplomatic partners, secrecy has become one of the institutions Earth must learn to govern. The gate may be hidden under a mountain, but its consequences are already out in the world. Sooner or later the people living in that world deserve to have a say.

## Source and interpretation notes (not spoken)

Key episode records: *Politics* (season 1), *Touchstone* (season 2), *Shades of Grey* (season 3), *Chain Reaction* (season 4), *Disclosure* (season 6), *Inauguration* (season 7), *Heroes* (season 7), and *New Order* (season 8). *Disclosure* briefs the British, French, and Chinese representatives alongside the already-aware Russian and American governments; it does **not** disclose the Stargate to the general population. *Inauguration* dramatizes President Hayes learning about the program, Kinsey's use of information and Woolsey's investigation. The essay's discussion of democratic legitimacy and oversight is a critical interpretation of the fictional arrangement, not a claim that the show establishes a particular real-world constitutional rule or that any particular disclosure policy would be safe in reality.
