# 009000 — Hammond, Fraiser, and the People Who Keep the Gate Open

**Track:** Stargate SG-1: Through the Gate. **Audience:** SG-1 fans and listeners interested in the supporting ensemble. **Prerequisites:** Basic familiarity with the Stargate program. **Scope:** Command, medicine, continuity, technical and administrative labor at Stargate Command. **Spoilers:** Major spoilers through season seven, including *Chain Reaction*, Cassandra's story, *Heroes*, and *Lost City*.

# Hammond, Fraiser, and the People Who Keep the Gate Open

An alarm sounds in a mountain in Colorado. A ring begins to turn. Somebody in a control room watches symbols lock into place; somebody checks whether the incoming travelers have identified themselves; somebody else stands ready at a massive metal iris that can keep a hostile arrival from getting into the room. In the infirmary, a doctor may have just finished treating a patient, and will soon discover that she has not finished her shift after all.

On the other side of that gate, four explorers might be saving a civilization, making first contact, or running for their lives. They can do none of those things for very long without the people inside the mountain.

*Stargate SG-1* has an immediately marketable idea: an ancient ring opens wormholes to other planets. It has a less glamorous but equally important idea: a discovery of that scale requires an institution. Someone must be responsible for sending people out, admitting them back, treating the wounded, maintaining the apparatus, recording what went wrong, and telling a superior officer when the latest brilliant plan is liable to kill everyone in the building.

The show's two great embodiments of that responsibility are General George Hammond and Doctor Janet Fraiser. Don S. Davis and Teryl Rothery make the Stargate Command a place where people work, argue, care for one another, and sometimes fail. Around them stand the technicians, engineers, guards, nurses, and other teams whose names we may not know even after ten seasons.

Without that supporting world, SG-1 would be four protagonists visiting a succession of sets. With it, the program has a home. And a home is not just where the adventure starts. It is the place to which the costs of the adventure return.

## The man behind the window

Hammond's physical position tells us almost everything about the difficulty of his job. The gate room is visible through glass from the command level above. He can see the people he sends through it. He cannot follow them and direct every decision. Once they pass through, communications may fail and his knowledge becomes partial, sometimes for hours, sometimes for days.

Military fiction often solves this problem by turning the general into an obstruction. The hero in the field knows what is right; the commanding officer sits at a desk and refuses to listen. Then the hero disobeys orders and proves the desk-bound fool wrong. Early SG-1 occasionally hints at this arrangement, but its ensemble gets much richer when Hammond turns out to be a good commander rather than a convenient obstacle.

He is neither omniscient nor indulgent. He must ask questions, keep records, weigh political consequences, and occasionally refuse a request that would make a wonderful episode if it happened to succeed. The audience likes Jack O'Neill for his improvisation. It can also see the significance of Hammond being accountable for Jack's improvisation.

Don S. Davis brought experience to that part. In interviews, he described serving as a United States Army officer and remembering commanders as varied people, not a row of interchangeable shouting men. He thought the initial conception of Hammond was too flat, and said the writers allowed him to make the character increasingly human. This is one of those production recollections that helps explain something plainly visible onscreen: Hammond's authority becomes more convincing as the series gives him more than authority to express.

Davis rarely needs a speech. His slightly weary pause before answering Jack, his refusal to raise his voice simply because somebody else has raised theirs, and his capacity to look alarmed while staying in command are parts of the performance. He often delivers some of the least spectacular lines in the show: questions about personnel, clearances, reports, and extraction. Those lines signal that the spectacular event has entered the world of consequences.

A weaker version of the character would either approve every plan or endlessly prohibit them. Hammond has to decide with incomplete information. His trust in SG-1 is therefore not automatic permission. It is a judgment accumulated through experience, and constantly exposed to the possibility of being wrong.

## The general who can be frightened

*Chain Reaction* gives Hammond a different sort of vulnerability. The opening suggests that the risks of command have finally exhausted him. He announces his retirement. Jack doubts the explanation, and he is right: Hammond has been pressured by people connected to the National Intelligence Department, the NID. To prove that they can reach him, they have arranged to pick up his granddaughters from school and then return them unharmed. The threat does not need to be spoken twice.

It would have been easier to make Hammond a hero who declares that he will never bow to intimidation. Instead, the episode lets him love his family. He refuses to turn the Stargate program into a pipeline for theft and private advantage, but also knows that his grandchildren did not volunteer to stand between him and a covert political operation. He steps away because the people threatening him have made the cost of staying intolerable.

For Jack, loyalty to his general becomes a problem to solve. He has to work around the normal chain of command, recruit the thoroughly compromised Harry Maybourne, and confront Senator Kinsey. That route is entertainingly disreputable. It also asks a serious question: what happens to principled command when other parts of the state use secrecy to evade accountability?

The replacement general, Bauer, illustrates one answer. He prizes aggressive technology acquisition, breaks up the team, brushes past Carter's objections, and orders a dangerous naquadah bomb test through the gate. His approach nearly causes a catastrophe at the SGC. Hammond is restored not because he possesses magical wisdom, but because a commander capable of listening to expertise and caring about his personnel is materially safer.

We should not make Bauer only a cardboard villain. The pressure to develop weapons against the Goa'uld is real. Earth is threatened by enemies with vastly superior technology. Jack's team regularly survives because somebody has brought home something useful. The temptation to turn all exploration into strategic extraction is understandable. The problem is that it can make the institution less intelligent about the risks it is taking, and less scrupulous about who bears them.

Hammond's caution is thus a kind of courage. It is easier, in a story built on explosions, to call for a bigger explosion than to say that the scientist should be heard, the endangered world should be considered, or the colonel should be given time to bring his people home.

He does sometimes take chances. He leaves the iris open longer than an investigator would prefer. He approves missions that are dangerous and politically troublesome. Sometimes he is right; sometimes the show quietly expects us to sympathize with a decision that ought to invite harder scrutiny. But that imperfection is part of the appeal. Command is not a method for eliminating uncertainty. It is the obligation to act while uncertainty persists.

## The doctor who does not live on another planet

Janet Fraiser's world is built from the same problem at a different scale. When the gate malfunctions, when an alien disease spreads, when a team returns with a person who has been transformed or possessed, it is Fraiser who must work out what can be done to a body and what should be done to a person.

She enters the series as a professional with a job. She is not introduced as another adventurer in search of an identity. She has medical authority and often uses it against people with higher military rank. Jack may lead the team, but in the infirmary he is a patient. General Hammond can ask for an update, yet cannot order an illness to end. Carter can explain astonishing phenomena, while Janet has to decide whether the explanation is sufficient to administer a treatment.

That redistribution of authority is vital to the show's sense of reality. There are spaces in which Jack's practical judgment is not enough. There are emergencies in which the most important person on the base is the one who knows whether the patient can survive another hour.

Consider *The Broca Divide*. Early in the first season, an alien infection causes humans to lose higher cognitive functions and exhibit frightening behavioral changes. The story has dated ideas about evolution and a laboratory mystery resolved faster than actual medicine would allow. Yet as an introduction to Fraiser's job it is useful. The team can bring back a pathogen by walking through the same gate that lets them bring back discoveries. Exploration is an epidemiological problem. The medical officer does not get to assume that everyone arriving from an interesting planet is healthy.

The SGC wants a clean division between mission and base, between foreign world and secure home. Fraiser's work constantly proves that the division is fragile. A microorganism, a parasite, an implant, an alien consciousness, or an inherited biological alteration may cross the boundary inside the people everyone is celebrating for returning alive.

She is also frequently the character who refuses the false certainty of the other specialists. An unresponsive colleague may not be dead. A familiar face may be under the control of something else. A patient can be physically stable and still be in extraordinary distress. The story needs someone willing to say that the tests are incomplete, the danger is not understood, or a person is not yet ready to go back on duty.

Teryl Rothery's performance makes this authority feel social rather than merely procedural. Janet can be brisk, gently amused, exasperated, afraid, and unmistakably attached to her patients. She is not a flawless healer who saves everybody. Her inability to save everyone is the reason her composure counts.

## What it means to take someone home

The clearest evidence that Janet is more than the head of an infirmary is Cassandra.

In *Singularity*, SG-1 discovers a child left alive after a Goa'uld attack has devastated her world. The apparent rescue turns into an additional threat: the child has been used as a carrier for a weapon intended to destroy Earth's Stargate. That makes her at once a person in terrible need and a danger to the facility that has sheltered her.

Carter refuses to abandon Cassandra to face the expected explosion alone. The disaster is avoided. Later, Janet becomes the child's adoptive mother. An alien survivor is no longer just an exotic case or an incident filed under an off-world designation. She has a household, a birthday, ordinary adolescent frustrations, and people who will worry about her when the world is not ending.

There is a remarkable expansion of the show's moral geography in that choice. We travel to other planets to meet endangered people; then one of those people becomes family. It is easy for a series to make the rescue of strangers feel complete when the heroes leave the planet. Cassandra continues to exist after the episode's credits.

*Rite of Passage* returns to that commitment with more danger. A dormant Goa'uld experiment in Cassandra's body becomes active as she grows up. Janet has to treat a daughter whose disease is bound to the history of an enemy who exterminated her people. Nirrti, responsible for that experiment, may be the only person who can reverse it.

The SGC faces a grim bargain. Releasing a dangerous Goa'uld might save a teenager. Holding her prisoner might prevent future harm to other worlds. Teal'c is especially conscious of the lives that Nirrti could destroy if freed. Janet is conscious of one life in front of her, a life she has promised to protect. At one point she threatens Nirrti with a gun.

It is a shocking act from the medical officer, and the episode does not become richer by pretending that the gun is a universally defensible solution. Janet's professional ethics and her responsibilities as a mother collide. Hammond must decide whether to make an agreement with a murderous adversary. Carter and Jack are not neutral either. This is not a problem with an easy calculation hidden inside it. It asks what it means to accept that distant victims are as real as the person you love.

For a show often associated with rescuing the innocent and defeating tyrants, it is a useful complication. Every member of the team can value life and still disagree about which decision most honors that value. Janet has earned the right to be morally interesting, not simply morally reassuring.

## The infirmary as another kind of first contact

In *Lifeboat*, an alien ship's passengers have been preserved as consciousnesses, and multiple minds find themselves occupying Daniel Jackson's body. A scenario that could serve as a display of Michael Shanks's acting range also becomes an extended problem in medical ethics. Who is the patient? Daniel, whose body has been commandeered? The displaced passengers, some of whom will otherwise die? Is restoring one person the same thing as killing someone else?

Janet must distinguish symptoms from identity while new identities appear before her. The episode lets the scientific and the humane questions coexist. Neurological observations do not decide whether another consciousness has a claim to survival. Compassion without an understanding of the machinery may achieve nothing. She has to use both.

This is another reason the infirmary is structurally important. The characters visit strange civilizations through the gate, but patients from those civilizations can also arrive inside a familiar, fluorescent-lit room. The idea of first contact does not end when a team carries a specimen or survivor back to Earth. It is often only beginning.

The doctor also has an authority that does not depend on being the most entertaining person in the scene. A surgeon's achievement is not always a revelation. Sometimes it is a stable heart rate, a patient's return to consciousness, or a chance to tell a family that their loved one is going to be all right. Those outcomes are not glamorous enough to form the weekly climax every time. Over years of television, they become part of the world the audience trusts.

That trust makes Janet's humor important. She is capable of treating the great explorers as troublesome adults who ought to know better, of getting straight to the medical point when they want to ramble, and of being a friend rather than a dispenser of antiseptic advice. The effect is subtle: when a patient is frightened, we believe Janet might be able to help, even before we know what the problem is.

## Everybody who knows which switch to throw

The mountain is populated by people who keep returning without the grandeur of a hero's introduction. There is Walter, played by Gary Jones, who spends much of his career at the controls of a device that might destroy the room. There is Sergeant Siler, played by Dan Shea, who can often be found where something needs repairing, testing, or carrying. There are nurses, security personnel, engineers, cooks, and the members of other SG teams.

These characters create a kind of continuity no mythological exposition can substitute for. When Walter reports an incoming wormhole, he does not need to remind us how the gate works. We know he has been there before. When an engineer enters with equipment, the problem feels like a maintenance task in an established facility rather than a new miracle invented for the scene. The same corridors, faces, and procedures become part of the language through which the audience understands danger.

Their work can be amusingly repetitive. A device shorts out. A test fails. Somebody is handed a form. Somebody else discovers that a supposedly secure room is not secure. SG-1 makes a habit of importing artifacts it does not fully understand into a mountain beneath a populated country. One can imagine the safety meetings that would follow if the show had unlimited time for them.

But the repeated routine is doing narrative work. It allows adventure to have a baseline. The familiar technician's voice can convey, in a few seconds, that this activation is different from every one before it. The alarm becomes more frightening because we have heard it a hundred times when disaster was successfully prevented.

A persistent supporting cast also prevents the base from seeming built exclusively for four stars. The field teams are not individually indispensable. There are other scientists, other soldiers, and a community whose expertise predates and outlasts a given crisis. We do not usually follow them on their missions, which raises a useful question about the point of view the series chooses. What would an episode look like if it followed the person whose job is to make sure an off-world rescue team can return, rather than the team performing the rescue?

The seventh-season episode *Heroes* asks something close to that question, though it does so through a documentary crew. We will give that episode its own close reading. For now, the important connection is that the people in the background have always been doing consequential work, even when the camera was elsewhere.

## An institution worth defending is still an institution

There is a tempting sentimental way to describe Hammond and Fraiser: they are the benevolent parents of a remarkable group of adventurers. The description captures their warmth, but it can hide what makes them interesting. A general and a military doctor operate within rules, hierarchies, secrecy, and obligations that extend beyond the team's private happiness.

Hammond may be an admirable leader without the Stargate program being an uncomplicated public good. Its activities are conducted in secret. It can make treaties, introduce diseases, destroy enemy ships, and bring new technologies into Earth's strategic arsenal without most of the people affected having any idea that it exists. The presence of a decent general does not answer every question about who should have authority over such decisions.

*Inauguration* makes this tension explicit. Newly elected President Hayes hears arguments about the competence of the SGC, with Richard Woolsey presenting incidents in which personal loyalties appeared to compromise operational judgment. His evidence is tendentiously chosen, but the underlying incidents are real. People have been endangered by the program. Friendships do influence decisions. An oversight process is not inherently malicious just because one of its political sponsors is Senator Kinsey.

Hammond can be both the person we want supervising the gate and someone who should have to explain his decisions to others. The distinction is easy to lose when the villains are unscrupulous enough that anyone who questions the heroes seems suspect. The best SG-1 stories understand that trust and accountability are supposed to reinforce each other.

Fraiser's situation is equally uneasy. Caring for a patient is different from protecting the national security interests of a secret military command. An alien child, a captive Goa'uld, and a wounded airman can make incompatible claims on the same institution. Medicine is supposed to be available on grounds other than strategic usefulness. Yet nearly everyone in this infirmary exists because of a program that chooses its risks strategically.

The characters do not resolve these contradictions once and for all. Instead, we are invited to judge their choices. Their decency is demonstrated not by never facing the conflict, but by continuing to recognize the people within it.

## The cost of coming home

By the time *Heroes* arrives, the audience has spent years watching Janet return the wounded to life. When the episode kills her on an off-world rescue mission, it changes the meaning of those earlier scenes. The people who cross the gate to recover SG-1 do not stand outside the danger. The person sent to save the soldier can be killed while saving him.

It is tempting to make her death the point of this lecture, because the loss is so painful and because it supplies an easy conclusion. That would be unfair to the character. Janet is not merely a future casualty being carefully developed for dramatic effect. She is the woman who adopted Cassandra, negotiated with frightened patients, confronted impossible diagnoses, and was trusted with the lives of nearly everyone who passed through the mountain.

The memorial makes an especially intelligent choice: it invokes the names of people who are alive because of Janet. A death can be counted once. The consequences of a medical career resist a simple count. Patients return to families, careers, and ordinary afternoons. Some of them eventually save other people. We cannot measure all of those effects by looking at the moment a life ends.

That is also true of Hammond. He does not normally save Earth by throwing the decisive grenade. He makes enough good decisions that people with different talents have the chance to save Earth. When *Lost City* puts him in command of the Prometheus during the defense of the planet, the moment is satisfying partly because we have watched him earn command through years of quieter decisions. The desk and the bridge belong to the same profession.

A science-fiction series can take its supporting personnel for granted precisely when it has successfully persuaded us that they will always be there. Their presence becomes part of the architecture. The cost is felt when a familiar voice is absent from the control room, or a colleague enters an infirmary and realizes the person who used to meet them there has gone.

## Keeping the gate open

Return to the alarm in the mountain. The gate turns. On the other side, an explorer runs toward the event horizon with an enemy in pursuit. On this side, people who may never receive a medal must decide whether to keep the connection open, close the iris, send reinforcements, summon medical help, or begin an evacuation.

The person running might be a hero. That is not the only kind of heroism in the scene.

Hammond and Fraiser help *SG-1* tell a story in which courage is not identical to recklessness, care is not identical to kindness, and expertise does not remove the need for judgment. They give the adventure weight by refusing to let the explorers float free of the lives affected by their choices. The technicians and nurses around them make that responsibility feel continuous, extending through ordinary shifts as well as famous crises.

For an interstellar travel program, success is often described as going farther: another galaxy, another technology, another gate address. The people in Cheyenne Mountain do something at least as important. They make it possible to come back, and to find a world still waiting for you when you do.
