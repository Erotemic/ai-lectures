**Track:** Stargate SG-1: Through the Gate. **Audience:** Fans; some jokes and examples assume familiarity with the characters. **Prerequisites:** The basic SG-1 premise. **Scope:** *Point of No Return*, *Wormhole X-Treme!*, *200*, and SG-1's comedy about its own form. **Spoilers:** Martin Lloyd's backstory and the comic premise and imagined sequences of the two anniversary episodes; minor late-series ensemble details.

# Wormhole X-Treme!, 200, and the Show That Could Laugh at Itself

A television crew is trying to film a scene about an extraordinary interstellar portal. The actors are dressed for adventure. Somebody has to explain how the portal works, somebody has to look grave, and somebody probably has to announce that the fate of the planet is at stake. Then a practical question arrives. The writers have described people walking through the portal. But on this television set, how exactly does an actor perform the action of walking through something that does not exist?

The imaginary series is called *Wormhole X-Treme!* Its fictional crew has a fictional problem, which happens to resemble a real problem faced by people who make science-fiction television. That nested absurdity is the beginning of one of *Stargate SG-1*'s most affectionate habits. The show knows that its own adventures depend on actors staring at painted objects, speaking impossible technical explanations with conviction, and running down corridors with enough urgency to persuade an audience that an entire galaxy is waiting at the other end.

And it thinks that this is funny.

The question is why the joke does not destroy the adventure. If a series openly mocks its technologies, its dramatic devices, its casting problems, and its solemn speeches, why should viewers go back to believing any of them? The answer is found in two anniversary episodes: the fifth-season *Wormhole X-Treme!*, which marks SG-1's hundredth episode, and the tenth-season *200*, which celebrates the two-hundredth. They are separated by years of changing cast, widening mythology, and increasing confidence. Both spend an episode looking at the show from outside the show. But they do it for different reasons, and together they explain something important about the kind of affection *Stargate* asks of its viewers.

## The man who knew too much

The funniest part of *Wormhole X-Treme!* begins a season earlier, in *Point of No Return*, with Martin Lloyd. Martin phones Cheyenne Mountain and says he knows about a secret device buried there. This is the sort of claim that should be easy to dismiss. People who claim knowledge of alien conspiracies are not difficult to find in fictional Colorado. Martin is nervous, confused, and uncertain whether his own memories can be trusted. The program investigates because he knows details he should not know.

What initially looks like an extended joke about conspiracy culture turns into a sadder story. Martin really is an alien. He and several companions escaped a war and came to Earth. His memory has been suppressed. When he attempts to recover his past and return home, he finds that the world he hoped to save has been devastated by the Goa'uld.

That background is easy to forget when Willie Garson begins playing Martin as a flustered television producer. Yet it changes the later comedy. His science-fiction show is not merely a clever mechanism for mocking SG-1. It is created by someone who has lost his original world and found in stories a way to arrange the fragments of his experience. He is trying to manufacture an adventure out of something that once frightened him and that he cannot entirely leave behind.

There is another joke inside the premise: the SGC can tolerate the televised version of its secret because fiction may provide useful cover. The closer the program comes to the truth, the easier it may become for officials to dismiss the truth as something a viewer has seen on television. A conspiracy theorist can turn into a television producer and thereby make his own former claims less credible.

This reverses the show's customary relationship with secrecy. Usually the viewer knows what the public does not. We watch people at the SGC discuss real alien civilizations while everyone outside the mountain goes about ordinary life. Martin invents a fake version of what the viewers know, and the fake is intended to hide the real. The result is a compact comic machine: we know a secret, the characters know the secret, and the people making a show about it get the secret wrong in wonderfully productive ways.

## The alien planet has a production schedule

In *Wormhole X-Treme!*, the SGC discovers that Martin has become associated with a television production bearing a startling resemblance to its own operation. The fictional team travels through an artificial portal. There are recognizable role equivalents: an officer, a scientist, an archaeologist, and a warrior. Characters argue about how the rules work. A performer wants a more satisfying part. The director has to make a scene happen whether its technical explanation is sensible or not.

The episode frequently relocates the grandeur of space opera to the daily irritations of work. A fictional hero must deliver an imposing line, but a camera placement or an acting choice interrupts. A technological crisis is also a scripting crisis. Someone has to ask whether an expository speech can be made understandable to an audience; someone else has to ask whether the audience will even care. The people producing heroic certainty are themselves uncertain, tired, and subject to schedules and other people's decisions.

That is the central source of the comedy. On one side is the viewer's delight in an enormous imaginary universe. On the other is the ordinary human labor required to put that universe on screen. The scale difference is vast. An actor must portray the end of a civilization while remaining alert to where the lens is, when a cue will occur, and whether someone will ask for another take.

*Stargate* is especially well suited to this kind of joke because its main characters are themselves workers. They do not travel the galaxy as wandering knights in a permanent state of inspiration. They write reports, take orders, repair machinery, attend meetings, quarrel over equipment, wait for test results, and sometimes lose an afternoon because the gate is not doing what the gate is supposed to do. A television crew's ridiculous procedures resemble the SGC's procedures more closely than either group would like to admit.

The real gate room and the fake gate set differ in degree rather than in dramatic structure. Both require people to turn an astonishing idea into something they can operate on a Tuesday. The joke works because the series has already persuaded us that doing the work is a worthy form of heroism.

## Technical dialogue and other impossible jobs

There are many ways to make bad science fiction. One is to invent a technology without inventing a limit. Another is to explain a limit in language so obscure that the audience cannot tell whether the characters have a plan. *SG-1* has spent years trying to occupy the narrow space between those failures. Its Stargate has addresses, a dialing process, a horizon that can be dangerous, and an iris. Its ships have power limitations and devices that fail when the plot needs them to fail. A character may deliver a speech about subspace or radiation because, in the world of the story, that character has been asked to solve a practical problem.

The *Wormhole X-Treme!* crew exposes the artifice behind this solution. A line of scientific explanation must sound plausible, give another character something to react to, and carry the scene to the next decision. The words perform several jobs at once. They tell the audience that the problem has a shape, tell the cast which reactions are required, and provide just enough resistance to make a solution satisfying.

The parody does not prove that technical language is meaningless. In the better episodes, Carter's explanations help viewers understand a choice. If the gate cannot be opened from one side, that constraint creates suspense. If a device has enough energy for one attempt, the characters have to choose carefully. The science is invented; the consequences are dramatically real.

That distinction is the foundation of the comedy. Once an audience understands a show's grammar, it can enjoy jokes about the grammar without forgetting the stories the grammar makes possible. A gag about somebody announcing a discovery too conveniently is funny because we have watched the real characters struggle through enough inconveniences to recognize the shortcut.

Even the show's most extravagant effects rely on restrained acting at the point of contact. Teal'c's composure is essential. Carter's confidence is essential. Daniel's indignation is essential. Jack's refusal to let a pompous person remain pompous is essential. The impossible idea becomes watchable because a character responds to it in a way we recognize. The anniversary comedy exposes the mechanism and, by exposing it, pays tribute to the performers who make it work.

## The problem with a television program about itself

Self-parody carries a risk: the jokes can become little certificates of superior taste. A show may announce that its old formulas were foolish and congratulate itself for noticing, while continuing to use the formulas as before. That is an easy form of cynicism. If the characters and writers are all above the adventure, the viewer is left wondering who is meant to care.

*Wormhole X-Treme!* largely avoids that trap because its real story still involves Martin, a frightened survivor whose memories and loyalties have consequences. The show within the show is a delightfully incompetent mirror, but Martin himself is not only a punch line. His history keeps the premise anchored. The actual SG-1 still has a job to do. The program's secrecy, its enemies, and the risk of somebody being harmed do not stop existing because the crew has found a funny television set.

That is an important point about humor in *Stargate* generally. Jack can make a remark while someone is threatening to destroy Earth without the threat becoming unreal. The joke is a response to danger, not a declaration that danger has evaporated. Teal'c can take a human custom with perfect literalness without being reduced to an idiot; the scene is funny because his judgment is often more sound than that of the humans attempting to explain themselves. Daniel can be irritated because he cares about accuracy, and Carter can be impatient because the experiment has consequences. Comedy depends on character commitments that persist after the laugh.

The hundredth episode works because it knows the difference between making fun of a storytelling habit and making fun of the audience for caring about a story. Its most successful jokes are recognitions shared with the viewer. They say: we know what this kind of episode sometimes asks you to overlook. We know that you know. We are grateful that you came along anyway.

## Six more years and a much larger toy box

By the time of *200*, SG-1 has become a different program. The original quartet has changed; Jack is no longer present every week; Mitchell and Vala have joined the continuing adventure. The show has traveled to ancient cities, fought with increasingly powerful technology, and accumulated enough continuity to make its own history a dense comic resource. Its staff and audience have lived through cast transitions, genre conventions, and the strange experience of watching a series survive beyond the point at which most shows disappear.

Martin Lloyd returns with a new problem. His *Wormhole X-Treme!* show has failed on television, but a film adaptation is under consideration. He wants help from the actual SG-1. They are, after all, experts on the adventures he has been fictionalizing. General O'Neill has allowed the consultation, and an interruption to the team's planned gate mission means they cannot simply escape the meeting.

This framing device is excellent because it turns the old comic conflict into a literal assignment. The heroes must sit in a room and think about what would make entertaining television. Characters who ordinarily struggle to explain their dangerous work to bureaucrats are now asked to explain how they would make that work more exciting for viewers. Their suggestions are predictably inconsistent. The science-minded members are bothered by implausible consequences. Mitchell likes an action sequence. Vala likes an opportunity for herself. Daniel becomes exasperated with the whole exercise.

The great pleasure is that these are not arbitrary sketches pasted onto familiar actors. They are the characters' priorities given permission to roam beyond the continuity rules. The episode temporarily suspends the normal requirement that an event must actually happen in SG-1's world. That freedom allows a whole collection of possible Stargates to be displayed, each one reflecting a different creative temptation.

The resulting program resembles a meeting in which every ridiculous idea was not merely discussed but somehow filmed. That is not a flaw concealed from the viewer. It is the episode's structure.

## The Furlings and the pleasure of not knowing

One of *200*'s opening inventions finally reveals the Furlings, an alien race long named as one of the great civilizations but rarely explained. The imagined beings are cuddly, almost toy-like. Then the scene races toward disaster and the destruction of their world. It is an absurd way to satisfy a long-standing mystery.

The joke has two targets. One is the audience's understandable wish to see a name from the lore given a face. The other is the storytelling instinct to answer every question with an event bigger than the last. Why merely meet a mysterious people when you can reveal them, endanger them, and obliterate their planet inside one extravagant opening sequence?

But the sketch also protects something valuable about the original series. The Furlings were compelling partly because we did not know much about them. An unvisited corner of a fictional world can carry more imaginative charge than an explained one. A galactic history that has a missing piece feels like a history. The comedy shows how quickly that feeling might disappear if every gap is filled by a loud spectacle.

This is a sophisticated kind of fan service because it offers the desired answer and demonstrates why the answer might have been disappointing. The audience gets to laugh at the desire for closure while still enjoying the thrill of seeing a familiar reference. Nobody has to be ashamed of wanting to know more.

## Replacement actors, puppets, and a universe of versions

Elsewhere in *200*, the film production encounters a recognizable television crisis: an actor may not be available, so the fictional production considers replacing people, explaining disappearances, or turning the problem into an opportunity. The episode plays with younger and more aggressive versions of the familiar characters. A puppet sequence recreates the team's early adventures with an entirely different form of performance. The company even wanders through tributes and parodies of other familiar screen worlds.

The reference to *Farscape* is especially pointed because Ben Browder and Claudia Black had been central performers in that earlier science-fiction series before joining *Stargate*. In the fantasy sequence, Michael Shanks and Browder play against the casting association rather than merely reproducing their earlier roles. A joke that might sound like another random genre reference becomes, for an informed audience, a compact meditation on how actors carry remembered characters from one fictional universe to another.

The jokes about casting have a second layer. SG-1 has actually experienced the difficulty of keeping an ensemble together. Jonas replaced Daniel for a year. Mitchell entered when the old team had scattered. Vala brought a new comic energy without attempting to copy an earlier member. The production's mock discussion of how to replace a star is funny because there is a real history of actors, contracts, availability, and audience expectations behind it.

The show does not resolve those tensions by laughing at them. It does something gentler. It allows the people who have lived through them to acknowledge how artificial the supposedly natural continuity of television can be. The fictional characters may possess biographies spanning a decade, but their existence on screen depends on performers who have lives outside the story. *200* lets that fact enter the comedy for an hour.

The puppet sketch works for a related reason. Substitute tiny articulated figures for the performers and the familiar arrangement is still immediately recognizable. The gate, the uniforms, the gestures, the command rhythms, and the distribution of roles have become a kind of visual language. The audience can identify the team even when nothing about its material embodiment is realistic. In an odd way, the sillier the representation, the more clearly one sees the discipline of the original performances.

## Vala pitches a place for herself

The most revealing joke may belong to Vala. She proposes a fantasy resembling *The Wizard of Oz*, with herself trying to find a place among the people who travel together. Martin recognizes the borrowed story and refuses to treat it as original. But the emotional shape of the pitch is recognizably Vala's. A woman who spent years getting by on improvisation and theft wants to belong to a group that will keep her.

The joke lands in two directions. We recognize a famous story wearing Stargate costumes, and we recognize a genuine character desire inside the borrowed costumes. Vala's self-insertion is ridiculous because she is shameless about wanting the film to include her. It is touching because she is not wrong to want inclusion. At that point in her development, membership in SG-1 is more than an administrative status. It is a possible answer to the lifelong problem of who will stay when she has nothing worth stealing.

This is what the best of *200* does. It gives the characters permission to be silly without erasing the emotional truths that make their silliness specific. Carter's objections to bad scientific writing arise from a lifetime of treating technical decisions as consequential. Daniel's impatience grows from years of trying to make other people attend to what he is actually saying. Mitchell's excitement is a continuation of his belief that being on SG-1 is the best assignment in the Air Force. Teal'c's imagined detective persona is funny partly because Christopher Judge can make an entirely theatrical costume feel as composed as a warrior's uniform.

The sketches are not equally successful. Some depend so heavily on one fleeting reference that a newcomer may hear only a strange change of costume. The anthology format sacrifices the mounting pressure of a conventional episode. A fan who comes for an Ori battle or a hard choice may find the collection of gags indulgent. Those are legitimate limits. *200* is an anniversary gift, not a template for every week of drama.

Yet it remains recognizably *Stargate* because the jokes are attached to a world in which the characters have long been asked to carry impossible ideas with a straight face. For one hour, they get to set some of those ideas down and look at them from across the table.

## The show that could survive a joke

There is a general suspicion that comedy and sincerity cannot occupy the same space. If you admit that the costume looks funny or the plot device is convenient, perhaps you have forfeited the audience's belief in your story. Some series defend themselves by refusing any such admission. Others defend themselves by treating every earnest moment as a joke before the viewer can do it for them.

*SG-1* finds a better arrangement. It is serious about its characters' work and relaxed about the fact that it is entertainment. The Stargate can be a dangerous device in one episode and a deliciously awkward visual effect in another. An actor can describe the fall of an empire with conviction and then appear in a scene mocking the length of the exposition required to explain the empire. Neither experience cancels the other.

Consider how much trust this requires. The writers must trust that the audience can hold two pictures in mind. In one, Teal'c is a former First Prime carrying the burden of liberation. In the other, he is an actor in an absurd television sketch, or an imagined detective standing under theatrical lighting. A viewer who knows the first figure can enjoy the second without confusing them. The character has not lost dignity because the performer is allowed to play.

The hundredth episode makes the audience aware of the labor of television. The two-hundredth makes the audience aware of the accumulated conversation between a television series and those who have watched it. In the first, Martin is a person making fiction from a terrible reality. In the second, the fiction has developed a history of its own, with missed opportunities, forgotten mysteries, dramatic conventions, and beloved performers whom no imaginary recasting can entirely replace.

The result is not a declaration that SG-1's own stories were always foolish. It is an acknowledgment that loving a story can include knowing exactly where it creaks. When a friend tells a familiar tale for the twentieth time, the listener may smile at the predictable pause before the punch line. The smile does not prove the friendship is over. Often it proves how long the friendship has lasted.

## Back to the real gate

At the close of *200*, the team eventually has a mission to attempt. The actual Stargate, with all its elaborate rules and the very expensive work of making those rules visible on television, is waiting. Martin is still a producer trying to get a project made. The characters are still colleagues who must decide what to do when the next alarm sounds. An episode that spent its time inventing impossible versions of their adventures can return to the ordinary business of the real fictional universe.

That return is essential. If the story ended by announcing that none of the rest was worth believing, the joke would feel thin. Instead the characters walk back toward the gate, and the familiar routine acquires a little extra warmth. We have been invited to see the camera crew hiding behind the adventure. We have also been reminded why we wanted the adventure in the first place.

*Stargate* can make fun of itself because it is confident enough to be earnest when earnestness is called for. It can risk an entire episode of ridiculous ideas because most episodes are prepared to let a sacrifice hurt, a scientific discovery astonish, and a friendship survive an argument. Its self-parody is not the opposite of that seriousness. It is another use of the trust those stories have built.

A fictional producer stands beside a fake portal and wonders how to make the scene convincing. Somewhere else in the same fictional universe, a real team of fictional explorers is preparing to pass through a portal that looks almost exactly like it. The difference is made by ten years of remembered choices, laughter, losses, and return journeys.

That may be the finest joke of all. The door was always made for television. The places it took us still feel worth revisiting.
