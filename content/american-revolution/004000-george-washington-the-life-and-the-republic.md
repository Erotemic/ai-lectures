# George Washington: The Life, the War, and the Republic, 1732–1799

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

In May 1796, a young woman named Ona Judge escaped from the president's household in Philadelphia. She was enslaved in George and Martha Washington's household. While the household prepared to return to Virginia, Judge had made preparations of her own. She later described moving her belongings in advance, waiting until the family was at dinner, and leaving. Friends in Philadelphia's free Black community helped her toward safety. The president's household advertised a reward. Washington subsequently pursued her return, even while recognizing the political difficulty of forcibly seizing a Black woman in a northern community sympathetic to abolition. Judge resisted, established a family in New Hampshire, and remained free.

At the time, George Washington was nearing the end of his presidency. He had led an army that defeated a European empire. He had presided over the convention that designed the federal Constitution. He had become a symbol of national independence and republican restraint. Yet his domestic life depended upon holding other human beings in bondage. To encounter Washington through Ona Judge's escape is not to deny his public achievements. It is to see that his life contained choices and contradictions that cannot be erased by calling him either a hero or a hypocrite and proceeding no further.

Washington's story is particularly compelling because almost nothing about its outcome was inevitable. A young provincial officer helped precipitate a war and surrendered his own fort. A planter who wanted recognition within the British Empire became commander of a rebellion against it. A general who lost major battles nonetheless outlasted a formidable imperial army. A victor accustomed to armed command relinquished it to civilian authority. A man who wished to leave office helped define the presidency by serving and then departing. To understand him is to understand a republic still deciding what power was for.

## The boy who wanted a place in the world

George Washington was born in 1732 into Virginia's plantation society, not into poverty, but not at the summit of the colonial aristocracy. His father, Augustine Washington, owned land and enslaved laborers. When Augustine died in 1743, George was eleven. His older half-brother Lawrence became an important model and connection, and the wealthy Fairfax family offered opportunities that a less connected young Virginian would not have enjoyed. Washington did not follow the extended university education available to some elite colonial men. He educated himself in practical mathematics, surveying, written correspondence, and the conventions of polite society.

A surveyor's work demanded more than drawing lines. Roads were scarce. Land claims overlapped. A surveyor had to read hills, streams, distances, and documents; work outdoors for long periods; translate measurements into legally consequential boundaries; and cultivate the trust of people who expected his work to decide questions of wealth. Washington's early excursions into the Shenandoah Valley helped him develop his lifelong interest in western land. They also exposed a central feature of colonial expansion: a line on a survey could create an English legal property claim without making the people who already inhabited the territory disappear.

As a teenager, Washington copied rules of civility into a school exercise book. The rules were not his invention. They were established prescriptions of courtesy, restraint, and conduct. Their later fame has sometimes encouraged the belief that they reveal an innately composed child already destined for greatness. A better reading is that a socially ambitious young man practiced the behavior expected of gentlemen. He would spend decades cultivating dignity, formal bearing, and control of public emotion. His contemporaries also knew that he could become angry. The remarkable thing about his self-command was that it was work, not magic.

Washington stood tall, rode well, and learned to inhabit a room with an imposing physical presence. Yet the young man could be sensitive to rank. British regular officers often enjoyed precedence over colonial officers, and Washington would repeatedly feel the slight of being treated as a provincial subordinate. That sensitivity mattered. Before he rebelled against British rule, he sought honorable service within it. He wanted recognition from the empire long before he became famous for resisting the empire's authority.

His position in Virginia rested on human exploitation as well as personal effort. He inherited enslaved people as a child, acquired others, and grew up in a society where planters treated their labor as inheritable property. The same young surveyor who learned to measure territory lived in a world that converted land and human lives into wealth for owners. Both would become foundations of his adult identity.

## The officer whose first war went badly

In 1753 Virginia's lieutenant governor, Robert Dinwiddie, sent the twenty-one-year-old Washington west to warn French officers against advancing into the Ohio Country. The region was claimed by imperial officials in Britain and France, but it was not an unclaimed chessboard. Haudenosaunee diplomats and Native peoples of the Ohio Country pursued their own political and territorial interests, sometimes cooperating with Europeans and sometimes resisting them. Washington traveled with guides and Indigenous companions, including the leader known to the British as the Half King, Tanacharison. His account of the journey was printed and circulated, elevating him into a recognizable colonial figure.

The following year he returned with armed men. In May 1754 his force and Indigenous allies attacked a French party at what became known as Jumonville Glen. The circumstances and interpretation of the encounter were disputed from the beginning. The French regarded the killing of the officer Joseph Coulon de Jumonville as an outrage against an envoy; British accounts emphasized the dangers of armed French encroachment. Washington then built a makeshift position in a meadow and named it Fort Necessity. In July, amid rain, confusion, and overwhelming opposition, he surrendered. The document he signed contained disputed language about the earlier French officer's death, with the difficulty of translation playing a role in subsequent controversy.

This was an inauspicious beginning for a man later imagined as incapable of defeat. It also placed him close to the origins of the North American war that became part of the wider Seven Years' War. Washington did not single-handedly cause that global conflict, but his missions and decisions were part of the escalating imperial confrontation. They were events in which Native diplomacy, colonial ambition, and European claims collided.

In 1755 Washington accompanied General Edward Braddock on an expedition toward the French position at the forks of the Ohio. The expedition ended in disaster at the Battle of the Monongahela. Braddock was fatally wounded and his force suffered terrible casualties. Washington survived, helped rally men during the retreat, and acquired a reputation for courage under fire. Surviving a defeat, however, was not the same as possessing a settled theory of victory. For years he experienced the difficulties of frontier command: inadequate money, recruits who came and went, vulnerable settlements, political interference, and no easy way to turn tactical movement into reliable regional control.

He was impressed by British military professionalism, yet frustrated by the condescension with which some British officers treated colonial service. He sought the kind of king's commission that would place him securely within an imperial hierarchy and did not obtain it. In 1758, after further service, he resigned from the Virginia regiment. He had acquired reputation and experience but also hard knowledge of military failure, the scarcity of supplies, and the unreliability of distant governments. Those lessons would return when he commanded an army against Britain.

## Marriage, property, and grief at Mount Vernon

In January 1759 Washington married Martha Dandridge Custis, a wealthy widow with two surviving children from her first marriage. George and Martha built a long partnership at Mount Vernon, which Washington had managed and later inherited. Their marriage increased his wealth and social standing. Martha had already managed a substantial estate as a widow; she brought experience, family obligations, property interests, and an enslaved workforce whose labor would enrich the household. The couple had no children together. George became a stepfather to Martha's son Jacky and daughter Patsy, and later a guardian of grandchildren.

Mount Vernon was not simply the peaceful rural refuge depicted in later patriotic prints. It was an estate governed by accounts, overseers, crop decisions, trade debts, land transactions, and forced labor. Washington experimented with agricultural methods and eventually shifted much of his enterprise away from tobacco toward grains and diversified production. He pursued an efficient, profitable property and made decisions about the daily work of people who were not free to leave. The plantation's size and prosperity depended on the lives of enslaved men, women, and children, whose own marriages, grief, skills, and aspirations were not reducible to items in an owner's ledger.

The Washington household was also a household of real affection and loss. Patsy, Martha's daughter, experienced severe seizures and died at seventeen in 1773. Jacky died in 1781 after becoming ill during the Yorktown campaign. George and Martha later helped raise two of Jacky's children. Washington could be stern about young people's education and conduct, and he took family responsibility seriously. These details matter because the general who appears on horseback in public images also spent years worried about the health, prospects, and deaths of people close to him. Martha, for her part, repeatedly traveled to military winter quarters during the Revolutionary War. The home and the army were not separate worlds.

Washington also wanted land in the west. His interests in the Ohio Valley were longstanding and financial, not solely strategic. The Proclamation of 1763 and the imperial effort to restrain settler expansion collided with the expectations of men who believed wartime service and speculation should open western opportunity. To Native nations, those expectations threatened existing homelands. Washington's experience as a surveyor and proprietor meant he understood precisely how property claims could become political commitments.

## An imperial gentleman becomes a revolutionary

By the 1760s Washington was active in Virginia politics, serving in the House of Burgesses and becoming entangled in the imperial controversies that followed Britain's victory in 1763. His positions moved through a sequence recognizable across colonial elites: objection to parliamentary taxation, participation in commercial resistance, increasing distrust of British coercion, and ultimately acceptance that armed defense would be necessary. He did not begin as a prophet of independence. He had spent much of his life trying to succeed as a British American gentleman.

He attended the First Continental Congress in 1774 and returned in 1775, when violence at Lexington and Concord transformed the political emergency. Congress faced a question that could not be answered by drafting another petition: who would command the forces gathering around Boston? Massachusetts leaders had substantial local experience but selecting a Virginian connected the New England rebellion to a broader colonial coalition. Washington appeared in military uniform; the surviving evidence does not justify treating that fact as proof of some private scheme. What matters is that Congress selected him as commander in chief in June 1775, and he accepted without asking for a general's salary, while expecting reimbursement of expenses.

The appointment was political as much as military. The colonies had no established national government capable of taxing, provisioning, or disciplining a unified force. The men around Boston were organized in formations with distinct colonial allegiances and short terms of service. Washington had to forge an army while conducting a war whose political end remained unsettled. At first Congress could still claim it defended inherited British liberties. Independence would be declared more than a year after fighting began.

When Washington reached Cambridge in July 1775, he found enthusiasm mixed with shortages, uneven discipline, and the challenge of maintaining an army within sight of a formidable enemy. He worked to improve organization, reinforce defenses, and acquire gunpowder and artillery. The arrival of heavy guns hauled from Fort Ticonderoga under Henry Knox helped make the British position in Boston difficult to hold. In March 1776 British forces evacuated the city. It was a success, but it was not evidence that a loose colonial army could always defeat professionals. The fighting at New York would show otherwise.

## Losing New York and saving an army

New York in 1776 was a strategic trap as well as a political prize. Its harbor and waterways favored British sea power, and the city held loyalists as well as patriots. Britain could bring troops by ship, shift them along the coast, and compel the Americans to defend several possible points of attack. Washington attempted to defend the city and fought the Battle of Long Island, also called Brooklyn, in August. British forces outmaneuvered the Americans. Washington's army narrowly escaped destruction, withdrawing across the East River under cover of night and favorable conditions. The city itself was eventually lost.

A general who wins every confrontation might never face the question that defined Washington's command: which mattered more, a famous city or the army that could contest another year? He learned, sometimes painfully, to preserve the field force rather than offer the British the decisive destruction they sought. His campaigns were not a continuous series of clever retreats. There were mistakes, failed defenses, and moments when his judgments deserved criticism. He held a difficult position at Manhattan, suffered defeat at White Plains, and watched American forts and troops fall to the British. By December 1776 the army had retreated through New Jersey. Enlistments were expiring; the political movement had to wonder whether it could survive the winter.

Then came the crossing of the Delaware River and the surprise attack at Trenton on December 26. Washington's men struck German troops serving Britain, took prisoners, and then found further opportunity against the British near Princeton in early January 1777. These were not victories that by themselves won independence. They were victories at exactly the point when military credibility and the army's continued existence were in doubt. Soldiers reenlisted or joined; opponents reconsidered the prospect of an effortless suppression; supporters found grounds for endurance. Their significance lay in the timing and in the evidence that the Americans could still strike.

Washington's achievement here was not simply battlefield audacity. He had to use intelligence, coordinate exhausted troops in miserable weather, move guns and men across a dangerous river, and take a risk measured against the worse risk of disintegration. The iconic painting of a standing Washington in a boat is an artwork of national memory. The real campaign was a feat of logistics and command by thousands of people, including boatmen, artillerymen, experienced officers, and soldiers whose feet and clothing bore the burden.

## The army he could not afford to lose

For much of 1777 and 1778, Washington contended with the limits of what Congress and the states could provide. Defeat at Brandywine left Philadelphia vulnerable; the British occupied it. An American attack at Germantown did not succeed. At Saratoga, farther north, American commanders and soldiers won a victory of enormous diplomatic consequence, but Washington did not command that battle. The war's successes belonged to many people, and Washington was sometimes criticized in the resulting struggle over military reputation and appointments.

The army spent the winter of 1777 and 1778 at Valley Forge. Popular memory has sometimes reduced it to a single tableau of freezing barefoot soldiers, followed by a miraculous transformation. The harder story involves food procurement, transport, sickness, pay, morale, military administration, and political will. Supplies sometimes existed in the countryside but could not be acquired and delivered reliably by the governments responsible for sustaining the army. Washington pressured Congress and state officials, relied on officers who could organize purchasing and inspection, and learned to conduct a war whose endurance was tied to administrative capacity.

Friedrich Wilhelm von Steuben helped develop a standardized program of drill and instruction. His work improved common procedures, allowing soldiers from different states to maneuver together more reliably. Martha Washington came to winter headquarters, as she did at other points during the war. A general's headquarters was a working political center that had to receive officers, correspond with civilian authorities, manage visitors, and maintain the confidence of allies. Washington's position depended on a web of labor and trust, not on solitary virtue.

Disease posed a threat at least as substantial as enemy fire. Washington understood smallpox as a strategic danger and made inoculation an army-wide policy under controlled conditions in 1777. It was a risky operation in the medical world of the time, requiring careful timing because inoculated soldiers could become temporarily ill and contagious. But leaving an army vulnerable to epidemic could be fatal to the entire war effort. Here military leadership involved a decision over public health, logistics, and competing risks rather than a battlefield charge.

The transformation of Washington's army was not complete after one winter. The weakness of Congress's revenue system persisted. Soldiers protested pay and conditions. Units mutinied at various times; political leaders feared what might happen when men with weapons believed civilians had broken promises. Washington needed both an army strong enough to fight and an army disciplined enough not to become its own government.

## Allies, ambition, and the long road to Yorktown

The American Revolution became a global conflict. France entered into formal alliance with the United States in 1778 after the American victory at Saratoga made American survival appear more credible. French assistance included money, arms, military expertise, troops, and eventually decisive naval power. Spain later fought Britain as an ally of France, though not through the same alliance with the United States. Britain's worldwide commitments multiplied. It had to defend maritime commerce and imperial possessions as well as campaign in North America.

Washington's strategic problem changed. He had to coordinate with a great power whose commanders had their own instructions, honor, resources, and interests. France had not entered the war to create a perfectly egalitarian American democracy; weakening Britain was a central motive. American officers had to learn that foreign aid was neither charity nor complete submission to American priorities. Washington cultivated relations with French commanders and with the Marquis de Lafayette, who became both a younger military colleague and a personally important figure. Their relationship reveals a warmer side of a man often remembered as forbidding, though the letters are better evidence of affection than any invented private conversation.

The British moved much of their military effort toward the southern colonies, seeking to exploit loyalist support and the economic value of the region. The result was a war of maneuver, occupation, raids, civil conflict, and brutal local reprisals. Washington remained concerned with the principal British force around New York, while American generals such as Nathanael Greene fought in the South. Greene's strategy was especially notable for refusing to confuse holding a single battlefield with winning a campaign. British victories could consume resources and leave the countryside no easier to govern.

In 1781 Washington and the French commander Rochambeau exploited an opportunity that depended upon the movements of the French fleet under de Grasse. Their forces marched south while British commanders failed to prevent Cornwallis from becoming trapped at Yorktown, Virginia. The French navy's control of the entrance to the Chesapeake prevented a British rescue by sea. American and French armies pressed a siege on land. Cornwallis surrendered in October. Washington deserved credit for coordination and judgment, but it is historically misleading to narrate Yorktown as the personal triumph of one commander. French ships, French soldiers, American Continentals, militia, laborers, and the decisions and mistakes of British commanders were all necessary parts of the outcome.

One of the private costs of the campaign was Jacky Custis's death from illness. The war that gave Washington a place in the world's political history also took a member of his family. Yorktown did not immediately end fighting everywhere, but it dramatically weakened the prospect of a British military victory and strengthened support for peace negotiations.

## Giving power away

The months after Yorktown posed a constitutional danger that was not obvious from battlefield maps. The United States had armed men who had endured years of hardship and expected payment. Congress had insufficient taxing power and often lacked the funds to meet those obligations. At Newburgh in 1783, discontent among officers became an organized political crisis. Washington confronted proposals and rumors that risked turning military grievances into coercion against the civilian government. In a carefully staged address to his officers, he pleaded for patience and warned against action that could damage the cause for which they had fought. In the remembered episode, he put on spectacles to read and drew attention to the physical cost of service. The familiar story should be understood as part of a documented political intervention, not a proof that the officers were simply won over by one sentimental gesture.

In December 1783 Washington resigned his commission before Congress at Annapolis. This deserves attention precisely because military victors in other eras have not always behaved this way. Resignation mattered institutionally: the army belonged to the republic, and its commanding officer was returning authority to a civilian body. It also mattered personally. Washington had repeatedly expressed the wish to return home, and Mount Vernon represented a life and property he had entrusted to others through nearly eight years of campaigning.

It would be too neat to say the event proved he had no ambition. His career from surveying to military command shows that he had ambition in abundance. The significant achievement was that he could place the legitimacy of his office, and eventually his place in memory, above its perpetual possession. Washington's sensitivity to reputation could encourage restraint; his desire to be honored and his willingness to yield power were not necessarily opposites.

Retirement did not last as he expected. The Articles of Confederation left Congress unable to tax directly and weak at resolving many interstate and national problems. Wartime debts, western conflicts, trade disputes, and domestic unrest encouraged calls for a stronger national system. Washington supported reform and presided over the Constitutional Convention of 1787. He was not responsible for every clause of the document, and his presence did not erase deep divisions over representation, executive power, and slavery. It gave the convention a figure in whom many Americans were willing to place trust.

## What does the first president do?

In 1789 Washington entered the first presidency under the new Constitution. Today the office comes surrounded by two centuries of law, ritual, administrative practice, and expectations. Washington had far fewer precedents. Someone had to decide how an executive should communicate with Congress, choose advisers, fill offices, organize ceremonial visits, enforce federal law, and represent the nation abroad. Every decision risked becoming a precedent.

He accepted a title simple enough to avoid the grand forms proposed by some contemporaries: president of the United States. His inaugural address portrayed government as a public trust. In practice he had to govern a republic that already contained the seeds of party conflict. Alexander Hamilton sought a national financial structure strong enough to consolidate public credit and support federal power. Thomas Jefferson feared the social and constitutional consequences of centralized finance and close dependence on commercial elites. James Madison's views also shifted as he opposed parts of Hamilton's program. Washington wanted national cohesion but could not govern without choosing among opposed policies. Attempts to stand above faction became harder as his administration made decisions with winners and losers.

Hamilton's proposals brought the federal assumption of state debts, funding of obligations, and the creation of a national bank. Supporters argued that a reliable fiscal system was the basis of independence: a government that could not borrow, pay its bills, or collect revenue would remain vulnerable. Critics worried that financiers and speculators would gain excessive influence and that the Constitution did not clearly authorize every institution Hamilton proposed. Washington signed the bank legislation after considering arguments about constitutional authority. This was a presidential choice, not merely a ceremonial approval of other men's decisions.

Foreign affairs soon made neutrality a test of sovereignty. The French Revolution and the war between France and Britain forced Americans to confront conflicting loyalties. France had helped secure independence; Britain was a crucial trading partner and a powerful naval state. In 1793 Washington proclaimed the United States' intention to remain neutral in the European conflict. Defenders saw prudence for a country not yet strong enough to fight another great war. Opponents worried that neutrality neglected commitments and republican solidarity. The controversy demonstrated how a president's interpretation of treaties and executive responsibility could shape national policy.

In 1794 rebellion against the federal excise tax on whiskey brought armed resistance in western Pennsylvania. Washington responded by mobilizing militia forces and, in a striking demonstration of presidential authority, personally accompanied the expedition for part of its movement. The rebellion dissolved without a major pitched battle. The contrast with 1775 is instructive. Washington had helped lead resistance to a distant legislature's claims. Now he argued that taxes enacted under constitutional representation were laws to be enforced. His critics could ask whether federal force had become too ready a tool; his supporters could insist that a government unable to enforce its laws would repeat the Confederation's weakness.

The same decade brought violent conflict in the Old Northwest, where Indigenous nations resisted U.S. expansion. A major U.S. defeat under Arthur St. Clair in 1791 exposed the cost of overconfidence. In 1794 an army commanded by Anthony Wayne defeated a Native coalition at Fallen Timbers; the subsequent Treaty of Greenville ceded enormous lands under pressure. From Washington's administration one could describe these policies as securing the frontier. From the perspective of Native communities, they involved military invasion and loss of territory. His earlier interest in western property and his new executive responsibilities intersected in ways that permanently affected others' sovereignty.

Washington's government also signed the Fugitive Slave Act of 1793, which strengthened the ability of claimants to pursue people who had escaped slavery across state lines. Here the federal state built under a revolution for liberty became an instrument in the maintenance of bondage. The legislation belongs in the same account as banking, neutrality, and the whiskey tax because all were exercises of the power he helped define.

## The people in the president's household

Presidential ceremony often obscures who performed its labor. The household Washington maintained in New York and Philadelphia included people who were enslaved at Mount Vernon, as well as wage workers and family members. Pennsylvania law offered paths to freedom for enslaved people brought into the state and kept there long enough, subject to exceptions and contested interpretation. The Washington household made efforts to avoid the operation of those provisions for its enslaved laborers by rotating them out of Pennsylvania. This was not the accidental contradiction of a private plantation far away. It was an arrangement of labor and legal power within the executive household itself.

Ona Judge's escape in 1796 makes the conflict between public ideals and private coercion impossible to disguise. Her own later account allows us to see the president's house from another position: not as a stage for republican virtue, but as a place where a young woman had to plan departure secretly because she was legally treated as property. Washington tried to recover her; she refused. Her success depended on a network of free Black people and sympathetic contacts who acted independently of the president's authority. She was not made free by Washington's changing opinion of slavery. She freed herself from his control.

The contrast is not an argument that every word Washington ever said about republican government was insincere. It is an argument that a person's principles are best examined alongside the power he chose to exercise. Washington's views on slavery changed over his life. In correspondence he expressed a desire for lawful abolition. He never used his prestige to launch a sustained public campaign to abolish slavery during his presidency, and he continued to benefit from forced labor. Private disapproval, even genuine disapproval, did not cancel the system operating on his estate and in his household.

In 1799, 317 enslaved people lived at Mount Vernon. Washington personally owned 123; many others were held by the Custis estate under legal arrangements attached to Martha's first marriage, and some were rented. The will he wrote before his death provided that those he owned outright would be emancipated after Martha's death, with William Lee to be freed immediately and supported by an annuity. Martha arranged for the liberation of the people legally owned by George to take effect on January 1, 1801. The will did not free everyone at Mount Vernon. Families whose members were held under different claims faced separation and continuing captivity. An important act of manumission and a grave limitation can both be stated without pretending they cancel each other.

## The choice to leave

By 1796 Washington wanted to retire. His second term had been bruising. The Jay Treaty with Britain was bitterly contested; critics accused the administration of favoring Britain, while supporters emphasized the dangers of another war and the value of securing trade arrangements. Newspapers attacked leading figures with a sharpness that often surprises readers who imagine the early republic as an era of universal deference. Washington had opponents. He also had a sense that he had become a necessary rallying point for a political system that ought not to depend on one man indefinitely.

His Farewell Address, published in 1796, warned against destructive party division and the dangers of becoming entangled in European rivalries. It was the product of consultation and revision, with James Madison and especially Alexander Hamilton involved in its drafting over time; it should not be treated as spontaneous dictation from an isolated sage. Its force lay in the conjunction of words and action. Washington did not seek a third term. The election of 1796 produced a successor, John Adams, and the government passed from one elected president to another.

After retirement Washington returned to Mount Vernon and his agricultural affairs. In 1798, during renewed tension with France, he accepted an appointment as senior commander if war required it, though he did not take the field. He died at home on December 14, 1799, aged sixty-seven, after a brief illness. Contemporary medical treatments, including extensive bloodletting, may have worsened his condition. His death prompted national mourning and rapidly expanding commemoration. Myths clustered around his childhood and character; the cherry-tree story, for example, appeared in an early nineteenth-century biography rather than in trustworthy evidence from his youth.

What, then, should remain after the monuments are put aside? Washington was an ambitious officer shaped by British imperial society. He experienced defeat, made military errors, learned to work with difficult allies, and sustained an army through a war of endurance. He helped give republican civilian authority precedence over military command. He helped design the office of president by making contested decisions, not by occupying a position above politics. He supported federal power in finance and law while exercising that power against Indigenous resistance and in a system that protected slavery. He was a husband and stepfather who knew attachment and grief; he was also a slaveholder whose freedom to act depended upon the constrained lives of others.

Perhaps his most influential public lesson was that political power must be transferable. A revolutionary general surrendered his commission; a first president left office. Neither act made all Americans equally free. Both helped establish conditions under which later Americans could challenge the republic through its own institutions. Washington did not complete the nation's argument about liberty. He helped create the stage on which that argument would continue, while leaving some of its hardest questions unanswered in his own life.
