# The Republic They Did Not Agree On: Confederation, Constitution, and the Limits of Liberty

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

When the war ended, the Americans had achieved an astonishing political result: they had broken from one of the world's largest empires. Yet their new republic could still fail. Independence answered who would not govern them. It did not answer who would govern them now, how much power the government should possess, or what it meant for a population to be represented.

The founders often appear together in paintings, grouped beneath columns and flags as though they were the members of a single plan. The truth is more interesting. They disagreed about debt, taxation, commerce, the authority of state legislatures, the meaning of popular sovereignty, religion, military power, and the risks posed by a concentrated executive. Their disagreements were not decorative differences within an inevitable constitutional design. They were the substance of the founding crisis.

## The first United States

During the war, the states operated under a loose central authority. The Articles of Confederation had been adopted by Congress in 1777, but did not take full effect until ratification was completed in 1781. The Articles described a union of states, each retaining substantial sovereignty, and provided a Congress in which states rather than individual people were the basic voting units. The central government had no general power to levy taxes directly on individuals. It depended on requests to the states for money.

That structure was not simply a mistake made by people too inexperienced to invent a constitution. Many Americans had just rebelled against what they considered an overreaching imperial authority. They feared permanent armies, distant officials, and taxation imposed without local consent. The Articles protected state governments and limited central power for reasons that made sense to their authors.

But those protections carried costs. Congress had trouble paying war debts, negotiating coherent commercial policy, and responding when individual states adopted conflicting measures. Currency and credit problems lingered. Soldiers who had fought for independence expected compensation, while taxpayers resisted new burdens. State governments could issue their own rules on trade, money, and debt, sometimes placing neighboring states at odds. An effective union needed mechanisms for collective action; an excessively powerful union seemed to threaten the very liberty the war had defended.

One can understand the problem by imagining thirteen households that agree to maintain a shared bridge. Each promises to contribute materials when asked. But no joint body can actually assess and collect the contribution. Some households pay; some do not; each has reasons. The bridge deteriorates, and those who have contributed begin to resent the others. That analogy simplifies the real constitutional disputes, but it catches one concern of the Confederation's critics: consent to a common project does not automatically give the project workable institutions.

## What the Articles of Confederation could actually do

The Articles of Confederation deserve better than the simple statement that the first American government was too weak to survive. The Articles created a league in which states retained substantial sovereignty and Congress possessed important responsibilities for war, peace, diplomacy, and common affairs. It could conduct negotiations, make certain treaties, and manage matters such as western lands. Its weakness lay in how these powers depended on compliance. Congress could request money from states but lacked a dependable direct taxing power, and it had no modern executive apparatus for enforcing its policies across the country.

Imagine Congress promising pay to soldiers and repayment to creditors, but then needing state legislatures to decide whether to send the necessary funds. One state might face drought, another postwar disruption, and a third voters angry about taxation. Even if most legislators accepted the federal obligation in principle, they might hesitate to contribute while neighboring states paid less. The result was not merely a shortage of money. It was a persistent mismatch between national decisions and the machinery available to carry them out.

Some features of the Confederation reflected legitimate fears, not careless design. Americans had rebelled against what they regarded as distant, unaccountable authority. They had little reason immediately to trust a powerful consolidated government. The requirement that states agree on important matters was intended to protect their political independence. Amendment of the Articles required unanimous state consent, however, which made institutional repair especially difficult. A safeguard against overcentralization could become an obstacle to adaptation.

The Confederation also made durable achievements. Under its framework the United States concluded peace, made important western territorial decisions, and adopted the Northwest Ordinance in 1787. But even the ordinance's promise of territorial government and restrictions on slavery operated amid unresolved Native land rights and the pressure of settler expansion. The Articles did not create a failed nation with no accomplishments. They created an arrangement that increasingly frustrated people who believed a republic required national capacity equal to its responsibilities.

## Who pays for the revolution?

The new republic inherited wartime obligations. Officers and soldiers, merchants, foreign lenders, and holders of government securities expected payment. Inflation and depreciating currency had redistributed losses during the war. Some people held paper promises worth far less than their face value; others had purchased them cheaply in expectation of eventual redemption. A decision about national finance was also a decision about whose sacrifice would be recognized and whose claims would be honored.

Farmers and debtors faced pressures in several states as governments tried to establish finances and enforce contracts. In western Massachusetts, the protests remembered as Shays's Rebellion in 1786 and 1787 brought armed resistance against courts and debt enforcement. Participants did not all hold a single program, and historians debate the relative force of taxes, credit conditions, and broader political grievances. For nationalists seeking stronger federal institutions, however, the rebellion provided alarming evidence that the Confederation lacked adequate means to maintain order. Others saw harsh state taxation and unresponsive elites as the real dangers.

The controversy exposes a vital tension. The new political vocabulary celebrated the people as the source of authority. But who were the people when a debtor resisted a court, or a crowd prevented legal proceedings? Was popular action outside regular government a defense of liberty, like the resistance of 1774, or a threat to republican law? A revolution had legitimated resistance to established authority. The republic now needed to decide when resistance should end.

## Philadelphia, 1787

In May 1787 delegates began gathering in Philadelphia to revise the national frame of government. What emerged was a plan for a new Constitution, not merely a corrected paragraph in the Articles. The delegates met in secrecy, debated proposals, and reached compromises that were often contingent and bitter.

A central issue was representation. The Virginia Plan gave much more weight to population, alarming smaller states. Other delegates pressed to preserve equality among states. The resulting Congress divided representation between a House apportioned by population and a Senate in which each state received equal votes. This familiar arrangement was not self-evident. It was a bargain among states with unequal populations and different visions of what the union represented.

The delegates also divided powers among a legislature, executive, and judiciary, gave the federal government taxing powers, and wrote mechanisms for lawmaking and enforcement. But the Constitution was not a machine that guaranteed harmony. Its checks and balances reflected apprehensions about both legislative domination and concentrated executive authority. The framers disagreed about how much popular election, elite selection, state influence, and central power were needed to keep a republic from collapsing.

The deepest moral fracture concerned slavery. The convention's text avoided the word in many of the relevant clauses while nonetheless protecting slavery through its compromises. Enslaved people were counted at three-fifths for purposes of congressional representation and certain taxation calculations. This increased the political influence of slaveholding states without granting the people being counted any corresponding political rights. Congress was barred from prohibiting the international importation of enslaved people before 1808. The Constitution also required the return of people held to service or labor who escaped to another state.

These arrangements did not simply leave slavery to a harmless future debate. They helped structure the balance of national power for decades. The framers produced a more capable national government at the cost of preserving and protecting a system incompatible with the Declaration's claim to human equality. Both truths belong in the founding story.

## Whose freedom, whose land?

The Revolution and founding also transformed Indigenous political realities. The new United States claimed western territory through treaties with European powers and began asserting jurisdiction across regions where Native nations lived, governed themselves, and negotiated as sovereign communities. The Northwest Ordinance of 1787 laid out a process for territorial government and admission of new states, prohibited slavery in the Northwest Territory, and announced legal protections. Yet the territory was not vacant. The plan for orderly settlement existed beside mounting pressures on Indigenous land, diplomacy, and independence.

For enslaved Black Americans, the period offered both openings and severe limits. Northern states began gradual emancipation measures, while others maintained slavery. Free Black communities expanded their religious, mutual-aid, and political organizing. Enslaved people petitioned and sued, seeking to use the language of natural rights to challenge their bondage. Yet in much of the South, slavery became more deeply entrenched. Emancipation in some jurisdictions did not create a single national freedom settlement.

Women had helped sustain the revolutionary cause but were largely excluded from voting and office under the new political arrangements. The ideal sometimes called republican motherhood assigned women an important role in educating virtuous citizens, while leaving the formal political rights of citizens overwhelmingly to men. The contradiction could be politically productive: people denied participation could point to the republic's own arguments for consent and equality. But a promise is not the same as its fulfillment.

## Why a stronger republic also frightened republicans

The Constitution's design can seem familiar enough to be inevitable: House and Senate, president and courts, elections and amendments. For people meeting in 1787, every element was a political choice. How directly should ordinary voters influence government? Would a legislature chosen across a large country understand local conditions? Would an executive with responsibility for enforcing laws begin to resemble a monarch? Could judges interpret the Constitution without becoming a separate source of uncontrolled political power?

James Madison and other advocates argued that a larger republic could make it harder for one faction to dominate everybody else. The Federalist essays developed the argument that representation and an extended political sphere could filter passions and create more durable government. Opponents replied that distance could make representatives less accountable and that powerful federal institutions might be captured by wealthy or well-connected interests. Some critics were not enemies of the Revolution or believers that state governments were always just. They were anxious that the new government might recreate the kind of inaccessible authority the revolutionaries had challenged.

The method of presidential selection reflected the same mistrust of simple formulas. The Electoral College was a compromise among different ways of choosing the executive, including legislative selection and direct popular participation. State legislatures initially had wide discretion in allocating electors. Enslaved populations could increase slave states' political weight through the Constitution's population calculations without those people gaining votes. The outcome cannot be explained simply as a clever neutral device for a large country; it was a product of specific bargains among political communities with sharply unequal definitions of membership.

The Bill of Rights answered genuine objections about federal power, but its initial scope had limits. The first ten amendments primarily constrained the new federal government rather than imposing the full modern set of constitutional protections upon the states. State religious establishments, franchise restrictions, and local legal practices persisted. The protections later associated with the Bill of Rights were shaped by constitutional argument and later amendments, especially after the Civil War. To say the founding generation adopted a Bill of Rights is not to say it settled every individual's civil status.

Ratification was therefore another stage of the Revolution's argument over authority, not a ceremony that finally ended politics. It expanded federal capacity, created checks within national institutions, and established new possibilities for collective action. It also preserved compromises that allowed slavery and territorial expansion to shape the country's future. What became a revered framework had been created by fallible participants who disagreed about whether it would succeed.

## Ratification was a public contest

The 1787 document did not become law merely because delegates signed it. State ratifying conventions debated whether the proposed federal government would protect or endanger liberty. Advocates of the Constitution, later commonly called Federalists, argued that a stronger national system could preserve union, manage collective problems, and restrain factions through a larger republic. Opponents warned about consolidated power, inadequate representation, insufficient protections for individual rights, and the dangers of a distant political class.

The essays known as *The Federalist*, principally written by Alexander Hamilton, James Madison, and John Jay, remain famous. But they were interventions in a political campaign, not neutral instructions for interpreting a completed constitution. Anti-Federalist writings also deserve to be read as serious constitutional arguments. Ratification depended on persuasion, local interests, and promises that the new government would add specific protections for rights.

The Constitution entered into operation in 1789. George Washington became the first president. The first Congress debated amendments, and ten were ratified in 1791 as the Bill of Rights. Those amendments restricted certain federal actions and expressed protections that have since been argued over, interpreted, and expanded. They were not a comprehensive settlement of citizenship or equality. They did not abolish slavery or recognize the political sovereignty of Indigenous nations.

## Was the Revolution completed?

The question contains a trap. If we define a successful revolution as one that fully realizes its own universal principles, the American case remained unfinished. But if we say nothing changed because severe exclusions persisted, we ignore the real creation of an independent republic, written constitutional institutions, new civic practices, and a political vocabulary that critics of inequality repeatedly used against the powerful.

The better approach is to distinguish achievement from completion. The United States secured independence and built a government that survived grave early disagreements. At the same time, it preserved slavery, incorporated territorial expansion into its national future, and maintained exclusions from political power. The founding created possibilities and barriers together.

This also helps explain why the American Revolution and the later Civil War belong to the same long history without being the same event. The founding generation fought over liberty, consent, property, and federal power while declining to resolve the status of millions of people who lived under slavery. Subsequent generations inherited both the Declaration's language and a constitutional structure that sustained slaveholding political influence. Americans would fight again over the meaning of union and the meaning of freedom, this time against one another in a war of catastrophic scale.

The most useful way to remember the founding is not as a finished monument. It was a series of consequential choices made under pressure by people who disagreed, whose achievements were real, and whose limits became the starting conditions for struggles they could not control.
