# Whose Revolution, Whose Freedom? Loyalists, Black Americans, Women, and Indigenous Nations

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

The American Revolution was both a war for independence and a collection of overlapping struggles that did not share the same meaning of freedom. It created a republic, but it also split families, displaced peoples, and opened paths to liberty for some who were excluded from the republic's founding political settlement. To understand these experiences is not to append a few missing names to the biographies of presidents and generals. It is to recognize that the same event could be a revolution, a civil war, a struggle for emancipation, or a catastrophe of territorial dispossession depending on where a person stood.

One of the best ways to enter this history is through the things people needed to decide. A merchant in New York had to decide whether to follow the Crown or the Continental Congress. An enslaved man in Virginia had to decide whether a British promise of freedom justified a dangerous flight. A woman in Massachusetts had to decide how to sustain a household and whether revolutionary claims of equality extended to her own life. A Haudenosaunee diplomat had to decide whether either side could be trusted to respect a nation's land. There was no single revolutionary choice that these people all faced.

## The neighbor who remained loyal to the king

Patriot narratives often treat a loyalist as a person who simply failed to understand the future. But in the 1770s the future was not yet knowable. Britain commanded an enormous empire, a powerful navy, and markets vital to colonial commerce. Many colonists believed grievances could be corrected within the existing imperial constitution. Some feared that protest crowds and committees were less accountable than the government they opposed. Some held religious commitments against rebellion. Some had personal ties to British institutions, official appointments, family members in Britain, or trading partnerships that would be ruined by a break.

A loyalist might be a prosperous official, but loyalty was not reserved for wealthy elites. The colonies were internally divided along lines of religion, ethnicity, class, local rivalry, and prior conflicts. Some tenant farmers supported the king against local landlords who were patriots. Others sided with patriots against powerful loyalist neighbors. In New York, the Hudson Valley, the Carolinas, and elsewhere, a person's decision could carry consequences that extended far beyond attendance at a political meeting. Neighbors organized militias. Houses were searched, farms seized, people assaulted, and families uprooted. Some patriot authorities enacted tests of loyalty, penalties, and confiscations. The war could become intimate and vindictive.

Consider how the language of legitimacy worked on both sides. Patriots could say that Parliament had violated fundamental constitutional rights and that a government ruling without consent had forfeited obedience. Loyalists could answer that armed rebellion itself destroyed lawful government, endangered property, and placed power in the hands of self-appointed committees. Neither claim automatically settled the question of whether the speaker treated a neighbor decently. In some counties a person could be coerced by representatives of both sides, sometimes in rapid succession.

When the British occupied New York City, it became a refuge and administrative center for loyalists and for many people seeking British protection. It also became a city whose population depended on military supply, whose housing and food markets suffered wartime strains, and whose future remained uncertain. At the close of the war, large numbers of loyalists left the new United States for British North America, Britain, and the Caribbean. Exile did not mean immediate prosperity. People had to petition for compensation, adapt to unfamiliar climates and communities, and live with the knowledge that their relatives might remain on the other side of a new border.

The consequence is important for how we understand independence. The Revolution did not simply unite a previously divided continent into a new nation. It also produced expatriates, dispossession, and conflicting memories. A household that celebrated 1783 as liberation might have lived next to one that experienced it as defeat.

## A war for liberty that enslaved people could use

Enslaved Americans did not need white patriots to teach them that freedom mattered. They had resisted and negotiated slavery in many ways before the Revolution, from flight and legal petitions to creating family and community institutions under coercion. But the war produced new opportunities because armed opponents needed labor, intelligence, transportation, and soldiers. The British offer of freedom to people enslaved by rebels was conditional and military in purpose. Lord Dunmore's proclamation in Virginia in 1775 was not a universal abolition decree. It offered liberation to some people enslaved by opponents who would join British forces. For those who could reach British lines, however, the opportunity could be life-changing.

Thousands of people fled plantations and households in the turmoil of war. Some reached British protection and worked as soldiers, guides, laborers, sailors, or workers attached to camps. Others endured appalling disease and hardship, were re-enslaved, or suffered after British troops moved away. The British Empire continued to maintain and defend slavery in other parts of its dominions; it did not become an abolitionist state by issuing tactical promises in North America. The important agency was the decision of the people who escaped. They confronted danger because they were making a claim to their own lives.

Other Black Americans served on the patriot side. Free men and some enslaved men fought in Continental units or local militias; in some circumstances military service was connected to promises of manumission, but implementation varied. Black military labor extended beyond combat. The ideological landscape was therefore more complex than assigning Black freedom to one flag and slavery to the other. The new United States fought a war under a Declaration of human equality while many of its most influential leaders continued to enslave people. Britain fought the rebellion while also using emancipation promises selectively to weaken its enemies.

At the end of the war the lives of Black loyalists depended in part on promises made by British officers and on what American slaveholders insisted was their property. The register historically titled the Book of Negroes recorded around three thousand Black people embarking from New York for British North America in 1783. It contains names, ages, descriptions, and fragments of personal histories: who had escaped from whom, where a person had worked, whether freedom was certified. The register is an official record, not a complete account of every Black person's experience. Some were denied passage; some did not receive the promised security; others reached Nova Scotia and confronted poverty, discrimination, and barriers to land ownership. Their names in the record nevertheless allow us to see individuals navigating the settlement of an international war.

Consider the logic of their choices. For a person who had escaped enslavement and lived under British military protection, a patriot victory could bring the threat of forced return to an owner. Departing the continent was not an expression of love for monarchy. It might be a necessary step to preserve the freedom already seized. One can understand the Black loyalist exodus without treating enslaved people as decorative participants in a contest among white political leaders.

## Phillis Wheatley and the vocabulary of independence

There is another type of evidence from the revolutionary years: written work by people excluded from formal political power. Phillis Wheatley was brought from West Africa to Boston as an enslaved child and became an internationally recognized poet. Her published poems demonstrated learning, formal literary skill, religious conviction, and a commanding use of the intellectual language of her age. Her achievement was recognized in a society that continued to doubt or deny the intellectual equality of African-descended people.

In October 1775 she sent George Washington a poem and a letter praising his appointment to command the American forces. Washington later replied appreciatively, complimenting her talent and inviting her to visit his headquarters if she came to Cambridge. We should not invent a conversation between them or assume that an invitation establishes a meeting. The exchange is striking enough as documented: a Black woman whose life had been constrained by slavery addressed a famous slaveholding commander as a public poet, and the commander replied as a reader of her work. Both used the elevated language of the age. Their positions in society were radically unequal.

Wheatley's career raises a larger question about the Declaration. Political language does not remain entirely under the control of those who first put it into a famous document. Once equality and natural rights are publicly invoked as reasons to overthrow government, excluded people can ask whether the reasoning applies to them. The answer is not always yes in law or practice. The possibility of making the demand has political power nonetheless. Black petitioners and writers were not merely waiting for the republic to notice them; they actively challenged it in its own terms.

## Women's labor and women's claims

It was impossible to sustain wartime society without women's labor, but political narratives have often classified that labor as background. Women operated farms and shops while men were away, organized consumer boycotts, produced supplies, cared for sick or wounded soldiers, managed correspondence and credit, and sometimes carried information across military lines. The work was difficult, variable, and unequal. A prosperous woman could direct a household with servants; an enslaved woman could be compelled to perform work for owners while also trying to protect her own family. A soldier's wife following a regiment faced risks distinct from those of an elite woman sending money or garments to an army.

Martha Washington's visits to winter headquarters are one documented example of the many functions women performed around military camps. They could provide domestic stability, coordinate hospitality, assist in correspondence, and sustain networks that linked officers to civilian society. Women who followed armies as laundresses, cooks, nurses, companions, or wage workers were not ornamental figures. Their presence often made encampments function. Yet their contributions did not translate automatically into political enfranchisement when independence was won.

In March 1776 Abigail Adams wrote to her husband John, then in Congress, urging him to remember women when making new laws. The phrase has become famous, but the letter does not need to be turned into a modern speech to be revealing. It applied the revolutionary moment to unequal power within marriage and law. John Adams replied with skepticism and humor, exposing the gap between political arguments about consent and the gender hierarchies many revolutionaries still considered ordinary. The exchange demonstrates that criticism of women's exclusion was present within revolutionary households, not invented by historians looking back.

After the war some states and communities expanded educational opportunities for women, and republicans increasingly argued that mothers had a duty to cultivate virtuous citizens. This could enlarge the public importance attributed to women's education while reinforcing the claim that their proper political function was indirect. New Jersey's early voting provisions temporarily allowed some property-qualified women to vote under certain interpretations and practices; the state later restricted the franchise to white men in 1807. This history resists a simple trajectory from old oppression to steadily growing rights.

## Indigenous nations and the war on their homelands

The Revolution was devastating in regions where colonial expansion and Indigenous sovereignty collided. Native nations were not a single political actor, and their decisions did not fall neatly into the patriot-versus-loyalist labels used by the coastal colonies. The Haudenosaunee Confederacy faced a painful division: many Mohawks, Senecas, Cayugas, and Onondagas supported the British side, while many Oneidas and some Tuscaroras supported the Americans. These descriptions are necessarily broad; different leaders and communities made different choices over time. What matters is that the war strained relations within a long-standing political confederacy.

Joseph Brant, a prominent Mohawk leader, allied with British power while pursuing the security and political future of his own people. Other Native leaders judged the Americans differently. To a colonial patriot, the American cause meant freedom from imperial interference; to a Native diplomat, an American victory could mean more settlers, more surveys, and fewer restraints on land hunger. The British were hardly reliable protectors of Native territory either. Indigenous leaders negotiated amid difficult alternatives, often knowing that European officers might promise support they could not or would not provide.

In 1779 the United States launched the Sullivan-Clinton campaign against Haudenosaunee communities aligned with the British. American forces destroyed towns, crops, and food stores across parts of present-day New York and Pennsylvania. This was not a minor frontier raid: it was a systematic attempt to break the capacity of communities to support the British war effort, with grave consequences for families facing winter displacement and hunger. American military accounts often describe the campaign in strategic language. The inhabitants experienced attacks on homes and agricultural landscapes that had taken generations to build.

The peace of 1783 did not invite those nations to sit as equal participants at the table where Britain recognized American independence. The Treaty of Paris used European diplomatic claims to define boundaries across regions inhabited and governed by peoples who had not agreed to be transferred. The new republic then pursued treaties, purchases, threats, and military campaigns to convert paper borders into settlements. For many Indigenous nations, American independence opened another prolonged struggle rather than closing a war for liberty.

## Testing the meaning of equality in a courtroom

A revolution can create principles whose legal consequences were not fully intended by the people who proclaimed them. In Massachusetts, the constitution adopted in 1780 declared principles of liberty and equality. Enslaved people and their advocates tested what those principles meant. Elizabeth Freeman, known earlier as Mum Bett, successfully sued for freedom in 1781. The connected cases involving Quock Walker, culminating in judicial decisions in the early 1780s, led Massachusetts courts to treat slavery as incompatible with the state constitution.

These legal changes did not mean New England was free of racism, that all Northern states abolished slavery immediately, or that equal citizenship followed. They do show a direct route by which people in bondage and their supporters turned abstract rights into an actionable legal claim. In Virginia and other slaveholding regions, political power and property interests blocked such results. The Revolution therefore produced different and sometimes contradictory legal outcomes within the same new union.

This is why the word freedom needs an object. Freedom from Parliament's taxation did not necessarily mean freedom from a slaveholder. Freedom to acquire western land could depend on denying Indigenous control of that land. Freedom to organize a local committee could coincide with pressure against neighbors who disagreed. And the same language of consent that justified independence could be used by women and Black Americans to challenge systems the founders left intact.

## A revolution with more than one ending

The Revolutionary War's conventional ending is the Treaty of Paris in 1783. For a Continental officer, that date might mark the long-awaited prospect of home and wages. For a loyalist family, it might inaugurate exile. For Black people evacuated from New York, it might begin a precarious new life across the border. For a Native nation facing expanding American settlement, it might not seem an end at all. For an enslaved person still held in South Carolina or Virginia, the Declaration's promises remained unfulfilled.

The purpose of studying these differences is not to replace the story of independence with a catalog of disappointments. Independence happened. The new republic endured and inspired arguments about representative government far beyond North America. But the Revolution's influence came partly from how many different people argued over the claims made in its name. The participants did not possess a common definition of liberty, and the winners did not settle its meaning for all time.

A historically adequate account must hold all those facts together. We can admire the political invention of independence, understand loyalist fears, respect the agency of enslaved people who risked flight, take women's claims seriously, and recognize Indigenous sovereignty without pretending every conflict was morally equivalent. The Revolution created a government. It also created a language, a set of memories, and unresolved conflicts that would keep changing what Americans believed that government owed to the people living under it.
