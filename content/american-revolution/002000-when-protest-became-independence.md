# When Protest Became Independence: Lexington, Philadelphia, and the Decision of 1776

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

At dawn on April 19, 1775, men with muskets gathered on the common at Lexington, Massachusetts. British regulars were moving from Boston toward Concord, where they intended to seize military stores. Riders and alarm networks had carried news of their approach. The militia assembling at Lexington was small. The British column was large, disciplined, and following orders in what its officers considered British territory. When shots were fired, several militiamen fell. Historians have not established beyond dispute who fired first.

The ambiguity of that first shot became part of the struggle over legitimacy. If British soldiers had attacked peaceable inhabitants, the patriots were defending their rights. If armed colonists had resisted lawful troops, the government was suppressing rebellion. Each interpretation asked listeners to decide who possessed lawful authority. People who had been arguing about sovereignty now found themselves having to fight about it.

The day did not end at Lexington. At Concord, militia resistance sharpened. As the regulars retreated toward Boston, colonial forces attacked along roads, walls, and fields. Both sides suffered casualties. By evening, British troops were back in Boston, and thousands of provincial militia were gathering around the town. The confrontation had transformed an imperial protest into a war, but it had not yet created a consensus for independence.

## A war whose objective was not settled

The Second Continental Congress assembled in Philadelphia in May 1775. Delegates faced an immediate contradiction. They were not a sovereign national government as we now understand the term, yet armed men were mobilizing in the colonies, and coordination required some authority. Congress adopted the forces besieging Boston as a Continental Army and selected George Washington as commander in chief. His appointment was political as well as military: a Virginian command helped make the struggle in Massachusetts look like a common continental cause.

Washington inherited not a ready-made national army but a collection of colonial forces with different enlistments, officers, supplies, and expectations. The army depended on provincial cooperation and the willingness of men to keep serving. In this world, the army's greatest problem was often not courage on the battlefield. It was how to stay an army at all: how to feed soldiers, pay them, equip them, prevent disease, and preserve the trust of communities required to sustain them.

Before Washington arrived in July, fighting had already intensified. In June, provincial forces fortified high ground across the Charles River from Boston. British troops attacked the position in the battle usually called Bunker Hill, though much of the combat centered on Breed's Hill. They eventually took the American works, but with heavy casualties. Patriot forces had been forced off the ground, yet the outcome made a simple British military solution look less certain.

Congress still tried to petition George the Third. Its Olive Branch Petition, adopted in July 1775, professed loyalty and asked for reconciliation. At roughly the same time Congress justified taking up arms. These were not necessarily examples of hypocrisy. A divided political movement could be prepared to fight while still seeking an agreement that preserved allegiance to the Crown. Reconciliation was becoming less plausible with each month, but the delegates had not entered the war already agreed on a declaration of independence.

The king declared the colonies in rebellion. Britain prepared a larger military response. The burning of Falmouth in Maine and the destruction of Norfolk, Virginia, in fighting around the turn of the year helped persuade more colonists that the imperial government was willing to use overwhelming force against its own subjects. News did political work as well as military work. It changed what people considered possible to trust.

## How a road in Massachusetts became a war

In April 1775 British authorities in Massachusetts moved to seize military stores that colonists had accumulated at Concord. Militia and local communication networks responded quickly. At Lexington, British regulars encountered militia on the common, and shots were fired. The surviving evidence does not establish with certainty who fired first. The larger movement continued to Concord, where armed conflict at the North Bridge gave the confrontation a second focal point. As the British troops withdrew toward Boston, militiamen fought along their route. Roads, walls, bends, and houses turned a military march into a series of encounters in which the retreating regulars were vulnerable.

The battle was shocking because a constitutional protest had become an exchange of lethal force between subjects of the same king. Thousands of armed New England men gathered around Boston and began a siege. The colonial forces had enthusiasm and local knowledge, but their organization, powder supply, and command arrangements were uneven. The British had professional troops and naval mobility, but controlling the countryside outside Boston was now much harder than occupying its principal town.

In June the British attacked fortified colonial positions on the Charlestown peninsula. The engagement called the Battle of Bunker Hill was fought substantially on nearby Breed's Hill. The British carried the ground, but at heavy cost. A British tactical victory demonstrated how expensive a direct assault on defended positions could be, while the colonial retreat showed the limits of an improvised army under intense pressure. Both sides learned that this would not be a short police operation.

Even then, independence was not the only conceivable outcome. Congress sent the Olive Branch Petition to George III in 1775 while also preparing for continued war. Some delegates wanted reconciliation, some considered it increasingly impossible, and many communities were divided. A wider military experiment in Canada failed, reminding Americans that anti-British sentiment in the thirteen colonies did not automatically extend to the inhabitants of Quebec. Colonial leaders had inherited assumptions about continental expansion that could clash with the wishes of other North Americans.

George Washington's selection as commander in chief connected New England's armed resistance to the rest of the colonies. But a Virginian at the head of the army did not instantly create a national army with a national treasury. The Second Continental Congress possessed remarkable political authority in practice and uncertain constitutional status. Congress could call for soldiers, issue paper promises, conduct diplomacy, and coordinate resistance, yet it depended heavily on state and local bodies for men, money, and supplies. Independence would require not only a declaration of political principles, but institutions that could survive the war those principles had helped bring about.

## The revolution of printed arguments

In January 1776 Thomas Paine published *Common Sense*. Its influence is difficult to measure precisely, but the pamphlet spoke in a register distinct from much earlier constitutional argument. Rather than ask whether Parliament had exceeded an established right, it asked what justification there could be for hereditary monarchy and permanent political dependence on an island across the ocean. Paine argued that America should be able to choose its own government, seek allies, and shape its own commercial future.

A pamphlet is not a command, and the people who read it were not blank slates. Local committees, provincial congresses, town debates, pulpits, and newspapers had been changing opinions for years. But *Common Sense* helped remove the awkwardness of saying the word independence. It suggested that a complete break could be a reasonable conclusion, not a reckless fantasy.

The pressure of war also transformed loyalism. Some people remained loyal to the king because they valued imperial stability, feared mob rule, depended on British institutions, or believed the Crown offered their communities greater safety. Others simply wanted to avoid a conflict that threatened family, land, and livelihood. Allegiance did not follow a simple division of wealthy loyalists and poor patriots, or any single regional pattern. Both camps contained people with different interests. Remaining neutral could itself be dangerous as local committees demanded declarations, oaths, supplies, and compliance.

The Revolution was therefore not only a war between an island and a continent. It was also a civil struggle within North America. Neighbors disputed sovereignty and could inform on, punish, or dispossess one another. A family's political choice might determine whether its members could remain in their home after the war. The eventual triumph of the patriots can obscure how uncertain the balance of loyalty looked while events were unfolding.

## Freedom had more than one meaning

For enslaved people, the growing war opened a separate and urgent calculation. What route, if any, might lead to liberty? In November 1775, Lord Dunmore, the royal governor of Virginia, promised freedom under specified conditions to enslaved people held by rebels who joined British service. This was a military policy, not a declaration that Britain was abolishing slavery throughout its empire. But enslaved people could act upon it. Some fled to British lines and took extraordinary risks to seek freedom. Many suffered exposure, disease, displacement, or renewed bondage.

Black people also fought for the patriot cause, including free men and men who secured service arrangements with promises of freedom. Enslaved people who did not carry arms could still exploit wartime dislocation, negotiate, petition, flee, or preserve families under conditions that were often terrifying. It is misleading to describe them merely as silent witnesses to a white constitutional argument. Their pursuit of liberty had its own purposes and sometimes put them at odds with both patriot slaveholders and British officers.

Indigenous nations had no reason to treat the British colonial dispute as the sole measure of their interests. Some sought neutrality; some chose alliances; some were divided, often under severe pressure. Settlers' hunger for land was not suspended by the language of natural rights. An independent colonial state might be more threatening to western homelands than a distant imperial government that sometimes restrained expansion. This fear was not irrational. The treaty that eventually recognized the United States would transfer British claims to land without treating the Indigenous peoples living there as sovereign parties to that bargain.

Women ran farms and workshops, followed armies, supplied soldiers, supported boycotts, and argued about politics. Abigail Adams urged her husband John in 1776 to remember women when establishing new laws; her appeal made plain that a new government could reproduce old hierarchies. The revolution widened the field of political speech without immediately widening the legal electorate to everyone who took part.

## The declaration and the decision

The decisive political motion came from Richard Henry Lee, a Virginia delegate, on June 7, 1776, calling for independent states. Congress postponed a final decision while delegates consulted their colonies and a committee prepared a public explanation. The committee included Thomas Jefferson, John Adams, Benjamin Franklin, Roger Sherman, and Robert Livingston. Jefferson composed a draft; others edited it, and Congress edited it further.

On July 2 Congress approved the independence resolution. On July 4 it approved the text that became the Declaration of Independence. The distinction matters. Independence was a political decision before it was a famous parchment. The Declaration was an explanation addressed to a broad audience: other governments, potential allies, colonists at home, and posterity.

It began with a proposition about political legitimacy, asserted rights that no king was supposed to bestow, and argued that government derived authority from consent. It then presented grievances against George the Third as a case that allegiance had been forfeited. Its language about equality became a resource for generations who challenged exclusion from the very republic it helped announce.

But its authors did not abolish slavery. Jefferson's draft had contained a grievance about the slave trade that Congress removed, and the final Declaration did not grant freedom to enslaved people. The principle that human beings were created equal sat beside systems of coercion preserved in most of the new states. It would take abolitionists, Black petitioners, enslaved resisters, later constitutional struggles, and a civil war to force further meanings from the words of 1776. The Declaration was both a revolutionary claim and an incomplete political achievement.

The document also spoke of Indigenous peoples in hostile language, reflecting the colonists' war on the frontier and their settler viewpoint. This is a reminder that universal claims can be spoken by people whose imagined political community is very narrow. Explaining the limitation is not denying the force of the claim. It is showing how later Americans contested who would be allowed to invoke it.

## Independence was a decision about government, too

Congress did not arrive at the Declaration of Independence through a single inspired afternoon. On June 7, 1776 Richard Henry Lee introduced a resolution that the united colonies were and ought to be free and independent states. The debate involved whether delegates possessed instructions from their own colonies, how much agreement should be secured before voting, and what political institutions would replace royal authority. Congress voted for independence on July 2 and adopted the Declaration's text on July 4. Those are two related but distinct decisions. July Fourth became the great commemorative date because it was the formal public declaration circulated under that date.

The Declaration's famous language about equality and rights was followed by accusations against the king. Its legal and diplomatic purpose was to justify a break in political allegiance to an international audience. It was not a complete framework for taxation, courts, military funding, or relations among the new states. Those problems were being addressed separately. States wrote or revised constitutions. Congress debated a confederation. Local authorities determined who could vote, how property would be treated, and how suspected enemies should be dealt with.

The Declaration was read aloud and printed, and royal symbols were sometimes destroyed. These performances were more than celebration. Public reading invited a community to recognize a new source of authority; destroying an emblem marked the visible end of an old one. But performance did not create unanimous consent. Loyalists remained. Many inhabitants had no vote. Enslaved people heard ideals their owners often refused to extend to them. Indigenous nations watched newcomers claim sovereign power over places whose political relationships were far older than the thirteen colonies.

There was a practical risk at the heart of the decision. Declaring independence narrowed the space for a negotiated restoration of former relations and made reconciliation on British terms more difficult. A rebellion might be defeated; an independent state needed recognition, credit, troops, and continued political confidence. When we remember July 1776 as the birth of a nation, we should remember that the nation then had to be constructed while its military survival was in doubt.

## Why losing New York could have ended everything

The British returned to New York in force in the summer of 1776. Their navy offered command of the harbor, rivers, and coastal movement; their army included German troops hired from European principalities, often lumped together under the name Hessians. Washington attempted to defend a complex region of islands and waterways against an opponent that could move by water more easily than his army could march. At Brooklyn, the British flanked and defeated American positions, and Washington conducted a dangerous withdrawal. The Americans ultimately lost New York City and endured a retreat across New Jersey.

One of the Revolution's central strategic facts appeared here: the British could win battles, take cities, and still face the problem of establishing durable political authority over a widespread population. But the other side of that fact was equally important. If Washington's army were destroyed, Congress might lose its central instrument of military resistance, and the movement could fracture. The survival of the field army mattered more than the possession of any one city. Washington did not always understand that distinction as clearly as he later would; the New York campaign was an education acquired through defeat.

December 1776 brought expiring enlistments, demoralization, and uncertainty about whether the army could continue. The attack at Trenton after the Delaware crossing, followed by Princeton, did not erase the earlier disasters or secure foreign recognition overnight. It demonstrated that the army still existed and could surprise the enemy. The victories bought confidence as well as time. In a war of competing political systems, confidence could be a strategic resource, because recruits, money, and overseas allies all depended on believing the cause might survive.

When people picture American independence, they often picture the signed Declaration. The military memory that belongs beside it is a retreating force that did not disappear, followed by a risky counterattack in the cold. The republic remained an idea worth arguing about only because people continued to organize, supply, and defend it while the outcome was unknown.

## The army still had to survive

The declaration did not win a battle. Indeed, the military situation soon became dangerous. Washington had forced the British evacuation of Boston in March 1776 after artillery from Fort Ticonderoga was brought to commanding positions, but Britain could concentrate enormous force near New York. During the summer and autumn of 1776, the Continental Army lost on Long Island and in subsequent operations, retreating through New York and New Jersey. The army's survival mattered more than holding a city it could not defend indefinitely. A declaration meant little if the new government collapsed before foreign powers would recognize it.

Washington's difficult crossing of the Delaware River and attack on Trenton in December, followed by success at Princeton in January 1777, did not end the war. The victories restored morale, attracted attention, and demonstrated that the Continental Army remained capable of initiative. They also involved men who were cold, hungry, and close to the end of their enlistments. In this war, morale and enlistment were strategic resources, not background color.

The new United States faced an extraordinary political problem: it had made a claim to sovereignty without a reliable tax system, a professional administrative apparatus, a navy equal to Britain's, or a guaranteed European ally. Its soldiers served under terms that could expire as campaigns began. Its finances were fragile. Some civilians supported independence enthusiastically, some opposed it, and many measured political claims against the damage war did to their households.

Nevertheless, a fundamental change had taken place between Lexington and Philadelphia. In April 1775, many colonial fighters might plausibly have said they were defending the rights of British subjects. By July 1776, their representatives had declared that the Crown itself was the problem. That transformation was not instant, unanimous, or safely reversible. It created the political object for which the army would continue fighting: not just the removal of a policy, or the restoration of a provincial assembly, but a separate United States whose survival remained very much in question.
