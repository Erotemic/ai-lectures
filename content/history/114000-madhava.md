# 114000 — Madhava of Sangamagrama: Infinite series on the Malabar coast

**Track:** History — People and ideas. **Focus:** Kerala mathematics and astronomy, Madhava's uncertain biography, numerical series for circles and trigonometry, corrections and convergence, the chain of students and commentators, Malayalam mathematical writing, and the modern recovery of the textual tradition. **Background:** fractions, angles, and the idea of successively improving approximations are explained in speech.

Start with a circle. Draw a diameter, then an arc from one end toward a point on the circumference. The arc has a length, but how can arithmetic reach it? Measuring with a flexible string gives an approximation. A geometric construction might give a relationship between straight lines and the curve. Yet suppose you want a calculation that can become as accurate as your time and numerical skill allow. You might seek an endless sequence of additions and subtractions whose accumulated value approaches the arc. That is the sort of reasoning preserved in the mathematical tradition associated with Madhava of Sangamagrama.

Madhava is usually placed in the fourteenth and early fifteenth centuries, roughly between 1340 and 1425, in what is now Kerala in southwestern India. Sangamagrama is commonly identified with the region near Irinjalakuda, though the precise identification rests partly on later tradition. His parents, education, household, and personal travels are not securely documented. No dependable contemporary portrait lets us know his appearance. This does not mean he was a mysterious solitary figure performing tricks far from the world. It means the historical record has preserved mathematical and astronomical writings much more effectively than private biography.

The environment in which he worked was a region of dense coastal commerce, agricultural settlements, learned Sanskrit culture, and local intellectual traditions. Astronomical mathematics had practical and scholarly roles: constructing calendars, predicting eclipses and planetary positions, determining seasonal cycles, and maintaining systems of time reckoning. Malayalam-speaking Kerala also sustained textual exchange through Sanskrit composition and vernacular commentary. The social history of who could teach and study this material was shaped by local institutions and hierarchies. We should not pretend that every scholar occupied the same position within them, nor assign Madhava a particular official post for which documentation does not survive.

The phrase Kerala school of mathematics is convenient, but it is a retrospective label. There was no modern building with a sign announcing an institute of infinite series and a single founder delivering lectures to a formally enrolled class. What historians can identify is a sequence of scholars, teachers, pupils and texts in a connected region over generations. Names include Madhava, Paramesvara, Damodara, Nilakantha Somayaji, Jyeshthadeva and others. The relationships among them are supported with varying degrees of confidence through commentaries, attributions and manuscript transmission.

That chain is essential because much of what is attributed to Madhava survives not in a mathematical book written in his own hand, but through later accounts of his results. Some astronomical compositions associated with him do survive, including works on lunar positions. The infinite series for which he is most celebrated are presented and discussed in writings by successors, often explicitly crediting him. The evidence is strong enough to describe an important program of research connected with his name; it is not strong enough to reconstruct every derivation as if we possessed his original notebooks.

That distinction can be pictured as a chain of witnesses. A later commentator says a method was taught by Madhava, then gives instructions or a derivation. A second text gives a related rule, perhaps in different words. An editor compares the two and asks whether they preserve one older result or two independent developments. The historian can reasonably conclude that a mathematical tradition recognized Madhava's role while remaining uncertain which parts of the later exposition he personally devised. Being precise about the source of an attribution is not a way of minimizing his achievement. It is how a credible history distinguishes evidence from accumulated honor.

The best-known result can be said aloud without any special symbols. Take four. Subtract four thirds. Add four fifths. Subtract four sevenths. Add four ninths. Continue by alternating the sign and moving through consecutive odd denominators. The sum approaches pi, the ratio of a circle's circumference to its diameter. This is the series later associated in Europe with James Gregory and Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz. In the Kerala tradition, it was investigated much earlier, through a different mathematical language and motivation.

Why should such a sequence converge to pi at all? The terms are getting smaller, and they alternate between subtraction and addition. The partial sums jump from one side of a limiting value to the other, with decreasing jumps.

After the first term the approximation to pi is four, far too large. After the second it is about two point six seven, far too small. After the third it rises to roughly three point four seven. The fourth falls below three again. This seesaw pattern is entirely predictable because the terms alternate. Each successive overshoot or undershoot is smaller than the last. If we can show that the first omitted term bounds the remaining error, then a finite sum comes with a quantitative guarantee. That idea makes the infinite into a practical tool: a calculation can stop once it has enough accuracy for its intended purpose, even if the full sum never ends.

This alone tells us there is a well-behaved limiting value for the particular series, but not what that value is. To identify it with pi, the terms must be connected to a geometric or trigonometric relation. The reasoning preserved by Kerala scholars does that through the relation between an angle, its tangent, and an infinite sum.

One can understand the connection by thinking about a rotating line. As an angle changes from zero toward a quarter turn, the ratio of its vertical rise to horizontal run changes. That ratio is the tangent. If we describe the angle in terms of the tangent, there is a series with alternating odd powers: the tangent, minus its cube divided by three, plus its fifth power divided by five, and so on. Modern mathematicians call this an inverse tangent series. At a forty-five-degree angle, the tangent equals one. Substitute one and multiply by four: the angle is one quarter of a half-turn, or pi divided by four in radian measure. That gives the alternating odd-denominator series for pi.

This relation is elegant; its numerical behavior is frustrating. At the input value one, the terms shrink extremely slowly. After just four terms, four times one minus one third plus one fifth minus one seventh is roughly two point eight nine five, not yet close to the modern decimal value of pi. Every additional term improves the long-run accuracy, but the improvement is painfully gradual. To compute many digits efficiently, one needs something better than performing an enormous number of alternating divisions.

The Kerala tradition developed ways of correcting or accelerating such approximations. Madhava is associated with remainder terms that account for the error left after stopping a series. This is a profound conceptual move. A finite computation is not judged solely by the current answer; it is accompanied by information about what the omitted infinite tail might still contribute.

Imagine asking for ten correct decimal places. With the raw alternating pi series at its slowest argument, the error bound shrinks like the reciprocal of the number of terms, not exponentially. A thousand terms are still inadequate for such precision. One must change the computational strategy. An accelerated formula may estimate the remainder from the point where the summation stops, or replace the original argument with smaller values that converge faster. In the Kerala tradition, corrections to partial sums were a serious mathematical object. They turn what could have remained a clever identity into a usable numerical method.

Imagine trying to weigh a bag of grain by adding smaller and smaller packets to a scale, but each packet alternates between increasing and decreasing the displayed total. If the packet sizes shrink slowly, you spend a long time hovering around the true weight. If you understand the pattern of the unadded packets, you can estimate the remaining adjustment in one step. A remainder formula is a mathematical expression of that insight. Unlike the grain packets, the quantities in a series may be negative; the analogy is about estimating unfinished work, not literal weighing.

The preserved texts include highly accurate numerical values for pi, with values commonly discussed to about eleven decimal places. Attributing a particular string of digits to Madhava personally requires checking the witness and its transmission: did the number occur in a work by him, in a later quotation, or in a commentary interpreting his rules? Historians of Kerala mathematics take these distinctions seriously. We should appreciate the extraordinary computational precision without pretending to possess an unbroken chain of autograph manuscript evidence.

The infinite-series program was not limited to pi. Results equivalent to series for sine, cosine, and inverse tangent appear in the tradition. A sine series begins with the angle itself, subtracts the angle cubed divided by six, adds the angle to the fifth power divided by one hundred twenty, and continues with alternating higher powers. A cosine series begins with one, subtracts the angle squared divided by two, then adds the fourth power divided by twenty-four, and continues. These formulas are familiar in modern analysis classes.

A modern student may wonder why the factorials appear in the denominators of sine and cosine expansions. Those denominators express the pattern of repeated adjustment needed to follow a curve whose change is tied to another trigonometric curve. In a calculus course the derivation comes through derivatives. In the Kerala material the route was geometrical and computational, as preserved in later explanations. When translating the result, it is useful to show the matching modern formula, but equally useful to ask what geometrical relationships and approximation procedures made that formula convincing to its historical users. Identical final coefficients need not imply identical paths of discovery. But modern students are reading them in the notation of Taylor series, developed and systematized in other settings. That notation is not a neutral transcription of fourteenth-century Kerala practice.

In astronomical calculation the need for such series is plain. Planetary and lunar positions involve angular quantities; tables must provide sines and corrections at many points. A numerical method that can improve precision, estimate a remainder, or compute a trigonometric value from an argument has obvious advantages. This is why the history of these series belongs in the same intellectual world as eclipse prediction and the correction of astronomical parameters. Pure mathematics and computational astronomy were not separate departments.

A central later text is the *Yuktibhasha*, composed in Malayalam by Jyeshthadeva in the sixteenth century. It gives extended mathematical rationales for results associated with the school. Its existence matters for two reasons. First, it provides evidence of reasoning, not just a list of formulas. Second, it is later than Madhava. If a proof appears in Jyeshthadeva's work, it does not follow automatically that Madhava wrote that precise proof in that form. A tradition can preserve an insight while transforming its demonstrations over several generations.

Nilakantha Somayaji, a major astronomer and mathematician of the same later tradition, provided commentaries and model revisions that are part of the evidence for earlier work. Paramesvara made extensive observations and mathematical contributions. Damodara and other teachers helped connect one generation to another. These figures should not be erased by calling every Kerala result simply Madhava's. Intellectual credit can acknowledge a leading originator and still specify the work of later authors who made the mathematics explicit, usable and teachable.

Some historians describe the Kerala achievements as a form of calculus. There is a defensible mathematical resemblance: infinite series, limit processes, geometric rationales and error analysis are central topics in what we now call analysis. Yet the label can mislead if it suggests that Madhava wrote Newton's and Leibniz's differential calculus in disguise. There is no evidence that he developed exactly the same symbolic derivative, integral and fundamental theorem framework found in European seventeenth-century mathematics. His achievement should be stated positively and precisely: techniques for infinite series and trigonometric computation with increasingly accurate approximation and, in the later tradition, articulated rationales.

The distinction matters because historical comparisons can become contests over priority. It is a real and important fact that results equivalent to European power-series formulas were known in Kerala long before their European publication. It is a different claim to say that Gregory, Newton or Leibniz learned those formulas directly from Kerala mathematicians. Portuguese and Jesuit contacts later connected the western coast of India with Europe, and some authors have proposed transmission routes. But no manuscript, letter, or documented translation has established a direct chain carrying these particular series into the relevant European discoveries. Historians can investigate the possibility without treating it as a demonstrated fact.

There is a subtler lesson in that comparison. Independent discovery is not a consolation prize. If people in different mathematical communities identify related truths under different incentives and using different languages, the result teaches us something about what mathematics makes possible. An infinite series for an angle can emerge from problems in astronomical calculation as well as from a broader early modern theory of curves. The differences in proof and presentation are part of the history, not an embarrassment to be removed by rewriting everything as if it were a modern calculus textbook.

The history of modern rediscovery is itself worth telling. In the nineteenth century, scholars connected with British colonial administration in India reported examples of the Kerala series to European readers. Charles Matthew Whish described important evidence in a paper published in 1834. His report did not instantly change the standard history of mathematics. It took later research to read manuscripts, interpret technical vocabulary, establish dates and identify the mathematical structure of arguments. Scholars including C. T. Rajagopal, M. S. Rangachari, K. V. Sarma and, later, Kim Plofker made essential contributions to reconstructing the record. This is not merely a story of someone discovering that an earlier people had already written down a famous formula.

The surviving evidence also has a linguistic history. A later mathematical commentary in Malayalam can explain a Sanskrit verse with examples and rationales suited to a particular community of readers. A modern historian must understand both the technical vocabulary and the mathematical practice of the period. If a Sanskrit instruction says to alternate two classes of terms, the sequence of operations matters; if a commentator offers a geometric justification, its diagram and assumptions matter. Translating everything into the name of a single European theorem can obscure the very reasoning that establishes historical priority. The harder work is to recover how the original tradition actually understood its methods.

It is a story of historians becoming better at reading mathematics outside a familiar language and symbolic system. A sentence about an arc and a sine might look like a numerical trick until its iterative structure is recognized. A procedure for a remainder might look like a correction table until one understands that it estimates the tail of an infinite calculation. Good historical scholarship must recover the actual mathematical work rather than just attach a modern name to a line of Sanskrit or Malayalam.

What, then, can we responsibly say about the person Madhava? We can say his name is central to the Kerala line of mathematical astronomy. We can say later writers explicitly attributed major series results to him. We can explain the geographic and intellectual setting in which those methods were developed. We can identify the surviving and lost textual evidence. We cannot narrate the precise day he first recognized an alternating sum, report the words he spoke to a particular student, or claim he felt triumphant when an improved value of pi appeared in his calculations. Those scenes are not in the record.

There is a temptation to fill this silence with a tale of genius working alone under the tropical stars. Such a picture is vivid but unsupported. The mathematics itself points away from it. Astronomical tables draw on earlier parameters; computational procedures demand testing and correction; commentaries remember teachers and predecessors; later scholars quote, explain and extend what came before. A community of work is visible even where an individual's daily life is not.

Return to the alternating series. Four, minus four thirds, plus four fifths, minus four sevenths. The partial sums wobble around pi, too slowly for a patient astronomer who needs many digits. The Kerala tradition saw not just an infinity of terms but the possibility of controlling the unfinished tail. That is a powerful response to the gap between a mathematical ideal and a finite working life. Madhava's precise private biography has largely disappeared, but through the writings and calculations of later scholars, a rigorous and inventive mathematical program remains. Its history belongs not only to the history of infinite series, but also to the history of people who kept another person's reasoning alive by understanding it well enough to improve it.

## Historical companion — not spoken

### Evidentiary chronology

- **c. 1340–1425:** Conventional approximate life span of Madhava of Sangamagrama. The exact location of Sangamagrama is a matter of identification and tradition.
- **Fourteenth–fifteenth centuries:** Series and astronomical work associated with Madhava; most original mathematical works lost, with astronomical writings surviving in part.
- **Fifteenth–sixteenth centuries:** Paramesvara, Damodara, Nilakantha Somayaji and other scholars transmitted, modified, and interpreted the mathematical astronomy.
- **Sixteenth century:** Jyeshthadeva's *Yuktibhasha* in Malayalam preserves detailed rationales for series and algorithms attributed to predecessors.
- **1834:** C. M. Whish's publication brought key Kerala series evidence to wider notice in English.
- **Twentieth century onward:** Manuscript study, editing and mathematical interpretation by scholars including Rajagopal, Rangachari, Sarma and Plofker clarified the results and their context.

### Mathematical companion

- **Alternating pi series:** \(\displaystyle \frac{\pi}{4}=1-\frac13+\frac15-\frac17+\cdots\). Four-term estimate \(4(1-1/3+1/5-1/7)\approx2.895238\); convergence is slow.
- **Inverse tangent (modern notation):** \(\displaystyle \arctan x = x-\frac{x^3}{3}+\frac{x^5}{5}-\cdots\), for \(|x|\le1\) with usual endpoint care. \(x=1\) yields the series above.
- **Trigonometric series (modern notation):** \(\displaystyle \sin x=x-\frac{x^3}{3!}+\frac{x^5}{5!}-\cdots\), \(\displaystyle\cos x=1-\frac{x^2}{2!}+\frac{x^4}{4!}-\cdots\). These are mathematical equivalents, not a claim about historical notation.
- **Error of alternating partial sums:** For \(\sum(-1)^k/(2k+1)\), the magnitude of the omitted tail is bounded by the first omitted term; improving an estimate from few terms requires accelerated methods or remainder correction.
- **Attribution:** Later authors preserve arguments and credit; do not equate every sixteenth-century proof with an autograph text of Madhava.
