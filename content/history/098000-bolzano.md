# 098000 - Bernard Bolzano: Proof, conscience, and censorship in Prague

**Track:** History - People and ideas. **Focus:** multilingual Habsburg Bohemia, family and Piarist education, mathematics and priesthood, social reform and political dismissal, life with the Hoffmann family, rigorous analysis, theories of logic and infinity, and delayed publication. **Background:** continuity and simple number sequences are explained in speech.

A theorem can outlive the institution that tried to silence its author. Bernard Bolzano was a Catholic priest and professor in Prague whose religious and social teaching drew suspicion from the Habsburg authorities. He was dismissed from his university position in eighteen nineteen, restricted in what he could publish, and pushed into a life sustained partly by a small pension and the help of friends. He continued to write about mathematics, logic, theology, and the improvement of society. Much of his mathematical work remained unpublished or little known during his lifetime. Later scholars recognized that he had developed ideas about proof, continuity, and infinity that belong near the beginning of the history of modern rigor. But to understand those ideas, we first need to meet the man in the politically divided city where he lived.

Bernard Bolzano was born in Prague in October seventeen eighty-one, when Bohemia formed part of the Habsburg domains. His father, Bernard Pompeius Bolzano, was of Italian origin and worked in the art trade; his mother, Maria Cecilia Maurer, came from a Prague merchant family. Both were devout Catholics. His father participated in charitable work, including efforts to establish an orphanage. The family had twelve children, but Bernard and his brother Johann were the only two who survived to adulthood. Bernard's health was often fragile. Childhood loss, religious education, and practical concern for people in poverty formed part of his early world, though none can be turned into a simple formula that predicts his later philosophy.

Prague itself was not socially or linguistically uniform. German was important in schools and administration, while much of the surrounding population spoke Czech. The Habsburg state sought to govern many peoples and regions within a single political order, and the French Revolution had intensified official fears about political dissent. Bolzano would later criticize discrimination and divisions between language communities, as well as antisemitism. It would be misleading to turn him into a modern nationalist with a ready-made political program. His concerns grew from religious and philosophical convictions about justice and from his attention to social life in Bohemia. Those convictions placed him at odds with an imperial government that increasingly equated public criticism with danger.

He attended schools run by the Piarists, a Catholic teaching order with a particular commitment to educating young people. In seventeen ninety-six he entered the University of Prague, where he studied mathematics, philosophy, and physics. He was strongly influenced by mathematical writers such as Abraham Gotthelf Kästner, who cared about the reasons a proof should count as convincing. He studied with František Josef Gerstner, a scholar associated with mathematics and engineering. Bolzano was drawn not only to getting an answer but to the structure of justification. What assumptions does a theorem really need? Which steps follow from the concepts at issue, and which introduce an unrelated geometric picture that merely makes the conclusion seem obvious?

In eighteen hundred he began theological study, despite doubts and his father's opposition to the choice. His mathematical interests did not cease. He completed a doctoral dissertation on the foundations of geometry and received his doctorate in eighteen oh four. Shortly afterward he was ordained a priest. A religious career and an exacting interest in mathematical proof may seem, to a modern reader, to pull in opposite directions. For Bolzano they were closely related parts of an inquiry into truth, duty, and clear reasoning. He was not the only cleric to study mathematics, nor did mathematical rigor require religious belief. But the combination mattered for the particular work he chose and the institutions in which he could teach.

In eighteen oh five he competed for university appointments, including a mathematics chair and a newly created chair concerned with the philosophy of religion. He performed well in the competitions. The university gave him the religious position, which was part of the Austrian state's strategy for strengthening orthodox religious instruction. There was a problem from the authorities' point of view. Bolzano used the post to present arguments for moral reform, social obligation, and more equal treatment of people. Instead of simply reinforcing obedience, his lectures invited students to examine the ethical basis of institutions. Official confirmation of his appointment was slow, and the authorities monitored him closely.

For years his public lectures and sermons attracted students while drawing criticism from conservative religious and political officials. He spoke about poverty, social justice, education, and the relation between different peoples living in Bohemia. These were not merely extracurricular opinions that later historians have attached to a mathematician. They were central to his paid work as a professor of religion. At the same time, he belonged to the Royal Bohemian Society of Sciences and continued to write mathematics. The two occupations were connected by his concern for rational argument, but they should not be collapsed into one. A claim about the limits of a sequence is not a coded political statement, and his dismissal did not occur because he proved a theorem about calculus.

Bolzano's mathematical publications appeared during this period. His early work on geometry argued that apparently self-evident relations still required proofs based on appropriate concepts. In eighteen ten he began a projected series on the foundations of mathematics. In eighteen sixteen he published a study of the binomial theorem. In eighteen seventeen he published a Purely Analytic Proof of the theorem that a continuous function which takes values of opposite signs must pass through zero. The title itself was a declaration of method. Bolzano wanted to establish a claim about continuity using analysis, not by asking the reader to picture an unbroken line on paper and treating the picture as proof.

Here is the familiar theorem. Imagine a continuous function whose value is negative at the left end of an interval and positive at the right end. Somewhere between, the value must be zero. A sketch makes the statement plausible. But a sketch leaves several questions unanswered. What is continuity? Why can a curve not somehow evade zero without a visible jump? Are we relying on physical intuitions about ink and paper, or on properties of the number system? A mathematical proof must explain why every function satisfying the conditions has the asserted property, not just why the functions we can draw seem to have it.

One useful modern reconstruction proceeds by repeatedly dividing the interval in half. Keep the half whose endpoints still have opposite signs, then repeat. The intervals become narrower and nest inside each other. Their lengths approach zero. To conclude that the shrinking intervals determine a point in the real numbers, one needs a completeness property of those numbers. Continuity then ensures the function takes value zero there. This is a modern way of making the idea intuitive; it should not be passed off as a verbatim transcript of Bolzano's seventeen eighteen-seventeen argument. His accomplishment was to press the proof in an analytic direction and to expose the demand for a more rigorous account of the real numbers.

Such work anticipated later developments in analysis, but the history of influence needs care. Augustin-Louis Cauchy published influential results about limits and continuity in the eighteen twenties; Weierstrass and others later shaped the standard definitions. Historians have not established a simple line in which every later author read Bolzano and copied his ideas. Some of his work circulated narrowly, and some manuscripts were not published for many decades. There is a difference between reaching a result early and causing its later adoption. Bolzano is important in both the story of mathematical ideas and the story of how even good ideas can remain almost invisible when their author lacks a secure institutional channel for publication.

In eighteen nineteen the political conflict caught up with his academic career. Imperial authorities removed him from his professorship. He faced restrictions on teaching, preaching, correspondence, and publication, and church proceedings continued over the following years. The records describe pressure to retract propositions judged objectionable, which he resisted. Different kinds of authority overlapped: the state had political interests, the university depended on permission to employ him, and church officers investigated doctrine. It is too simple to call this solely a disagreement between science and religion. Bolzano was himself a priest, and some church figures supported him. His conflict concerned religious interpretation, political obedience, social criticism, and the government's fear of reform.

Dismissal changed his life materially. With his teaching income gone, his future writing depended partly on a limited pension and the support of people willing to host him. He spent time with Josef and Anna Hoffmann near the village of Těchobuz in southern Bohemia, as well as periods in Prague with his brother. The Hoffmann household became a place where he could study and write. Anna and Josef were not just anonymous benefactors. Their willingness to provide shelter and companionship helped sustain a body of work produced outside an ordinary university career. Later when Anna became ill, their household arrangements changed, and Bolzano returned to Prague. His mathematical life cannot be separated from these concrete acts of hospitality.

One of his largest projects was Wissenschaftslehre, commonly translated as Theory of Science, published in eighteen thirty-seven. It concerned logic, the structure of propositions, consequence, and what it means for a statement to be true. Bolzano asked questions that twentieth-century logicians would recognize. Does the truth of a statement depend on who thinks it? How do ideas combine? Under what substitution of concepts does a conclusion follow from premises? His answers were embedded in a vast philosophical system, not in the notation of contemporary mathematical logic. Their later importance lies partly in the precision with which he separated a proposition itself from the act of someone believing or asserting it.

He also planned a comprehensive mathematical work often called the Theory of Quantities. Much remained unfinished or in manuscript. This makes it hard to trace immediate influence. Manuscripts may contain valuable results without changing what mathematicians elsewhere know. Editors, students, and later historians must reconstruct what was written, when it was written, and who actually had access to it. If a result appears in a posthumously printed work, that date belongs to the surviving publication history; it cannot by itself establish the exact date of discovery. That uncertainty is especially difficult to resolve for a thinker whose professional voice had been restricted by the authorities.

His work on infinity offers another vivid example. People had long noticed a paradox: if an infinite collection is truly a collection of distinct things, how can it have the same size as one of its proper parts? Consider the positive whole numbers: one, two, three, four, and so on. Now consider only the even positive numbers: two, four, six, eight, and so on. We can pair every whole number with precisely one even number by doubling it. No even number is left out. The evens are a proper subset, but there is a one-to-one pairing between the two collections. Finite sets cannot behave this way. Bolzano discussed correspondences of this kind in his reflections on infinity.

A mature modern theory of cardinal numbers would be developed later, above all by Georg Cantor and his successors. Bolzano's analysis anticipated questions and examples important to that theory, but it was not already Cantor's completed set theory. His Paradoxes of the Infinite was published in eighteen fifty-one, three years after his death, with editorial work by Franz Prihonský. The posthumous appearance makes the history especially easy to distort. A twentieth-century reader can hear a modern theorem in Bolzano's words and forget that the formal vocabulary, the surrounding debates, and the social conditions of publication were different.

Bolzano remained engaged with the scholarly life of Prague when circumstances allowed. He returned to activities of the Royal Bohemian Society of Sciences and continued discussions with friends and students. His breathing difficulties grew worse as he aged. He died in Prague in December eighteen forty-eight, a year of European revolution that transformed many of the political circumstances in which he had lived. We should not force the ending into a neat story of vindication. He did not live to see the full reception of his mathematical manuscripts, and political restrictions had imposed genuine costs. Yet neither had he stopped thinking or writing simply because an institution deprived him of a chair.

The tension between proof and authority runs through his life. In mathematics Bolzano did not want a theorem accepted merely because its diagram looked persuasive or its author was famous. In moral and political reasoning he resisted the idea that an official position made a proposition true. Those two attitudes have family resemblances, but they are not the same argument. One concerns the logical requirements of demonstration; the other concerns human institutions and conscience. His work becomes more intelligible when both are kept in view.

There is also an irony in the label precursor. Later generations have called Bolzano a precursor of modern logic, of rigorous analysis, and of set theory. That recognition is deserved where the historical evidence supports specific anticipations. But a precursor is always defined from the future, by what other people eventually made important. Bolzano was not living in preparation for the nineteenth century's later textbooks. He was a Prague professor, priest, critic of injustice, family member, friend, and mathematician who thought that arguments should be justified and that ideas could be pursued even when permission to teach had been withdrawn. To read his work only as a collection of early versions of later theories would be to miss the person who wrote it.

The next time you hear the statement that a continuous function crossing from negative to positive must equal zero somewhere, remember that its simplicity was once part of a difficult philosophical and mathematical problem. What are numbers, and why does a proof based on them compel assent? Bolzano wanted answers that would survive more than a reassuring picture or an appeal to authority. His life shows that the quest for such answers can be carried on inside institutions, against them, and sometimes beyond their reach.

## Historical companion (not narrated)

### Chronology

- **1781–1805:** born in Prague in Habsburg Bohemia; Piarist schooling; university mathematics and philosophy; mathematical doctorate in 1804, ordination, and appointment to philosophy of religion.
- **1805–1819:** taught religion and ethics; debated social reform, equal treatment of Czech-speaking and German-speaking communities, and discrimination; published work on mathematical foundations and the 1817 analytic proof of a zero between opposite signs.
- **1819–1848:** dismissed under imperial pressure; restrictions on teaching, preaching, and publication; wrote with support from brother Johann and Josef and Anna Hoffmann; *Wissenschaftslehre* (1837); died in Prague.
- **1851 and later:** *Paradoxes of the Infinite* published posthumously under the editorship of Franz Prihonský; many manuscripts and works were recovered or studied much later.

### Mathematical companion

**Intermediate value theorem, modern form.** If \(f:[a,b]\to\mathbb R\) is continuous, and \(f(a)<0<f(b)\), then there exists \(c\in(a,b)\) with \(f(c)=0\). A modern bisection proof uses completeness of the real numbers. This is an exposition of the theorem's logic, not an assertion that Bolzano used current epsilon-delta notation.

**Infinity.** The bijection \(n\mapsto 2n\) pairs \(\mathbb N_{>0}\) with its proper subset of positive even integers. This feature of infinite sets later became central to cardinality, but one should distinguish Bolzano's discussions from Cantor's later system. The famous **Bolzano–Weierstrass theorem** is named retrospectively; precise statements and proofs should be checked rather than projected unchanged back onto the 1817 publication.

**Historical cautions:** Bolzano's removal concerned his public religious, moral, and social teaching under an authoritarian Habsburg state, not a theorem about continuity. He was a Catholic priest; a generic "science versus religion" narrative is inaccurate. Separate the dates of manuscripts, publication, and later reception. His results on infinity anticipated later topics but did not amount to Cantor's full theory, and the modern bisection proof is an explanatory reconstruction.
