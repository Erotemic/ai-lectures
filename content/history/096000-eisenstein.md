# 096000 - Gotthold Eisenstein: Number theory under pressure in Berlin

**Track:** History - People and ideas. **Focus:** precarious family and health, Berlin school and scholarship, the journeys to Britain and Ireland, Gauss and Humboldt, Crelle's journal, reciprocity and elliptic functions, the revolutions of 1848, and contested mathematical credit. **Background:** elementary polynomials and primes are introduced through examples.

Mathematics can look from a distance like a procession of permanently employed professors. Gotthold Eisenstein was not one of them. He produced a torrent of research in Berlin while struggling with illness, unstable income, and dependence on patrons who could persuade the Prussian state to support him. His contemporaries recognized exceptional mathematical talent. That recognition did not bring an ordinary secure career. When revolution reached Berlin in eighteen forty-eight, an arrest and the suspicion it generated could threaten his livelihood regardless of what his manuscripts proved. The question of how someone works under such conditions belongs inside his mathematical biography, not in an afterword to the results.

Ferdinand Gotthold Max Eisenstein was born in Berlin in April eighteen twenty-three. His father, Johann Konstantin Eisenstein, had served in the Prussian army but struggled to find steady civilian work. His mother, Helene Pollack, played an important role in his education. The family had Jewish origins and had converted to Protestantism before his birth, a detail that matters in a society where religious identity could still affect social prospects. The household's finances were uncertain. Eisenstein also suffered repeated childhood illnesses, and his five siblings died young. Later writers sometimes build a complete account of his personality around poor health. The evidence supports a much more limited conclusion: serious illness and family loss were persistent facts of his life, and he nonetheless sustained demanding study.

His mother taught him to read at an early age. He had a talent for music, especially piano playing and composition, that continued after mathematics became his main occupation. A childhood spent with books and music did not remove the need for schooling or money. Different schools affected him differently; one imposed a harshly regimented atmosphere, while later teachers recognized his mathematical interests and supported them. In his teens he studied calculus from Euler and Lagrange, often well beyond the standard curriculum. He began to attend lectures by Peter Gustav Lejeune Dirichlet while still a schoolboy. These were substantial intellectual resources available in Berlin, but access to them did not mean that he had the financial position of a university professor's son.

In eighteen forty-two he bought a French translation of Carl Friedrich Gauss's Disquisitiones Arithmeticae. This work, published decades earlier, organized number theory into a formidable subject with powerful techniques for divisibility, congruences, and quadratic forms. It offered Eisenstein both problems and a model of ambition. Why should relations among integers admit such rich laws? Could Gauss's results on squares and quadratic residues be extended to higher powers? The questions would guide much of his short research career. An older book became an active intellectual companion to a young reader at a moment when the family's prospects were unsettled.

His father had traveled to England in search of work, and Eisenstein and his mother joined him there. The family spent time in England, Wales, and Ireland, moving as the prospects for employment changed. Eisenstein read mathematics during the journeys and continued to play music. In Dublin in eighteen forty-three, he met William Rowan Hamilton, whose work reached across algebra, geometry, optics, and mechanics. Hamilton gave him a paper concerning Abel's work on equations of the fifth degree. It is a vivid intersection of mathematical lives: a young German traveler, whose family's movements were dictated partly by employment, met an established Irish mathematician and encountered another source of difficult problems. Eisenstein wanted to remain in Dublin, according to biographical accounts, but returned to Berlin with his mother.

He completed his school examinations in eighteen forty-three and enrolled at the University of Berlin. His early research moved rapidly into questions that established mathematicians took seriously. August Leopold Crelle, the influential editor of a mathematical journal, recognized the promise of his submissions. Crelle's journal mattered because it could turn a manuscript written by someone outside a senior professorship into work read by specialists across Europe. In eighteen forty-four Eisenstein published an extraordinary number of papers and problems in its pages. The volume of writing was not a sign of an effortless life. It reflected a person urgently developing several interconnected ideas while trying to secure a position from which to continue.

Crelle introduced the young mathematician to Alexander von Humboldt, the explorer and scientific organizer whose influence extended into the Prussian court. Humboldt did more than offer praise. He arranged introductions, pressed officials for financial grants, and contributed money himself. The surviving correspondence makes clear that support was repeatedly negotiated. A stipend might arrive late or be renewed only for a short period. Humboldt could recommend a young mathematical genius to a monarch, but he could not simply create a permanent professorship. Their relationship shows how science in nineteenth-century Prussia depended on more than intellectual merit: patrons, ministries, reputations, and court politics could decide whether someone had food, housing, and time to work.

In June eighteen forty-four Eisenstein traveled to Göttingen to meet Gauss. Gauss had read some of his papers and admired them. A later account preserves striking praise conveyed through Humboldt, but the lasting historical significance is less the wording of a compliment than what it represented. Eisenstein's number theory had reached the mathematician whose earlier book had drawn him into the subject. He also met the scholar Moritz Stern in Göttingen, with whom he corresponded. Letters and personal introductions continued to substitute for stable institutional belonging. In eighteen forty-five the University of Breslau awarded him an honorary doctorate, with help from leading mathematicians including Ernst Eduard Kummer and Carl Gustav Jacobi.

The mathematics was neither a single theorem nor a simple continuation of Gauss. Eisenstein studied quadratic and cubic forms, higher reciprocity laws, and elliptic functions. Quadratic reciprocity tells us, in a strikingly structured way, how the possibility of solving a square congruence modulo one odd prime is related to a corresponding problem for another. The theorem's symmetry is not obvious from elementary arithmetic. Eisenstein investigated extensions involving cubes and fourth powers. Such laws require new arithmetic objects and methods: sometimes ordinary integers do not provide enough structure to express how factors behave. Gauss, Kummer, Jacobi, and Eisenstein approached these questions in overlapping but distinct ways.

An example makes the problem concrete without reproducing the historical calculations. Modulo seven, the nonzero squares are one, two, and four. That means the equation x squared equals three has no solution when arithmetic is carried out modulo seven. If the modulus changes, the possible squares change too. Quadratic reciprocity establishes a relationship between apparently separate questions about primes. Higher reciprocity asks what analogous laws exist for cubes and other powers. It leads toward algebraic number theory, where numbers can live in larger systems and factorization is studied in new ways. Eisenstein helped push this research into difficult territory, especially around cubic and biquadratic reciprocity.

His work on elliptic functions was equally inventive. These functions grow out of integrals that cannot generally be expressed using only ordinary algebraic operations, exponentials, logarithms, and elementary trigonometry. Abel and Jacobi had already transformed the subject. Eisenstein developed an independent analytic approach involving series with delicate convergence behavior. A sum over many terms can give useful identities, but the order of summation may matter when absolute convergence is absent. That is not a technical nuisance one can simply ignore: it affects whether a manipulation proves a statement. Later mathematicians, including Kronecker and André Weil, studied aspects of Eisenstein's contributions. Some ideas became part of the long development of elliptic and modular functions.

Recognition and rivalry were interwoven. Eisenstein's relations with Jacobi could be strained, particularly when their work touched related questions of priority. Kummer was another important figure in higher reciprocity, and the coexistence of active approaches made questions of precedence sensitive. It would be easy to dramatize the story as one neglected genius against the establishment. That would miss the evidence that establishment figures also promoted him and that genuine mathematical differences were being worked out. A fair portrait should credit the mathematicians who helped him while acknowledging the conflicts that affected him.

The revolution of eighteen forty-eight interrupted far more than an academic discussion. Street fighting broke out in Berlin during the March upheaval. Eisenstein was in a building from which shots were fired at royal troops; he was arrested, although the building was not his. According to historical accounts, he was released the following day after harsh treatment. Suspicion that he had republican sympathies damaged the willingness of officials to maintain financial support. It is important not to present the arrest as proof that he had fired a weapon or belonged to a political organization. The record shows an arrest during the fighting and the consequences of political suspicion. Even a short encounter with state power could have lasting effects on a researcher dependent on government funds.

Humboldt continued to advocate for him. The correspondence is unusually useful because it documents support in money as well as in words. Attempts to arrange a permanent position failed. Eisenstein lectured as a Privatdozent, often with his teaching restricted by illness. He was not denied all recognition: Gauss nominated him for the Göttingen Academy, and in eighteen fifty-two he became a member of the Berlin Academy. But an academic title is not the same thing as secure health or income. The juxtaposition is stark: European mathematical institutions honored his work while the young man struggled to sustain the conditions necessary to keep producing it.

One elementary theorem attached to his name is the Eisenstein criterion for the irreducibility of a polynomial. A polynomial is irreducible over the rational numbers when it cannot be factored into lower-degree polynomials with rational coefficients. For instance, consider x cubed plus three x plus three. Every coefficient except the leading one is divisible by three; the constant term three is not divisible by nine. These observations give a quick proof that the cubic cannot be factored over the rationals. The modern criterion states a general version of this test for an appropriate prime. Its appeal is that a simple divisibility pattern can establish something about every possible rational factorization without attempting to list the factors.

The name should not erase a priority complication. The nineteenth-century mathematician Theodor Schönemann had published an earlier, more general irreducibility result before Eisenstein's eighteen fifty treatment. A historical study of their papers documents that Schönemann protested the subsequent attribution. Calling it the Eisenstein criterion is standard modern terminology; saying Eisenstein was the first person to prove every version of the idea is not. The name attached to a theorem often records how it came to be remembered, not who first discovered every part of the result.

Eisenstein's health deteriorated. Tuberculosis limited his work and eventually killed him in Berlin in October eighteen fifty-two, when he was twenty-nine. Humboldt arranged assistance and attempted to secure the chance for recovery abroad, but it came too late. An account of the funeral records the elderly Humboldt following the coffin. The detail is moving not because it substitutes emotion for history, but because it brings a relationship of many years back into view: the patron who had petitioned kings and ministries had also cared for the younger man through repeated practical crises.

A short life can tempt a biographer into speculation: what would Eisenstein have proved at forty, at sixty, with a stable chair and better health? There is no responsible answer. We can identify the publications, letters, colleagues, disruptions, and later influence. We can see how reciprocity laws and elliptic-function methods passed into fields that flourished after his death. But we should not use an imaginary longer career to replace the one he lived. His real achievement was already substantial. He helped make higher number theory a central nineteenth-century research enterprise while lacking the institutional security later generations associate with professional mathematics.

There is a larger historical lesson in the relation between Eisenstein and Gauss. The older mathematician had produced a book whose ideas could travel through libraries, translation, and students; the younger one read it while his family moved in search of employment, and turned some of its unsolved questions into new mathematics. From there came journals, letters, mentors, rivals, and patrons. It is a history of ideas, but also a history of their material transmission. Eisenstein's manuscripts show what he could do with mathematics. His life shows how much was required to let him do it.

## Historical companion (not narrated)

### Chronology

- **1823–1843:** born in Berlin to Johann Konstantin Eisenstein and Helene Pollack; extensive childhood illness and family bereavement; mathematical schooling, study of Gauss; family travels to Britain and Ireland; met William Rowan Hamilton in Dublin.
- **1843–1847:** University of Berlin; publications in Crelle's journal; support from Alexander von Humboldt; meeting with Gauss in Göttingen; honorary doctorate from Breslau; research in higher reciprocity and elliptic functions.
- **1848–1852:** arrested during the March fighting in Berlin, released but placed under political suspicion; unstable support and worsening illness; election to scientific academies; death from tuberculosis in 1852.

### Mathematical companion

For a polynomial \(f(x)=a_nx^n+\cdots+a_0\) with integer coefficients, **Eisenstein's criterion** states that \(f\) is irreducible in \(\mathbb Q[x]\) if there exists a prime \(p\) such that

\[p\nmid a_n,\qquad p\mid a_i\;(i<n),\qquad p^2\nmid a_0.\]

The example \(x^3+3x+3\) works with \(p=3\). The criterion is sufficient, not necessary. The credit record includes the earlier work of Theodor Schönemann (1846) and his dispute over Eisenstein's later publication; readers should not confuse a standard eponym with a complete priority history. Eisenstein's work on higher reciprocity and elliptic functions was wider than this introductory test.

**Historical cautions:** Being arrested in a building during the March uprising does not establish participation in shooting at troops.
