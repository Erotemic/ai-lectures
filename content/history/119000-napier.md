# 119000 — John Napier: Logarithms in a divided Scotland

**Track:** History — People and ideas. **Focus:** Napier's household and landholding in sixteenth-century Scotland, religious controversy, astronomy and calculation, the original logarithm tables, collaboration with Henry Briggs, and practical computing rods. **Background:** multiplication and geometric progressions are explained from first principles.

Imagine trying to multiply two numbers with seven or eight digits, then doing it thousands of times while preparing a table for astronomers. No electronic calculator, no computer, and no convenient mathematical reference already containing the answers. Every new calculation costs labor and invites error. In the early seventeenth century John Napier offered a remarkable escape: use a table to turn a difficult multiplication into a much simpler addition. His logarithms helped change astronomical and scientific calculation. Yet the man who published them was not primarily a professional mathematician employed by a university. He was a Scottish landowner and a forceful participant in the religious conflicts of his time.

Napier was born in 1550 at Merchiston, near Edinburgh, into a family of Scottish landholders with political connections. His father was Archibald Napier, and his mother was Janet Bothwell. These were turbulent decades in Scottish history. The Reformation changed the legal and religious institutions of the country, the authority of monarchy and church was contested, and alliances with powers elsewhere in Europe had immediate domestic consequences. To understand Napier's life, it helps to resist the modern division between a scientist's work and everything called private politics. His theological writing was a major part of his public identity.

He entered the University of St Andrews at a young age, generally said to have been thirteen. The documentary record does not establish that he completed a conventional degree. Accounts of later travel on the Continent appear in biographies but are not securely documented in detail. What is established is that he returned to take part in family and estate affairs. He married Elizabeth Stirling, with whom he had children; after her death he married Agnes Chisholm and had further children. Household organization, land management, and the obligations of a landed family occupied much of his life. Napier did not support his mathematical work by holding an academic appointment.

It would be easy to imagine that, living near Edinburgh, he was isolated from contemporary science. That would be misleading. Scholarly books, correspondence, astronomy, military and surveying needs, and religious printing circulated throughout Britain and continental Europe. Mathematical calculation had become increasingly important for navigation, mapmaking, astronomy, and the expanding use of trigonometry. A landholder with books, education, and resources could participate in research without being a university professor, though the absence of a regular academic post made the shape of his work different.

Scottish political history had consequences for a person who possessed land, public connections, and strong religious opinions. The rule of Mary, Queen of Scots, ended with her abdication, and James the Sixth's reign brought continued disputes over Protestant church organization and royal authority. In 1603 James also became king of England, creating a union of crowns without erasing the two kingdoms' separate institutions. Napier's public theology should be understood within that changing setting. It was a way of arguing about threats, loyalties and the future of the realm, not merely an abstract hobby in biblical numerology.

Napier's most prominent early publication was not mathematical. In 1593 he published *A Plaine Discovery of the Whole Revelation of Saint John*, an interpretation of the biblical Book of Revelation. He wrote from a strongly Protestant standpoint and reacted to fears that political and religious forces might threaten Scotland's Reformed institutions. The book circulated beyond Scotland and was translated. To treat this as an embarrassing detour before the real scientific career would be false to the chronology and priorities of the man himself. Religious prophecy and polemic were serious intellectual commitments for Napier, even though modern readers may approach the calculations with more enthusiasm.

The setting also reminds us that the early history of modern mathematics did not take place in a world already divided into separate departments called mathematics, astronomy, theology, and public policy. Learned people pursued several of these at once. Napier could spend long periods studying scripture, manage land, and then work on a mathematical device for easing calculation. One should not infer that his theological opinions directly generated logarithms. The more modest point is that the same life contained those activities, and none was trivial to him.

Before logarithms, a scientist might use tables of trigonometric values. If an astronomical problem involved many angles, each sine and cosine could require careful computation or lookup. But multiplying two long values remained laborious. When calculating planetary positions or surveying distances, repeating those multiplications could consume days. Reducing the number of laborious operations could change which problems were economically and practically solvable. Even a calculation method with no new theorem about the stars could transform astronomy by making existing theory usable.

The cost of hand computation was not just a matter of patience. Each multiplication could introduce an error that then propagated through subsequent steps. A long astronomical table depended on a chain of calculations, and checking the chain could require nearly as much work as making it. A transformation that replaced multiplication with addition therefore saved two resources: time and opportunities for arithmetic mistakes. Tables were still vulnerable to printing and rounding errors, but they concentrated much of the difficult arithmetic in one carefully prepared reference. People could then check a shared table rather than independently redo every expensive step.

The key is a relation between two kinds of progression. One sequence increases by equal additions: zero, one, two, three, four. Another increases by repeated multiplication: one, two, four, eight, sixteen. If we label each doubling by the number of times the doubling occurred, multiplication of powers becomes addition of labels. Four times eight is thirty-two; in the labels, two plus three equals five. This is the basic modern logarithmic idea. A logarithm is a number assigned to a positive quantity so that multiplication of quantities corresponds to addition of their logarithms, provided the same base and normalization are used.

For base ten, for example, one thousand has logarithm three because ten multiplied by itself three times gives one thousand. One hundred thousand has logarithm five. Multiply the original numbers and you get one hundred million, whose logarithm is eight. The two large-number multiplication has become three plus five. For numbers that are not exact powers of ten, a sufficiently fine logarithm table supplies approximate values. The accuracy of the final multiplication depends on the table and how carefully one interpolates between entries.

The familiar modern inverse relationship is between exponentials and logarithms. If ten raised to the third power equals one thousand, the base-ten logarithm of one thousand is three. The usefulness is not limited to powers of ten. Approximate logarithms of numbers such as two, three, and seven allow almost arbitrary positive quantities to be multiplied by adding table entries and then looking up the reverse transformation. This requires careful interpolation when the desired input lies between printed entries. The table user is doing approximate numerical analysis, even if the instructions are expressed as practical rules rather than theorems about continuous functions.

That sounds straightforward when stated in the notation of later algebra. Napier's actual construction in the early seventeenth century was different. He developed a mathematical analogy involving moving points, one traveling at constant speed and another slowing as a distance remaining decreased. The changing motion linked an arithmetic change of one measure with a proportional change of another. His published logarithms were organized around sines and a scaling constant of ten million, useful for the trigonometric computations of the time. Their normalization did not match the modern common logarithm or the modern natural logarithm.

That historical distinction matters because the word Napierian is sometimes used for natural logarithms, those with base e. It is tempting to imagine Napier publishing tables of the natural logarithm we use in calculus classes. He did not. His original table placed the logarithm of the whole sine at zero and used a special scale, with logarithms increasing as the associated sine decreased. The underlying relation can be connected mathematically to natural logarithms, but this is a modern interpretation, not his original presentation.

Napier's choice of sines and ten million becomes clearer when one imagines a printed trigonometric table with a fixed number of decimal places. Multiplying by a large scaling constant lets values be stored as integers of convenient magnitude, reducing the need to write and interpret long fractions. His logarithm convention was tied to this practice. In modern notation the relation can be expressed using an ordinary logarithm after introducing a scale and a sign, but doing so too quickly would hide the historical choice. The inventor began with the computational objects astronomers actually used, not with a calculus textbook's natural exponential function.

Napier labored for years to build tables. We should imagine neither a moment of magic nor an automated industrial process. Tables require a method for generating numbers, a procedure for checking them, and decisions about rounding. If the table is inaccurate, every user repeats the error. If it is difficult to read, the promised time savings vanish. In 1614 Napier published *Mirifici Logarithmorum Canonis Descriptio*, describing the logarithmic table and its use. The original Latin title refers to a description of a wonderful canon of logarithms. This was a publication aimed at people doing heavy computations, not just at a handful of abstract number theorists.

Readers in different regions also approached the new tool through translations and explanations. A printed Latin treatise could circulate in learned networks, but a working calculator might benefit from an English account or a compact set of instructions. The transformation from specialist publication to routine practice required teaching users how to find two entries, add them, and reverse the lookup. A numerical table is not self-explanatory. This is part of the reason Briggs and other educators were so important in the aftermath of the 1614 publication: a method has to be made teachable before it can become infrastructure.

The response was rapid because the problem was so recognizable. Astronomers and mathematical practitioners had strong reasons to reduce multiplication and division to simpler operations. The printed tables also had social limits: a book had to reach a user, the user had to understand the new method, and the output had to be checked. Napier's achievement depended on printers, translators, and mathematicians who taught the procedure to others. The practical revolution was not completed by publication alone.

Henry Briggs, a professor at Gresham College in London, became one of the most important early collaborators. He recognized the value of the tables and sought a normalization easier for everyday decimal calculation. Briggs visited Napier in Scotland in 1615; they discussed modifying the tables so that the logarithm of one would be zero and powers of ten would receive convenient values. Briggs returned again in 1616. Later accounts preserve recollections about who suggested exactly which normalization, but they agree on the important collaboration: Napier's original concept and Briggs's sustained calculation helped establish the common logarithmic tables used widely afterward.

The difference between the original and the improved tables is more than a matter of taste. A well-chosen convention simplifies interpretation. If the logarithm of one is zero, multiplying a number by one leaves the logarithm unchanged, just as arithmetic demands. If the logarithm of ten is one, each power of ten shifts the decimal scale by an integer. Decimal logarithms make calculations with ordinary base-ten numerals especially convenient. Briggs undertook the enormous work of computing accurate tables, publishing early tables in 1617 and his larger *Arithmetica Logarithmica* in 1624, after Napier's death.

Other researchers developed logarithmic methods independently or in related forms. The Swiss instrument maker and mathematician Joost Bürgi investigated a logarithmic progression and published his work later. It is difficult to reduce the history to a single person inventing a completely finished idea in one isolated year. Napier published first and created a powerful form; Briggs and others made related choices and expanded practical tables. The chronology of printing, manuscript work, and mutual awareness is part of the historical problem.

The rods have an additional educational value. A child who has learned the multiplication table can see why the diagonal entries work: each position contributes to a particular power of ten, and carrying moves a group of ten units into the next place. A mature mathematical practitioner might prefer logarithms for enormous products, but the rods help with everyday arithmetic without first requiring a logarithm table. These were different interfaces to numerical work, each suited to particular users and problems. It would be inaccurate to treat the rods merely as a prototype of the logarithmic tables or the tables as a written version of the rods.

Napier also pursued more tangible calculating aids. His so-called bones were rods or strips marked with numerical information arranged to help carry out multiplication. A set of rods representing a number could be laid together, and a chosen row would give the relevant partial products. The user read values along diagonals and performed short additions with carrying. It is not a modern pocket calculator, but it mechanizes some of the bookkeeping in arithmetic and reduces the mental burden of repeatedly recalling a multiplication table.

Take the number four hundred sixty-seven and multiply it by six. A strip for four, one for six, and one for seven can display the individual products twenty-four, thirty-six, and forty-two, each placed in tens-and-units form. Read across the rods in the proper diagonal order and carry as needed; the result is two thousand eight hundred two. The rods do not remove every addition. They organize the small products so that a longer calculation can be performed consistently. For a user confronting many repeated multiplications, organization itself is a valuable technology.

Napier described his rods in *Rabdologiae*, published in 1617, the year of his death. The same publication discussed related approaches to calculating. His interests in mechanical arithmetic and logarithmic tables were complementary rather than competing: one reduced arithmetic to different operations, the other made those operations easier to execute. Both are examples of information encoded in material form. A chart or rod does not discover the answer by inspiration; it stores a relationship someone has already painstakingly constructed.

Another part of his mathematical legacy concerns spherical trigonometry, a subject important for astronomy and navigation. Triangles drawn on a sphere follow relationships different from triangles in a flat plane. Napier introduced useful rules and mnemonics for right spherical triangles, often called Napier's rules or analogies. These did not replace the geometry of the heavens; they made its numerical applications more manageable. The connection to logarithms is direct: trigonometry supplies many of the multiplicative calculations that logarithmic tables were designed to simplify.

Napier died in 1617 at Merchiston. His son Robert subsequently published a fuller account of the construction of the logarithms in 1619. The difference between the 1614 description and the posthumous construction matters to historians asking how the tables were developed. A table can announce a useful method without revealing every step by which it was calculated. Later publication of construction details allows researchers to reconstruct the reasoning with greater confidence while still acknowledging the role of editing and family custody of manuscripts.

As printed tables became common, they also shaped the teaching of science. A student could learn a physical law, arrange a trigonometric relation, and then use a table to make a prediction. More of the curriculum could concern which model to use and how to interpret measurements, because the arithmetical workload had been reduced. At the same time a table user needed numerical discipline: mantissas, characteristic parts, interpolation, and sensible checking of decimal place. The change was not simply from hard calculations to effortless ones; it was a redistribution of intellectual labor between table makers and table readers.

The broader afterlife of logarithms illustrates why a computational tool deserves a place in the history of mathematics. In the seventeenth century the ability to multiply through addition of table entries reduced clerical labor and made demanding astronomical calculations more practicable. In the centuries afterward, logarithmic scales contributed to slide rules, navigation, engineering, statistics, and mathematical analysis. Today's computer may calculate logarithms so quickly that a student never sees a paper table. The mathematical transformation remains important, including in the analysis of growth, decay, and orders of magnitude.

But the history should not be narrated as though logarithms were an inevitable destination from medieval arithmetic to the computer. They appeared in a particular setting, among people who needed trigonometric calculations and had access to printing, mathematical correspondence, and time-consuming hand computation. Napier was a landowner, a polemical religious writer, a parent, and a mathematical inventor. Briggs was a teacher and indefatigable tabulator. Printers and later table makers helped turn a new mathematical relationship into standard practice.

Return to the astronomer who needs to multiply two difficult numbers, again and again. A logarithm table does not change the celestial objects being studied. It changes the amount of human work required to study them. That is why Napier's invention mattered. It made a mathematical relationship into infrastructure for scientific inquiry. And the person who made it was not sheltered from political and religious disputes. His life shows that the making of mathematics can occur within, rather than apart from, the conflicts and obligations of a specific society.

## Historical companion — not spoken

### Chronology and evidence

- **1550:** Born at Merchiston to Archibald Napier and Janet Bothwell.
- **1560s:** Entered St Andrews at a young age; details of continental travel remain debated and are not treated here as firm biography.
- **1593:** Published *A Plaine Discovery of the Whole Revelation of Saint John*, a major theological and political work.
- **1614:** *Mirifici Logarithmorum Canonis Descriptio* introduced the published logarithmic table and uses.
- **1615–1616:** Henry Briggs visited Napier; discussions led to improved decimal conventions.
- **1617:** *Rabdologiae* on calculating rods and methods; Napier died.
- **1619:** Robert Napier published *Constructio* posthumously; **1624:** Briggs's *Arithmetica Logarithmica*.

### Mathematical companion

- **Modern logarithmic rule:** \(\log_b(xy)=\log_b x+\log_b y\) for \(x,y>0\) and \(b>0\), \(b\ne1\). A finite printed table is approximate.
- **Napier's original logs:** Linked to a geometric/kinematic model scaled around \(10^7\), and not identical to \(\log_{10}\) or \(\ln\). Avoid equating the historical term *Napierian* with the original tables.
- **Briggs:** Improved base-ten table conventions with Napier's collaboration and substantial independent numerical labor.
- **Napier's bones:** Rods display single-digit multiplication results in place-value arrangements for diagonal addition and carrying.
- **Spherical trigonometry:** Different from Euclidean plane-triangle trigonometry; astronomical needs made efficient computational aids valuable.
