# 053000 — Leonhard Euler I: Basel, Bernoulli, and the first Petersburg years

**Track:** History · People and ideas. **Focus:** a Swiss education, Russian academy life, family and first mathematical breakthroughs. **Background:** historical curiosity; all mathematics needed for the examples is introduced in narration.

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

In the middle of the eighteenth century, a mathematician could spend years helping to make maps, evaluating ship designs, arranging an academy's finances, advising on canals, and teaching people he might never meet through printed letters. All this could be part of the same job as discovering a new theorem about infinite series. Leonhard Euler's working life makes little sense if we detach the mathematics from those duties. The equations familiar to modern students are the surviving shorthand for a much larger career carried on through households, letters, experiments, institutions, and the changing patronage of European rulers.

Euler lived from seventeen oh seven to seventeen eighty-three. He was born in Basel, grew up nearby in Riehen, worked for many years in Saint Petersburg and Berlin, and ended his life back in Saint Petersburg. Those cities occupied different political worlds. Basel was a Swiss republic with a distinguished mathematical tradition. Saint Petersburg was a new imperial capital that recruited foreign specialists. Berlin was becoming an ambitious royal center of learning under Frederick the Second of Prussia. None was a neutral backdrop. At every move Euler faced questions about money, family, security, academic loyalty, and the sort of intellectual work a ruler would support.

Basel was small beside Paris or London, but its mathematical culture reached far beyond Swiss borders. At the end of the seventeenth century the brothers Jakob and Johann Bernoulli had made the new calculus a central part of their work. Their disagreements and correspondence with Leibniz, l'Hôpital, and others brought students into a European argument over what a changing quantity could mean and how one could calculate with it. We can call this a scientific community, provided we do not picture a modern graduate department. Students had to negotiate access through patrons, family networks, private instruction, books, and letters. A particularly gifted student could find guidance, but a paying position afterward was another matter.

His father, Paul Euler, was a Protestant minister who had studied mathematics while preparing for the church. His mother, Margaretha Brucker, came from another minister's family. The household expected Leonhard to acquire the education appropriate for religious service. Paul could also teach him mathematics, and the family had a connection with the Bernoullis, one of the most influential mathematical families in Europe. Johann Bernoulli had studied alongside Paul, and later became Leonhard's most important early guide.

When Leonhard entered the University of Basel in seventeen twenty, the boundary between general philosophy, theology, and mathematics was still quite different from the modern division into academic departments. The boy was preparing for a profession his family knew and respected. He completed work in philosophy, including a comparison of the views of Descartes and Newton, and began the theological course. But the mathematics took hold. Johann Bernoulli was too busy to offer a conventional private course. In Euler's own later account, the arrangement was more demanding: Bernoulli told him to read difficult works independently and come on Sundays with the places he could not understand. That account, preserved as an autobiographical recollection, gives us something more useful than an origin legend. It shows a student being taught how to read, struggle with, and question advanced mathematics rather than waiting for every step to be dictated.

Euler's father eventually consented to the change in direction. By the time the young man finished his studies, he had written on mechanics and submitted a prize essay about how ships should be rigged. The subject may look far from pure mathematics, but navigation and shipbuilding drew on the same new methods of motion, forces, and calculation that interested leading analysts. Euler did not win the Paris Academy's first prize for the essay; he placed second. He also failed to obtain a professorship at Basel. His first major professional opening was therefore not at home.

Euler's early work also reveals what he inherited from his teacher without becoming merely Johann Bernoulli's disciple. The calculus had already acquired rival notations and contested philosophical foundations. Its practitioners could obtain impressive answers to problems involving tangents, falling bodies, or areas, while leaving open questions about infinitesimals and infinite sums. The younger Euler learned the successful methods and then made the transition from ingenious individual tricks to large systems of calculation. One of his lifelong concerns was to show how an entire family of apparently different problems could be reduced to a manageable set of equations. He could be bold about manipulating infinite expressions and remarkably careful about the details of a mechanical model; the mixture tells us about the eighteenth century, not just about his individual temperament.

The journey away from Basel was a professional migration, not a ceremonial tour. Switzerland trained more educated young men than its own universities and churches could always employ. Petersburg offered pay and the possibility of serious work, but it was far from home, involved language differences and difficult travel, and depended on the uncertain fortunes of a dynasty. The death of Empress Catherine the First in the very year Euler arrived left the young academy vulnerable. Its existence did not simply follow from the wishes of Peter the Great; people had to renew that commitment with salaries, decrees, books, and places to work. Some foreign scholars left. Euler stayed and made the foreign appointment into his principal career.

The invitation came from Saint Petersburg. Peter the Great had tried to remake Russia's technical and administrative institutions, and the academy established under his successors was part of that larger enterprise. It sought scientists from abroad to train people, investigate practical problems, and help give imperial prestige a scholarly form. Daniel Bernoulli and Jakob Hermann were already connected with the Petersburg academy. Their presence mattered: Euler was entering a community, not simply accepting a lonely appointment in a faraway country. In seventeen twenty-seven he traveled across Europe and arrived in the Russian capital. His assigned duties shifted toward mathematics and physics, and for a time his academy position was supplemented by a naval appointment.

It is easy to imagine his Petersburg work as uninterrupted research. The academy was more complicated. Its scholars needed salaries, printing facilities, instruments, contacts in other countries, and enough stability to pursue questions that did not immediately interest administrators. Euler worked with Daniel Bernoulli, the geographer Joseph-Nicolas Delisle, and the mathematician and correspondent Christian Goldbach. Letters could sustain an argument long after a colleague had moved away. Goldbach, for example, posed number-theoretic questions whose significance survived the particular correspondence. When Daniel Bernoulli returned to Basel in seventeen thirty-three, Euler inherited the senior mathematics position. This was not merely an honor. The improved income changed what he could afford to do in his private life.

Daniel Bernoulli's presence mattered intellectually as well as personally. The two men approached questions about fluids, vibrating strings, and the relation between forces and motion with shared technical language but different emphases. Later historical accounts sometimes arrange their correspondence as a contest to decide who invented a formula first. Such contests can obscure how rapidly an idea changed as one scholar posed a question, another tried a method, and a third found a new application. Petersburg gathered mathematicians, astronomers, naturalists, and mechanics under an institutional roof, but it could also separate them through rival claims to funding and influence.

Christian Goldbach provided another kind of partnership: one maintained by letters and by problems chosen for their own sake. A question now called Goldbach's conjecture asks whether every even integer greater than two is the sum of two primes. The conjecture is not Euler's theorem, nor did Euler establish it. The exchange matters because it shows a mathematician famous for mechanics spending time on a problem that was not obviously useful to a state. The same hands that drew up maps could fill pages with divisibility, remainders, and the surprising behavior of prime numbers. In eighteenth-century practice those interests were not yet distributed among separate professional specialties.

In January seventeen thirty-four, Euler married Katharina Gsell, whose family also had Swiss connections and whose father was a painter working in Saint Petersburg. Their home grew busy. Thirteen children were born to the couple, although only five survived infancy. These facts belong in the story of a working mathematician. The academy did not provide the modern separation between a laboratory career and a family sphere. Education, illness, domestic support, and successive bereavements were part of the conditions under which work was written. Later recollections describe Euler thinking through mathematics while children were around him. That should not be turned into a claim that family life presented no demands; it shows a household and a scholarly practice occupying the same time and space.

Euler helped prepare maps of the Russian Empire, studied mechanics and ship design, wrote on astronomical and physical problems, and developed techniques in infinite series and differential equations. His work had a special ambition: to make problems of motion and changing quantities systematically calculable. Newton had provided powerful principles of mechanics. Euler and his contemporaries increasingly expressed those principles in an analytical language that could be adapted to many different physical settings. His book on mechanics in the seventeen thirties was one important step. Meanwhile the questions being exchanged with colleagues ranged from the distribution of numbers to vibrating strings, from astronomical orbits to the mathematics of the simplest networks.

His 1736 Mechanica helped recast the movement of physical bodies through analysis. Newton's Principia had shown how much could be inferred from laws of motion and geometrical argument. Euler sought procedures that could take the changing position of a body, write down relations for its motion, and produce calculable results. The contribution was neither a repudiation of Newton nor simply a translation from geometry into algebra. It was a shift toward describing whole classes of possible motions using the same formal tools. That ambition would eventually matter for bodies moving in fluids, for planetary calculations, and for engineering problems that no single elegant diagram could settle.

Consider a particular situation: a body moving through a straight line with a speed that changes over time. If we know how the speed changes at each instant, we still need a way of reconstructing the distance traveled. Calculus connects those questions, integrating a rate of change to find accumulated movement. A real physical model adds gravity, resistance, or the interaction of several objects. Euler's importance lies partly in making it natural to ask for equations that could handle those additions. The explanation here is modern and elementary, not a page copied from Mechanica; it shows why the mathematical style was useful.

The decade also brought serious illness and deteriorating vision. Euler himself associated some eye problems with the strain of cartographic work; modern historians have questioned any simple explanation that attributes a specific loss of sight to overwork. The degree and timing of the damage are not completely recoverable from recollections and portraits. A familiar story portrays blindness as the almost magical price of genius. It is less misleading to say that Euler had significant sight problems at various stages of his career, yet continued to work within a growing circle of colleagues and assistants whose intellectual labor mattered.

Another problem came from the streets of Königsberg, where four land regions were joined by seven bridges. Could a walker cross every bridge exactly once? Euler set aside the shape of the river and represented each region by a point and each bridge by a line connecting two points. The question then became one of parity. Apart from the start and finish, each arrival over a bridge must be paired with a departure over another. Such a walk can therefore have at most two regions with an odd number of bridges. Königsberg had four, so the walk was impossible. Euler's 1736 analysis is now treated as an origin point for graph theory, though its later vocabulary did not yet exist.

The bridge problem is sometimes presented as proof that all of Euler's mathematics was an abstract game. It actually shows his flexibility. By removing distances and angles from a city map he discovered a simpler kind of structure: connection without measurement. In other work he did exactly the opposite, adding fine-grained measurement to the behavior of ships and planets. He was not committed to making every problem more abstract or more concrete. He asked which details were essential to the answer.

A second example came from the sum of squared reciprocals. Consider adding one, then one quarter, then one ninth, then one sixteenth, and so on. The sum clearly grows, but the additions become rapidly smaller. What is its exact value? Euler established that the infinite total is pi squared divided by six. This is striking because pi first appears to belong to circles, whereas the series is built from ordinary whole numbers. Euler's routes to this result used manipulations involving infinite products and power series that require care by modern standards. We should not pretend that every step of his eighteenth-century arguments was written in the later language of uniform convergence. The achievement lies both in the discovery of a precise connection and in the powerful new tools it inspired others to justify and extend.

Why should a sum of fractions know anything about a circle? One starting point is to compare algebraic expressions that vanish at certain values. For a simple polynomial, such as the product of x minus two and x minus three, the roots two and three determine factors and therefore constrain the coefficients. Euler's calculation treated the sine function, which has infinitely many zeros, as if a related product over those zeros could be used to compare coefficients in a power series. The resulting relation identified the sum of reciprocal squares with pi squared over six. A modern analyst must justify the convergence and the interchange of limiting processes; Euler's published reasoning belongs to an earlier framework. The insight was not merely that one pretty identity came out right. He demonstrated a way to connect the behavior of functions to the arithmetic of their roots.

This is also a good place to resist the idea of a single father of every result. The sum was already a famous challenge associated with the Bernoulli family. Euler answered it and extended the methods; the question, the readers who recognized its difficulty, and the language he inherited all mattered. Meanwhile, his academy colleagues and his wife and children made the working conditions in which the result could be communicated and preserved. The next decades would carry this program to Berlin, but the distinctive features of Euler's mathematical life were already visible in Petersburg: fast-moving correspondence, intensive calculation, the demands of state employment, and the hope that a general method could turn many difficult problems into ordinary work.

## Historical companion (not narrated)

### Historical chronology and interpretation
- Born in Basel in 1707 and raised in Riehen; studied theology and philosophy as well as mathematics under Johann Bernoulli's guidance.
- Sought positions in Basel; his work on ship masts received recognition in the Paris Academy competition, but he did not secure a local chair.
- Reached Petersburg in 1727 amid a succession crisis and uncertain support for the imperial academy.
- Succeeded Daniel Bernoulli in the senior mathematical post in 1733; married Katharina Gsell in 1734; researched mechanics, maps, number theory and series.
- Published *Mechanica* and the Königsberg bridges paper in the 1730s. His account of eyesight deterioration has contested causes.
- In 1741 left for Berlin at Frederick II's invitation.

### Mathematical display notes
- A connected graph has a trail using every edge exactly once only if it has zero or two vertices of odd degree (with the circuit special case when all degrees are even). Euler's Königsberg bridge arrangement has four odd-degree land regions. This is a modern restatement of his obstruction.
- The Basel problem: \(\sum_{n=1}^\infty n^{-2}=\pi^2/6\). Euler's historical infinite-product manipulation differs in its justification from later convergence theory.
- Contemporary introductory model: \(dx/dt=v(t)\) and \(x(t)-x(0)=\int_0^t v(s)\,ds\). This is not a quotation from the 1736 *Mechanica*.
