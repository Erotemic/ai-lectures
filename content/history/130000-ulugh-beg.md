# 130000 — Ulugh Beg: Measuring the sky in a city of empire

**Track:** History — People and ideas. **Focus:** Timurid Central Asia; mobile imperial court, Samarqand's schools and observatory, collaborative mathematical astronomy, trigonometric tables, political breakdown, and the transmission of a star catalogue. **Background:** Angles, fractions and an elementary idea of a mathematical table are explained in context.

Imagine trying to measure a star's place without a camera, a motorized telescope, an electronic clock, or a computer. You know that the star will appear to move across the sky during the night. You can build a sighting instrument, mark a direction, take readings, and compare them with observations made years earlier. But the whole operation is vulnerable to small errors. A tiny uncertainty in an angle can become a large uncertainty in a table that someone else will use to predict where the star should appear. And a table intended to last a generation has to outlive the instrument and the observer.

In fifteenth-century Samarqand, in the region now called Uzbekistan, a ruler named Ulugh Beg made this problem the business of an extraordinary mathematical institution. He supported a school, gathered astronomers and mathematicians, and built an observatory with a measuring arc large enough to be partly embedded in the ground. He also understood the mathematics himself. The resulting star catalogue and astronomical tables were neither the private discoveries of a solitary prince nor merely a ceremonial tribute to a powerful patron. They were the work of a community assembled and sustained by political power, and they survived a political catastrophe that destroyed much of the world in which they had been made.

Ulugh Beg was born in 1394 in Soltaniyeh, in the region of present-day Iran. His given name was Muhammad Taraghay; Ulugh Beg is the name by which history usually remembers him. His grandfather was Timur, the conqueror whose campaigns transformed vast territories of Central and Western Asia. It is tempting to tell this story as a contrast between a violent grandfather and a peaceful grandson, as if a love of astronomy erased the conditions that made the observatory possible. The real history is less comfortable. Ulugh Beg inherited the resources, expectations, rivalries and military responsibilities of a conquering dynasty. His access to scholars and skilled workers did not exist apart from that inheritance.

Timur's court was a moving court. Its leaders, families and retainers followed campaigns and journeys, and Ulugh Beg spent part of his childhood inside that mobile political world. Samarqand was an imperial center, but the child was not simply raised in a fixed palace overlooking a future university. When Timur died in 1405, his dominions did not pass quietly to a single uncontested successor. Rival branches of the family struggled for power. Ulugh Beg's father, Shah Rukh, eventually established himself as the dominant Timurid ruler, governing chiefly from Herat, in what is now Afghanistan. He placed the young Ulugh Beg in charge of Samarqand and the surrounding region of Transoxiana in 1409.

The geographical names matter here. Soltaniyeh is in present-day Iran; Herat is in present-day Afghanistan; Samarqand is in present-day Uzbekistan. None of those modern national boundaries describes a fifteenth-century passport or citizenship. They are useful ways to locate places on a present-day map. The political reality was the Timurid realm, its provincial governments, its cities, trading networks, military households and cultivated courts. A future reader who searches for mathematics in Uzbekistan must be able to find this lecture, but must not be led to think that Ulugh Beg ruled the modern republic of Uzbekistan.

How was mathematics taught within such a courtly setting? There were manuscripts and commentaries, teachers who answered questions, and students who had to learn not just results but computational procedures. Astronomical knowledge depended on numbers written in systems that might seem unfamiliar today. Much calculation used sexagesimal fractions, inherited from older astronomical practice: a unit was divided into sixty parts, each of those into sixty smaller parts, and so on. It resembles the way we still divide a degree into minutes and seconds, and an hour into minutes and seconds. A result carried to five or six sexagesimal places can encode remarkable precision. But the places have to be kept straight. One misplaced digit in a handwritten table can make a carefully observed star appear to wander.

Shah Rukh's court at Herat and Ulugh Beg's court at Samarqand were both nodes in the intellectual and artistic culture of the Timurid world. They were not interchangeable institutions. Political patronage drew skilled workers and scholars into a place, and the cost of their work had to be supported. The famous buildings at Samarqand expressed authority through architecture, but their scientific value depended on activity that could not be seen from a ceremonial facade. Teachers needed students, astronomers needed clear nights and reliable instruments, and calculators needed time. A school that lasted long enough for pupils to become colleagues could create something a one-time royal commission could not.

A teenager could be appointed to govern a province without being independent of his father. Shah Rukh remained the senior power; Ulugh Beg's authority depended on political relationships that were always subject to change. Still, decades of relative control gave him the ability to create institutions. He supported monumental architecture and religious education, and he made mathematical sciences an important part of the intellectual life of his capital. This was not the founding of science in Central Asia. It was a new concentration of resources within a far older tradition of scholarship in Arabic and Persian, including the astronomy of Baghdad, the observatory at Maragha, and the mathematical achievements of scholars across the Islamic world.

Around 1417, building began on Ulugh Beg's celebrated madrasa at the Registan in Samarqand; it was completed around 1420. A madrasa is often translated as a school, but that English word can make its intellectual setting sound too narrow. Such institutions could teach religious subjects alongside forms of logic, mathematics and astronomy; their character depended on teachers, patronage and local circumstances. Ulugh Beg wanted people who could do difficult mathematical work, not merely decorate his court with learned titles. Among the important scholars associated with the Samarqand circle were Qadi Zada al-Rumi, Jamshid al-Kashi and, later, Ali Qushji.

Al-Kashi's letters to his father give historians a particularly valuable kind of evidence: an account from someone working in the mathematical environment rather than a legend invented centuries later. In these letters he praises Ulugh Beg's mathematical ability and describes discussions in which the ruler took a serious part. We need not convert this praise into a claim that Ulugh Beg personally calculated every entry in the astronomical tables. A patron who can understand hard mathematics contributes differently from a patron who cannot, but the astronomical program depended on observers, instrument makers, calculators, teachers and editors. The letters help establish that the prince's engagement was real. They do not eliminate the contributions of his colleagues.

The letters from al-Kashi are particularly instructive because they complicate the portrait of the patron. Al-Kashi was not a nameless assistant who supplied digits to a royal author. He had already developed sophisticated mathematical and astronomical methods, and he appears in the record as an independent researcher with judgments about his colleagues' abilities. Qadi Zada was an important teacher and mathematician in his own right. Ulugh Beg's intellectual participation should be understood in this setting: he could discuss a problem with people whose expertise was indispensable, and he could enable those people to work together. That was a form of mathematical leadership, but it was not the same thing as solitary ownership of every result.

What did those colleagues need to know? Astronomy required geometry, numerical approximation and sustained record keeping. To locate a star, an astronomer could measure an angular distance, perhaps at the moment the star crossed a north-south reference plane called the meridian. Measurements made on different nights have to be related to a consistent coordinate system. A spherical sky demands spherical geometry: a triangle drawn between directions on a sphere does not obey exactly the same rules as a triangle drawn on flat paper. The arc of a great circle, a circle whose center agrees with the sphere's center, is a natural way to express distances between directions. The daily turning of the sky, changes with season, and the location of the observer all enter the calculations.

Take a familiar example. On a flat map, north and east look like perpendicular directions. On a globe, paths of constant longitude meet at the pole. A triangle whose vertices are the North Pole and two points on the equator can have two right angles at the equator, plus another angle at the pole. Its angles add to more than one hundred eighty degrees. If you try to compute stellar directions using only flat geometry, the errors become substantial as the triangles grow. Astronomers had long developed methods for spherical triangles, but a major observatory needed them in a form that could be computed repeatedly and reliably.

Suppose an astronomer observes a star at its highest point in the sky, the instant it crosses the local meridian. The angle from the horizon to the star is its altitude. At a known observing latitude, that altitude supplies information about the star's declination, its position north or south of the celestial equator. The precise formula depends on which side of the zenith the star passes. Some objects never rise at a given place, and others remain above the horizon all night. Consequently a catalogue made at Samarqand cannot contain a direct local observation of every star visible from every part of Earth. The observatory's geographical latitude was itself part of the geometry, not incidental background decoration.

One can also ask how errors enter the result. Near the horizon, starlight passes obliquely through more atmosphere and bends perceptibly. A thick engraved line on an instrument may correspond to several possible readings, and two observers may not sight a faint object in quite the same way. Even a magnificent fixed instrument cannot repeal atmospheric refraction or imperfections in human measurement. Good tables require a combination of observations, mathematical corrections, comparisons and judgment about which digits have earned their place.

That brings us to trigonometric tables. The sine of an angle is a ratio that describes a direction. In a right triangle, it is the length opposite the chosen angle divided by the hypotenuse. For astronomers, that ratio is a computational tool: once values have been tabulated accurately, one can translate between angles and lengths without constructing a new triangle every time. The sine of thirty degrees is one half. The sine of sixty degrees is about zero point eight six six. Those are ordinary values. A demanding astronomical table needs many more angles and many more correct digits.

The real difficulty is that an error in one entry can contaminate later computations. Suppose a table gives a sine value too large by one part in a thousand. For some everyday measurements this might be acceptable. For a great angular instrument designed to resolve small differences in stellar positions, one part in a thousand is enormous. A much finer table becomes valuable only when the observations and the reduction procedures justify it. Writing many digits does not automatically make a number accurate. The mathematics has to provide them, and the instrument has to support a comparable degree of confidence in the angle being measured.

A striking problem discussed in accounts of the Samarqand work was the sine of one degree. We can make its structure understandable without asking the listener to memorize a long decimal. There is a standard trigonometric identity: the sine of three times an angle equals three times the sine of the angle, minus four times the cube of that sine. If we call the sine of one degree an unknown number, then the known sine of three degrees gives us a cubic equation in that unknown. It is a little like knowing the dimensions of a shape built from three repeating pieces and asking for the dimension of a single piece. The relationship is exact; extracting the unknown may require approximation.

One can improve a first guess by checking how far the cubic equation is from being satisfied and then adjusting the guess. Repeating the correction yields increasingly accurate digits. This is the broad mathematical idea behind numerical root finding. Accounts credit the Samarqand circle, with Ulugh Beg's own mathematical participation, with exceptional precision in this calculation. But we should not collapse a collaborative tradition into one person's pen. Al-Kashi was himself a formidable calculator, and precise attribution of particular procedures is a historical question. What matters mathematically is that trigonometric accuracy was a research problem, not a matter of simply copying an inherited table.

The tables also reveal a difference between a number that can be calculated to great precision and a physical observation that can be trusted to that same precision. The sine of one degree is a mathematical constant. In principle its digits can be computed again and again without observing the sky. The measured position of a star, by contrast, has to be inferred from an instrument at a particular place and time, and the uncertainty in that measurement cannot be eliminated by adding digits to a numerical table. The collaboration between exact numerical work and imperfect observational evidence was one of the deepest features of mathematical astronomy. It remains familiar in modern science, where sophisticated models often produce more decimal places than measurements can defend.

The astronomical institution gave such calculations a physical counterpart. In the 1420s, Ulugh Beg established the great observatory at Samarqand. Sources give differing dates for the observatory's foundation or construction phases, and we should not turn that difference into a fictional precise opening day. The site became known for a huge fixed meridian instrument, part of whose curved measuring surface was set below ground. If one wants to measure an angle very finely, a long arc has an advantage: a tiny difference in direction corresponds to a larger distance along its circumference. Imagine marking one degree on a small classroom protractor. Now imagine spreading that same degree along an arc tens of meters in radius. The marks can be separated more clearly, although construction, alignment, refraction and reading errors still matter.

Why put part of such an instrument underground? A tall free-standing structure would move slightly with temperature, wind and settling. Embedding a substantial portion in rock helps stabilize the reference geometry. The surviving trench and marble remnants at Samarqand still show that the observatory was built around a very ambitious physical geometry. That material evidence matters. We are not relying only on manuscripts that praise a prince. We possess remains that constrain what the building and its measuring program could have been.

An instrument alone does not supply a star catalogue. Someone must decide which stars to observe, when to observe them, how to identify them consistently, how to correct the readings, and how to compare the new coordinates with earlier catalogues. Ptolemy's ancient catalogue had traveled through centuries of astronomical study. Al-Sufi, in the tenth century, had produced another influential treatment of the fixed stars. The Samarqand astronomers inherited that literature. They also encountered the problem of precession: the coordinate grid tied to Earth's orientation slowly changes relative to the distant stars. A position quoted for one epoch cannot be carried indefinitely into another by assuming the grid itself has remained fixed.

Imagine labeling the location of a bookshelf in a room, and then slowly rotating the entire floor plan while leaving the shelf where it was. The coordinates change even though the shelf does not move. Precession is not literally a turning floor, and stellar motions can matter as well, but the analogy captures why a copied ancient coordinate may no longer match a new measurement. One can update old coordinates by calculating the expected displacement, or one can measure again. These approaches are not interchangeable. Updating assumes the old observation and the correction are reliable. Measuring again confronts the sky with new instruments and new sources of error.

The catalogue associated with Ulugh Beg's *Zij-i Sultani* is important because it included independently remeasured stellar positions, not merely a uniform update of Ptolemy. A zij was more than a list of stars. It was an astronomical handbook of numerical tables and procedures for calculating the positions and movements of heavenly bodies. It could contain calendar systems, the motion of the Sun and planets, and the trigonometric values needed to use the other tables. A star catalogue is only one part of that working apparatus. To say that a zij was useful is to say that it allowed a trained reader to perform a family of computations that might otherwise require a major observational establishment.

Modern descriptions differ on the exact number of stars in the catalogue and on the year in which the whole work should be considered complete. Some give an early completion date around 1437 for the star list, while other accounts treat the astronomical tables as reaching their later form in the early 1440s. The difference is understandable if the catalogue, the larger handbook, later revisions and different manuscript witnesses are distinguished. We should not pretend that a book assembled through a long institutional program sprang into existence on one unambiguous date. The evidence is strong that the Samarqand circle made an important new catalogue in the fifteenth century. It is less simple to assign every leaf of the surviving textual tradition to one instant.

The power of the work can be seen in its calculated values of time. Astronomers needed the length of the year, but there is more than one meaning of a year depending on what celestial reference is chosen. A calendar attempts to keep civil dates aligned with recurring seasonal or astronomical events. A fractional error in the year, repeated annually, eventually becomes a visible drift. A table with a carefully determined year length therefore had consequences for calendars and other computations. Precision was not only a contest between great astronomers. It was the difference between a table that remained serviceable and one that had to be corrected repeatedly.

The observational program also belonged to an older culture of astrology and court patronage. We do history badly when we project a clean modern partition between astronomy as science and astrology as superstition back into every medieval household. Calculating planetary positions had practical and cultural uses that included the calendar, the times of religious observance and astrological judgments. Ulugh Beg's observatory was mathematically serious without being a twenty-first-century physics department. A ruler could regard knowledge of the heavens as politically impressive, intellectually beautiful and practically useful at the same time.

The observational results were valuable partly because they could be checked against other material. If a star recorded as lying in one direction actually appeared elsewhere, the discrepancy might arise from an inaccurate old observation, precession, a copied number, or an error in the new reduction. An observatory could not settle this by an appeal to the rank of its patron. It required another observation and an explanation. There was thus a productive separation between political power, which built the institution, and mathematical authority, which had to be earned by demonstrable computation. That distinction makes the Samarqand story more interesting than a tale of a prince who happened to admire numbers.

There was a further tension. The mathematics that grew inside Ulugh Beg's patronage did not guarantee the security of his government. His father Shah Rukh died in 1447, and the long-standing arrangement of power was shaken. Rival Timurid claims could no longer be managed simply by relying on the senior ruler's authority. Ulugh Beg tried to defend his position and became caught in military conflicts, including a struggle involving his son Abd al-Latif. After defeat in 1449, he was killed. The surviving accounts describe a violent dynastic end. A simplified story sometimes makes him the victim of people who hated science as such. Politics, inheritance and military power are sufficient to explain a great deal of the conflict; the evidence does not support turning the entire episode into a timeless duel between astronomers and religion.

Nor did mathematics cease the moment its patron died. Scholars carried training, methods and texts away from Samarqand. Ali Qushji is especially important to this later movement, including teaching associated with the Ottoman world. Manuscripts were copied, discussed and translated. By the seventeenth century, Ulugh Beg's star positions had reached European readers as well: Thomas Hyde published a Latin and Persian edition at Oxford in 1665. This does not mean that every European astronomer simply adopted the Samarqand tables, or that the tables were unaffected by the changing standards of observation after Tycho Brahe. It means that the measurements entered a wider historical conversation in which astronomers compared catalogues from different centuries and regions.

Consider what happens when we compare the work of Samarqand with the later European observatories. By the sixteenth century, much finer instruments and new observing programs had changed the standards of stellar measurement. Tycho Brahe's work in Denmark did not make every earlier catalogue worthless. Earlier positions preserved information about changes and historical observational practice. Astronomers could use them as objects of comparison, but only by respecting their sources and accuracy. That is one reason translated manuscripts remained useful centuries after the original observatory ceased to function: they carried not only predictions but also evidence about what earlier observers had measured.

There is a second afterlife, written in stone. By the modern period the observatory had largely vanished as a working building. In 1908 the archaeologist Vladimir Viatkin identified and excavated important remains. What survived was not a complete fifteenth-century scientific campus, but parts of a large meridian instrument that confirmed the extraordinary scale of the project. Archaeology adds a kind of evidence that biographies and old tables cannot supply on their own. A written account may exaggerate a building's magnificence; a measured surviving arc imposes physical limits on what we are entitled to say.

A biography of Ulugh Beg is therefore also a biography of a collaboration. Al-Kashi's mathematical calculations, Qadi Zada's teaching, Ali Qushji's continuation of the scientific tradition, the anonymous craftspeople who prepared instruments, the scribes who copied tables, the ruler who assembled the resources, and the observers who repeated measurements all belong to the achievement. Giving them their places does not diminish Ulugh Beg. It helps explain his most durable contribution: he made sustained, exacting mathematical investigation possible in a major Central Asian city.

There is a reason the sine of one degree makes a fitting image for the whole life. The angle is small. The numerical challenge is real. The result depends on previous knowledge, a clever mathematical transformation, a method of approximation and checking. Its value lies not in appearing mysterious but in being reproducible. A prince could command a palace or an army, but he could not command a cubic equation to be true. The number had to survive independent reasoning. In a world of courts and dynastic claims, Ulugh Beg invested in an enterprise whose claims could be inspected by people who would live long after his rule ended.

Return, finally, to the sky over Samarqand. A star passes the meridian; the observer makes a mark; the calculator converts the angle; a table records the result; a later scholar copies the table into another language; centuries after that, an archaeologist traces the old measuring arc beneath the ground. Political institutions fall and names change. The observation cannot escape every error, but it can be checked, compared and improved. Ulugh Beg's life reminds us that mathematical precision is not divorced from human history. It is made by people, paid for by institutions, threatened by politics, and sometimes preserved in forms more durable than the states that supported it.

## Historical companion — not spoken

### Timeline, geography, and attribution

- **1394, Soltaniyeh (present-day Iran):** Birth of Muhammad Taraghay, known as Ulugh Beg, into the Timurid ruling family.
- **1405–1409, Timurid realm:** Death of Timur, succession conflicts, consolidation of Shah Rukh's rule; Ulugh Beg takes charge of Samarqand and Transoxiana in 1409.
- **c. 1417–1420, Samarqand (present-day Uzbekistan):** The Registan madrasa project and recruitment of scholars, including Qadi Zada al-Rumi and Jamshid al-Kashi.
- **1420s, Samarqand:** Observatory founded and developed; sources disagree on whether to count an early foundation, construction, or completion date as the starting year.
- **c. 1437 to early 1440s, Samarqand:** Star catalogue and associated *Zij-i Sultani* materials developed in stages. The star-list date and the larger handbook's completion date need not coincide.
- **1447–1449, Transoxiana:** Shah Rukh's death, dynastic conflicts, Ulugh Beg's defeat and killing in 1449.
- **1665, Oxford (present-day United Kingdom):** Thomas Hyde issues a Latin/Persian edition of the star catalogue; **transmission location, not a place Ulugh Beg visited**.
- **1908, Samarqand:** Archaeological identification and excavation of the observatory's surviving monumental instrument; **reception, not a lifetime event**.

### Mathematics to accompany the listening

For an angle \(\theta\), \(\sin(3\theta)=3\sin\theta-4\sin^3\theta\). With \(s=\sin 1^\circ\) and a known high-precision value of \(\sin3^\circ\), solve \(4s^3-3s+\sin3^\circ=0\) for the small positive root. The branch of the cubic is essential: not every root has the value sought. A high-precision modern check is \(\sin1^\circ\approx0.0174524064372835128\). Numerical root finding and precision claims are discussed as features of the Samarqand astronomical program; **do not attribute all of al-Kashi's computations solely to Ulugh Beg**.

In astronomical coordinates, a measured position is tied to a reference epoch. Precession can change the coordinates of a direction over centuries even if the distant star is treated as fixed. A newly observed catalogue and a purely updated copy of an old catalogue are different historical products. The *Zij* combines tables, instructions, astronomical models, and star positions; it is not simply one modern-style spreadsheet of stars.

### Evidence limits

- The fifteenth-century Timurid Empire must not be conflated with the modern countries Iran, Uzbekistan or Afghanistan. Present-day borders are map groupings only.
- Contemporary evidence from al-Kashi's letters supports Ulugh Beg's active mathematical engagement but not the claim that all calculations were his personal independent discoveries. A specialist comparison of surviving treatise traditions shows that even methods later attributed broadly to Ulugh Beg can synthesize distinct work by al-Kashi and the Timurid mathematical circle.
- There is inconsistent terminology around the giant meridian instrument, sometimes called a quadrant or sextant. Avoid projecting a perfectly reconstructed floor plan from surviving remains alone.
- Different historical accounts date observatory establishment to the early or late 1420s, and date the star-list versus the finished *Zij* differently. Metadata uses intervals and a qualified event label.
- Narratives that present his death exclusively as punishment for scientific belief oversimplify a documented Timurid succession struggle.
