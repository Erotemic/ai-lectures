# 149000 — Dorothy Crowfoot Hodgkin: Making the invisible visible

**Track:** History · People and ideas. **Background:** No prior crystallography or biochemistry is required. The lecture develops X-ray diffraction, electron-density maps, the phase problem, and the molecular structures of penicillin, vitamin B12, and insulin in the context of Hodgkin's life.

A few milligrams of penicillin can be enough to save a life, but in the early 1940s there was a problem that mattered to chemists for an entirely different reason. They did not agree about the shape of its molecule. They knew what penicillin could do to certain bacteria. They had evidence about the atoms it contained and about some of the chemical reactions it could undergo. But between the formula on paper and a three-dimensional arrangement of atoms lay a difficult question. What was actually joined to what? Did the molecule contain the strained four-membered ring proposed by one group of chemists, or a different ring system supported by others?

The stakes extended beyond the pleasure of settling an argument. A reliable structure could help chemists understand the behavior of an antibiotic and explore related compounds. Yet looking at penicillin directly was impossible with an ordinary microscope. Light is too coarse a probe to distinguish atoms in a molecule. Dorothy Crowfoot Hodgkin, working in Oxford, approached the question by a different kind of seeing. She would grow crystals, send X-rays through them, measure the scattered beams, and reconstruct what the atoms must be doing inside. The decisive work was a collaboration involving other crystallographers and industrial chemists. In 1945 it made a strong case for the four-membered beta-lactam ring. The molecule had answered an argument that experiments with test tubes alone had not resolved.

Hodgkin became famous for that result, and later for the structures of vitamin B12 and insulin. But the most revealing fact about her career may be the difference between the speed of an X-ray exposure and the years required to understand it. One photograph could contain thousands of spots. Turning those spots into a chemical structure demanded mathematics, educated guesses, painstaking laboratory work, new instruments, colleagues with different skills, and the determination to keep asking questions after an answer seemed out of reach. Her life is a history of science acquiring the ability to make an invisible world intelligible.

Dorothy Mary Crowfoot was born on May 12, 1910, at Guizeh near Cairo, in Egypt. She was the oldest of four daughters of John Winter Crowfoot and Grace Mary Crowfoot, usually called Molly. Her father worked in colonial educational administration in Egypt and Sudan and later became an archaeologist. Her mother pursued archaeology and textile history, eventually becoming a respected scholar of ancient fabrics in her own right. Their interests made the world of the distant past tangible: a piece of pottery or a woven thread could serve as physical evidence for a story about human life.

Her parents' careers unfolded within British colonial institutions in Egypt, Sudan, and elsewhere in the Middle East. Those political arrangements shaped their access to archaeological sites, schools, and scientific contacts. Dorothy grew up with the advantages of educated, internationally connected parents, but she did not simply live with them in one continuous family home. After the First World War began, she and her sisters spent long periods in England while their parents worked abroad. That separation appears in biographies as a real part of the family's life. We do not need to invent conversations or private feelings to see how unusual it was for a child to have England as a place of schooling and North Africa as a place of continuing family attachment.

As a girl, Dorothy developed a practical enthusiasm for substances that could be handled and examined. She made crystals from solutions. She collected minerals. A small chemistry set helped turn curiosity into experiments. At school in Beccles, Suffolk, chemistry was not a subject all the girls could automatically take. Accounts of her education record that she and another girl were allowed to study it with boys. The barrier is worth noticing not because every subsequent achievement must be explained by an obstacle, but because scientific education is made from institutional choices. Who gets into the room matters before any discovery can happen inside it.

When Dorothy was about fifteen, she read a book by William Henry Bragg called Concerning the Nature of Things. Bragg and his son, William Lawrence Bragg, had helped establish how X-rays could reveal patterns inside crystals. Their work had been recognized with the Nobel Prize in Physics in 1915. In the book, the senior Bragg described a powerful enlargement of human sight. The claim was not literally that a scientist could point a camera at a molecule and take its portrait. Rather, the arrangement of atoms could be deduced from how waves interacted with an ordered structure. For Dorothy, the promise joined two interests that were already present: the beauty of a regular crystal and the desire to find out what it contained.

In 1928 she entered Somerville College at the University of Oxford to study chemistry. The college had been established for women at a time when women faced severe limits on university study and academic careers. Oxford had only recently begun granting degrees to women on the same formal basis as men. Dorothy encountered serious chemistry there, including the arguments by which chemists inferred molecular structures from reactions. The intellectual puzzle was obvious to her. If a proposed structure could explain many chemical observations, could a physical experiment show whether the proposed arrangement was really present?

In the final year of her Oxford studies, she began a crystallographic research project with H. M. Powell on organometallic thallium halides. It was a suitably difficult apprenticeship. The chemicals were not the medicines that would later make her name; they were a way to learn how crystals form, how to take reliable diffraction data, and how chemical knowledge and geometry might be fitted together. She was learning an instrument and a way of reasoning. Neither was a push-button method. There were choices about which sample to study, how to keep it stable, where to position it, and what to infer when the result looked ambiguous.

In 1932 Dorothy moved to Cambridge to work with the physicist and crystallographer J. D. Bernal. Her later Nobel lecture described the introduction as a fortunate series of encounters. Bernal's laboratory crossed subject boundaries readily. There, crystals and liquids and biological material were all possible objects of physical investigation. At a time when the large molecules of living things were still mostly beyond direct structural analysis, the researchers began to ask whether proteins could be studied by diffraction. One notable achievement was obtaining revealing X-ray photographs from wet crystals of pepsin, an enzyme, rather than drying the crystals and destroying the regular arrangement needed for meaningful data.

Think about that choice to keep a crystal wet. In many ordinary crystallographic experiments the crystal is a hard, stable mineral or salt. A protein crystal is a delicate ordered assembly containing large amounts of water. If the water is removed, the positions of the molecules may change or the ordered structure may disintegrate. A damaged crystal gives information about the damaged specimen, not the living molecule as it was arranged in a hydrated environment. This was an early lesson in a theme that would recur throughout Hodgkin's career: a measurement is only as meaningful as the physical preparation whose behavior it records.

To see why the method mattered, imagine a pond with several rows of posts standing in the water. Send gentle ripples across the pond. Each post sends out its own disturbance. At some positions the disturbances reinforce one another, producing a strong wave; elsewhere they cancel. The spacing and orientation of the posts change the directions in which reinforcement occurs. A crystal is vastly smaller and the waves are X-rays, but the principle has a family resemblance. Electrons in the crystal scatter an incoming X-ray wave. Because many molecules repeat regularly, their scattered waves can reinforce each other in certain directions. A detector records a pattern of bright reflections rather than a familiar picture of the molecule.

The spacing of atoms is measured in units called angstroms, each one ten-billionth of a metre. Visible-light wavelengths are hundreds or thousands of times larger than those spacings. Suitable X-rays have wavelengths comparable to distances between atoms, so their interference patterns can report atomic-scale geometry. The Braggs found that the angles of strong reflection relate to wavelength and the spacing between families of planes in a crystal. In its simplest form the relation says that twice the plane spacing, multiplied by the sine of the reflection angle, equals a whole number of wavelengths. This alone is already extraordinary: an invisible spacing can be inferred from a measured angle.

But the spacing of crystal planes is not the same as the full structure of a complicated molecule. Suppose a repeated musical chord reaches us through a wall. We might determine which notes are present by analyzing its frequencies, yet that information by itself is not the score. Similarly, a diffraction experiment records many reflections, each carrying partial information about how electrons are distributed in space. The pattern must be assembled mathematically. There is a precise tool for this: a Fourier sum. A complicated distribution can be expressed as a combination of waves with different spacings and directions. Add the appropriate waves, and the result can approximate the electron density throughout the crystal.

Here is the catch that dominated crystallographic problem solving for decades. A wave has an amplitude, which tells us something about its strength, and a phase, which tells us how it is shifted relative to other waves. The intensity of a diffraction spot allows us to estimate the amplitude of its underlying scattering wave. It does not directly tell us the phase. And when we add waves to reconstruct electron density, the phases matter enormously. Two sets of waves can have similar strengths and combine into quite different spatial patterns if their shifts are changed. This is the phase problem. An X-ray photograph cannot simply be enlarged until atoms appear; some of the information needed for the reconstruction must be supplied or inferred by other means.

Researchers developed clever ways around the difficulty. If a crystal contains a particularly heavy atom, such as iodine, that atom may scatter X-rays more strongly than its lighter neighbors. Its contribution can help locate it, and its position can help estimate the missing phases for other reflections. In favorable cases, one can compare related crystals with and without a heavy-atom substitution. Another strategy uses a calculation called a Patterson map, in which information accessible from measured intensities shows likely vectors between atom positions, even before the complete molecular arrangement is known. None of these techniques is magic. Each depends on assumptions about the specimen and on careful checking of what the data permit.

There was also a practical problem that can be hard to appreciate in an era of automated computation. An electron-density map requires thousands of numerical operations. In the 1930s and 1940s, these were not sent to a small computer sitting beside the diffraction apparatus. Analysts used mechanical aids, numerical tables, hand calculations, and physical devices for summing trigonometric terms. Hodgkin later recalled buying a set of Beevers–Lipson strips, an ingenious aid for Fourier summation, for five pounds. The apparatus sounds quaint now; at the time it opened possibilities that otherwise would have demanded an exhausting amount of arithmetic. When a modern crystallographic program draws a molecular model, there is a history of decades of labour inside that apparent ease.

Dorothy completed her doctorate at Cambridge, awarded in 1937, with research concerned with sterols and X-ray crystallography. She had already returned to Oxford in 1934 to take a research fellowship at Somerville, where she built a working scientific home. Much of her early work took place in cramped university accommodation associated with the Museum of Natural History. Sophisticated research does not always start in a purpose-built laboratory. It often starts where a few committed people can secure space, instruments, stable specimens, and time to think.

Her early sterol studies established an important part of the method. Sterols include complex molecules related to cholesterol, with a framework of fused rings. Chemists had proposed such shapes from reactions, but definitive three-dimensional evidence was difficult. Hodgkin's collaborators used a derivative containing iodine; the iodine made an excellent structural landmark. Harry Carlisle worked on three-dimensional electron-density calculations that helped establish the atomic arrangement. The result strengthened confidence in a stereochemical picture inferred by chemists, and it showed that sophisticated organic structures could be investigated through crystal geometry. The discovery was not simply of one more molecule. It extended a technique to questions that the technique had once seemed too primitive to answer.

During those years she also built a family. In 1937 Dorothy married Thomas Lionel Hodgkin, a teacher and historian whose later work focused on African history and politics. They had three children. Their marriage joined two people with demanding intellectual careers and independent public commitments. Accounts from colleagues and family describe an open household filled with visitors, discussion, and students. It would be inaccurate to turn this into a story of a scientist whose family life disappeared behind the laboratory, or into a story of effortless harmony. Maintaining research while raising children was a practical undertaking, supported by relatives, friends, institutional help, and the couple's own arrangements.

Dorothy also lived with rheumatoid arthritis. Pain in her hands had brought her to a doctor as a young researcher, and a severe attack followed in 1938. The disease progressively affected her joints and mobility. She continued working with instruments and crystals, later using help when moving or manipulating things became difficult. The important historical point is neither that physical pain supplied a secret source of genius nor that personal endurance should be romanticized. Her scientific life depended on adapting work to changing physical circumstances, in a laboratory and intellectual community willing to make that work possible.

When the Second World War made penicillin a strategic scientific question, Dorothy had already developed the habit of choosing molecules whose form mattered to their biological action. Alexander Fleming had reported the antibacterial effect of the mould's substance in 1929, but a discovery in a laboratory was not yet a widely available medicine. The Oxford team associated with Howard Florey, Ernst Chain, and their colleagues worked to turn penicillin into a usable treatment, while wartime industrial programs tackled production and purification. Determining its chemical structure was a different task. The problems were connected, but they had different investigators, evidence, and objectives.

Chain and Edward Abraham urged the crystallographers to attempt penicillin's structure. Hodgkin's 1964 Nobel lecture records that early sodium penicillin crystals were grown from only three milligrams of material sent from the United States to Britain during the war. Kathleen Lonsdale helped with the transfer of specimens and expertise. Here was a research problem that joined universities, industry, and wartime logistics: a tiny sample travelled across the Atlantic; crystals grew in one place; X-rays were measured elsewhere; mathematicians and chemists compared interpretations across institutions. Scarcity encouraged ingenuity, but it also made every experiment consequential. Waste a preparation, and there might not be a quick replacement.

The chemical controversy concentrated on the core of the molecule. One interpretation involved a beta-lactam, a ring with four atoms, including a nitrogen; another interpretation associated with Robert Robinson involved a different structure. A four-membered ring is strained compared with some more relaxed molecular arrangements. That strain helps make the beta-lactam chemically reactive. The structural distinction was not a matter of how to draw a decorative diagram. It altered predictions about how the antibiotic could react, how it might be synthesized, and why certain chemical transformations occurred.

Hodgkin did not solve this problem alone. Barbara Low worked with her at Oxford, and Charles Bunn and A. Turner-Jones contributed from Imperial Chemical Industries. Their joint effort used more than one salt of penicillin, different diffraction datasets, and increasingly persuasive electron-density interpretations. Heavy atoms were helpful but did not make all the missing information fall immediately into place. The solution required repeated comparison of imperfect maps, along with chemical reasoning. By 1945 the diffraction evidence established the four-membered ring and ruled against the rival formula. The published record came later, with some wartime details constrained by secrecy. It would be a mistake to say that the structural solution, by itself, produced wartime penicillin supplies; the broader campaign to manufacture the drug involved microbiologists, physicians, fermentation specialists, and industrial engineers.

There is an appealing scientific discipline in this story. Robinson was a distinguished chemist. Hodgkin respected the chemical achievements of people who questioned the model. But the evidence had to decide the structure. Even then the crystallographers checked whether the X-rays might have changed their sample. A sceptical chemist had suggested that an unstable four-membered ring seen in the experiment might have been created by the very rays used to measure it. Hodgkin and Low tested the crystals' biological activity after exposure. That kind of control matters: the experiment is a physical event, not an oracle. A result becomes stronger when a plausible alternative explanation has been investigated, not merely dismissed.

The structure of penicillin helped establish how a class of antibiotics was put together. Later work clarified how beta-lactam drugs interfere with bacterial cell-wall synthesis and how resistance can destroy their activity. Those later biological explanations should not be read backwards as if every part of the mechanism were known in 1945. The work had answered a structural question decisively and enlarged the possibilities for subsequent chemistry. It was also evidence that a molecular shape deduced from diffraction could settle a medical question important enough to shape an industry.

The next major problem announced itself as a little collection of dark red crystals. In 1948 researchers were isolating a factor useful in treating pernicious anaemia, a dangerous disorder in which the body fails to produce healthy red blood cells. It became known as vitamin B12. The practical history of the treatment had begun long before anyone knew the molecule's structure, with clinical observations about liver extracts and blood formation. Now, scientists wanted to discover what that active factor actually was. Lester Smith brought early B12 crystals to Hodgkin's laboratory. The first diffraction work indicated a molecular mass around fifteen hundred, an astonishing challenge for structural analysis at the time.

Unlike penicillin, whose core was small enough to make a disputed structural formula comparatively manageable, vitamin B12 was a complicated three-dimensional construction. It contained a cobalt atom at the center of an organic framework, but almost everything about the arrangement of the surrounding groups had to be discovered. Cobalt was a gift to the crystallographer because it scattered X-rays more strongly than many lighter atoms. It was not, however, a blueprint. Knowing that an atom of cobalt sits somewhere in a molecule is nothing like knowing how all the carbon, nitrogen, oxygen, hydrogen, and other atoms are connected around it.

This problem brought chemical degradation, analysis of related compounds, crystallographic calculation, and an expanding international network into contact. Researchers who separated or synthesized chemical fragments supplied clues. Chemists investigating the corrin ring, the distinctive system that surrounds the cobalt, tested possible structures. Kenneth Trueblood, visiting Oxford from California, offered the use of a powerful computer for calculations that would have been daunting in Britain. Hodgkin remembered the offer decades later. It was not a marginal convenience. X-ray crystallography was becoming a science in which computational capacity could determine whether a plausible calculation was practical at all.

There is a temptation to describe the next few years as a march from one perfect measurement to the answer. Hodgkin's own account was less tidy. Investigators moved through partial interpretations, discarded arrangements, and repeated calculations. A three-dimensional map improves as a model improves: a heavy-atom position gives tentative phases; tentative phases give a rough density; the rough density suggests other atoms; new atomic positions lead to better phases and a clearer map. This is a feedback loop between measurement and model. Its danger is that an attractive model may tempt you to see what you expect. Its strength is that independent chemical evidence, different crystalline forms, and additional diffraction data can force a proposal to survive tests rather than merely look convincing.

The central features of the vitamin B12 structure emerged in the middle of the 1950s. The work was reported in stages, with the 1955 structural breakthrough and important publications in 1956. It revealed the corrin framework around cobalt and a molecular organization that had been difficult to imagine from chemical formulas alone. Its importance was partly practical: a structure could guide chemists who wanted to synthesize variants or understand biochemical transformations. But it was equally a landmark in the development of molecular science. A highly elaborate natural compound could now be treated as a three-dimensional arrangement of atoms supported by measurements, not as an untested drawing arrived at through chemical ingenuity alone.

Consider the cobalt at its center. Metals in biological molecules can do more than sit motionless in an elegant ring. The metal's electronic properties and the bonds to groups attached above and below the ring can help produce distinctive chemical reactions. Work after the initial structural determination explored different forms of cobalamin and related coenzymes. In her 1964 Nobel lecture, Hodgkin discussed evidence for a bond directly connecting cobalt and carbon in a biologically active B12 coenzyme. That finding mattered because it gave biochemists an atomic framework for asking how the molecule participates in rearrangement and methyl-transfer reactions. It did not mean that a complete mechanism for every B12-dependent enzyme had suddenly been solved.

A photograph of an electron-density map from this period can look like a strange topographical chart. It is made of contours surrounding peaks; peaks can suggest where atoms lie. Today a student may rotate a protein on a screen and see smooth spheres and sticks in seconds. Hodgkin's group had to move from data to a physical model through hours of hand interpretation and calculations distributed across the available machines. A model was not the data. It was a proposed arrangement consistent with the data, sometimes displayed with beautiful confidence only after many rounds of criticism. That distinction became one of structural biology's most important working habits.

In 1964 the Royal Swedish Academy awarded Dorothy Hodgkin the Nobel Prize in Chemistry for X-ray determinations of the structures of biologically important substances. She was the third woman to receive the chemistry prize, after Marie Curie and Irène Joliot-Curie. The prize recognized a body of work, not a lucky discovery made in a single week. She had already been elected a Fellow of the Royal Society in 1947 and had received its Royal Medal in 1956. A newspaper's framing of her Nobel award as a story about an Oxford housewife demonstrated how inadequate some public categories remained for a scientist whose research group had transformed a field. But the more important historical record is in the papers, instruments, students, and structures.

Her Nobel lecture contained an almost startling admission. She said that, for much of her working life, she had been trying to solve structures rather than successfully solving them. The remark was not false modesty. Structural research often produces long stretches of incompleteness. A specimen fails to grow well; the reflections are weak; phases cannot be assigned reliably; a promising map turns out to be misleading. The neat version of a research career, built from dates of prizes and published results, erases those intervals. Hodgkin's own account restores them. Scientific ability includes judging when a problem is worth returning to, when to try a different measurement, and when an attractive result is still not secure.

Indeed, she had been returning to one particularly difficult problem since 1934: insulin. Insulin is a hormone released by the pancreas and essential for regulating the use and storage of nutrients, especially glucose. Its deficiency in people with type 1 diabetes can be treated with externally supplied insulin. Researchers had begun isolating insulin and using it as a treatment in the early 1920s. Crystals became available for study, and Hodgkin recognized very early that a hormone whose medical action depended on its molecular behavior would be an important subject for structural analysis. Her curiosity preceded the computing power and experimental procedures needed to satisfy it.

An insulin molecule is a protein made of two chains of amino acids joined by disulfide bonds. Frederick Sanger and colleagues established the amino-acid sequence of insulin in the 1950s. That was a remarkable achievement, but it did not solve Hodgkin's question. A protein's sequence tells us the order of chemical units along a chain. A three-dimensional structure tells us how that chain folds, which residues are brought close together, and how different protein molecules associate with one another. A list of the beads on a string is not the shape of the string after it has folded into a knot. For a hormone, that extra information can be essential to understanding its storage, release, and binding behavior.

Insulin added another complication. In crystalline and some storage forms, several molecules assemble together. Two molecules may form a dimer; six may form a hexamer associated with zinc ions. The crystal arrangement is not necessarily the form that engages the cell's receptor. One must understand the relationship between the individual molecular fold and these higher-order assemblies rather than assuming the lattice is a frozen photograph of a protein in action inside a body. Even with good crystals, the measurements must be interpreted through that biological distinction. A structure can illuminate a mechanism without by itself being a movie of that mechanism.

The research continued through changes of personnel and technology. In the 1950s and 1960s methods for dealing with protein crystals improved, new diffractometers made the collection of reflections more systematic, and access to electronic computers transformed the calculations. Structural protein work by Max Perutz, John Kendrew, and their collaborators showed that very complex protein structures could be solved using diffraction and laborious phasing strategies. Hodgkin's group drew upon advances shared across the international crystallographic community while developing solutions for the particular difficulties of insulin. The speed of progress was uneven. Decades elapsed not because no one cared, but because the problem sat near the changing frontier of what instruments, methods, and mathematical computation could do.

A paper published in Nature in November 1969 reported an electron-density map of rhombohedral insulin crystals at a resolution of 2.8 angstroms. The author list matters. It included Margaret Adams, Tom Blundell, Eleanor Dodson, Guy Dodson, M. Vijayan, Ted Baker, Marjorie Harding, Dorothy Hodgkin, B. Rimmer, and S. Sheat. Their contributions were not identical, and it would take a much more detailed laboratory history to reconstruct every decision. But the paper was explicitly collective work. Those names represent experimental preparation, data collection, phase determination, computation, model building, and criticism, all of which had to come together for the solution.

One of the younger members of the insulin group, Tom Blundell, later recalled the atmosphere of an Oxford laboratory populated by researchers from several continents. Eleanor and Guy Dodson, colleagues from Australia and New Zealand respectively, were central to developing and interpreting the insulin structures. M. Vijayan, a crystallographer from India, later wrote about the support and conversations within the group. This was not a room in which a senior scientist handed out an answer and juniors copied it down. It was a place where people acquired tools from one another and where they could make different parts of a difficult problem move forward at the same time.

The 1969 result provided an atomic model at a resolution sufficient to establish major structural features, not a perfect map of every electron. Better measurements and refinements followed, including higher-resolution work published in 1988. Later research also examined forms of insulin that behave differently in pharmaceutical preparations and in solution. Structural biology is iterative even after a celebrated first solution. New accuracy can change individual atomic placements; a different crystal can reveal a change in molecular arrangement; a comparison with biological experiments may show why one configuration matters more than another. Hodgkin was present at the transformation of insulin from an impossibly difficult target into a subject that teams could investigate in increasingly detailed ways.

That final triumph cannot be separated from the scientific community she helped build. Hodgkin's Oxford group trained and collaborated with researchers who later established their own laboratories. She made room for students who wanted to pursue difficult problems without surrendering independent judgment. Some of her students and associates have described a manner of leadership that relied more on patient questions than on forceful instructions. Such recollections are affectionate, and memory can polish a portrait. The lasting evidence of a research culture is also in the published collaborations, in long-lived scientific relationships, and in the work those younger scientists went on to do elsewhere.

One of Hodgkin's former students at Oxford was Margaret Roberts, who would later become Margaret Thatcher. Their subsequent political views differed sharply. This fact sometimes appears in accounts as a miniature drama: a future Conservative prime minister taught by a scientist deeply committed to internationalist and left-leaning causes. But a student's later political career does not tell us what a tutor thought about every classroom exchange, and the contrast should not substitute for their documented relationship. What it does reveal is the broad institutional influence of a teacher whose students entered lives far beyond crystallography.

Hodgkin was interested in international scientific cooperation for reasons that reached beyond laboratory convenience. Her upbringing had connected places divided by empire; her adult research crossed national borders; and the Second World War and nuclear age made the possible uses of science a public question. She supported medical assistance for Vietnam during the war, visited countries with which Britain had tense relations, and took a leading role in the Pugwash Conferences on Science and World Affairs. She served as president of the Pugwash movement from 1976 to 1988. Those efforts attempted to keep communication between scientists alive even during political conflict, and to bring technical expertise to the question of preventing nuclear catastrophe.

Her travels included scientific engagements in the Soviet Union, China, and India. These were not just occasions to receive honours. They were part of a sustained belief that knowledge should be shared across political boundaries and that friendship between researchers could exist alongside disagreement between governments. The archives document extensive correspondence, visits, and organizational work, though a catalogue of papers is not a transcript of every conversation. She also engaged with the International Union of Crystallography, an organization created to give a growing international field common venues and professional standards. The scientist who had once struggled with an individual diffraction photograph was now helping sustain the institutions through which diffraction became a worldwide practice.

Her arthritis remained a real presence in later decades. It sometimes made travel difficult and made the physical handling of research equipment and papers harder. Colleagues helped with mobility, while she continued to participate in conferences and scientific discussions. The Royal Society's later biographical account preserves scenes of her concentration at the time the insulin model was being finished, despite painful swelling in her feet. These details deserve accuracy rather than decorative sentiment. Disability changes what work requires; it does not erase scientific agency. Her achievements belong to her, and the practical assistance of family, students, and coworkers belongs in the history of how they were made possible.

She received many honours beyond the Nobel Prize, including the Order of Merit and the Royal Society's Copley Medal. She died on July 29, 1994, in Ilmington, Warwickshire, where she and Thomas had a family home. The research she helped develop did not end with her. Protein crystallography became central to structural biology, alongside later techniques such as nuclear magnetic resonance and cryo-electron microscopy. Modern researchers may collect data in minutes that once required days, and process calculations that previously consumed years of human arithmetic. Yet the reasoning remains recognizable. What exactly has been measured? Which part of the model is inferred? How much confidence should be assigned to a shape that has emerged from imperfect data?

Return finally to the penicillin crystals of the 1940s. They did not carry an answer legibly printed inside them. They carried patterns of scattered X-rays. To turn the patterns into a structure, Hodgkin and her colleagues needed chemistry, mathematics, instruments, controls, imagination, and scepticism. The same mixture, expanded through decades and by many more collaborators, revealed the astonishing architecture of vitamin B12 and eventually the fold and assembly of insulin. A great strength of Hodgkin's science was its refusal to treat seeing as mere looking. We know what we see because we can explain how the evidence produced it, and because we have tested what else might have produced it instead.

## Historical and scientific companion (not spoken)

### What diffraction actually reconstructs

For elastic scattering by a periodic crystal, the Bragg condition can be written as \(n\lambda=2d\sin\theta\). A measured reflection is associated with a reciprocal-lattice index \((h,k,l)\); its complex structure factor is approximately \(F_{hkl}=\sum_j f_j\exp\{2\pi i(hx_j+ky_j+lz_j)\}\) for a model with atoms at fractional coordinates \((x_j,y_j,z_j)\). The measured intensity is proportional (after corrections) to \(|F_{hkl}|^2\). It yields the magnitude, not the phase of \(F_{hkl}\). With phase information, a three-dimensional Fourier synthesis can recover an electron-density map. Do not describe these maps as direct photographs of atoms; maps are experimentally constrained reconstructions, with resolution, model dependence, disorder, measurement error and crystal packing effects.

- **Heavy-atom and related-crystal methods:** Locate a comparatively strong scatterer and use model or isomorphous comparisons to estimate otherwise missing phases. These strategies need validation of isomorphism and chemical identity.
- **Patterson synthesis:** A Fourier transform of intensity data gives a map of interatomic displacement vectors, not a direct conventional electron-density map. Heavy atoms can produce conspicuous vector peaks.
- **Iterative refinement:** Coordinates and scattering models are repeatedly tested against measured intensities. Crystallographic structure is not inferred from chemical plausibility alone.
- **Modern context:** Computational pipelines and contemporary beam sources accelerate data collection and analysis, but do not remove the need to check sample integrity, resolution limits, refinement quality, and biological interpretation.

### Penicillin: an attribution and chronology correction

Fleming's antibacterial observation, Florey and Chain's Oxford development of clinical applications, transatlantic fermentation and manufacturing, and structural work were separate but interacting histories. Hodgkin did **not** discover penicillin or single-handedly enable its wartime production. Charles Bunn, Barbara Low, A. Turner-Jones, Kathleen Lonsdale, Chain, and Edward Abraham have documented roles in the structural campaign or specimen flow. Hodgkin's Nobel lecture explicitly credited Bunn and Low with the ingenuity behind methods that solved challenging salt-crystal maps. A wartime 1945 structure solution and subsequent fuller published reporting should not be collapsed into one publication date. The beta-lactam conclusion addressed the disputed molecular ring system; the later enzyme-level mechanism of penicillin action was established through additional research.

### Vitamin B12: a sequence of discoveries, not a single moment

Lester Smith supplied vitamin material, and work by chemists and biochemists identifying, degrading, and studying cobalamins was crucial. The 1955 structural breakthrough and mid-1950s papers concern the cobalamin scaffold; different B12 coenzymes and their chemical behavior were investigated subsequently. Hodgkin's Nobel lecture discusses Kenneth Trueblood's freely offered computer calculations, the cobalt-containing corrin nucleus, and the unusual cobalt–carbon bond in the adenosylcobalamin coenzyme. Do not claim that determining cyanocobalamin's structure alone explained the mechanisms of all B12-dependent enzymes or that crystallography synthesized the molecule.

### Insulin: distinguish sequence, 3-D fold, and physiological state

Sanger's work established the insulin amino-acid sequence; the Hodgkin-group crystallographic study established major elements of its three-dimensional structure. The 1969 paper in *Nature*, *Structure of Rhombohedral 2 Zinc Insulin Crystals*, has ten coauthors and reports a 2.8-angstrom electron-density map. Its resolution is neither atomic certainty for every hydrogen nor the final word: subsequent structures, variants, refinements, and dynamics matter. In crystals and certain pharmaceutical storage preparations, insulin can form dimers and zinc-coordinated hexamers; active receptor binding involves other association states. The reported 1988 1.5-angstrom structures represent a later stage of refinement, not a correction that nullifies 1969.

### Evidence and interpretive boundaries

The Nobel lecture is first-person evidence for Hodgkin's retrospective scientific account and collaborator credits; it is not a neutral log of all events. The Royal Society, Somerville, and the Science History Institute supply institutional biographies.
