# 125000 — Michael Atiyah: Geometry across borders

**Track:** History — People and ideas. **Focus:** An Anglo-Lebanese family between Khartoum, Cairo and Britain, Cambridge and Princeton, topological K-theory, the Atiyah–Singer index theorem, collaboration with physicists, major scientific institutions, and mathematical internationalism. **Background:** functions, shapes and differential operators are introduced before index theory.

How can a problem about solving differential equations be answered by looking at the shape of a space? For most of mathematical history, those subjects had different languages. One asked for functions and their derivatives; the other for holes, twists, and the arrangement of geometric objects. Michael Atiyah spent a lifetime making such boundaries less rigid. His work with Isadore Singer on the index theorem created a lasting connection between the analysis of equations and the topology of the spaces on which they are defined. But Atiyah's interest in connections was not confined to mathematical concepts. His childhood moved among countries and languages, his research depended on friendships across institutions, and his later career was deeply concerned with the international organization of science.

Michael Francis Atiyah was born in London in 1929. His father, Edward Selim Atiyah, was Lebanese and had studied at Oxford; his mother, Jean Levens, came from a Scottish family. Edward worked in Khartoum when Sudan was governed within the British-Egyptian colonial system. He also wrote and engaged in public debate about the politics of the Middle East. Michael therefore grew up in a family situated between cultural worlds that the British imperial order did not treat as equal. His father had a British university education and a complicated relationship with British status and political identity. These circumstances belong to Atiyah's history, although we should not assume that a particular theorem was caused by the politics of his home.

His early childhood was spent largely in Khartoum, with travel among relatives in Britain and elsewhere. During the Second World War the family moved through Lebanon; he also attended Victoria College in Cairo and later Alexandria. These were not simply scenic stops on a future mathematician's journey. Wartime travel, colonial education, and the social composition of an international boarding school shaped the kinds of people and languages he encountered. In recollections he emphasized the intellectual benefit of a multilingual environment and the challenge of schooling among older pupils. Unlike someone who studies only within a single national university system, he grew up accustomed to adjusting to several settings.

Mathematics was not the only subject that interested him. He remembered being attracted to chemistry before returning to mathematics, partly because mathematical reasoning seemed to offer more structure and less sheer memorization. That recollection is useful not because it proves mathematics is superior to chemistry, but because it conveys his intellectual taste. He wanted patterns that could be reconstructed, relationships that could be recognized, and principles from which other facts followed. His later work repeatedly sought ways to recognize the same structure in subjects that appeared different on the surface.

After the war he attended Manchester Grammar School in England, where demanding competition and a strong teacher helped develop his love of projective geometry. In that subject the essential objects are points, lines and incidence relations, and figures may be transformed without destroying the relations one is studying. Atiyah later remembered the elegance of this geometry as an enduring attraction. He also encountered William Rowan Hamilton's quaternions, a language for rotations that would eventually connect to geometry and mathematical physics. These early interests should not be read as destiny, but they help explain why his later mathematical imagination was so visual and structural.

He won a scholarship to Trinity College, Cambridge, in 1947, but compulsory national service delayed his full entry. He used part of that interval to read mathematics. When he joined Cambridge he worked in an environment where the heritage of Hardy, the famous Tripos examinations, and modern mathematical research coexisted. A student could still encounter classical projective geometry while hearing about the new topological and geometric ideas changing the subject elsewhere. Atiyah excelled academically, but the important step was the decision about what kind of research to pursue.

His doctoral advisor was W. V. D. Hodge. Hodge's work connected algebraic geometry, differential forms and topology, and his advice drew Atiyah toward a more modern perspective on geometry. In the mid-1950s Atiyah began using tools that came partly from European developments in sheaf theory and topology. That choice is a good example of collaboration across intellectual cultures. Hodge did not simply hand Atiyah a completed theory; he helped place a gifted student within conversations where different traditions could be combined. Atiyah's first research benefited from precisely that kind of movement between older geometrical pictures and newer abstractions.

He married Lily Brown in the 1950s, and family considerations would remain part of his later career decisions. Professional biographies can conceal these practical realities by listing an appointment at Cambridge, another at Oxford, then Princeton, as if the only relevant variables were mathematical opportunities. Atiyah and his family moved through major scientific centers, and the work of sustaining relationships was part of that life. Historians can record moves and institutional choices without fabricating private discussions about each one.

Research at the Institute for Advanced Study in Princeton exposed him to another community of mathematicians. He also collaborated with Friedrich Hirzebruch, whose work in Germany connected complex geometry with topology. Together they developed topological K-theory, a way of studying spaces by organizing vector bundles over them. Alexander Grothendieck was developing related K-theoretic ideas in algebraic geometry; the topological and algebraic theories should not be merged into a single act of invention by one person. One of Atiyah's characteristic achievements was to recognize that the language of vector bundles could reveal topological information that ordinary numerical invariants could not easily capture.

What is a vector bundle? Imagine a sphere and attach a small vector space to every point. At first, pretend each point carries an arrow that can point in several directions. If all the local arrow spaces fit together in a globally untwisted way, the bundle is simple. But it is possible to have local pieces that look ordinary while the entire arrangement twists around the space. A familiar one-dimensional example is the Möbius band, where a strip has only one side because of the way it is attached. A bundle records such twisting systematically. K-theory studies bundles by allowing direct sums and certain formal differences, producing invariants of the underlying space.

That sounds abstract. But when a mathematical object has a good invariant, questions about continuous deformation become manageable. If two spaces or two bundles have different invariants, they cannot be equivalent in the way under discussion. Atiyah's work showed that methods from algebra could organize these geometric distinctions. The details are demanding, yet the motivation is accessible: instead of tracking every point of a complicated shape, search for quantities and structures that remain unchanged under the relevant transformations.

Vector bundles made the relationship between geometry and algebra concrete. Suppose we have a sphere and want to select, continuously, a nonzero arrow tangent to it at every point. The hairy-ball theorem says we cannot do that on an ordinary two-dimensional sphere: at least one chosen tangent vector must vanish. On a torus, the situation is different. This is an elementary illustration of how topology constrains global geometric data. It is not itself a computation in K-theory, but it explains why a collection of local vector choices can contain global information that no single coordinate chart reveals.

One special feature of topological K-theory is that it does not merely count the dimension of the fibers. Two bundles can have fibers of the same dimension at every point yet differ in how they are assembled. Formal subtraction of bundles turns this information into a group. A topologist can then compare spaces through that group, relate it to other invariants, and discover regularities that are almost invisible in a direct coordinate calculation. Bott periodicity became central to the subject, showing a recurring pattern in the relevant stable homotopy information. The names and ideas belong to a network of researchers; Atiyah's contribution was to place them in a powerful theory and develop their consequences.

The collaboration for which Atiyah is best known was with Isadore Singer. Singer's expertise in analysis and geometry complemented Atiyah's topological viewpoint. The Atiyah–Singer index theorem, announced in the early 1960s, relates the index of an elliptic differential operator to topological data. The analytic index is the number of independent solutions of the homogeneous equation minus the number of independent obstructions to solving the corresponding inhomogeneous equation, under appropriate functional-analytic conditions. The theorem says this difference, which looks like something one must calculate from difficult differential equations, can often be obtained from characteristic classes and other geometric information.

Consider a deliberately simpler analogy. We want a periodic function whose derivative equals a prescribed periodic function. If the proposed derivative has a nonzero average over a full period, the task is impossible: the integral of a derivative around a loop is zero. So there is one obstruction to solvability, the average. Meanwhile, constant functions have zero derivative, so the solution is never unique without an additional condition; there is one dimension of ambiguity. The difference between those two dimensions is zero. This toy problem illustrates why the index counts both solutions and obstructions. It is not a proof of the general Atiyah–Singer theorem, which handles far more complicated elliptic operators on manifolds.

Why should the difference be topological? In favorable situations, continuous changes in the coefficients of an operator cannot alter the index. Individual solutions may appear, disappear or combine, but the signed balance remains stable. Geometry supplies robust invariants of the space and bundles on which the operator acts. The theorem identifies an analytic balance with a topological expression. It is powerful precisely because a problem that looks computationally local can have a global answer.

A mathematical result of this generality can change other people's questions. A differential geometer might ask how curvature is distributed across a surface. An analyst might ask how many solutions to a linear operator equation exist. A physicist might ask how fermion fields behave in a gauge background. The index theorem does not make these questions identical, but it supplies shared invariants and a language for passing between them. Its power is partly negative: it can show an analytic problem cannot have the expected answer because topology supplies an obstruction. That is why finding a global invariant can sometimes save one from an otherwise endless local calculation.

Another important distinction concerns the role of beauty and proof. Atiyah often looked for a conceptual reason behind a formula, and he was drawn to arguments that connected geometry with analysis. But a compelling geometric picture does not itself settle the hypotheses of an index theorem. Ellipticity matters; compactness and boundary conditions matter; the relevant operator must have finite-dimensional kernel and cokernel in the chosen setting. Explaining these restrictions helps a listener appreciate the actual achievement rather than treating topology as a magic device that solves any equation by inspection.

Atiyah's work did not remain within one corner of topology. With Raoul Bott he developed related methods involving fixed-point theorems and elliptic operators. Later his conversations with theoretical physicists encouraged mathematical work on gauge theory, instantons, monopoles and the geometry of field equations. Geometry and physics informed each other, but contributions must be attributed with care. Physicists, including those working on quantum field theory, supplied problems and conceptual insights; mathematicians such as Nigel Hitchin, Simon Donaldson and many others developed new tools. Atiyah was an influential participant and organizer within a large network, not the sole inventor of modern mathematical physics.

One of the most striking later episodes came from the study of instantons, special solutions of the Yang–Mills equations that became important in mathematical physics. Working with Nigel Hitchin, Atiyah found ways to relate these fields to algebraic geometry. Vladimir Drinfeld and Yuri Manin in the Soviet Union were pursuing related construction methods. In the late 1970s the two groups recognized that they had arrived at closely connected conclusions, leading to the four-author Atiyah–Drinfeld–Hitchin–Manin construction, often abbreviated ADHM. It offers a concrete algebraic construction of instantons in the specified setting. The episode is especially characteristic of Atiyah's career: research moved across geometry, physics and an international scientific divide, while the eventual publication credited all four collaborators.

It is tempting to assign this collaboration to one celebrated name because the acronym begins with A. But the historical interest lies in the nearly simultaneous insight and the willingness to publish together. A mathematician's reputation is enhanced, not diminished, by a precise account of what coauthors did. Atiyah's story repeatedly returns to the same lesson: new mathematical structures are often recognized at the meeting point of several existing lines of work.

His recognition was substantial. He received the Fields Medal in 1966, at the International Congress of Mathematicians in Moscow. He and Isadore Singer shared the Abel Prize in 2004 for their index theorem and its mathematical influence. These awards mark different stages of a long career, but the most characteristic feature of his professional life may be the continuity between research and institution building. He served in major academic posts, including a period at the Institute for Advanced Study, a return to Oxford, and leadership at Trinity College, Cambridge.

He helped establish the Isaac Newton Institute for Mathematical Sciences and became its first director in 1990. He was also prominent in the Royal Society and in the development of European mathematical organizations. Scientific institutions matter because they create opportunities for people to meet, disagree, teach, and work together. A conference can connect someone with an unsolved problem to someone else with a technique; a fellowship can make a collaboration financially possible; a strong institute can give young researchers time to think. Atiyah's investment in such structures matched his belief that mathematics advances through the crossing of boundaries.

During the later decades of his career he spoke publicly about relations between mathematics and physics, education, peace, and the responsibility of scientists in society. Such civic roles did not mean that every public assertion he made was a proved mathematical theorem. He sometimes pursued speculative proposals, especially late in life. The proper historical account distinguishes well-established achievements such as K-theory and index theory from ideas that did not gain the same scientific acceptance. One can respect the earlier work without presenting every later claim as correct by association.

Another route into Atiyah's mathematical outlook is the theory of Riemann surfaces. A complex curve may look locally like a patch of the complex plane, yet its global shape can differ dramatically: the Riemann sphere has genus zero, a torus genus one, and higher-genus surfaces have more handles. Complex analysis asks about functions and differential forms living on these spaces. The Riemann–Roch theorem relates the dimensions of certain spaces of such objects to the topology of the curve. In a basic case, the analytic index of the operator that tests whether a smooth function is holomorphic equals one minus the genus. This is one approachable ancestor and special illustration of the wider index-theorem philosophy. The formula is striking because the answer depends on a global integer describing the shape.

Suppose the surface is topologically a sphere. One minus the genus is one. On a torus the corresponding index is zero. These numbers are not raw counts of holomorphic functions; they are signed differences between solution spaces and obstructions, in a precisely defined complex-analytic setting. The lesson is that a small topological number can control an analytic question that would otherwise invite complicated local calculations. Atiyah's work generalized this kind of reasoning through much broader families of operators and geometric settings.

He also helped train mathematicians who reshaped the field. Simon Donaldson's work on four-dimensional geometry and gauge theory grew in a setting deeply influenced by Atiyah's interests and questions. The important history is not a one-way transfer of ideas from master to student. New results by younger researchers changed the research program for older mathematicians too. This kind of intellectual reciprocity is one reason Atiyah's career as a mentor and organizer mattered alongside his own theorems.

Atiyah died in 2019 in Edinburgh. By then the index theorem had become part of the shared language of topology, analysis, geometry and theoretical physics. Yet a listener who remembers only its name misses what made this mathematician distinctive. He was the child of an international family growing up within colonial and wartime institutions; a student formed by classical geometry but drawn toward new structures; a collaborator who learned from Hodge, Hirzebruch, Singer, Bott and many others; and an institution builder who treated scientific contact as a public good.

His mathematical story can be summarized without reducing it to a slogan. A problem about differential equations seems to ask for an analytic calculation. A topological invariant seems to describe the global shape of a space. The index theorem brings the two into relation. Atiyah's career made that kind of passage—between methods, people, countries, and fields—one of the defining movements of twentieth-century mathematics. The result was not merely an answer to one equation. It was a way of recognizing when two different questions had, underneath them, the same structure.

## Historical companion — not spoken

### Chronology and documentary basis

- **1929:** Born in London to Edward Selim Atiyah and Jean Levens; childhood substantially in Khartoum and across the Middle East.
- **1940s:** Schooling in Cairo/Alexandria and later Manchester Grammar School; national service before Cambridge.
- **1949–1955:** Trinity College, Cambridge; doctoral work under W. V. D. Hodge.
- **1950s:** Research travel to Princeton; collaboration with Friedrich Hirzebruch; development of topological K-theory with antecedents and parallels in Grothendieck's algebraic K-theory.
- **Early 1960s:** Atiyah–Singer index theorem; subsequent work with Raoul Bott and others.
- **1966:** Fields Medal.
- **1970s–1990s:** Oxford, Princeton IAS, leadership in mathematical societies, physics collaborations and institutional work.
- **1990:** First director of the Isaac Newton Institute and master of Trinity College.
- **2004:** Shared Abel Prize with Isadore M. Singer.
- **2019:** Died in Edinburgh.

### Mathematical companion

- **Topological K-theory:** Formal differences of isomorphism classes of vector bundles provide a generalized cohomology theory. Atiyah and Hirzebruch developed it in dialogue with other topological and algebraic work.
- **Fredholm index:** For a Fredholm operator \(D\), \(\mathrm{ind}(D)=\dim\ker D-\dim\mathrm{coker}\,D\).
- **Periodic derivative example:** \(D=d/d\theta\) on smooth periodic functions has a one-dimensional kernel (constants) and one-dimensional cokernel (failure of zero-mean condition); index zero.
- **Index theorem:** Under its hypotheses, an elliptic operator's analytic index equals a topologically determined index, expressible through characteristic classes. This is not the claim that an arbitrary PDE can be solved from topology alone.
- **Historical credit:** Singer is coauthor of the theorem; K-theory has significant algebraic antecedents in Grothendieck; Bott, Hirzebruch, Hodge, and physicist collaborators must not be erased.

**Historical cautions:** Do not merge Atiyah–Hirzebruch topological K-theory with Grothendieck's algebraic K-theory into a single discovery. The index theorem concerns elliptic operators under specific conditions, not arbitrary differential equations. The periodic example is pedagogical and not a derivation of the theorem. The legacy of his early major results does not validate every later speculation.
