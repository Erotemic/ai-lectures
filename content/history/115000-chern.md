# 115000 — Shiing-Shen Chern: Geometry across wars, universities, and continents

**Track:** History — People and ideas. **Focus:** Chern's training in China and Europe; the displacement of Chinese universities during war; Élie Cartan, Gauss–Bonnet and characteristic classes; Chicago, Berkeley, international institution-building; and the mathematical idea of detecting global structure through local curvature. **Background:** curved surfaces and the idea of measuring angles are introduced in speech.

A piece of geometry can survive a remarkable amount of moving. Imagine a mathematician studying the curvature of a surface in one country, carrying ideas through a university displaced by war, and finding that a formula first known for surfaces can be extended to spaces of far more dimensions. That was part of the life of Shiing-Shen Chern. His discoveries helped change geometry, but his life also tells a story about the places where mathematical research could be done, the places where it could not, and the institutions people built so that the next generation would not have to start again from almost nothing.

Chern was born in 1911 in Jiaxing, in China's Zhejiang province. The Qing dynasty fell during the year of his birth; the decades following it saw new republican institutions, competing military and political authorities, and ambitious educational reforms. It would be misleading to treat his mathematical career as a departure from a timeless, unchanging China. He grew up in a society rebuilding higher education while experiencing repeated political instability. His father worked in law and government. Chern's early education eventually took him to Tianjin, where at fifteen he entered Nankai University. There he encountered advanced geometry through Li-Fu Chiang and mathematical books that connected the young university with European research.

Nankai was still building the research environment that older institutions could take for granted. An established mathematical department might offer several specialists, a seminar, and a library of current journals. Chern learned in a smaller setting with a dedicated teacher and demanding books. He read geometry as a subject of constructions, transformations, and relations among figures. One effect was an early habit of seeking the structure behind a calculation. The future international authority on differential geometry began in a newly forming Chinese research culture that had to build many of its own academic routes.

In 1930 he moved to Tsinghua University near Beijing for graduate work. He studied under Dan Sun and became deeply interested in projective differential geometry. In this field one asks what remains unchanged when shapes are projected and transformed, much as a photograph changes lengths and angles but may preserve intersections and straightness. Chern's reading included the German geometer Wilhelm Blaschke. When Blaschke visited Tsinghua in 1932, the encounter helped open a path to Hamburg. Fellowships and international invitations were not ornamental recognition. They were practical channels through which an emerging mathematical community obtained access to teachers, conferences, books, and research questions.

Chern went to Hamburg in 1934 and completed a doctorate under Blaschke in 1936. Germany was already under Nazi government. Research continued there, but its institutions were being reshaped by persecution and political coercion. Chern's mathematical experience cannot be described simply as an apprenticeship in a neutral center of excellence. The universities from which he learned had lost Jewish and politically targeted scholars, and international mathematics was being reorganized by forced movement. In Hamburg he also attended lectures involving Erich Kähler's approach to differential equations and exterior forms, ideas he would later connect with a different geometric tradition.

One important consequence of studying in different traditions was learning to use multiple descriptions of the same object. A projective geometer may care about lines and incidence under projection; a Riemannian geometer may care about distances and angles; a differential geometer studying a connection may care about how moving vectors change. The subject can look divided into rival dialects. Chern learned to move between them. That ability became valuable when a problem asked for information that should not depend on any one coordinate system. The historical meetings in Hamburg and Paris therefore show up inside the mathematical content of his later research, although no single lecture or teacher mechanically caused a theorem.

The next stop was Paris. There Chern studied with Élie Cartan, whose work on differential forms, moving frames, and the geometry of curved spaces had developed a language different from the classical projective-geometric methods. The distinction mattered. An older approach might describe a surface by choosing coordinates and differentiating quantities associated with those coordinates. Cartan's methods encouraged attention to geometric information that did not depend on the initial choice. Chern's later results grew from understanding both languages, not from abandoning everything he had previously learned. He met mathematicians including André Weil and Henri Cartan, people whose lives would intersect with his again across changing continents.

In 1937 Chern accepted a professorship in China and started the journey home. The Sino-Japanese War expanded that year. Tsinghua, Peking University, and Nankai were forced from their normal campuses. Their academic community moved first to Changsha and then, as the war continued, to Kunming in southwestern China, where the institutions operated together as National Southwest Associated University. University buildings, teaching plans, travel, and libraries were all vulnerable. Professors and students continued teaching and research under conditions that made ordinary scholarly concentration difficult. Chern's move home had become a move into a displaced university.

The National Southwest Associated University brought together students and scholars with distinct academic backgrounds, often under severe physical constraints. Teaching did not mean reproducing a peaceful campus somewhere else. Books and equipment could be scarce, travel dangerous, and time irregular. Yet research groups could persist through shared problems, mimeographed notes, conversation and the fact that someone still had the training to answer questions. When a mathematician later helps establish a new institute, it can be tempting to attribute that act solely to personal ambition. Chern's experience gives a more concrete framework: research traditions are fragile when classrooms, libraries and appointments can be abruptly moved or destroyed. Preserving a teacher-student chain is itself an intellectual achievement.

Kunming was not merely a delay before his more celebrated American work. Chern taught there between the late nineteen-thirties and 1943. He had to communicate sophisticated geometry in a setting strained by war, to students who might themselves become the people who rebuilt Chinese mathematics. He married Shih-Ning Cheng in Kunming in 1939, establishing a family while the university community was still displaced. They later had two children. The family appears in institutional biographies because Chern's repeated movements involved personal arrangements as well as mathematical invitations. He was not a mind transferred between institutes without connections to people.

In 1943 he reached the Institute for Advanced Study in Princeton. Wartime travel from southwest China was extremely difficult; Chern later described an extraordinary route involving military aircraft through India, Africa, and South America before reaching the United States. The Institute offered an environment where he could work intensely on geometry, in conversation with mathematicians whose manuscripts and current ideas were not readily available in wartime Kunming. By the end of the period, he had developed the result that became a major turning point in his career: a generalization of the Gauss–Bonnet theorem to higher-dimensional curved spaces.

To appreciate what he accomplished, start with a more familiar two-dimensional picture. Draw a triangle on a flat sheet of paper. Its interior angles sum to one hundred eighty degrees. Draw a triangle with edges along great-circle arcs on a sphere, and the sum can be greater. Curvature makes a difference that careful angle measurement can detect. For a whole closed curved surface, a classical result associated with Gauss and Bonnet links the total curvature with an integer describing the surface's topology. For a sphere the Euler characteristic is two. For a torus, the shape of a doughnut, it is zero. The surface can be squeezed, stretched, or bent while that integer remains the same, provided it is not torn or glued into a new topology.

There is a striking meeting here. Curvature is local: it asks what is happening near one place on a smooth surface. Euler characteristic is global: it records structural information about the entire surface. Gauss–Bonnet says that, integrated across the surface with the proper normalization, curvature produces a topological answer. It is not claiming every region of a sphere has the same curvature or that geometry and topology are identical. It gives one rigorous bridge between them.

There is a second way to understand Euler characteristic without drawing a doughnut. Take a polyhedral approximation to a closed surface and count its vertices, subtract its edges, then add its faces. For a sphere-like object the answer is two. For a torus it is zero. A chosen triangulation changes the number of vertices and edges, but not that alternating total. This is an extraordinary kind of stability: the arithmetic is performed on local pieces of a chosen mesh, yet the answer captures something about the entire underlying surface. Gauss–Bonnet links this count to another kind of local data, smooth curvature. The surface's topology is not directly visible in an infinitesimal patch, but a carefully organized global sum recovers it.

For mathematicians in the early twentieth century, extending this bridge beyond surfaces presented a conceptual obstacle. What counts as curvature in four dimensions, six dimensions, or more? A person cannot visualize the shape simply by imagining a more complicated drawing. The structure has to be expressed through quantities that make sense regardless of coordinates. Chern's proof in the nineteen-forties drew on differential forms and connections to describe an appropriate curvature expression in even-dimensional manifolds. In modern language, integrating the Euler form yields the Euler characteristic under the hypotheses of the generalized Gauss–Bonnet theorem.

A manifold is simply a space that looks locally like ordinary Euclidean space, although its overall form may be curved or connected in complicated ways. A sphere is a two-dimensional manifold: a small patch looks almost flat even though the complete surface is round. A four-dimensional manifold obeys the same local principle with four independent directions. Connections describe how to compare directions as one moves through the space. Curvature registers the failure of such comparisons to return unchanged after a small circuit. Exterior differential forms provide a way to combine these measurements that does not rely on a preferred coordinate grid.

The full formula is technical, but the conceptual sequence is accessible. First choose local geometric information—how directions turn and bend. Next combine that information in a way that survives a change of coordinates. Finally assemble it across the entire manifold. The resulting total can be constrained to equal a topological invariant. Instead of trying to inspect the whole space by eye, the mathematician builds a quantity from local data and proves it cannot vary continuously in arbitrary ways. This is part of why Chern's work was so influential beyond a single theorem.

Chern returned to China after the war and undertook a different kind of work. At the Academia Sinica he helped establish an institute for mathematics, first amid postwar reconstruction and then amid renewed civil conflict. The Chinese Civil War threatened the academic infrastructure just as a generation of mathematicians was trying to rebuild after the Japanese occupation. Chern's life again became a question of whether a mathematical community could sustain itself. In 1949 he moved to the United States, taking a position at the University of Chicago. He worked in a major center of geometry and encountered André Weil again, now a fellow participant in the American university system shaped by European and Asian migrations.

The Chicago years were important in making characteristic classes part of modern geometry. Here is the underlying problem. Imagine attaching a little vector space at every point of a sphere. At each point the vectors might represent directions in which an object can move. These vector spaces can sometimes be assembled into one uniform system; in other cases they twist as they pass around the underlying space. A vector bundle is the mathematical structure describing this collection of attached vector spaces. Characteristic classes assign topological information to such bundles, and Chern classes do this in particular for complex vector bundles.

One simplified way to feel the issue is to ask whether one can choose a nonzero tangent arrow at every point on a sphere, continuously and with no sudden jump. The hairy-ball theorem says no for the ordinary two-sphere. Such theorems reveal that apparently local choices are constrained by global structure. Chern classes belong to a richer theory, and this arrow example is only an analogy, not a definition of those classes. But it makes the stakes clear: local linear algebra does not always fit into a global arrangement without an obstruction.

The word connection in mathematics might sound like a social metaphor, but here it is a specific geometric structure. On a sphere, imagine carrying an arrow while walking along lines so that it turns as little as possible according to the surface's own geometry. Complete a small loop. The arrow may come back pointing in a different direction. The mismatch reveals curvature. On a flat sheet, moving an arrow around a loop produces no such rotation when the surface is treated with its ordinary flat geometry. In higher-dimensional geometry the analogous measurement is encoded by curvature forms. This makes characteristic-class formulas less mysterious: they are systematic ways to aggregate information about how local directions fit together globally.

Chern and other geometers developed methods for computing characteristic information through curvature. This is often called Chern–Weil theory, reflecting the contributions of Chern and André Weil and the related mathematical tradition. A curvature form seems to depend on a choice of connection, but appropriate combinations determine cohomological classes independent of that choice. The distinction between a representative formula and the underlying class is central. If the chosen representation changes, the topological information remains. This is one of the great working advantages of connecting geometry with topology: different coordinates and constructions can lead to the same invariant.

The field became a collaboration across generations, not a sequence of papers written by one person. Chern trained students, corresponded with experts working on topology and geometry, and benefited from schools that used increasingly abstract concepts. His research belonged to a network including Weil, Cartan, and later geometers such as Shing-Tung Yau. Students carried geometric methods into new questions, and the language of bundles and characteristic classes became standard in mathematics and theoretical physics. Chern's achievements were distinct, but their influence depended on their being taught and reworked by other people.

In 1960 he left Chicago for the University of California, Berkeley, where his presence helped make the department a leading site for geometry. Berkeley offered more than a research office. It meant the capacity to recruit and train mathematicians, convene seminars, and sustain a field in which problems might take years to solve. Chern had seen universities survive displacement, and he now had the institutional position to make long-term mathematical work possible for others. An international university career, in his case, was both an opportunity and the consequence of political events that had forced repeated choices about where to live.

In 1974 Chern and James Simons published work introducing what are now called Chern–Simons forms and invariants. Their subject grows out of the same differential-geometric machinery but asks a somewhat different question. When certain characteristic forms have zero curvature contribution in the relevant dimension, one can study related quantities defined through the connection itself. In suitable settings these yield invariants with profound consequences for topology and mathematical physics. Later connections to gauge theory and quantum field theory were developed by many mathematicians and physicists. It would be anachronistic to describe the nineteen-seventies paper as if its authors were already working inside every later physical application.

Chern's role as an institutional founder also continued. With Calvin Moore and Isadore Singer he helped establish the Mathematical Sciences Research Institute in Berkeley in the early nineteen-eighties. An institute organized around focused research programs has a different purpose from a department committed to semester teaching. It brings visiting researchers together for sustained exchanges. Its success depends on logistics, funding, and scholarly judgment as well as on mathematical celebrity. Chern's willingness to undertake this work was part of his contribution to research culture, not an afterthought detached from the theorems.

For younger mathematicians from China, a world-famous geometer's connection to the place where he had trained could change what seemed professionally possible. Graduate study, exchange visits, and collaboration depend on people willing to introduce students and provide institutional support. Chern was associated with efforts to reconnect Chinese and international research communities and took a direct interest in the Nankai institute's work. This did not erase the structural difficulties of research funding or political boundaries, and he did not alone reconstruct a national mathematical system. It shows the scope of the work he considered worth undertaking after theorems had already made him famous.

In the nineteen-eighties he renewed institutional engagement in China, helping develop the mathematics institute at Nankai University in Tianjin. His career had come almost full circle: the student who entered Nankai when its mathematics program was developing was now returning with experience building and leading international institutes. Political relations between China and the United States had changed enormously over his lifetime. Mathematical collaboration could again travel in directions previously restricted by war and revolution. Chern continued to divide his attention between teaching, scholarship, and the creation of durable opportunities for younger researchers.

He received major honors, but the more telling measure of influence is how his ideas became tools used by people who never met him. A graduate student studying bundles, a geometer calculating curvature on a manifold, or a physicist following a topological quantity through a gauge field works in mathematical territory transformed by the developments associated with Chern. These applications do not make every nearby theorem his invention. They show how a small number of conceptual bridges can change what other researchers are able to formulate.

Chern died in Tianjin in 2004. By then he had experienced university life in republican China, Nazi-era Germany, wartime France and China, postwar Princeton, Chicago, Berkeley, and a later renewed Chinese research community. The line of travel is not a sightseeing itinerary. Each location represented a distinct arrangement of teachers, students, professional security, funding, and political power. The mathematics was carried through those arrangements, sometimes obstructed by them and sometimes made possible by them.

Return to the curvature of a sphere. If you inspect only one small patch, you may learn how the surface bends without knowing what the whole object is. Add local information correctly, however, and something global becomes visible. Chern's mathematical achievement was to make that relationship far more general and more useful. His historical achievement was different but related: he helped connect local communities of mathematicians across a world repeatedly divided by conflict. Neither story explains the other mechanically. Together they make a fuller portrait of a person who cared about both the ideas and the conditions in which they could be pursued.

## Historical companion — not spoken

### Chronology and evidence

- **1911:** Born in Jiaxing, Zhejiang, during the political transition from the Qing dynasty.
- **1926–1930:** Mathematical training at Nankai in Tianjin; subsequent graduate research at Tsinghua.
- **1934–1936:** Hamburg doctorate under Wilhelm Blaschke; Paris work with Élie Cartan followed.
- **1937–1943:** University displacement during the Sino-Japanese War, culminating in teaching at the Southwest Associated University in Kunming; marriage to Shih-Ning Cheng in 1939.
- **1943–1945:** Institute for Advanced Study, Princeton; higher-dimensional Gauss–Bonnet theorem.
- **1946–1948:** Postwar research and institute-building at Academia Sinica, in renewed civil conflict.
- **1949–1960:** University of Chicago; then UC Berkeley, continuing as researcher and organizer.
- **1974:** Chern–Simons work with James Simons; **1982:** founding involvement with the Mathematical Sciences Research Institute.
- **1985 onward:** Support for the Nankai mathematical institute; **2004:** died in Tianjin.

### Mathematical companion

- **Gauss–Bonnet on a closed oriented surface:** \(\int_M K\,dA=2\pi\chi(M)\). For a sphere \(\chi=2\), for a torus \(\chi=0\). State the closedness/orientability assumptions; surfaces with boundary need a boundary term.
- **Generalized theorem:** In even dimensions, a properly normalized characteristic form built from the curvature integrates to the Euler characteristic. This is not just a coordinate formula for a sphere.
- **Chern classes:** Cohomology classes of complex vector bundles; Chern–Weil expresses real-coefficient representatives in terms of curvature. The choice of connection affects the form, not its cohomology class.
- **Chern–Simons:** Developed with James Simons in 1974. Later gauge-theoretic physics includes extensive contributions by other researchers.
- **Attribution:** Distinguish the nineteenth-century surface theorem from Chern's higher-dimensional construction and the multi-author development of characteristic classes.
