# 074000 - Peter Gustav Lejeune Dirichlet: Paris, Berlin, a mathematical household, and the distribution of primes

**Track:** History - People and ideas. **Focus:** the Rhineland under Napoleon, French mathematical education, General Foy's liberal household, Humboldt's patronage, Berlin and Göttingen universities, Rebecka Mendelssohn, Jacobi, mathematical teaching, Fourier series, number theory, and the burdens of academic employment. **Background:** integers, functions and elementary calculus; the mathematics is explained as it arises.

The mathematician who helped make number theory an analytic science was born in a town that belonged to the French Empire and made his career in German universities. Peter Gustav Lejeune Dirichlet's life is often reduced to two phrases: primes in arithmetic progressions, and the Dirichlet problem. Yet those phrases conceal a remarkable journey between two intellectual worlds. He learned mathematics in Paris in the aftermath of Napoleon, taught soldiers and university students in Berlin, became part of the Mendelssohn family's musical world, and moved to Göttingen when Carl Friedrich Gauss died. He helped educate a generation of mathematicians even while university bureaucracy consumed time he badly needed for research.

Dirichlet was born in February eighteen oh five in Düren, between Aachen and Cologne. The region had been brought under French administration by revolutionary and Napoleonic expansion. The political frontier would change again after eighteen fifteen, when the Rhineland passed to Prussia. The boy grew up in a landscape where language, law and political authority could change without a family moving house. The family name has its own cross-border history. His ancestors came from the country around Liège, and Lejeune Dirichlet reflects a family name associated with Richelet. Some accounts simplify this as French ancestry; surviving family research gives a more specific regional history.

His father was the postmaster in Düren. The family was not a dynasty of research mathematicians. A boy who spent pocket money on mathematics books had to find teachers and institutions able to carry him further. He attended school at Bonn, then a Jesuit school in Cologne, where Georg Simon Ohm was among his teachers. Ohm is now remembered for a law of electrical circuits, but his teaching belonged to an era before physics and mathematics had settled into the professional boundaries familiar today. For Dirichlet, school offered both formal instruction and an example of mathematical thinking applied across subjects.

Around eighteen twenty-two, still a teenager, Dirichlet decided that Paris offered the education he wanted. The best universities in the German states were not yet uniformly the centers of advanced mathematical research that they would become later in the century. France, by contrast, had built a network of institutions shaped by the Revolution, the Napoleonic wars and the needs of engineering. Dirichlet traveled with a treasured copy of Gauss's *Disquisitiones Arithmeticae*. That book was not easy reading. It organized number theory as a disciplined investigation of congruences, reciprocity and patterns among integers, and Dirichlet returned to it repeatedly.

The journey did not begin smoothly. He contracted smallpox soon after arriving. Once recovered, he listened to lectures at the Collège de France and other Paris institutions. There he encountered the world of Fourier, Legendre, Poisson and their colleagues. French analysis offered techniques for differential equations, heat, astronomy and mechanics; Gauss's book offered a different set of questions about integers. His mature originality came partly from learning to connect these traditions rather than choosing one over the other.

In eighteen twenty-three he became a tutor in the household of General Maximilien Foy, teaching German to members of the family. Foy was a veteran of Napoleon's armies and a prominent liberal opponent of the restored Bourbon government. His home was more than a source of income for a young scholar. It was a place where politics, literature and science intersected. Dirichlet's introduction to a network of educated adults was therefore not mediated only by lecture halls and examinations. His eventual career owed something to access granted through a private household in politically contentious Paris.

Before he was twenty-one, he attacked Fermat's Last Theorem for the fifth power. Earlier work by Fermat and Euler had dealt with other exponents, but there was no general proof. Dirichlet presented a substantial part of the fifth-power case to the Paris Academy in eighteen twenty-five. Legendre, one of the referees, completed the remaining case by a separate argument. Dirichlet subsequently found his own way through it. The collaboration and sequence matter. Later textbook histories sometimes announce a single name beside a completed theorem, losing the distinction between a young mathematician's paper, a referee's contribution and subsequent independent completion.

Foy died late in eighteen twenty-five. The household that had given Dirichlet financial security and intellectual access could no longer shelter his prospects. Alexander von Humboldt, the scientist and influential Prussian adviser, helped him return to the German academic world. The entry requirements were awkward. A German university appointment could demand a doctorate, a formal habilitation and proficiency in Latin, none of which fit neatly with the education Dirichlet had actually received in Paris. Cologne granted an honorary doctorate and he took up work at Breslau. His case illustrates a recurring paradox of professionalization: institutions may need an exceptional researcher and still struggle to recognize the path by which that researcher learned his craft.

Breslau did not provide the community he hoped for. He moved to Berlin in eighteen twenty-eight, appointed to teach at the military college while also entering university teaching. The military appointment gave him an income and a route into the capital's scholarly world, but it also produced a permanent obligation. His research time was repeatedly divided among teaching, preparation, meetings and administration. In letters late in his Berlin career he complained about the number of lectures he had to deliver each week. The burden is important to remember when treating his papers as the inevitable output of a mind protected from ordinary labor.

Berlin also gave him a family. In eighteen thirty-two he married Rebecka Mendelssohn Bartholdy, the younger sister of the composer Felix Mendelssohn and the musician Fanny Hensel. Rebecka was a singer, pianist, reader and correspondent in her own right. She had grown up in a family whose home was a center of concerts, debate and conversation. She knew languages, followed public lectures and maintained an extensive network of letters. She should not appear in Dirichlet's life merely as the sister of more famous siblings. The State Library in Berlin preserves correspondence that shows her interests, friendships and independent presence within the family's intellectual culture.

Their home connected mathematical conversations to chamber music and the social life of the Prussian capital. The couple had four children, one of whom died in infancy. Visiting scholars could encounter a household in which mathematics was one strand of a larger cultural exchange. The famous mathematician's published papers tell little about arrangements of household time, care and friendship; Rebecka's surviving letters allow a fuller account of the life in which those papers were written. They also caution us not to confuse Dirichlet's preference for concentrated mathematical thought with the whole personality of a husband or father.

The musical household also gives us documentary evidence that is different from the usual archive of mathematical correspondence. Rebecka attended public intellectual lectures, exchanged letters with Fanny and other relatives, and organized musical gatherings after the family moved to Göttingen. Letters preserved in Berlin describe visits, performances, worries and everyday choices. They are especially useful because mathematicians' biographies have so often reconstructed a marriage only from a husband's publications. Here there is a second written voice, well educated and socially connected, which lets historians recover a larger part of the family's world. The importance of the evidence is not that musical talent explains a prime-number theorem. It is that a mathematical career occupied a real household with competing claims on attention, affection and practical responsibility.

His friendship with Carl Gustav Jacobi was especially productive. Jacobi worked at Königsberg and brought tremendous energy to elliptic functions, determinants, mechanics and number theory. The two men differed in style, yet admired one another's research. When Jacobi became seriously ill and was advised to travel south, Dirichlet advocated for financial support, with help from Humboldt. In eighteen forty-three they traveled to Italy with colleagues including Charles Borchardt. These journeys were not simply holidays. Nineteenth-century mathematical communication still depended on letters, personal visits, academy meetings and networks that could cross political frontiers more effectively than individual universities.

To understand Dirichlet's central mathematical achievement, imagine making a list of positive integers that ends with the same pattern of digits. Consider the numbers four, nine, fourteen, nineteen and so on: each is five more than the previous one. Some are prime and many are not. Does the list nevertheless contain infinitely many primes? Or consider three, seven, eleven, fifteen and so on. Again there are primes and composites. The interesting condition is that the starting number and the step have no common factor greater than one. If they shared a factor, every term would inherit it, and only a possible initial exception could be prime.

Euclid had shown that there are infinitely many primes without asking how they are distributed among such lists. In eighteen thirty-seven Dirichlet proved that every arithmetic progression with relatively prime starting value and step contains infinitely many primes. This answered a question connected to earlier number theorists including Legendre. It was also a new way of attacking integer problems. He did not discover the result by inspecting progressively larger lists and extrapolating. He related the arithmetic conditions to analytic expressions whose behavior could be studied with methods resembling those used for series of real or complex numbers.

One tool is now called a Dirichlet character. In elementary form, think of assigning carefully chosen periodic weights to the integers, weights designed to distinguish one remainder class from another. These weights can be inserted into sums involving reciprocals of powers of integers. Such series, later written with the letter L, allow information about primes to be extracted from analytic behavior. The hard part is not merely writing down a series. It is proving enough about its convergence and about its value near a critical point to prevent the primes in a desired progression from running out. In particular, a nonvanishing result is essential. This kind of argument helped establish analytic number theory as a field in which analysis and arithmetic could genuinely answer one another's questions.

To see what the general theorem adds, take the integers that leave remainder three upon division by four: three, seven, eleven, fifteen, nineteen, and so on. A relatively elementary variant of Euclid's argument shows that infinitely many primes occur in this particular class. The class leaving remainder one is harder to control by that same direct trick. Dirichlet's method applies to both and to every permitted remainder class for every modulus. This is why the theorem is not just the observation that primes sometimes appear in a list. It provides a systematic answer that does not depend on a lucky congruence. The proof's analytic machinery also changed what mathematicians thought could be gained by studying a series or a complex-valued function: not simply a numerical approximation, but a statement about the unlimited supply of prime numbers.

Dirichlet returned to arithmetic repeatedly. He worked on quadratic forms, class numbers and the arithmetic of more general number systems. A theorem now bearing his name describes the structure of the units in rings of algebraic integers: roughly, after a finite torsion part, the invertible elements are organized by a finite collection of independent generators. That language is modern. Dirichlet's own work belonged to an era before the later abstract definitions of rings and groups had acquired their familiar textbook form. His lectures on number theory, edited and expanded after his death by Richard Dedekind, became important in transmitting both his results and the methods of his school.

Analysis furnished an equally consequential set of problems. Joseph Fourier had represented heat distributions using sums of sines and cosines. Such representations were powerful but raised questions that were not settled by successful calculation. Does a Fourier series really converge to the function one began with? At which points, and under what conditions? Dirichlet supplied influential sufficient conditions and distinguished behavior at continuous points from behavior at jumps. The values assigned at a jump are not simply an inconvenience of notation: the series may approach the average of one-sided limiting values. This forced a clearer separation between a formula, the function it represents and the conditions under which the representation works.

At the same time he helped change the language of functions. Older treatments often tied the idea of a function to a single analytic expression. The nineteenth-century discussion increasingly permitted a function to assign a value to each input without requiring one uniform formula of a familiar kind. It would be inaccurate to claim that Dirichlet alone invented the modern concept. Fourier's examples, other analysts' practices and later work all mattered. But his studies made the broader viewpoint hard to avoid: mathematics needed to decide what a function was before it could assert that every function possessed a particular representation.

A different part of his reputation concerns boundary-value problems. Suppose a region is bounded by a closed surface and the temperature is specified on that boundary. Can one find a stable temperature distribution inside, with no internal sources of heat? Under appropriate conditions the desired function is harmonic: its value balances neighboring values in the way expressed by Laplace's equation. Dirichlet's approach sought a function minimizing an energy integral, a quantity built from the squares of its spatial derivatives. Later mathematicians made this approach rigorous only after clarifying existence and compactness questions. Riemann famously used a principle associated with Dirichlet in his own work, and Weierstrass exposed gaps in some of the arguments then in use. The history belongs to a chain of mathematical repair rather than a single definitive invention.

Other ideas carrying Dirichlet's name, including a familiar elementary principle of counting, are not equally attributable to one published discovery. The pigeonhole principle says, in its simplest form, that placing more objects than boxes forces at least one box to contain multiple objects. It is much older in spirit than its modern label. The useful historical distinction is between an idea's ancient elementary content, the mathematician who used it effectively and the later convention of attaching a name to it.

Dirichlet was admired as a teacher. Riemann, Kronecker and other younger mathematicians were influenced by his lectures. Dedekind later edited his number theory lectures in a way that made them influential beyond a single classroom. Such pupils and editors are part of a mathematical career. One person produces results, but other people preserve, reorganize, criticize and teach them until they become the common language of a field. The famous Berlin school was not built by one master lecturing into an empty room.

By eighteen fifty-five Dirichlet wanted relief from the work that had accumulated around him. When Gauss died and Göttingen offered the vacant professorship, he first tried to negotiate less military teaching in Berlin. The Prussian ministry was slow to act. He accepted Göttingen. Only afterward did Berlin offer the concessions he had sought. Göttingen promised time for thought and a different university environment, but this improvement came late in his life. Here too, the shape of a scientific career depended on a decision by administrators who were not writing the mathematics themselves.

The last years brought illness. Dirichlet suffered a serious cardiac episode during a visit to Switzerland in eighteen fifty-eight. Rebecka died later that year, a loss felt across a family whose correspondence records a life of music and friendship. Dirichlet died in Göttingen in May eighteen fifty-nine. He was fifty-four. His published legacy was impressive, but there is no reason to imagine that he had finished all he intended to do.

Dirichlet's biography is a study of intellectual movement. From Düren he carried Gauss's book to Paris; from Paris he carried analytic methods back to a German university system still remaking itself. In Berlin, scholarship, military teaching, family music, friendships and academic politics occupied the same days. His theorem about primes is exact, but the life around it was not a neat theorem with a single author and a clean conclusion. It was a succession of institutions and relationships through which a new style of mathematics became possible.

## Historical companion (not narrated)

### Chronology and evidence cautions
- **1805–1822:** Born in French-administered Düren; education in Bonn and Cologne, including teaching by Georg Simon Ohm; traveled to Paris with Gauss's *Disquisitiones*.
- **1823–1828:** Tutor to General Foy's family; contribution to exponent-five Fermat theorem, with Legendre's independent completion; Foy's death, Humboldt's support, Breslau and Berlin appointments.
- **1832–1855:** Marriage to Rebecka Mendelssohn Bartholdy; four children; productive number theory and analysis; collaboration and travel with Jacobi; heavy Berlin university and military-college teaching.
- **1855–1859:** Succeeded Gauss at Göttingen; Rebecka died in 1858 following a stroke; Dirichlet died in 1859 following serious illness.

**Attribution cautions:** The fifth-power Fermat proof combines distinct work by Dirichlet and Legendre. Dirichlet's 1837 theorem is an early foundation of analytic number theory, not the first use of analysis in arithmetic. Modern character/series notation and a modern statement of the unit theorem are retrospective teaching formulations. Weierstrass's later criticisms of the Dirichlet principle concern proof of minimizers' existence, not the automatic falsity of the physical boundary problem. Rebecka was an intellectual participant in her own right, not merely a link to Felix or Fanny.

### Mathematical display companion
- For integers $a,d$ with $\gcd(a,d)=1$ and $d\geq1$, Dirichlet's theorem states that $a+nd$ is prime for infinitely many integers $n\geq0$.
- A Dirichlet character $\chi$ modulo $d$ is a periodic completely multiplicative function, zero when its argument is not coprime to $d$. The associated $L$-series is $L(s,\chi)=\sum_{n=1}^\infty \chi(n)n^{-s}$ for $\Re(s)>1$.
- For piecewise smooth periodic $f$, Fourier series at a jump tend to $(f(x^-)+f(x^+))/2$, under sufficient standard hypotheses; not every arbitrary function is covered by this statement.
- The classical Dirichlet energy is $E[u]=\int_{\Omega}|\nabla u|^2\,dx$. Showing that an infimum is attained needs additional hypotheses and mathematical work.
