# 105000 - Archimedes: The geometer of Syracuse, between proof and war

**Track:** History — People and ideas. **Focus:** the city of Syracuse and its rulers; mathematical correspondence with Alexandria; discoveries in geometry, mechanics and floating bodies; war-machine stories and their evidence; the Roman siege; and the transmission of the *Method* through the Archimedes Palimpsest. **Background:** circles, areas, ratios and simple balances are explained in speech.

The surviving life of Archimedes contains two very different kinds of stories. In one, a man walks into a bath, notices water rising, and runs through the streets shouting that he has found the answer to a king's puzzle. In another, that same man sends difficult propositions to mathematicians across the sea, deliberately withholding the proofs to see who can produce them. The first is a famous anecdote written long after the event it claims to describe. The second comes from a preface that Archimedes himself wrote. The difference matters. One tells us what later generations wanted a mathematical genius to look like. The other lets us hear a mathematician speaking about his work, his rivals, and the obligations of proof.

Archimedes lived in Syracuse, a wealthy Greek-speaking city on the island of Sicily, in the third century before the common era. His life is conventionally placed from about two hundred eighty-seven to two hundred twelve before the common era. Syracuse had a complicated political position between the Greek kingdoms, Carthage and the expanding Roman republic. Mathematics here was not the activity of a person detached from the state. Rulers needed ships, fortifications, money, measurements and machines. Archimedes's works would circulate among scholars, but his reputation also reached people who knew very little geometry and cared a great deal about weapons.

He names his father Phidias, an astronomer, in his work The Sand Reckoner. That is a rare personal fact grounded in his own writing. Other aspects of his family remain obscure. Later authors connect him to Hieron the Second, the ruler of Syracuse, and describe personal or familial relations. Archimedes dedicated The Sand Reckoner to Gelon, Hieron's son. The dedication is strong evidence of a relationship with the ruling circle, but it does not give us a diary of domestic life or an exact family tree.

Archimedes knew the mathematical world of Alexandria, then one of the major intellectual centers of the eastern Mediterranean. Whether he studied there formally is uncertain, but he corresponded with mathematicians associated with the city, including Conon of Samos. His texts also address Dositheus and Eratosthenes. The correspondence gives us a view of ancient research that can be surprisingly familiar. A result is sent to a friend. The friend is asked to check it, admire it, extend it or find a proof. New mathematics moves through people before it settles into a book.

In the preface to On Spirals, Archimedes describes sending statements of propositions without proofs and says that some people claimed the results without actually proving them. He even reports including false statements as a test of that pretense. We need not treat every sentence of an author's self-description as impartial evidence about a rival. Yet the distinction he draws is fundamental: finding a pattern is one achievement, proving it is another. Authority is not earned merely by saying that a proposition is true.

Consider a circle. Everyone can see that its circumference grows when its diameter grows. But how can we determine the relationship precisely without assuming a formula we are trying to discover? Archimedes placed regular polygons inside and outside the circle. Their perimeters provide lower and upper bounds on the circumference. If the polygons have more sides, the bounds get tighter. With careful geometric inequalities, he obtained bounds for the ratio we call pi: greater than three and ten seventy-firsts, and less than three and one seventh. The upper bound, twenty-two divided by seven, became familiar to generations of students, although it is an approximation, not the exact value of pi.

This was not a matter of collecting enough measurements to guess a decimal. It was a proof using inequalities. The polygons could be constructed with a compass and straightedge, and each step followed from relations among sides and angles. A single circle drawn in sand might suggest a number, but the argument establishes a bound for ideal circles, not merely for the diagram on a particular afternoon.

Archimedes also worked on the area of curved figures. In The Quadrature of the Parabola, he found that a region bounded by a segment of a parabola and a chord has an area four thirds the area of a certain inscribed triangle. Imagine beginning with that triangle, then filling in two of the remaining gaps with smaller triangles, then four more, and continuing. The successive additions form a geometric pattern. Modern mathematics can describe the pattern with an infinite geometric series; Archimedes gave arguments of the kind known as exhaustion, showing that the difference between the constructed approximation and the target cannot remain positive beyond a specified bound.

A proof by exhaustion has a distinctive tone. Instead of saying that an approximation appears closer and closer, it asks what would follow if an alleged difference were still there. Suppose one claimed that the actual area exceeded the proposed answer. By adding enough of the constructed pieces, the remaining unfilled area could be made smaller than that supposed excess. The claim would contradict the construction. Similar reasoning excludes an answer that is too large. This logic helped Greek mathematicians reason about curved areas without writing the notation of later integral calculus.

Archimedes's research on the sphere and cylinder has a personal note too. Ancient sources say he considered his relation between their volumes and surface areas among his proudest achievements. For a sphere fitted exactly inside a cylinder of the same diameter and height, the sphere's volume is two thirds the volume of the cylinder. The sphere's surface area is also two thirds the cylinder's total surface area, counting its two circular ends. He wanted the corresponding figures represented on his tomb, according to the later account of Cicero, who said he located the grave while serving in Sicily. The anecdote is not Archimedes's own instruction preserved on stone, but it conveys how his geometric work was remembered after his death.

That relationship deserves a moment of attention. The sphere and the cylinder look so different. One is curved everywhere; the other has flat ends and a straight-sided wall. An ordinary ruler can measure the cylinder's height and radius easily, while the sphere seems to resist direct measurement. Archimedes discovered a stable ratio between them. The lesson is not that every curved surface can be solved by fitting it into a simple container. It is that comparison with a carefully chosen figure can turn a difficult measurement into an exact relationship.

His interests also included centers of gravity, levers, and the equilibrium of bodies. A balance responds not only to how much weight is on each side but also to the distance of that weight from the pivot. If a weight is moved farther from the pivot, a smaller weight on the other side may balance it when placed farther away appropriately. Archimedes developed mathematical treatments of these relations in On the Equilibrium of Planes. One should not describe him as the first person ever to use a lever. People had used balancing devices and mechanical advantage long before his lifetime. His distinctive contribution was to turn such observations into an organized theory.

That theory forms a bridge to a different problem: what happens when a body floats or sinks in water? In On Floating Bodies Archimedes investigates equilibrium under the action of liquid. The principle now bearing his name says that a body immersed in a fluid experiences an upward force equal to the weight of fluid it displaces. If you push a light ball into water, you feel the water trying to raise it. A floating boat settles until the displaced water weighs as much as the boat. What matters in a simple hydrostatic model is not whether the body is made of wood or stone alone, but the relation between its average density, volume and the surrounding fluid.

The popular story says Archimedes tested a crown to discover whether a goldsmith had mixed cheaper silver into the gold, using water displacement while bathing. Vitruvius recounts this tale in a Roman architectural work, centuries after Archimedes lived. We cannot treat the shouted word Eureka or the precise experiment as a verified eyewitness report. Even reconstructing a sufficiently accurate test for a small difference in crown composition raises practical difficulties. The underlying mathematical point about displaced water is sound. The bath scene is a story about discovery whose historicity is much less secure.

Another famous phrase is often attached to Archimedes: give me a place to stand, and I will move the earth. Various ancient sources connect him to the extraordinary power of mechanical devices. The image captures the logic of the lever, but it is a slogan, not a theorem about lifting a planet with real materials. A sufficiently long lever introduces questions about the lever's weight and strength, the position of the fulcrum, and how a force is applied. Mathematics idealizes; engineering must also account for the material world.

The work called The Sand Reckoner shows a different side of his imagination. Archimedes wanted to demonstrate that very large numbers could be named and manipulated systematically. Rather than declaring that the grains of sand in the universe must be beyond counting, he developed a system of magnitudes large enough to represent an upper estimate for them. In the text he refers to a model attributed to Aristarchus of Samos in which the Earth moves around the Sun. Mentioning that hypothesis does not establish Archimedes's own commitment to heliocentrism. It shows a researcher using an astronomical model as part of an ambitious calculation and confronting the limits of ordinary numerical language.

These projects belonged to a world in which written works were copied by hand, sometimes transported by sea, and vulnerable to loss. We do not possess Archimedes's original manuscripts. His mathematical heritage reached later readers through fragments, translations, commentaries and successive copies. That makes the content of his letters and prefaces especially valuable: they supply a little of his own authorial voice, even though we know it only through later textual witnesses.

His career ended in war. After the death of Hieron the Second, Syracuse's political relations shifted amid the conflict between Rome and Carthage. Roman forces besieged the city under Marcus Claudius Marcellus. Ancient historians describe defensive machines associated with Archimedes: missile throwers, devices for striking or lifting ships, and other ingenious ways of using mechanical advantage. Some descriptions may preserve real engineering; others became more elaborate in retelling. Accounts of mirrors focusing sunlight to burn enemy ships are much less secure and should not be presented as established devices used in the siege.

The conflict is important not because the mathematician became a fictional superhero defending a city alone, but because the siege reveals the fragility of a research life under military power. The armies were fighting for territory and political control. Archimedes's expertise was drawn into that conflict, willingly or under expectations we cannot recover. When Syracuse fell to Rome in two hundred twelve before the common era, Archimedes was killed. The ancient sources give differing stories about the immediate circumstances. In some he was absorbed in a diagram and did not recognize a soldier's command; other accounts differ about the encounter. There is no reason to select the most picturesque version and present it as certain.

The most astonishing later episode in his history concerns a book whose pages had been erased. In the Middle Ages, parchment containing copies of Archimedes's writings was reused for a religious manuscript. The old mathematical writing remained partly visible beneath the later text. In nineteen oh six, the scholar Johan Ludvig Heiberg recognized important Archimedean material in this palimpsest. Modern imaging has helped scholars recover more of the underlying writing. The object known as the Archimedes Palimpsest has given historians access to texts that otherwise would have remained lost or known only indirectly.

One recovered work, The Method of Mechanical Theorems, is especially revealing. Archimedes presents a way of investigating geometric quantities by imagining figures cut into thin slices and comparing them as if they were balanced mechanically. This is not a modern integral calculated with a ready-made symbol. Nor did he regard the mechanical reasoning alone as a substitute for full geometric demonstration. He presented it as a way to discover relationships that could then be proved rigorously. The contrast between finding and proving is the same one visible in his letters to Alexandria.

Think of a complicated curved region divided into imagined strips. One can compare each strip with a strip from a simpler region and ask how their weights would balance around a pivot. If the balance relation holds for the assembled figures, it can suggest a precise ratio between areas or volumes. The beauty is the choice of representation: instead of seeing an indivisible curved figure, we see pieces connected through equilibrium. The limitation is equally important. An imagined infinitely thin slice is a heuristic object unless the argument establishes what happens as the slices become finer. Archimedes understood the distinction well enough to make it explicit.

The Palimpsest itself is a reminder that discovery continues after the discoverer dies. The manuscript was copied, reused, damaged, studied and imaged by people separated from Archimedes by many centuries. A scholar in Copenhagen, medieval scribes, a modern museum, specialists in Greek handwriting and imaging scientists all became part of the survival of his mathematical thought. The historical record is not simply stored and waiting. It must often be reconstructed from physical traces.

Archimedes has been described as a predecessor of integral calculus, mathematical physics and mechanical engineering. Those comparisons can be illuminating if we preserve the differences. He did not possess the general calculus notation of Newton and Leibniz. He did not work with the modern concept of pressure fields or differential equations. And he did not invent every machine later given his name. He did something more historically specific: he produced rigorous answers to problems about curves, bodies, balances and fluids, and he described methods by which unknown relationships might be found.

His story is compelling without turning the bath legend into fact or making a Roman soldier the sole explanation of the end of Greek mathematics. There were other mathematicians, other cities, and later traditions of investigation. What Archimedes left was a collection of difficult arguments that people continued reading because they reveal a mind at work. In Syracuse he belonged to a city facing the pressures of Mediterranean power. In his letters he belonged to a wider republic of mathematical correspondents. In his surviving propositions he belonged to neither court nor army, but to anyone willing to follow the proof. Those are three different histories, and his life stands where they meet.

## Historical companion (not narrated)

### Chronology and evidence

- **c. 287 BCE:** conventional birth date; Archimedes identifies Phidias, an astronomer, as his father.
- **Third century BCE:** mathematical writing and correspondence with colleagues in Alexandria, including Conon; links with the Syracusan ruling circle.
- **c. 213–212 BCE:** Roman siege of Syracuse; military devices described in Roman and Greek historical narratives; death during the city's capture in 212 BCE.
- **Later antiquity:** Vitruvius, Plutarch and Cicero transmit different stories, composed long after some or all of the events.
- **1906 to present:** Heiberg's recognition and later imaging of the Archimedes Palimpsest recover parts of *The Method* and other works.

### Mathematical companion

**Bounds on pi.** \(3+10/71<\pi<3+1/7\). The familiar \(22/7\) is an upper bound, not an identity. Methods use inscribed and circumscribed regular polygons.

**Sphere and circumscribed cylinder.** For a sphere of radius \(r\), \(V_{sphere}=(4/3)\pi r^3\). The circumscribing cylinder has radius \(r\) and height \(2r\), hence \(V_{cylinder}=2\pi r^3\). Their ratio is \(2/3\). For surface area including cylinder ends, \(4\pi r^2/(6\pi r^2)=2/3\).

**Parabolic segment.** Archimedes showed the area of a parabolic segment equals \(4/3\) the area of a specified inscribed triangle. This is an exact result supported by exhaustion, not simply a visual guess about nested triangles.

**Buoyancy.** \(F_b=\rho_{fluid}gV_{displaced}\) in the standard simple constant-density hydrostatic model. An object's weight, submerged volume, and density determine whether it floats or sinks in equilibrium.

**Heuristic versus demonstration.** In *The Method*, mechanical balancing helps discover geometric relations. Archimedes distinguishes the suggestive method from deductive proof.

**Historical cautions:** The crown experiment and cry of Eureka appear in a later account by Vitruvius, not a verified contemporary testimony. Ancient accounts of his death disagree. Burning mirrors should not be treated as established siege technology. Mechanical devices attributed to him are not all proven inventions. The *Method* is a discovery technique, distinct from his final geometric proofs.
