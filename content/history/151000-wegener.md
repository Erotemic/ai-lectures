# 151000 — Alfred Wegener: The continents that would not stay still

**Track:** History · People and ideas. **Background:** No prior geology is required. The lecture develops continental drift, evidence from fossils and past climates, the problems with Wegener's proposed mechanisms, the polar expeditions and the later plate-tectonic synthesis.

In the autumn of 1930, a German expedition was trying to keep a research station alive in the middle of Greenland. The station was called Eismitte, literally the middle of the ice. It was so far from the coast that a journey to it was an undertaking in its own right. Supplies meant food and fuel for people intending to remain through the polar winter. A missed delivery was not an inconvenience. It could end the experiment and threaten the people carrying it out.

The expedition leader, Alfred Wegener, had reason to know the danger. This was his fourth journey to Greenland. He had crossed the ice sheet before, tested instruments in polar weather, taught meteorology, and spent much of his adult life in a setting where a small error in measurement or planning could matter enormously. In September he had set off to bring help to the inland station. The transport systems that were supposed to make the expedition efficient had been defeated by conditions on the ice. Men and dogs were now doing work that machines had promised to do.

By the end of October, Wegener and two companions reached Eismitte. One companion, Fritz Loewe, was so badly affected by frostbite that he could not make the return journey. The Greenlandic expedition member Rasmus Villumsen went back with Wegener toward the coast. They did not arrive. Wegener's body was found the following spring; Villumsen was never found. The most familiar account has Wegener dying from a heart problem brought on by exertion, but no witness saw the final event, and his last diary disappeared. We should not turn a reconstruction into an observed fact.

The story is often told as a scientist who died before his greatest idea was vindicated. The idea was that continents move. Today children learn that Africa and South America were once connected. That does not make the discovery simple, and it certainly does not make Greenland an incidental last chapter. Wegener was a meteorologist and polar researcher for whom the behavior of the Earth was always a problem of physical evidence. The question he asked about the positions of continents grew out of a life spent studying atmosphere, ice, land, and the past together.

To understand the idea, we must return to the world he was born into, long before anybody could map the ocean floor with modern instruments.

Alfred Lothar Wegener was born in Berlin on November first, 1880, when the German Empire was young. His father was a theologian and teacher of classical languages. The family also spent time in Zechlinerhütte, in the lake and forest country north of Berlin. Alfred and his older brother Kurt were drawn toward mathematics and natural science. They grew up during a period when weather observation was becoming more systematic, electric communications were spreading, and scientific expeditions could bring measurements from places that had hardly been represented on maps.

Wegener studied in Berlin, Heidelberg, and Innsbruck, and finished his astronomy studies in Berlin in 1904, with a doctorate awarded there in 1905. The dates come from the Deutsches Museum archival biography; some short timelines compress the two events. The turn he made next is more revealing than the difference between those dates. He was interested in what could be measured in the sky, but also in what could be measured between the ground and the sky. He entered the world of atmospheric research: the study of pressure, temperature, winds, humidity, and the energy that makes weather possible.

That field was acquiring new instruments. At the surface, a thermometer records the air immediately around it. The atmosphere above may be very different. If you want to predict weather, you need to know not just what the air is doing at your feet, but how conditions change with altitude and over a wide region. Balloons and kites could lift measuring equipment where an observer could not easily go. Wegener worked at the Prussian aeronautical observatory at Lindenberg, learning how to make these measurements and how to interpret them.

In April 1906 Alfred and Kurt Wegener took a free balloon into the air and remained aloft for more than fifty-two hours, setting an endurance record. That achievement was a feat of patience and logistics, but it was also a piece of the new science. Sustained time in the air made it possible to study changes above the surface. Instruments and humans had to survive the same long interval. The brothers' collaboration belonged to a growing practical tradition in which meteorology was no longer a succession of reports from separate towns. It was the measurement of a connected three-dimensional system.

Later that same year Wegener joined a Danish expedition to northeastern Greenland led by Ludvig Mylius-Erichsen. Greenland was not an empty landscape waiting for Europeans to give it a name. Inuit communities had centuries of knowledge about conditions in the Arctic, and expeditions depended in many ways on Greenlandic skills, equipment, routes, and labor. The European scientific party brought instruments and specific research questions; it was not creating knowledge about the island from nothing.

Wegener's assignment concerned the atmosphere. He used kites and balloons to investigate upper air in conditions much more severe than those around Lindenberg. Cold affects batteries, instruments, ropes, fingers, the time needed to perform the simplest operation. Wind is both something a meteorologist hopes to measure and a force that can stop a measurement. The first Greenland expedition lasted from 1906 to 1908. Several members of the larger expedition, including its leader, died on a separate northern exploration journey. Wegener returned with observations, practical experience, and a closer understanding of what polar exploration demanded from a team.

Back in Germany, he taught at the University of Marburg. A lecturer's life was less dramatic than an Arctic expedition, but it required a different kind of endurance: explaining a field clearly enough that other people could use its methods. In 1911 he published a major work on the thermodynamics of the atmosphere. Thermodynamics studies the relation between heat, work, pressure, and energy. For a weather system these are not abstract variables. Warm air changes density. Rising air expands as surrounding pressure falls and can cool. Water vapor may condense, releasing energy and changing the motion again. To study the atmosphere seriously is to ask how one part of a planetary system affects another.

A separate influence came from Wladimir Köppen, one of the central figures in modern climatology. Köppen worked on patterns connecting temperature, precipitation, and vegetation across regions. He could help Wegener see that climate was not only a matter of one thermometer or one storm; it had a geographical distribution and a history. Köppen also became his father-in-law. Wegener married Köppen's daughter Else in November 1913, after returning from his second Greenland expedition. Else Köppen had her own intellectual abilities and linguistic skills and later worked as a translator, writer, and editor. Their marriage was also a scholarly household, not merely a name entered in a list of family relations.

Before the marriage, though, an idea had interrupted Wegener's meteorological career. In 1910 he noticed the apparent correspondence between the opposing margins of Africa and South America on a map. He did not invent that observation. Thinkers for centuries had noticed that coastlines appeared to fit, and some had suggested that the landmasses had once been connected. One earlier advocate, Frank Bursley Taylor, proposed a form of continental movement before Wegener's famous presentations. Wegener's importance lies not in being first to look at a map, but in assembling independent kinds of evidence into a testable picture of the Earth's history.

A coastline can be misleading. It changes with erosion, sediment, sea level, and the shape of coastal plains. The edge of a continental shelf, now mostly submerged, can be a better guide to the underlying shape of a continent, as later reconstructions demonstrated. Even a good geometric fit is not proof by itself. There are many ways to move paper shapes until something looks plausible. Wegener needed to show that a proposed fit also made sense of facts that had been collected for entirely different reasons.

In 1911 he encountered reports of matching fossils across what was now the Atlantic. It was possible, according to an established geological explanation, that long-vanished land bridges had once connected remote continents. But what would it mean if instead the continents themselves had separated? Fossil organisms and their distributions could then be understood without inventing a sequence of vanished connections that happened to serve each example. The idea was dangerous in the productive scientific sense: it would make demands on rocks, climates, mountain belts, and the map of the world at once.

On January sixth, 1912, Wegener spoke before a geological association in Frankfurt about the origin of oceans and continents. He repeated the idea in Marburg soon afterward. He was thirty-one and trained primarily as an astronomer and meteorologist, not a traditional field geologist. His audience had good reason to ask demanding questions. How could hard continents move? Through what material? Over what time? What could supply the force? The proposal challenged a commonly accepted picture of a mostly fixed geography, but it also invited a discussion about exactly what would count as evidence for a dynamic Earth.

The proposed Earth had an earlier configuration of land in which the present continents had belonged to a much larger connected mass, later called Pangaea. Over immense spans of geological time the mass fragmented, its pieces separated, and oceans opened between them. The essential claim was not that the modern continents had once been nearer one another for a day or a century. It was that whole landmasses could move across the globe over millions of years, turning paleogeography into a problem of motion.

If you are hearing this idea for the first time, imagine trying to solve a history puzzle when the pages of its book have been separated and rearranged. A coastline is the visible edge of one page. A fossil found on either side of an ocean is a sentence cut in two. A mountain belt is a line that seems to continue into another country. A deposit from an ancient glacier is a clue about the position of the climate at the time it formed. Any one clue might have another explanation. A reconstruction becomes powerful when the same arrangement makes several kinds of evidence fall into place at once.

Consider fossils. Mesosaurus was an extinct reptile whose remains were found in parts of South America and southern Africa. A proposal that the two regions had once been joined offers a geographic explanation for a distribution otherwise divided by a wide Atlantic Ocean. Fossils of the seed fern Glossopteris occurred across southern continents that are now far apart. Other organisms supplied further clues. Their modern separation does not force a conclusion by itself; organisms can disperse, and land bridges were plausible proposals in other contexts. But the joint pattern of multiple kinds of life becomes stronger when it fits the same geological reconstruction.

Now consider mountains and rock units. Rocks of similar age and structure occur along the Atlantic-facing edges of continents that have since moved apart. In some cases geological belts that end at a coast appear to resume on the opposite shore when the continents are restored to former positions. Geologists do not judge such matches by color on a tourist map. They ask about mineral content, deformation, age, and the sequence in which the rocks formed. The test is whether the reconstructed belt behaves like one geological feature with a coherent history.

Climate supplied perhaps the most satisfying line of evidence for a meteorologist. Some regions that are warm today preserve rocks deposited by great ice sheets in deep geological time. Ancient ice can leave scratches on bedrock and carry poorly sorted debris that later becomes rock. The traces are not simply proof that the whole planet was frozen at the same time. They record particular ice movements in particular places. On a map with continents in their current locations, deposits from a large ancient southern glaciation can look scattered and difficult to explain. Restore the southern continents into their earlier configuration, and many traces align into a broader high-latitude ice-sheet system.

Wegener developed this argument with Köppen, using the evidence of ancient climate zones to test the proposed continental positions. In 1924 they published a collaborative study of climates in the geological past. It is easy to list Köppen as an important influence and stop there, but this was a substantive research collaboration. Climatology was not just background information borrowed to decorate a theory of rocks. It provided a way to ask whether a reconstruction of ancient geography produced a plausible distribution of deserts, tropical conditions, and ice.

There is a subtle step here. Suppose a layer of rock indicates an old desert where you now find a damp, cool climate. That could mean the climate changed without the land moving. It could mean the land moved across latitude. It could involve changes in ocean circulation, the atmosphere, or the Earth's axis. These are different explanations, and the rock alone may not discriminate among them. But combine fossil belts, geological continuity, and deposits indicating the direction of ice movement, and the possible reconstructions become more constrained. Wegener's achievement was to notice that Earth history was not a set of unrelated local mysteries.

All of this was happening during a life that kept drawing him back to Greenland. In 1912 he joined a new Danish expedition led by Johann Peter Koch. They overwintered in eastern Greenland and in 1913 crossed the northern part of the inland ice sheet toward the west. The journey required hauling equipment across an immense surface where food and fuel had to be counted as carefully as distances. Horses were used for transport as well as human labor, an approach that proved costly to the animals under such conditions. The expedition obtained meteorological and glaciological measurements, but it did so through exhausting travel, uncertainty, and risk.

Wegener's surviving expedition writings show more than heroic postures. There are practical concerns about provisions, living arrangements, his relationship with Else, and the everyday calculations that occupy people working far from home. This is a useful correction to the portrait of a scientist whose mind never leaves a grand theory. He was trying to learn about ice, weather, and the future of his household. He was also trying to finish the evidence for his reconstruction of ancient continents.

After his return in 1913, he married Else and went back to university work at Marburg. Their household would include three daughters. The beginning of the First World War in 1914 altered the available choices. Wegener was called into military service as a reserve officer, served at the western front, and was wounded. After further injuries he was regarded as unfit for ordinary front-line duty and worked in military meteorology. Weather knowledge mattered to wartime aviation and other operations, but the war also damaged scientific networks and interrupted normal academic life. We do not need to invent his private feelings to recognize the changes it imposed.

In 1915, amid the war, Wegener published the first book edition of The Origin of Continents and Oceans. It was not the last word of a single sudden revelation. He revised the book as new results appeared, issuing later editions in 1920, 1922, and 1929. Scientific publications are sometimes remembered as monuments fixed at one date, but Wegener kept using new evidence and replying to objections. The theory was a working research program as well as a public argument.

A good scientific argument must do two different jobs. It must establish that an observation calls for explanation, and it must show how a proposed process could produce the observation. Wegener made remarkable progress on the first job. He had a much harder time on the second. To see why, we need to picture the material underneath our feet, not just the positions of countries on a map.

A modern map divides the world into land and ocean. That division is not the fundamental division in plate tectonics. Continental crust is generally thicker and less dense than typical oceanic crust. Both are part of a rigid outer layer that can move, but this outer layer is broken into plates that include both continental and oceanic material. They move over a mantle that is mostly solid rock, though it can deform slowly under stress over very long periods. This is not a picture of continents sailing through a liquid sea of molten rock. It is a picture of a shell made of moving plates, with material created, recycled, compressed, and displaced along their boundaries.

Wegener did not have that model available. He imagined the continents moving through or relative to the material beneath them, and entertained forces connected with Earth's rotation and gravitational attractions, including the tidal action of the Moon and Sun. Those proposed forces could not account for the required movement in the way he needed. The oceanic crust posed a severe mechanical problem. If continents were to plow bodily through it, what stresses would be required? Would the rocks survive? How could the process produce the observed mountain belts without contradicting known physical behavior? The objections were not all conservative resistance to change. Some were direct challenges to the dynamics of his picture.

Without separating evidence for continental movement from Wegener's proposed mechanism, the story collapses into a parable about one visionary and a roomful of fools. The actual history is more instructive. A scientist can assemble persuasive evidence for a phenomenon while offering an inadequate mechanism. A critic can identify a real flaw in the mechanism and still reject too much of the underlying evidence. Progress may require the community to separate those judgments, then develop an explanation that keeps the successful clues and replaces the failed physics.

Wegener himself understood that the forces were not adequately established. By the fourth edition he treated the full mechanical answer as an open problem. He did not abandon the idea that continent positions had changed, because the fossil, geological, and climate reconstructions still demanded an explanation. But he could not simply declare the motion solved. The unanswered problem was a reason for further research, not a trivial detail to brush aside.

His theory found supporters. Arthur Holmes, a British geologist, was interested in how slow circulation within Earth's interior might drive large-scale movement, developing a mantle-convection proposal around the end of the 1920s. Alexander du Toit, working in South Africa, took continental connections seriously and developed comparative geological evidence. Émile Argand and others also contributed to mobile-Earth thinking. Their specific models were not identical, and modern tectonics cannot be assigned retroactively to them in complete form. They show that Wegener did not spend two decades alone against a unanimous profession.

Resistance was also not uniform. Continental drift provoked different responses in Europe and North America, and debates occurred in geological societies, textbooks, and specialized journals. A widely discussed American symposium held in 1926 included critics and advocates; its papers appeared in print later. Historians disagree about which meetings and publications mattered most in hardening opinion. What is clear is that criticism covered evidence, mechanics, professional disciplines, and sometimes personal authority. Wegener was a meteorologist claiming to explain important geological phenomena. The fact that he worked outside a conventional geological career gave some critics an easy target. It did not remove their obligation to examine the evidence, and it did not remove his obligation to answer mechanics.

It is also worth imagining the alternatives available at the time. A land bridge could explain why closely related fossils appeared on separated continents. A climate belt might shift while the land stayed in place. Mountain ranges might arise through contraction of a cooling Earth. Each explanation could serve a local problem. What continental drift promised was not merely another explanation for a fossil. It was one geography that could be used to test all those observations together. The challenge was to decide whether a broad explanatory framework was worth retaining when a vital mechanism was still unknown.

After the war, Wegener moved to Hamburg in 1919 to work at the German maritime weather service. He helped lead meteorological research and became associated with the University of Hamburg. The public scientist arguing over continents was also involved in weather, upper-air physics, and the institutions that made observations useful. His career at this point was not that of an exile from every scientific community. He had professional responsibilities, students and colleagues, scientific publications, and a family. But an established full professorship in Germany did not come easily. In 1924 he accepted a chair in meteorology and geophysics at the University of Graz in Austria.

Graz became an important home. Wegener taught, published, and continued to revise his geographical arguments. He and Else worked amid the economic and political aftereffects of the First World War, in a European academic world with changing boundaries and resources. The household moved between professional obligations and family life. Else's ability to manage language, manuscripts, and scientific correspondence helped make the work possible, as the documentary record of her translations and later editorial work demonstrates. An argument about the history of continents rested in part on ordinary acts of reading, editing, writing letters, and helping other people understand what had been measured.

Then Greenland drew him back. There is a temptation to treat the 1930 expedition as if Wegener went north simply because opponents had refused his ideas. The sources do not support that as a sufficient explanation. He had been a polar scientist for decades, and the expedition had its own ambitious goals. An ice sheet is a record of climate and a physical object with a shape, motion, temperature, and internal structure. Understanding it requires measurements at the coast and inland, at the surface and beneath it, over more than a single brief summer.

In 1929 Wegener undertook a preparatory expedition to plan a more demanding project. The main German Greenland Expedition began in 1930 and aimed to establish stations at different positions across the ice sheet, including a central overwintering station. The researchers wanted to compare weather from one location to another, improve knowledge of the ice surface and thickness, and understand polar conditions over time. Some equipment used new transport technologies, including motorized propeller sledges. Such machines were attractive because moving scientific instruments and fuel across ice is exhausting and expensive. But a machine designed for an imagined surface may fail on the actual one.

The expedition's plan encountered trouble early. Sea ice delayed unloading equipment and supplies. The machines did not reliably perform their expected transport duty. It became impossible to move enough provisions to Eismitte on the intended schedule. At the inland station, researchers including Johannes Georgi and Ernst Sorge were preparing to remain through winter. A stranded station would not only waste scientific effort; it could leave people in serious danger. Wegener chose to lead another journey inland with members of the expedition, including the meteorologist Fritz Loewe and Greenlandic sled-team participants.

A map can make a distance seem manageable. The route to Eismitte was measured in hundreds of kilometers. Each day's travel had to be paid for in effort and supplies, and colder weather reduced the margin for error. The journey did not go as first planned. Members of the Greenlandic parties turned back as conditions and risks worsened; the remaining team lost much of its ability to carry the intended cargo. Wegener, Loewe, and Villumsen continued and reached Eismitte on October thirtieth. Loewe needed care for frostbitten feet. Wegener's fiftieth birthday fell on November first while they were at the station.

There is an important person in this story whom older narratives often compress into a descriptive phrase. Rasmus Villumsen was a young Greenlandic expedition worker. His own full story is much less well represented in the surviving records than the story of the German leader, but he was not simply a prop in someone else's final chapter. He undertook the journey, made a return attempt with Wegener, and also died. We should speak his name and avoid pretending that the sources give us access to his final thoughts.

Wegener and Villumsen set out westward from Eismitte on or just after November first, intending to return toward the coast. The exact date and cause of Wegener's death are not established by a contemporary witness. When searchers located his body in May 1931, it had been buried carefully; that burial is commonly attributed to Villumsen, who was subsequently lost. The last diary was not recovered. The evidence supports that both men died on the return, but not a narrated account of their last conversation, final decision, or minute of death. For a life spent demanding evidence, that restraint is appropriate.

The inland scientific work nonetheless left a legacy. The surviving researchers measured aspects of Greenland's weather, snow, and ice, including how the ice changed with depth. Later glaciologists would use and extend these observations. Wegener's death did not erase what the expedition had learned, but it brought the uncertainty and costs of polar field research sharply into view. The observations were produced by a team and by people who wintered at Eismitte after the expedition leader was gone.

In the decades after 1930, the continental-drift controversy continued. Textbooks did not simply switch their diagrams the moment Wegener's body was found. The transformation came much later, when a new generation of instruments revealed what the oceans themselves were doing. To understand why this changed the argument, we should examine the floor of the Atlantic rather than its two distant coasts.

Before systematic ocean-floor surveys, the deep sea was in many places little better mapped than the interiors of remote ice sheets. Echo sounding and later oceanographic instruments changed that. Scientists found a long system of submerged mountain ranges, with ridges running through ocean basins. Marie Tharp, Bruce Heezen, and other researchers contributed to making the shape of the ocean floor intelligible; their work belongs to a later discovery story and should not be handed backward to Wegener. A ridge beneath the Atlantic suggests that the ocean basin is a geological structure with an active history rather than a permanent gap that has always looked the same.

Harry Hess and Robert Dietz advanced ideas about the spreading of the sea floor. Imagine new rock forming near the center of an oceanic ridge, cooling as it moves away on either side. At the same time, older oceanic material can descend into the mantle at deep-sea trenches. This changes the mechanical picture completely. Continents no longer need to bulldoze through a static ocean floor. They travel as parts of plates with the surrounding crust, while new crust is created at spreading centers and other crust is recycled at boundaries.

How could a geologist test that picture? One beautiful test came from magnetism. When molten basalt cools, magnetic minerals can preserve a record of the direction of Earth's magnetic field at that time. The field has reversed many times in geological history. If new oceanic crust forms at a ridge and spreads outward, successive bands of rock should carry a pattern of magnetic orientations arranged in corresponding stripes on either side of the ridge. It is a prediction about the symmetry of the sea floor. Frederick Vine and Drummond Matthews gave a powerful account of this mechanism in 1963, alongside other contributions to paleomagnetism and ocean exploration.

Think of a conveyor belt laying down alternating dark and light fabric. The fabric is not changing color at random. Each strip records the color of the material deposited at that moment, and both sides carry the sequence away from the center. The analogy is imperfect because oceanic plates are neither belts nor fabric, but it captures the evidence: a history encoded in position. Later age measurements confirmed that ocean-floor rock generally becomes older as one moves away from an active ridge. Earthquake distributions and the geometry of deep trenches supplied still more constraints. By the late 1960s a general theory of plate tectonics had emerged.

This later synthesis vindicated Wegener's central contention that the continents had changed relative positions on an immense scale. It did not vindicate every part of his theory. His proposed forces were not the solution, and the modern plates are not simply the continents he had imagined sailing through a softer ocean floor. The distinction is not an insult to his achievement. It is the actual shape of scientific advance: a powerful insight survives because later evidence makes it sharper, while details that fail are replaced.

There is a simple quantitative way to feel how geological time makes motion possible. Suppose two places now separated by three thousand kilometers were together a hundred million years ago. A hundred million years is an enormous interval, but the required average separation rate is only about three centimeters per year. Three centimeters is roughly the width of two fingertips. It is almost nothing to a person watching the ground for an afternoon. Extended over geological time, it reorganizes oceans and continents. This is a modern scale illustration, not a rate Wegener measured directly in 1912.

That scale explains why the debate was hard. People do not watch the Atlantic widen while they eat breakfast. Wegener assembled indirect observations that pointed toward very slow movement over immense intervals. The eventual solution required measurements of places his generation could scarcely observe, plus a better physical account of the solid Earth's long-term deformation. He had been right to regard the map as a history that could change. He had not been able to see the whole machine.

His life is often divided into two disconnected stories: the man who guessed the continents moved, and the man who disappeared in Greenland. That division misses the continuity. Meteorology had taught him that a physical system could not be understood by one isolated observation. Polar work had taught him to trust instruments only when he understood their conditions and to depend on other people in obtaining data. His geological hypothesis required joining observations across disciplines. The book's revisions showed that an explanation must change as evidence accumulates. The last expedition pursued a different question, but it grew from the same commitment to measuring the Earth rather than only describing its visible surface.

Nor did his legacy arrive through one triumphant verdict. The evidence that established plate tectonics came from many people: field geologists, oceanographers, physicists, instrument makers, ship crews, and interpreters of magnetic records. A biographer can recognize Wegener as a major precursor without converting every later discovery into a consequence of his personal brilliance. He did something precise and consequential. He helped turn the positions of the continents from a backdrop into a scientific question with answers that could be tested.

Return once more to Greenland in 1930. On an inland ice sheet, a long journey can move people only a few kilometers in a brutal day. On a geological timescale, an entire continent can travel thousands of kilometers while no single person sees it go. Wegener's science lived between those scales. He measured weather that changed from hour to hour and ice that had built up over centuries; he argued for landmasses that changed position over millions of years. He could not solve every part of the puzzle. He showed that the puzzle was there, and that its scattered pieces belonged to one Earth.

### How the evidence works

**Continental reconstruction is an inference from several partially independent datasets.** A coastline match alone is not reliable: shoreline shape varies with sea level, deposition and erosion. Later fits at the continental shelf edge provide a better geometric starting point, but geological and paleontological correlations supply more stringent tests. A particular fossil across ocean basins is not a unique proof of drift because dispersal and land-bridge mechanisms can also be considered. The cumulative fit of distribution, stratigraphy, structure, and paleoclimate is what matters.

**Paleoclimate:** Permo-Carboniferous glacial deposits and striations across former Gondwana are difficult to reconcile with modern continental positions without additional assumptions. A reconstructed arrangement places many of the deposits at higher ancient latitudes. This does not imply climate depends only on latitude; topography, ocean circulation, atmospheric composition, and orbital forcing matter too. Köppen and Wegener co-authored *Die Klimate der geologischen Vorzeit* (1924); the book was not Wegener's solitary work.

**Modern plate tectonics is not Wegener's exact mechanical model.** The lithosphere comprises plates containing oceanic and/or continental crust plus uppermost mantle. On geological timescales the underlying mantle can deform despite remaining mostly solid. Spreading, subduction, transforms and other plate-boundary processes govern motion. The gravitational and rotational or tidal forces proposed by Wegener could not carry the mechanical burden he gave them. Mantle convection ideas attributed to Arthur Holmes and the 1960s synthesis developed after and alongside multiple other researchers' work.

**A scale illustration:** 3,000 km divided by 100 million years = 0.00003 km/year = 0.03 m/year = 3 cm/year. This is purely illustrative and not a measurement of a specific paleogeographic event or plate-boundary speed.

**Magnetic stripes:** Cooling oceanic basalt acquires thermoremanent magnetization, and seafloor spreading can preserve a roughly symmetric chronology of polarity reversals around ridges. Regional complexities and differential spreading complicate the idealized diagram. Oceanographic mapping, age dating, earthquakes and trenches supply complementary evidence; this was not the confirmation available to Wegener in 1912.

### Attribution, dates, and evidence limits

- **Precursors:** Cartographers and natural philosophers had long noticed matching coasts, and Frank Bursley Taylor discussed large-scale continental movement before Wegener's main publications. Wegener's achievement lay in synthesizing several forms of independent earth-science evidence and arguing publicly for a mobile continent hypothesis. Do not describe him as the first person ever to suggest continents had been joined.
- **Doctoral chronology:** The Deutsches Museum archival biography distinguishes finishing his studies in 1904 from receiving the astronomy doctorate in 1905. Some short summaries combine these dates. The spoken account follows the more specific archival chronology.
- **Marriage:** The Deutsches Museum archival finding aid specifies **16 November 1913** for his marriage to Else Köppen. The Alfred Wegener Institute's timeline places the marriage under a section headed **1914**, apparently grouping the post-expedition period with the start of war. This lecture follows the museum's date.
- **First Greenland expeditions:** The 1906–1908 Danish *Danmark* expedition was led by Ludvig Mylius-Erichsen. The 1912–1913 expedition was associated with Johann Peter Koch and a northern ice-sheet crossing. The 1929 trip was preparatory; the 1930–1931 expedition was a separate organized scientific program. Avoid compressing these into three identical crossings.
- **1930 death:** The precise day and medical cause of Wegener's death are inferred, not directly witnessed. Wegener was found buried in May 1931; Rasmus Villumsen did not return and his body and the missing last diary were not recovered. The wording 'presumed to have died of heart failure' is a retrospective reconstruction.
- **Scientific debate:** It is demonstrably false that every geologist rejected him. Arthur Holmes and Alexander du Toit are important supporters; later historians differ over the significance of the 1926 symposium. Serious mechanical objections existed, and some dismissive institutional reactions went beyond them. A clean 'the genius was right and the critics wrong' narrative conceals both facts.
- **Posthumous confirmation:** Wegener did not formulate the modern framework of moving lithospheric plates, ridge spreading, trench subduction and magnetic stripe tests. The USGS names later contributions from Hess, Dietz, Vine, Matthews, Wilson and others. Maria Tharp's and Bruce Heezen's ocean-floor cartography is later work; it is not a Wegener result.
- **Else Köppen Wegener:** A translator, writer and editor, she supported the documentation and transmission of polar accounts and contributed to the preservation of her husband's work. Avoid treating her simply as an anonymous wife or attributing all climatological work to Alfred alone.
