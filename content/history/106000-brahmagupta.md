# 106000 - Brahmagupta: Debts, zero, and the mathematics of the sky

**Track:** History — People and ideas. **Focus:** seventh-century India; mathematical astronomy at Bhillamala and Ujjain; Sanskrit verse and commentarial learning; negative numbers and zero; equations, cyclic quadrilaterals and indeterminate solutions; disputes with predecessors; and later transmission to the Arabic-speaking mathematical world. **Background:** arithmetic and simple quadratic equations are introduced in speech.

What is a negative number when the quantities being counted are ordinary things? If there are seven apples on a table, you can point to them. But if someone says that the table contains negative seven apples, the sentence makes no literal sense. Now change the situation. A person has seven coins but owes another person nine. The coins are real, the debt is real, and the difference matters even if the person possesses no visible collection of negative coins. That shift, from a pile of objects to a system of relationships, is central to the mathematical world of Brahmagupta, a seventh-century Indian astronomer and mathematician. He treated positive quantities as fortunes, negative quantities as debts, and zero as a quantity governed by rules.

Brahmagupta lived roughly from five hundred ninety-eight to six hundred seventy of the common era, according to conventional biographical dates. Even those dates need some qualification. For an ancient mathematical author we often know the date of a book more securely than a birthday. His father is named as Jishnugupta in the biographical tradition, but there is little reliable evidence for a detailed family story. Brahmagupta's own work places him at Bhillamala, usually identified with modern Bhinmal in Rajasthan, when he composed the Brahmasphutasiddhanta in six hundred twenty-eight. He later became associated with Ujjain, a major center of Indian mathematical astronomy. Exactly how his appointments and travels unfolded is not documented as the career of a modern professor would be.

Ujjain was already a center of mathematical astronomy, not merely a location that happened to produce a famous individual. The earlier author Varahamihira worked in that world, as did thinkers concerned with calendars, planetary positions, eclipses and the computation of time. Astronomical calculation required mathematical procedures that could be repeated, revised and taught. The text did not have to appear in a modern research journal to be a serious technical work. It might appear in Sanskrit verse, organized for memorization and comment, with algorithms compressed into lines that later readers could expand.

The title Brahmasphutasiddhanta is commonly translated as something like the Correctly Established Doctrine of Brahma. It is a work on astronomy, but its mathematical chapters have had a long life of their own. That combination is easy to miss when an account identifies Brahmagupta simply as the man who invented zero. People had used place-value devices and empty positions in counting long before him, in more than one mathematical tradition. His importance lies in providing an influential systematic account of how to calculate with zero and signed quantities, not in a single moment when a previously unknown symbol was suddenly drawn for the first time.

To understand the problem, consider the distinction between a placeholder and a number. In a place-value numeral system, a blank in the tens position distinguishes a number such as one hundred and five from fifteen. That is one role for a zero symbol: it helps locate other digits. But what does it mean to add zero, subtract zero, or multiply by zero? What does a zero amount do to a debt? To state rules for these operations is to treat zero not merely as a gap in written notation but as something that participates in arithmetic.

Brahmagupta supplied rules for operations involving fortunes, debts and zero. A fortune plus a fortune gives a fortune. Two debts together give a larger debt. A debt subtracted from zero becomes a fortune, because removing an obligation increases the balance. A fortune subtracted from zero becomes a debt. The product of two debts is a fortune; the product of a fortune and a debt is a debt. These verbal rules have a family resemblance to the signs that students now write before integers. But they arise in a different instructional setting, where words carry the relation and symbolic notation is not yet a line of plus signs and parentheses familiar from a modern algebra book.

Try a small account. You have eight coins and owe eleven. The balance is a debt of three. If someone cancels five units of that debt, you are not yet rich. You now owe six, and your balance improves from negative three to positive two only if the original eight coins are still available to be counted against the reduced obligation. It is important to keep track of what a number represents rather than to let verbal examples substitute for operations. A model of fortunes and debts is useful because it makes signed subtraction intelligible, but the mathematical rules apply more broadly than any particular market transaction.

The rules for zero are especially instructive because Brahmagupta did not get everything right by modern standards. He understood that adding zero leaves a number unchanged and that multiplying a number by zero produces zero. But his discussion of division involving zero does not give the consistent modern treatment. Today division by zero is not defined in ordinary real-number arithmetic. If one tried to declare that any number divided by zero had a fixed numerical answer, multiplication would break the usual inverse relation on which division relies. For instance, a number times zero is always zero, so it cannot recover a nonzero dividend.

This is not a reason to mock an earlier scholar for failing an examination written fourteen centuries later. It is a reason to appreciate the difficulty of extending a number system coherently. Each new rule must agree with the old rules where both apply, and the new operations must not produce contradictions. Brahmagupta's work documents an important step in that extended investigation. Its errors belong to the history as genuinely as its successes do.

His mathematical program also contained procedures for linear and quadratic equations. Consider the question: what positive number, when squared and then increased by ten times itself, gives thirty-nine? Today we would write an equation with an unknown called x. Brahmagupta's world approached such problems through verbal forms and technical procedures, not the identical algebraic notation of a twenty-first-century page. The answer is three, because nine plus thirty equals thirty-nine. But a mathematician needs more than the ability to recognize one answer; the aim is a method that handles an entire class of problems and explains when a proposed solution is admissible.

One way of understanding the structure is to imagine completing a square. Start with a square of unknown side length. Add rectangular strips whose total area represents ten times that side. Add the four missing corners to turn the figure into a larger square, then recover the original side length. This geometric explanation is familiar from the algebra of al-Khwarizmi, writing later in Baghdad, and serves here as a comparison of different traditions rather than a claim that Brahmagupta used an identical diagram or method for every equation. Both belong to a history in which general calculation grew through procedures organized around particular kinds of unknown quantities.

Some of Brahmagupta's most impressive work concerns equations that ask for whole-number solutions, not arbitrary real numbers. Imagine two square numbers differing by a fixed multiple of another square. A simple example is the equality between nine and eight times one plus one. In modern notation, three squared minus eight times one squared equals one. There are much larger solutions to the same equation. Seventeen squared minus eight times six squared is also one. How could a mathematician generate or recognize such pairs without a long blind search?

Brahmagupta developed a composition principle that lets solutions of certain quadratic forms be combined. Take two expressions of the form one square minus a fixed number times another square. Their product is again an expression of that form. For a modern listener the identity is easy to verify by multiplication, but its power lies in its use. Once a suitable initial relation is known, it can be combined with others to produce new integer solutions. In later European mathematics, equations of this family are often associated with the name Pell. That name is historically misleading as a label for their invention. Indian mathematicians studied the questions centuries before Pell, and Brahmagupta's contribution must not disappear beneath a much later European name.

Take the first two pairs we just mentioned, one and three, and six and seventeen, under the equation y squared minus eight x squared equals one. They are not merely isolated curiosities. Composition relates them. We can square the expression three plus the square root of eight, formally obtain seventeen plus six times the square root of eight, and check that both have the same norm, one, when multiplied by their corresponding expressions with minus signs. This modern explanation makes an algebraic pattern visible; it is not a transcription of Brahmagupta's Sanskrit notation.

Another famous result concerns a quadrilateral, a four-sided figure. A quadrilateral is cyclic when all four vertices lie on one circle. If the four side lengths are known, there is a remarkable expression for its area. Take half their total length. Subtract each side length from this half-perimeter. Multiply the four differences together and take the square root. For a cyclic quadrilateral this gives the area. A square of side two illustrates the rule: its half-perimeter is four, each difference is two, and the square root of two multiplied by itself four times is four, the familiar area.

The condition that all vertices lie on a circle is essential. Four given side lengths can form quadrilaterals with different angles and different areas. If someone applied the expression to every possible quadrilateral, the result would be false. The wording of Brahmagupta's text and the role of that condition have been debated by historians; some interpret the context as specifying the intended cyclic figures even where the condition is not restated in each rule. This is another place where a modern named formula is simpler to print than the historical act of interpreting the original mathematics.

Brahmagupta's work was not confined to equations and areas. Astronomical practice required calculating planetary positions and improving tables. Trigonometric values were useful because periodic motion can be represented by angles and arcs. Brahmagupta used interpolation methods to estimate values between entries in a table. Modern mathematicians can recognize relationships to later numerical interpolation, but we should resist describing an ancient computation as a premature version of a specific modern algorithm merely because algebraic forms resemble one another. The original goal was to make particular celestial calculations possible with the available tables and methods.

He also argued with predecessors. Aryabhata, writing earlier in India, had proposed a striking explanation of apparent celestial movement involving the rotation of the Earth. Brahmagupta opposed that view and preferred a stationary Earth. Astronomers could be brilliant at calculation while holding physical theories that later evidence would reject. That is not a contradiction. A numerical scheme can predict some observations accurately while relying on a mistaken model of what is actually moving. Brahmagupta's disagreements about cosmological assumptions, measurements and approximation show a mathematical culture where authority was contested, rather than a single line of discoveries proceeding by unanimous agreement.

The distinction between mathematical exactness and astronomical approximation is also crucial. The area formula for a cyclic quadrilateral is an exact mathematical relation under its conditions. A stated length of the solar year is an estimate that depends on observations and an astronomical model. Brahmagupta produced multiple estimates across his writings, and not every later revision was an improvement by current measurement. The two activities belong in one book because astronomical practice needed rigorous mathematical procedures, but their kinds of evidence were different.

In six hundred sixty-five, near the end of his long career, Brahmagupta produced another astronomical work, the Khandakhadyaka. It circulated within a tradition of textbooks, commentaries and calculations. We should imagine students learning procedures, specialists testing tables against observed positions, and commentators unpacking terse verses. Those activities are not visible in the way a university departmental archive might be visible, but their traces remain in the texts later scholars copied and discussed.

The later movement of Indian astronomical learning into Arabic scholarship is a consequential part of the story. During the eighth century, texts and methods associated with Indian astronomical traditions reached Baghdad, where scholars working under Abbasid patronage developed new astronomical tables and mathematical literature. It would be misleading to claim that one journey, one book, or one translator carried the entirety of Indian mathematics unchanged into the Islamic world. Sources were selected, compared with Persian and Greek materials, translated into different technical languages and revised. Brahmagupta's writings were part of a larger transregional inheritance, not the sole origin of everything later produced in Baghdad.

For historians this movement also creates an attribution problem. A calculation surviving in a later Arabic text might derive from an Indian procedure, an independent development, or a technique modified during transmission. Similar formulas do not prove a direct chain of borrowing, and the absence of a surviving manuscript does not prove there was no influence. Good historical writing keeps multiple possibilities in view while distinguishing the evidence for each.

Brahmagupta's biography, then, is not a story about a child discovering a marvelous symbol. We know relatively little about his childhood or private relationships. The more illuminating human setting is the world of astronomers who prepared tables, defended methods, disputed predecessors, taught from memorized verses and relied on colleagues to preserve difficult writing. Within that world, Brahmagupta put fortunes, debts and zero into a more systematic arithmetic and developed substantial methods for geometry and indeterminate equations. He was willing to criticize predecessors and he made mistakes of his own.

There is a temptation to tell the history of mathematics as a relay race in which one civilization hands an invention to another and disappears. Brahmagupta's life resists that picture. Indian mathematical astronomy had its own questions and internal arguments. Later Arabic scholarship was creative and selective, and the European traditions that developed afterward were not the only place where mathematical thought continued. The full legacy is not a set of national claims over who invented zero first. It is a history of people trying to make calculation reliable, and of ideas being reinterpreted across languages and centuries.

Return to the debts and fortunes with which we began. The insight is not just that a debt can be represented by a minus sign. It is that a relationship absent from the pile of visible objects can be treated as a quantity, manipulated according to rules, and inserted into a larger system of reasoning. Brahmagupta's mathematical life helped make such reasoning systematic. The rules were not all complete, and the source record is not a modern autobiography. But a great deal of mathematics begins when someone decides that a quantity that cannot be laid on a table can still be studied exactly.

## Historical companion (not narrated)

### Chronology and context

- **c. 598:** conventional birth year. A detailed private biography is not recoverable; father's name is recorded in the tradition.
- **628:** *Brāhmasphuṭasiddhānta* written at Bhillamala (Bhinmal), combining astronomical material with important arithmetic, geometry and algebra.
- **Seventh century:** scholarly association with Ujjain's mathematical-astronomical school; exact institutional sequence uncertain.
- **665:** *Khaṇḍakhādyaka* includes astronomical tables and interpolation methods.
- **Later centuries:** commentaries including Pṛthūdakasvāmin; Indian methods studied and adapted in Islamic astronomical scholarship.

### Mathematical companion

**Signed arithmetic.** Brahmagupta used the terminology of fortunes (positive quantities) and debts (negative quantities). His operations for addition, subtraction and multiplication anticipate much standard integer arithmetic, while the treatment of division by zero does **not** agree with modern arithmetic. Do not call the latter an accepted rule.

**Composition of quadratic forms.** For fixed nonsquare positive \(N\),
\[(a^2-Nb^2)(c^2-Nd^2)=(ac+Nbd)^2-N(ad+bc)^2.\]
Thus solutions to \(y^2-Nx^2=1\) can be composed. For \(N=8\), both \((y,x)=(3,1)\) and \((17,6)\) solve the equation.

**Cyclic quadrilateral.** With side lengths \(a,b,c,d\) and semiperimeter \(s=(a+b+c+d)/2\), \(K=\sqrt{(s-a)(s-b)(s-c)(s-d)}\) holds when all four vertices lie on a circle. The condition is indispensable; interpretations of the original text's implicit geometry are debated.

**Astronomical predictions.** Interpolation of trigonometric tables is a computational approximation, not an exact identity analogous to the quadrilateral formula.
