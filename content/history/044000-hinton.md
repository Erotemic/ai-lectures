# 044000 — Geoffrey Hinton: How can a network discover a representation?

**Track:** History · People and ideas. **Theme:** connectionism, gradient learning, Boltzmann machines, deep representation learning, and scientific responsibility. **Background:** arithmetic, functions, probability, basic derivatives introduced as needed.

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

Suppose you have a collection of photographs, and you want a machine to recognize which pictures contain a bicycle. One approach is to write a list of features: two roughly circular wheels, a frame, handlebars, and perhaps a person above the seat. But a side view, an overhead view, a photograph in shadow, and a bicycle partly hidden behind a wall may look so different that the list becomes brittle. Another approach is to design a system whose internal features are themselves adjusted by experience. You do not write the final description of a wheel. You give the system an opportunity to learn descriptions that help it solve the task.

Geoffrey Hinton made that second approach the organizing question of his career. He was born in London in nineteen forty-seven and grew up in a family with a strong tradition of scholarship. In interviews he has spoken about the pressures that this history created and the difficulty of finding the subject he actually wanted to pursue. He was not a child who proceeded without hesitation along a straight academic path. At Cambridge he changed subjects repeatedly, studying physics, physiology, philosophy, and psychology. In a Nobel Prize conversation many years later, he explained that he found advanced mathematics difficult in his early education, and that psychology, as he encountered it, did not answer his deeper question: how does a mind work?

The important thing about this story is not a claim that mathematics was unnecessary for him. His eventual research became intensely mathematical. The point is that his question came first. He wanted an explanation of learning and perception that would not consist entirely of instructions hand-written by a programmer. Through artificial networks, he began trying to turn this question into a system of equations and experiments.

Hinton completed his doctorate in artificial intelligence at the University of Edinburgh in nineteen seventy-eight. Early work took him through research positions in Britain and the United States. He later worked at Carnegie Mellon and moved to the University of Toronto, associated with the Canadian Institute for Advanced Research. This was not the obvious route to professional safety. At various times, statistical learning, symbolic artificial intelligence, and theories of cognition disagreed sharply about whether networks of simple numerical units could ever explain or reproduce complicated thought. Hinton continued to investigate such networks through periods when they attracted comparatively little enthusiasm.

What is a network doing when it learns? Start with a very small example, much smaller than a modern image recognizer. It receives one number, x, between zero and one. It multiplies x by a weight, w, adds a bias, and passes the result through a smooth squashing function. The output is another number between zero and one, interpreted for the moment as a predicted probability. If the input corresponds to an example whose correct label is one, but the network predicts one half, the prediction is wrong. To improve it, we need to know whether increasing the weight will make the error smaller.

The calculation uses the derivative: the rate at which the error changes when we change the weight a little. For a particular simple choice, let the input be one, the correct answer be one, the bias be zero, and the weight initially be zero. The squashing function gives an output of one half. Define error as one half of the squared difference between prediction and target. Its value is one eighth. If we differentiate this error with respect to the weight, the result is negative one eighth. That negative sign is instructive. A small increase in the weight will lower the error. An optimizer can therefore move the weight upward, taking a step opposite the gradient.

The essential idea is local: quantify how changing a parameter affects the objective and take a step that should improve it. A large network has many parameters, arranged in layers. The difficulty is that a parameter in an early layer affects the final answer only through many intermediate calculations. Backpropagation uses the chain rule of calculus to carry sensitivity backward from the output, reusing intermediate derivatives. If one change affects a hidden feature, and that feature affects the output, multiplying the relevant derivatives tells us how the first change affects the error. Rather than recomputing every influence separately, the algorithm shares the work efficiently across the network.

The difficulty becomes clearer in a two-stage network. The first stage multiplies an input by a weight and transforms it into a hidden activation. The second stage multiplies that activation by another weight and produces a prediction. The final error depends directly on the second weight, but only indirectly on the first. We cannot decide how to change the first weight by looking at the output layer alone. We must ask how the error changes with the prediction, how the prediction changes with the hidden activation, and how that activation changes with the first weight. Multiply those three rates of change. That product is the sensitivity of the error to the first weight.

Now imagine a hundred layers. A naïve method might perturb each parameter separately and rerun the entire network to estimate its effect on the error. For millions of parameters, that is too expensive. Reverse-mode differentiation allows one backward sweep through the computational graph to calculate gradients for all those parameters, with an amount of work on the order of a small multiple of the forward evaluation for many common networks. The optimization objective is still a human-chosen mathematical expression. Backpropagation does not discover what a correct objective should be. It makes changing the internal parameters in response to a chosen objective computationally tractable.

This distinction is crucial when interpreting claims that a neural network understands something. A network may correctly classify a picture by learning a pattern that correlates with the object instead of its defining structure. Suppose every training photograph of a bicycle happens to be taken outdoors. The learned predictor might rely partly on patches of grass or sky. Its loss can be low on the training distribution and high on a new setting where the bicycle is indoors. The derivatives can all be computed perfectly while the learned solution generalizes badly. The power of gradient training and the need for careful evaluation are two separate facts.

Another challenge is that deep chains of derivatives can become extremely small or sometimes unstable, making early-layer learning difficult. Different activation functions, initialization methods, normalization techniques, architectures, and optimizers were developed across many decades by many researchers to make training practical. The history of deep learning is not a story in which one nineteen eighty-six publication instantly made every later network possible. The algorithm was one enabling idea within a larger technological and scientific development.

Hinton, David Rumelhart, and Ronald Williams gave a highly influential account of learning internal representations by error propagation in nineteen eighty-six. The historical distinction matters: they did not invent every mathematical ingredient of reverse-mode differentiation from scratch, and the chronology of backpropagation has earlier contributors. Their work helped show a broad audience why gradient learning through multiple layers could produce useful internal features. The learned features were not required to resemble named objects. A hidden unit might respond to an edge, a texture, or a more abstract combination. What mattered was whether the representation supported later computations.

Consider a network that must tell handwritten threes from handwritten eights. The original data are thousands of pixel values, and the final answer is one bit of classification. A simple handwritten rule such as count the holes in the drawing immediately fails when handwriting is incomplete or the scan is noisy. A hidden layer can learn intermediate distinctions that are valuable even though no one explicitly named them. A second layer can combine those distinctions. The hierarchy matters because many visual patterns are compositions of simpler patterns, but we should not assume that every hidden unit has a tidy human-readable meaning. Representations can be distributed: a concept is encoded by a pattern across many units rather than assigned to one symbolic slot.

Hinton was deeply interested in this difference between a distributed description and a symbolic label. In a classic symbolic representation, a token can stand directly for bicycle, and rules can refer to that token. In a distributed representation, the system might use a thousand numbers whose joint pattern carries information about bicycles, wheels, backgrounds, and related concepts. Such a representation can share structure between examples. If bicycles and motorcycles overlap in their visual features, the same low-level learned responses can help with both. But this flexibility also makes interpretation harder. What has the system learned, and when will its internal pattern stop being reliable?

In the nineteen eighties Hinton also worked with collaborators on Boltzmann machines, networks of stochastic binary units described using an energy function. The connection to statistical physics is not metaphorical. In a Boltzmann distribution, configurations with lower energy receive higher probability, while temperature controls how sharply probability favors them. A learned network assigns energies to joint patterns of visible and hidden units. Training tries to reshape the distribution so observed data patterns become more probable and the model's unconstrained samples become a better match for the data.

Imagine two visible units recording whether a drawing has a left loop and a right loop. We would like the network to regard certain combinations as common and others as rare. If each unit were treated independently, it could not model a preference for the two units appearing together. A hidden unit, connected to both, can create a dependency. With enough hidden structure, the model can capture correlations that a list of separate frequencies misses. The mathematical challenge becomes learning a distribution over many interacting states, where exact sums can be enormously expensive because the number of possible configurations grows exponentially with the number of units.

Boltzmann machines posed both conceptual and computational problems. One must compare patterns seen when the visible data are fixed with patterns sampled from the model itself. These are sometimes called positive and negative phases of learning. If the model already predicts the data well, the competing adjustments balance. If not, the weights change to favor the observed structure. For large networks, obtaining good samples and computing those expectations can be difficult. Hinton's later work with restricted Boltzmann machines, contrastive divergence, and deep belief networks offered practical compromises and new experiments. The point is not that one architecture solved every learning problem. It is that probability distributions over hidden representations could be trained as computational objects.

This work was collaborative throughout. Students, postdoctoral researchers, and colleagues did not merely execute ideas handed down by one person. They produced methods, experiments, objections, and new applications, and the record of neural-network research is a web of overlapping contributions. Hinton's biography should name those relationships when specific results are discussed. In the nineteen eighty-six backpropagation paper, Rumelhart and Williams are essential; in the later image-recognition breakthrough, Alex Krizhevsky and Ilya Sutskever are essential. A portrait organized around one researcher is not a license to erase the people with whom he worked.

The world eventually changed around these arguments. Faster processors, large labeled datasets, and new training methods made multilayer networks much more practical. In twenty twelve, a convolutional neural network built by Krizhevsky, Sutskever, and Hinton achieved a conspicuous improvement on the ImageNet image-classification benchmark. The work was not proof that neural networks could solve vision in general; benchmark results depend on the data and conditions being tested. It did, however, make many researchers and companies reconsider what could be learned from images at scale rather than supplied as engineered rules.

Hinton's Toronto group had also helped transform automatic speech recognition. The same broad principle applied to streams of acoustic measurements: learn representations that support a difficult prediction task, instead of requiring every intermediate unit to correspond to a traditional hand-built category. Deep networks became increasingly capable, but this did not establish that their information processing was identical to that of biological brains. Hinton has long drawn inspiration from neuroscience while also exploring methods that work in machines even when their relationship to biology remains contested.

His personal history makes the persistence intelligible without reducing it to a legend of a solitary genius. In public interviews he describes a strong curiosity about how people remember, recognize, and think. At the same time, his answers can be disarmingly concrete. An interview conducted during Nobel celebrations discussed carpentry as a hands-on hobby. The detail matters because it places an abstract theorist in a world of materials and physical feedback, but it would be excessive to claim that carpentry caused one of his algorithms. Evidence supports an interest, not a psychological origin story.

Hinton worked part-time at Google from twenty thirteen to twenty twenty-three while retaining his academic connection with Toronto. In twenty twenty-three he left Google and spoke more publicly about possible risks from increasingly capable artificial intelligence. Some of his concerns involved misuse, automation, concentration of power, and the possibility that future systems might become difficult to control. Other researchers disagree about timelines, mechanisms, or what policy would best reduce those dangers. These are forecasts and judgments, not established mathematical theorems. Their place in the biography is that a scientist who helped build powerful tools began publicly questioning the consequences of their deployment.

That shift makes the mathematical story more complicated, not less. The chain rule does not tell us whether a trained network's behavior is safe. A low training loss does not imply that a system will behave well in every deployment context. A distribution fitted to historical data may inherit the biases in those data. The same ability to form internal representations that makes learning effective can make it hard to explain what internal features are responsible for a particular decision. An engineering demonstration, a formal proof, and a social prediction are different kinds of evidence; sound scientific judgment keeps them apart.

In twenty twenty-four Hinton shared the Nobel Prize in Physics with John Hopfield for foundational discoveries and inventions enabling machine learning with artificial neural networks. The Royal Swedish Academy of Sciences emphasized the connection to statistical physics. The prize recognized long-established work, not a conclusion about the safety of current or future AI systems. It was also not a prize for a single formula that one could write on a blackboard. The history includes the mathematical structure of energy-based models, the ability to learn internal representations, and decades of computational experimentation by many people.

Return to the photograph of a bicycle. A hand-written description might identify a wheel by a carefully specified geometric test. A learning system instead adjusts parameters so certain intermediate patterns help classify examples it has seen, and perhaps new examples as well. The gradient tells it how a small parameter change would affect the chosen loss; backpropagation makes that gradient efficient to compute; probabilistic models offer another way to learn structure from distributions of examples. None of those statements guarantees complete understanding, human equivalence, robustness, or safety. They describe mechanisms that can be tested and improved.

Hinton's career follows a question from psychology into calculus, statistical mechanics, computer science, large-scale engineering, and finally public arguments about responsibility. Its lesson is not that persistence alone produces a breakthrough. It is that a difficult idea has to survive contact with equations, working machines, skeptical colleagues, students who improve it, and a world changed by its eventual success. The intellectual question remains vivid: how can a collection of simple adjustable parts discover a representation that nobody wrote down in advance? The practical question now stands beside it: what must we know about the resulting system before we trust it with consequential tasks?

## Mathematical and historical companion (display only)

### One-neuron gradient calculation

Let \(\sigma(t)=1/(1+e^{-t})\), \(\widehat y=\sigma(wx+b)\), and \(L=\frac12(\widehat y-y)^2\). Then
\[
\frac{\partial L}{\partial w}=(\widehat y-y)\widehat y(1-\widehat y)x.
\]
At \(w=b=0\), \(x=y=1\), \(\widehat y=1/2\), \(L=1/8\), and \(\partial L/\partial w=-1/8\). For sufficiently small positive learning rate \(\eta\), the update \(w\leftarrow w-\eta\,\partial L/\partial w\) increases \(w\) and decreases the loss.

### Energy-based model

A common binary Boltzmann-machine convention uses states \(s_i\in\{0,1\}\) and symmetric couplings \(w_{ij}\):
\[
E(s)=-\sum_{i<j} w_{ij}s_i s_j-\sum_i b_is_i,
\qquad
p(s)=\frac{e^{-E(s)/T}}{\sum_{s'}e^{-E(s')/T}}.
\]
The visible marginal sums over hidden states. Exact gradients generally involve model expectations, which can be computationally expensive. This companion uses a pedagogical convention, not a verbatim specification of an original experiment.

### Attribution and historical caution

The 1986 *Learning representations by back-propagating errors* paper is by David E. Rumelhart, Geoffrey E. Hinton, and Ronald J. Williams. Reverse differentiation has a broader earlier history; this paper's importance was the demonstration and dissemination of representation learning. The 2012 ImageNet system is jointly credited to Alex Krizhevsky, Ilya Sutskever, and Geoffrey Hinton. The 2024 physics prize was shared by Hinton and John Hopfield. Concerns about AI risks are reported as Hinton's public assessments and are not presented as established predictions.
