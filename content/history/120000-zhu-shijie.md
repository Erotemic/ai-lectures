# 120000 — Zhu Shijie: The Jade Mirror and algebra on a counting board

**Track:** History — People and ideas. **Focus:** Chinese mathematics under the Yuan dynasty, the traveling-teacher record, the *Suanxue qimeng* of 1299, the *Siyuan yujian* of 1303, multi-variable equations, counting-rod notation, earlier algebraic traditions, and the later survival and recovery of his books. **Background:** unknowns, equations, and table arrangements are explained without assuming symbolic algebra.

Imagine a teacher setting out a problem without writing an x, a y, or an equals sign. Instead, the teacher arranges counting rods on a board. Their position carries meaning: one place represents the constant term, another a quantity multiplied by an unknown, another a square of that unknown. A new arrangement can express a second unknown, then a third. By moving and combining coefficients in a systematic way, the teacher transforms a difficult problem into something that can be solved step by step. This is an entry into the mathematics of Zhu Shijie, a scholar of thirteenth- and fourteenth-century China whose books represent a remarkable development in algebra.

Compared with the lives of European mathematicians documented by universities and personal letters, Zhu's private biography is sparse. His dates are conventionally given as around 1260 to 1320, but the precise years are uncertain. One valuable statement survives in a preface written for his work in 1303. The writer Mo Ruo describes Zhu as coming from Yanshan, near the region of the Yuan capital Dadu, and as having traveled widely for more than twenty years while attracting students. This is a historical witness, not a diary in Zhu's hand. It supports the image of a teacher whose mathematics circulated through travel, but it does not tell us which roads he took or what he said in a particular classroom.

The political setting was the Yuan dynasty, established by Mongol rulers over China. After the conquest of the Southern Song in 1279, the country was governed within one imperial system, although the experience of that system was far from uniform. Administrative networks, military power, regional differences, and the status of different populations all influenced the movement of people and knowledge. The phrase united China cannot be used as a substitute for this complex history. Still, the connection of regions that had previously belonged to competing states helped enable some movement of texts, teachers, and technical methods between northern and southern centers.

The inherited tradition was not uniform across centuries. Successive dynasties supported different administrative systems, and methods moved through textbooks, commentaries, and practical teaching. Some techniques were known mainly in certain regions until political and scholarly networks helped spread them. When a later author referred to an old method, this could acknowledge a genuine lineage while leaving us uncertain about the exact chain of teachers. Zhu's books appeared after many generations of work with counting rods and polynomial coefficients. The new contribution lies in their synthesis and extension, not in the existence of algebraic thinking itself.

Chinese mathematics had a long tradition before Zhu. The *Nine Chapters on the Mathematical Art* and Liu Hui's third-century commentary developed methods for measuring land, calculating volumes, solving systems of linear equations, and explaining why numerical procedures worked. In later centuries mathematicians including Jia Xian, Qin Jiushao, Li Ye, and Yang Hui worked with polynomial calculations, numerical algorithms, and increasingly sophisticated uses of counting rods. Zhu inherited and extended that tradition. He did not invent Chinese algebra by himself and did not introduce the first concept of an unknown quantity into it.

What does it mean to do algebra on a board? When a modern student writes three x squared plus five x minus two, the powers and coefficients appear in a line of alphabetic symbols. An earlier system can place the coefficient three in a position assigned to squared terms, five in the position for first powers, and negative two in the constant position. The pattern of positions explains what each number means. One need not use letters if the organization of the workspace preserves the relation. This is a different symbolic technology, not an absence of abstract thought.

The counting rods themselves were physical instruments for calculation. Their orientation and placement could distinguish numerical values and place-value positions. Zero could be represented by an empty place in the layout. Numbers could be operated on in ways comparable to written place-value arithmetic, but the physical board made intermediate stages visible and adjustable. The whole procedure required conventions shared by teacher and student. Without knowing those conventions, a modern reader could mistake an organized polynomial for an unrelated grid of numbers.

The decision to write a teaching book has historical significance beyond its contents. A celebrated solver of advanced problems can still fail to communicate methods to others. A textbook changes the audience by arranging material so that an earlier lesson prepares for a later one. In a world of itinerant teachers and manuscript copying, such an arrangement can preserve practice beyond face-to-face instruction. Zhu's first book became part of the evidence that his expertise could be taught. The surviving preface to his later work's account of increasing numbers of students is consistent with that role, without giving us a census of the people who attended his lessons.

Zhu's first known surviving major book was published in 1299, commonly called *Suanxue qimeng*, or *Introduction to Mathematical Studies*. It was an instructional work. The instructional book places Zhu Shijie in the classroom as well as among advanced algebraists. A mathematician could organize knowledge for students beginning with practical numerical problems, then lead them toward procedures whose underlying principles were far more general. The didactic structure is evidence of what he thought learners needed; it is not proof that we know the precise sequence of lessons given in each city he visited.

In an instructional text, examples may concern business exchanges, geometry, measurement, or the manipulation of numerical quantities. We should not assume every numerical problem was copied directly from a contemporary merchant's daily accounts. Mathematical teaching often uses stylized situations, as textbooks do today. Their historical significance is that the learner moves from an accessible problem to a systematic calculation. Zhu's educational project developed a shared technical language for students who might not already be expert in high-degree algebra.

The four unknowns also force us to notice a difference between naming a quantity and organizing the products of quantities. With two unknowns, a squared term might refer to the square of the first unknown, the square of the second, or their product. With four unknowns, many combinations and higher powers become possible. A spatial notation must give each kind of term a stable place so the operator can recognize what is being added or eliminated. This is a sophisticated data-organization problem. Its solution is visible in the coefficient arrays and geometrical layout preserved in later copies of Zhu's text.

Four years later, in 1303, he published the work for which he is most famous: *Siyuan yujian*, usually rendered *Jade Mirror of the Four Unknowns* or *Precious Mirror of the Four Elements*. The title refers to four kinds of unknown, commonly described through traditional terms such as heaven, earth, human, and matter. These are not four physical elements in the chemical sense. They are ways of naming independent quantities within an algebraic representation. The text demonstrates how equations involving several unknowns can be expressed and manipulated so that solutions become accessible.

Start with a simple modern example. Suppose two quantities add to ten and differ by two. We could call them x and y and write x plus y equals ten and x minus y equals two. Add the equations and the y terms cancel, leaving twice x equals twelve. So x is six and y is four. The key is elimination: combine equations to remove an unknown. Ancient Chinese methods for linear systems already used organized arrays of coefficients and elimination-like operations. Zhu's ambitious work extended polynomial representation and elimination to more complicated relationships involving higher powers and multiple unknowns.

Now make the problem harder. Suppose the total area of two squares is fifty-two, and the side lengths differ by two. Then x squared plus y squared equals fifty-two, while x minus y equals two. From the second relation, x equals y plus two. Substitute into the first: y plus two, squared, plus y squared, equals fifty-two. Expanding gives two y squared plus four y plus four equals fifty-two. Rearranging and dividing by two yields y squared plus two y minus twenty-four equals zero. The positive solution is four, and x is six. A pair of relations in two unknowns has become a single polynomial equation in one unknown. This is a small demonstration of the kind of strategy that becomes much more demanding when more unknowns and higher degrees enter.

Elimination sounds simple when the example contains only two linear relations. In a higher-degree system the intermediate expressions can grow enormously. Substituting one expression into another may create new powers and coefficients, and the final equation can be difficult to solve numerically. An organized counting-board method is valuable partly because it makes those intermediate stages systematic. We should not claim that Zhu possessed every algorithm now taught for polynomial systems. The historical work establishes that he handled substantial classes of multivariable problems through procedures encoded in the arrangement of coefficients.

Zhu's *Jade Mirror* showed ways to represent expressions with up to four unknowns and eliminate them systematically. It also assembled problems concerning sequences, geometrical patterns, and numerical relationships. The work is not a modern textbook that states a general theorem and proves it in twenty-first-century algebraic notation. Many procedures appear through worked problems and coefficient arrangements. Recovering their logic requires reconstructing the operations from the textual instructions and the spatial structure of the tables. A translator who simply replaces every arrangement with symbols can clarify a calculation while hiding how the original mathematicians actually carried it out.

The book includes a triangular arrangement of binomial coefficients. Today a student may call it Pascal's triangle, but it appeared in Chinese mathematical contexts centuries before Pascal. Zhu explicitly presented the diagram as an old method rather than claiming its invention. Related coefficient triangles appear in work associated with Jia Xian and Yang Hui. Credit matters here because a familiar European label can conceal an older history. The triangle belongs to many cultures of mathematical calculation; its arithmetic structure is not a proof that one named figure alone discovered it.

Take the expansion of a sum squared. One plus x, squared, becomes one plus two x plus x squared. The coefficients are one, two, one. Cubing the same expression gives one, three, three, one. The rows follow a simple recurrence: an interior number is the sum of the two numbers directly above it. Such a table is useful for raising binomials to high powers without repeatedly multiplying out the whole expression. The arrangement provides an algorithm, and its usefulness does not depend on naming it after a later European mathematician.

Many high-degree polynomial procedures can be understood through a second idea: divide work into nested stages. In modern notation, a polynomial such as two x cubed plus three x squared plus four x plus five can be evaluated as x times the quantity x times the quantity two x plus three, plus four, and then add five. This rearrangement requires fewer multiplications than computing every power independently. Methods often associated with Horner in modern textbooks have earlier Chinese histories involving Jia Xian, Qin Jiushao, and subsequent authors. Zhu's polynomial practice belongs in that history, but no single formula should be attributed to him merely because he wrote a later influential book.

There is an instructive contrast with the *Liber Abaci* of Leonardo of Pisa, written about a century earlier in the Mediterranean. Leonardo's book taught numerical computation through the Hindu-Arabic numeral system, including extensive commercial applications. Zhu's teaching materials belonged to a Chinese rod-calculation culture and supported different forms of polynomial manipulation. Both authors helped make methods usable beyond a narrow circle of specialists, yet a shared desire to solve practical problems does not imply direct contact. Comparing them illuminates different historical technologies for representing and teaching mathematical operations.

The presence of advanced algebra in a teaching and problem collection challenges a misleading story of mathematical progress in which algebra leaps from one isolated inventor to another across continents. By the time Zhu wrote, Indian, Islamic, Chinese, and European communities already had distinctive methods for numerical and algebraic reasoning. They differed in notation, types of problem, and standards of explanation. Similar solutions need not demonstrate direct borrowing. Establishing transmission requires texts, dates, routes and witnesses. The historical task is to understand what Zhu knew from his own predecessors and how his books later moved, not to force every achievement onto a single global timeline.

A text kept in a Korean library may preserve information lost in Chinese collections, but that does not mean its wording is automatically identical to the original. A copyist may reproduce a mistaken numeral, a printer may reset a diagram, or an editor may add a preface explaining an unfamiliar method. It is especially easy to damage mathematical material where position determines meaning. A blank space can be a zero, a column can stand for a power, and a shifted rod can alter the equation. Modern editions and translations therefore have to interpret physical layout as carefully as written words.

The survival of his works adds another layer. *Suanxue qimeng* fell out of circulation in China for a long period, while copies were preserved in Korea and Japan. It was later recovered and reintroduced to Chinese readers through nineteenth-century scholarship. The *Jade Mirror* also survives through a complicated sequence of manuscript and printed copies, including later prefaces and commentaries. Some versions contain copying errors or editorial changes. The historian therefore works not with a pristine document transported unchanged from 1303, but with witnesses that require comparison.

This is more than a problem for librarians. A missing counting rod, a miscopied coefficient, or a diagram with altered spacing can change the result of an algebraic computation. Reconstructing a method requires knowing whether an error belongs to Zhu, to a later copyist, or to an editor who tried to correct the text. Scholars including Jock Hoe have studied these difficulties in detail, translating mathematical procedures and explaining the array operations without treating modern notation as the only legitimate language of algebra.

The text also reflects an educational style in which examples and algorithms carry much of the explanatory weight. A reader may be given a problem that looks numerical and concrete, then be asked to reproduce a general procedure through a succession of similar calculations. This differs from a modern course that announces an abstract theorem before applying it to a single example. Neither style is inherently more mathematical. Each builds competence by teaching which transformations are permitted and how to recognize success. That is important when judging the level of abstraction in the *Jade Mirror*: absence of modern variables or theorem headings cannot be taken as absence of systematic reasoning.

A striking feature of the historical record is its combination of a teacher's mobility and a book's far longer travels. The preface tells us Zhu spent years attracting students across regions. The books then circulated across generations and across East Asia, eventually returning through scholarly recovery after periods of loss or reduced use. A mathematical idea can survive because it is embedded in a teachable procedure, copied into another manuscript, or explained by a scholar who no longer shares the author's city or century. That kind of continuity is an essential part of mathematical history.

The number of problems in the *Jade Mirror* is itself revealing. It gives several introductory worked examples and then a much larger collection of problems that use the machinery in varying forms. Such a collection invites the student to practice transferring a method from one set of quantities to another. Unlike a single brilliant calculation, a repertoire of hundreds of structured exercises suggests a concern for teaching general competence. The historian still has to be careful: the survival of many problems does not tell us how many students mastered them, where they were taught, or what a particular lesson sounded like.

There is also a limit to what a responsible biography can claim. The available sources do not tell us Zhu's private motivations, his day-to-day relations with students, or how he reacted when a difficult algebraic problem finally yielded. We cannot produce a convincing scene of his final lecture or assign him a precise childhood anecdote. We can, however, identify the mathematical environment in which he worked, the teaching ambition visible in the first book, the technical audacity of the second, and the later readers who made his methods legible to new audiences.

What, then, was the achievement? It was not simply using four unknown letters before other people used four unknown letters. It was demonstrating a sophisticated spatial and computational algebra with several independent quantities, connecting it to systematic elimination and numerical procedures, and preserving it in a form students and later scholars could study. A single worked example in modern equations can hint at the idea, but the historical achievement belongs to the counting-board system itself and to the repertoire of problems Zhu could handle with it.

Return to the counting board. Four families of positions represent different unknowns, and their coefficients can be rearranged until a complex system gives way to a simpler one. For someone trained only to look for x and y on paper, the layout may at first seem strange. That unfamiliarity is a problem for the modern reader, not a limit of the earlier mathematician. Zhu Shijie's life is known only in outline, but the mathematics and the books remain substantial witnesses. They show a traveling teacher working within a rich tradition of Chinese algebra and leaving methods that survived, disappeared from some shelves, and were eventually recovered through the efforts of later scholars.

## Historical companion — not spoken

### Chronology and evidence

- **Thirteenth to early fourteenth centuries:** Conventional approximate life dates, often given c. 1260–1320, not securely documented birth/death certificates.
- **1279:** Yuan victory over the Southern Song unified the political administration of China after prolonged conquest; conditions and regional experiences varied.
- **Before 1303:** Preface by Mo Ruo describes Zhu as a mathematics teacher from Yanshan who had traveled for more than twenty years. Treat as a contemporaneous third-party statement, not a personal diary.
- **1299:** *Suanxue qimeng* (*Introduction to Mathematical Studies*) published.
- **1303:** *Siyuan yujian* (*Jade Mirror of the Four Unknowns*) published.
- **Later centuries:** Textual copying, regional preservation in East Asia, varying editorial practices, and nineteenth-century recovery and study.

### Mathematical companion

- **Two-variable elimination example:** \(x+y=10\), \(x-y=2\) gives \(x=6\), \(y=4\); illustrates elimination without asserting this is a specific example copied from Zhu.
- **Quadratic example:** \(x-y=2\), \(x^2+y^2=52\) gives positive solution \((x,y)=(6,4)\). Also an explanatory reconstruction, not a translation.
- **Polynomial positional notation:** Coefficients arranged by powers and unknowns rather than alphabetic symbolic expressions; placement and rod convention convey the algebraic structure.
- **Triangle of binomial coefficients:** Known through earlier Chinese scholars including Jia Xian and Yang Hui; Zhu did not claim invention.
- **History of nested polynomial evaluation:** Earlier Chinese work precedes Horner and Zhu; avoid claiming Zhu invented Horner's algorithm.
