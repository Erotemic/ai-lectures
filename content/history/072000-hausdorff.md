# 072000 - Felix Hausdorff: A writer, a geometer of spaces, and a life under persecution

**Track:** History - People and ideas. **Focus:** German-Jewish urban culture, astronomy, literature as Paul Mongré, the formation of topology, universities in Leipzig, Greifswald and Bonn, Nazi exclusion, colleagues' efforts, and the final deportation threat. **Background:** historical curiosity; sets and geometry introduced along the way.

The name Hausdorff appears in mathematics wherever people ask what it means for two points to be separated, for a set to have a dimension, or for an infinite family of spaces to behave coherently. A student may encounter a Hausdorff space before learning anything about Felix Hausdorff. Yet he did not spend his entire life doing topology. He wrote philosophy and plays under a pseudonym, moved in literary and musical circles, investigated astronomical optics, taught at several German universities, and lived through the destruction of the political order in which his scholarship had been possible. His work helped give modern mathematics a language of abstract space. His final years reveal what happened when a state denied that a person with such a life and career belonged in its universities or even in his own home.

Hausdorff was born in Breslau in November eighteen sixty-eight, in what was then Prussia and is now Wrocław in Poland. His father Louis worked in the textile trade, and his mother Hedwig came from the Tietz family. The family was Jewish and financially comfortable. During Hausdorff's childhood they moved to Leipzig. There he grew up in an urban culture with strong traditions of music, publishing, education and scholarship. The society around him still carried legal and social distinctions between religious communities, but the material prosperity of his family gave him options not available to all young people.

As a schoolboy Hausdorff was drawn to literature and music as well as mathematics. Accounts of his youth report that he considered a career as a composer, but his family encouraged him to choose another path. It would be a mistake to tell this as the picturesque prelude to an inevitable mathematical destiny. For many years his artistic interests remained central. He did not treat writing as an embarrassing diversion from serious work. He wrote books, poems, reviews and dramatic works, built friendships among artists, and used a separate name for a public literary life.

At university he studied mathematical astronomy and related subjects, spending time in Leipzig and other German cities. His doctorate, awarded in Leipzig in eighteen ninety-one, concerned astronomical refraction, the bending of light through Earth's atmosphere. The subject required both mathematical models and knowledge of real observations. Light reaching a telescope does not arrive along an ideal straight path through empty space. The atmosphere changes its direction, and an astronomer must correct for that distortion to identify a star's position accurately. Hausdorff's earliest professional mathematics therefore belonged to measurement and optics, not to the abstract topology later attached to his name.

He completed the additional qualification needed to lecture independently at Leipzig, his habilitation, in eighteen ninety-five. German university careers then had many stages between receiving a doctorate and holding a secure professorial chair. The title *Privatdozent* often implied teaching rights without the security of a permanent appointment. Hausdorff's family resources made this situation less financially dangerous for him than for someone dependent on a salary, though it did not eliminate the question of professional recognition.

His pseudonym was Paul Mongré. Under that name he published philosophical and literary work in the late eighteen nineties and early nineteen hundreds. One book explored ideas associated with Nietzsche; another challenged metaphysical claims and considered how one should distinguish an empirically accessible world from speculative explanations. He also published poems and a play. His artistic circle included musicians and writers, and the composer Max Reger was among the people he knew. An academic biography that leaves out Paul Mongré misses an intellectual life in which questions about meaning, language, order and artistic form were not separate hobbies stored outside the laboratory.

In eighteen ninety-nine he married Charlotte Goldschmidt. The marriage belonged to a period when he continued to cultivate the literary world while working as a teacher and mathematical scholar. Later family records refer to their daughter, and Charlotte's sister Edith Pappenheim would become central to the family's final years. The women in this story should not appear only at its tragic conclusion: they were members of a household and social network over decades, even where surviving sources give much more detail about the male professor's publications than about their own lives.

His literary work is valuable historically for more than the surprise that a topologist also wrote poetry. The pseudonym allowed a professor working within a specialized scientific institution to enter public arguments about philosophy and culture under a different literary identity. That culture included intense discussion of Nietzsche and the authority of scientific explanations. Hausdorff did not simply reject philosophy in favor of mathematical certainty. He engaged with questions of what can be known and what can reasonably be asserted, while producing work whose aesthetic ambitions were not reducible to a scientific theorem. Later accounts that call the writing a youthful distraction miss how long and seriously he pursued it.

There is, however, no need to convert every artistic choice into a hidden mathematical cause. It would be speculation to say that a certain poem made him invent topology. What is historically supported is that his artistic and mathematical work coexisted for years and that his friendships crossed the boundary between the university and the arts. The separation of disciplines in a later textbook makes that coexistence easy to forget.

Hausdorff's mathematical interests shifted toward ordered sets and then toward set theory and topology. He arrived at these questions just as the meaning of infinity was changing. Georg Cantor and others had revealed that infinity was not a single undifferentiated concept. Infinite sets might have different cardinalities, and one could study orders extending far beyond the familiar sequence of whole numbers. At the same time, analysts were trying to understand continuity and convergence without assuming that every interesting object lived inside a line or an ordinary Euclidean plane. There was a need for clear concepts able to describe many sorts of spaces.

From the early nineteen hundreds Hausdorff studied partially ordered sets, transfinite numbers and questions related to the continuum. A partial order is one in which some objects can be compared by a relation such as inclusion while other pairs remain incomparable. Think of a collection of subsets: one set may be contained in another, yet two overlapping sets need not contain each other. Unlike the ordinary order of numbers, this relation does not force every pair into a single line. The mathematical interest is that hierarchies, constraints and systems of inclusion naturally have this structure. Hausdorff helped turn questions about order into a sustained part of set theory.

The full potential of this approach became visible in his book *Grundzüge der Mengenlehre*, published in nineteen fourteen. The title means roughly *Principles of Set Theory*. The date puts its appearance at the outbreak of the First World War, a moment when the European university world was about to undergo upheaval. The book gathered and reorganized results from several mathematical traditions, including work by Fréchet and other predecessors, and it introduced a systematic treatment of metric and topological spaces. The framework was not manufactured in isolation. It was a synthesis at a moment when mathematicians were learning that definitions could connect fields which had been studied separately.

It may help to understand what was so powerful about a space defined abstractly. On the ordinary line, two points are close if the difference of their coordinates is small. On a surface, closeness may be measured by paths along the surface. But what if our objects are functions? One might call two functions close if their values differ only a little at every point. Or one might care only about an average of their differences. These are distinct notions of closeness and lead to distinct ideas of convergence. A general theory of spaces gives mathematicians a way to state exactly what kind of closeness they are using without continually recreating all the tools of analysis.

A Hausdorff space has a particular separation property: distinct points can be placed in disjoint neighborhoods. Imagine choosing a small region around one point and a separate region around another so that the regions do not overlap. In familiar Euclidean space this is easy. In some abstract topological spaces it is impossible. The property protects certain kinds of uniqueness, such as the uniqueness of limits of convergent sequences or nets in appropriate contexts. The name attached to it is not evidence that Hausdorff alone invented topology. His role was to make the abstract framework clear and powerful enough that generations of mathematicians could use it as a foundation.

Consider why the separation property matters by comparing two mathematical examples. On the ordinary real line, two different numbers can be surrounded by sufficiently small non-overlapping intervals. But imagine a peculiar space whose open sets have been chosen so coarsely that every nonempty open neighborhood includes a particular fixed point. Some different points in such a space cannot be separated by neighborhoods. Many of the habits learned in school geometry are no longer automatic. Abstract topology asks us to state exactly which assumptions we need before we make a familiar inference.

This framework eventually let analysts, geometers and algebraists state results in a shared vocabulary. Hausdorff was building on others' efforts, including Fréchet's work on metric spaces. What he achieved was a particularly successful synthesis. The contribution is easier to appreciate when we see why abstraction was needed: it let mathematicians say not 'I have proved it on this kind of curve' but 'I have proved it in any space with these specified properties.' That is a much more portable kind of statement, provided the hypotheses are actually checked.

He developed this mathematics while moving between German universities. In Leipzig he had an academic position and a rich cultural circle. In nineteen ten he went to Bonn, influenced in part by the encouragement of Eduard Study, another mathematician whose conversations mattered to his career. He then accepted a professorship in Greifswald, where he worked during the period surrounding the publication of *Grundzüge*. When he returned to Bonn in nineteen twenty-one, he was recognized as an important mathematician. The university appointment represented both intellectual standing and a stable place in the academic life of the Weimar Republic.

His research continued beyond the famous book. In nineteen nineteen he published work that helped create a new way of talking about dimension. The ordinary expectation is that a curve has dimension one, a surface dimension two and a solid region dimension three. But fractal sets may have complexity between those simple categories. The middle-third Cantor set, formed by repeatedly removing the middle part of an interval, has dimension between zero and one under the measure-theoretic definition associated with Hausdorff. Earlier work by Carathéodory on measures contributed to the construction; Hausdorff developed its use in defining fractional dimensions. This did not merely give an exotic answer. It extended the vocabulary by which mathematicians describe irregular forms.

A strange-looking result in his work also belongs to the history of paradoxes of decomposition. Certain subsets of a sphere can be rearranged in mathematically surprising ways if one accepts strong set-theoretic choices and disregards ordinary physical constraints on cutting and moving material. Later the Banach–Tarski paradox made this family of ideas famous. Hausdorff's earlier result was an important antecedent. It is not evidence that a real ball of clay can be cut into a few ordinary pieces and enlarged. The sets involved are not physical pieces with well-defined ordinary volume. In this respect his mathematics challenged intuition by being more precise, not by abandoning logical rules.

There was a social dimension to the fame of the nineteen fourteen book. Its reception depended on researchers who could teach the ideas, write textbooks and develop examples. A rigorous definition may look as if it floats free of authors and institutions, but it takes a community to make such definitions habitual. By the nineteen twenties Hausdorff was one of the scholars who had helped mathematicians regard abstract spaces as ordinary objects of study. Even his creative work on fractional dimension reached far beyond one particular constructed set: it gave later analysts a way to investigate irregular boundaries and measures that ordinary geometric intuition could not classify.

He did not himself invent the later scientific uses of fractal geometry or anticipate every application. The path from his papers to modern mathematics involved many researchers and decades of work. Treating his name as the label for a method without acknowledging that development would repeat the mistake of presenting a founder as though he had worked alone.

The nineteen twenties also changed German society. The First World War had ended in military defeat, the empire had fallen, and the Weimar Republic faced political conflict and economic shocks. A professor in Bonn could still teach, publish and participate in international mathematics, but the public world around his university was not stable. Hausdorff was part of a German-Jewish intellectual culture whose contributions had been woven deeply into science and the arts. That belonging would be systematically attacked after the Nazis took power in nineteen thirty-three.

The Nazi state excluded Jewish academics through law, bureaucratic pressure and public humiliation. Hausdorff's position became untenable, and he was forced into retirement in nineteen thirty-five. The phrase 'retirement' can disguise the coercion. It did not mean a scholar freely deciding to finish his career after many years of work. It meant that the university in which he had taught no longer permitted him to hold his place because of racist state policy. Even after losing his chair he continued to work on mathematics, but his opportunities to publish and maintain scholarly contact were sharply constrained.

Some colleagues and former students tried to help. He sought a path out of Germany, including writing to Richard Courant in nineteen thirty-nine about the possibility of research support abroad. Courant, himself an émigré from Nazi Germany, could not secure a position for him. It is tempting to imagine that international fame must have guaranteed a refuge. It did not. Immigration restrictions, age, money, professional appointments, family circumstances and the worsening political situation narrowed the available routes. The failure to emigrate was not a failure of mathematical merit.

Erich Bessel-Hagen, a Bonn mathematician who maintained contact with him, left letters recording concern for the family's situation. Those letters are evidence of the isolated conditions in which Hausdorff lived, and of the limited help one friend might offer under a regime of terror. Local measures became increasingly severe: public marking, forced removals and plans for the confinement of Jewish residents. The family was threatened with internment and eventual deportation. This was not an abstract change in university administration but an assault on their safety and right to live freely.

In January nineteen forty-two Hausdorff, his wife Charlotte, and Charlotte's sister Edith received an order connected with transfer to a camp at Endenich. Facing that threat, they took their own lives. Felix and Charlotte died on the twenty-sixth of January; Edith died shortly afterward. A surviving farewell letter is one of the documents historians use to understand their decision. It conveys fear about what would happen to them and concern for the people they would leave behind. There is no need to turn it into a dramatic scene or claim to know thoughts the letter does not disclose. The persecution is documented; the coercive context should remain central.

It is important to state what this ending means. Hausdorff's death was not the inevitable last chapter of a sensitive philosopher or an abstract-minded mathematician. It occurred because an organized state campaign stripped Jewish people of rights, jobs, possessions and ultimately safety. His mathematics survived because students, libraries, publishers and colleagues preserved and developed it. His family did not receive the protection that an intellectual community, on its own, could not guarantee.

Today the name Hausdorff appears in metric geometry, topology, set theory and the theory of fractal dimensions. The Bonn mathematics institute bearing his name makes a public connection between his scholarship and the university that once employed him and then lived through his exclusion. Remembering him only as a victim, however, would be another kind of erasure. He was also a writer who chose a literary pen name, a student of astronomy, a friend of artists, a husband, a father, an inventive mathematician and a teacher. Those lives belong to the same person. The point of historical biography is to keep them together, and to understand how much was lost when the political world refused to allow them to continue.

## Historical companion (not narrated)

### Chronology and historical cautions
- **1868–1904:** Born in Breslau, raised in Leipzig; astronomical refraction doctorate (1891), habilitation (1895), literary and philosophical work under Paul Mongré; married Charlotte Goldschmidt in 1899.
- **1904–1921:** Set and order theory; Bonn (1910), Greifswald chair (from 1913), *Grundzüge der Mengenlehre* (1914), work on dimension (1919), Bonn professorship (1921).
- **1933–1942:** Systematic Nazi exclusion and compulsory retirement (1935); failed efforts to leave Germany, including a letter to Courant; internment and deportation threats; death of Felix and Charlotte Hausdorff in January 1942 and of Edith Pappenheim shortly afterward.

**Evidence and ethics:** Distinguish a person’s 1935 coerced removal from ordinary retirement. The 1914 topology synthesized work by Fréchet and others; the 1919 dimension built on Carathéodory's measure construction. The Bonn and Greifswald institutional biographies differ slightly on the family's move from Breslau to Leipzig (1870/1871); no precise year is needed in spoken narration. Do not imply the family's deaths followed from a private pathology: the Nazi regime's deportation threats are documented. The wife and sister-in-law's roles should not be hidden in a shorthand reference to Hausdorff alone.

### Mathematical display companion
- A topological space is **Hausdorff** if for any distinct \(x,y\) there are open sets \(U\ni x\), \(V\ni y\) with \(U\cap V=\varnothing\).
- Cantor set Hausdorff dimension: \(\dim_H(C)=\log 2 / \log 3\). This differs from its topological dimension, which is zero.
- Hausdorff's sphere-decomposition work is mathematical set theory under choice-like assumptions; it is **not** physical duplication or a measure-preserving operation on ordinary solids.
