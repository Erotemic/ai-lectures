# 095000 - Claude Shannon: Relays, uncertainty, and the making of information

**Track:** History - People and ideas. **Focus:** Michigan education, MIT's analog computing work, relay logic, Bell Laboratories and wartime secrecy, the 1948 communication theory, Betty Shannon and family, playful mechanisms, and the distinction between information, meaning, and reliable communication. **Background:** ordinary probability and binary decisions are introduced in the narration.

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

A telephone engineer in the nineteen forties dealt with a maddening variety of signals. Voices came through wires, telegraph pulses carried letters, radios used waves, and noise corrupted all of them. One kind of system measured voltage; another counted pulses; a third had switches that clicked into place. It might seem that each required its own theory. Claude Shannon's remarkable move was to ask what they had in common. A message is selected from possibilities, carried through a channel, and recovered by a receiver. Once that simple description is separated from the particular apparatus, questions that had seemed to belong to individual technologies become questions of mathematics. How much information is being sent? How much room is there to send it? How much noise can a system tolerate without losing the message?

The engineer who made these questions precise was also known for juggling, inventing odd machines, and approaching intellectual work as a form of play. Both sides of Claude Shannon matter. The equations did not emerge from a detached philosopher wondering about the nature of meaning. They emerged from someone who loved building things, working with circuits, and seeing what a mathematical expression could tell him about a physical device. His career developed in particular institutions: a Midwestern public university, the Massachusetts Institute of Technology, and Bell Laboratories. Their research equipment, industrial needs, and wartime connections were part of the conditions in which he worked.

Claude Elwood Shannon was born in April nineteen sixteen in Petoskey, Michigan, and grew up in Gaylord. His father worked in business and public office; his mother, Mabel Wolf Shannon, was a teacher and school principal. Young Shannon enjoyed making and repairing devices, as well as mathematics. Accounts of his childhood describe interests in telegraphy and cryptograms. These are not certificates of a predestined career. Plenty of children liked puzzles and wires. But his interests gave him a practical vocabulary for questions about transmission, signals, and secrets. As a schoolboy he worked as a messenger for Western Union, coming into contact with a communications industry that would later be transformed by theory.

At the University of Michigan he studied both mathematics and electrical engineering, receiving bachelor's degrees in nineteen thirty-six. That combination was important. An engineer might understand how relays and switches behaved but lack an algebraic description of a complicated network. A mathematician might know formal logic without having assembled a switching circuit. Shannon learned to move between these languages. At MIT, where he went for graduate work, he joined the laboratory around Vannevar Bush's differential analyzer. This was an analog calculating machine made of shafts, wheels, gears, and linked mechanisms. Its motion modeled mathematical relations so that physical movement could help solve differential equations.

The differential analyzer was not a prototype of a modern laptop. It was large, mechanical, labor-intensive, and subject to the practical constraints of its materials. The machine required people to set up connections and to manage controls, including relays. Watching those circuits suggested a correspondence that made Shannon's name. A relay was open or closed. Logical propositions were true or false. The two states could be represented symbolically, and arrangements of relays could be described by rules of logical combination. Once the circuit was an expression, designing it could become an exercise in algebra rather than trial and error.

The decisive paper was A Symbolic Analysis of Relay and Switching Circuits, published in nineteen thirty-eight. Its thesis history deserves a qualification. The research and an early thesis version belong to the late nineteen thirties, while MIT's archived master's thesis bears a formal issue date of nineteen forty. The published nineteen thirty-eight paper is therefore the clean anchor for the public result. Shannon acknowledged assistance from colleagues including Bush, Frank Hitchcock, and Samuel Caldwell. The contribution was not that he invented Boolean logic. George Boole, Augustus De Morgan, and later logicians had already transformed the study of logical relations. Shannon demonstrated that the algebra could serve as an engineering method for constructing and simplifying switching networks.

Consider a machine that should run if two safety contacts are closed, unless an emergency override is active. The engineer could draw wires and contacts and hope that the arrangement worked. With Boolean expressions, the desired condition could be written first. Contacts in series correspond to a logical AND: both must permit a current path. Alternative parallel paths correspond to an OR: either can suffice. A symbol for an inverted condition corresponds to a NOT. The expression can be manipulated to find an equivalent and potentially simpler implementation. That is a mathematical reason why the connection mattered. It turned a large family of practical circuit problems into a family of symbolic design problems.

Shannon did not stop at the switching thesis. He completed graduate work in mathematics as well, including a doctoral dissertation on an algebraic approach to genetics. His ability to range across subjects was not evidence that every one of his ideas reshaped a field. Some became famous, others remained narrower or exploratory. The common thread was an interest in finding useful abstract structures in complicated mechanisms and processes. He would later apply that habit to communications, games, cryptography, and experimental machines.

Bell Telephone Laboratories became his main research home in nineteen forty-one, after earlier summer work there. The corporate laboratory served a vast communications system. Its scientists and engineers were asked to improve signals and switching, but the organization also made space for theoretical investigation. The Second World War intensified the demand for reliable communications and secrecy. Shannon worked on classified questions involving secure speech systems and on the mathematics of fire-control systems. His wartime work should not be retroactively merged into one secret invention. Different people and teams built equipment, developed methods, and addressed the particular requirements of military communication. The laboratory and its collaborators made it possible for Shannon to explore problems whose solutions would eventually have much wider uses.

After the war, results on secrecy were published in his nineteen forty-nine paper Communication Theory of Secrecy Systems. It distinguished mathematical security from merely making a cipher difficult to guess. Some systems can be secure even against an adversary who has unlimited computing power, but such security demands strong conditions on the key and its use. A one-time pad has perfect secrecy under an ideal model when the key is truly random, at least as long as the message, used only once, and kept secret. Those requirements are severe. This is not a claim that Shannon alone invented the one-time pad or practical cryptography. It is part of how he helped supply a mathematical framework for thinking about security guarantees.

His best-known paper arrived in nineteen forty-eight in the Bell System Technical Journal. Its title was A Mathematical Theory of Communication. The paper appeared in two installments and rested on earlier research by communications theorists including Harry Nyquist and Ralph Hartley. Those predecessors had already investigated limits related to signaling and the logarithmic measure of possible messages. Shannon extended the work to probabilistic sources, noise, and the question of reliable transmission. He drew a line between the technical task of reproducing a selected message and the semantic question of what the message meant. This was a methodological choice, not a denial that language has meaning.

Imagine two boxes. Each produces a long sequence of binary symbols. The first is a fair coin: zero or one with equal probability. The second produces zero ninety-nine times in a hundred, with an occasional one. Both use the same alphabet and both send one symbol at a time. Yet their uncertainty is different. If you know what the second box usually does, its next symbol is less surprising. Shannon's entropy measures average uncertainty in a probabilistic source. A fair binary choice carries one bit of entropy. A heavily biased binary choice carries less than one bit. This is not a claim about how important, beautiful, or meaningful a message is to a reader. It is a statement about the statistical information needed to distinguish possible outcomes.

The distinction leads to compression. A text file contains repeated patterns. If some letters or words occur more often than others, there may be more economical ways to encode the same information, especially over long sequences. The entropy places a limiting scale on average lossless compression under stated probabilistic assumptions. It does not tell a programmer that every particular short message can be shortened. If all possible messages of a given length could be replaced by shorter unique messages, there would not be enough shorter strings available to represent them. Compression saves space by exploiting structure and accepting that some possible inputs may not compress.

Noise asks a different question. Suppose a communications line flips a transmitted bit occasionally. A naive system could repeat everything many times, becoming extremely slow. Is there a rate at which information can be sent and decoded reliably even though the channel is imperfect? Shannon defined channel capacity and proved that, for suitable models, rates below capacity can be approached by codes with error probability made arbitrarily small as coding blocks grow. Rates above capacity cannot achieve the corresponding reliable communication guarantee. The phrase arbitrarily small is essential. The theorem does not promise zero errors for a finite machine, and it does not hand an engineer one specific practical code. It says there is a mathematical boundary between possible and impossible long-run performance.

The elegance of this result can obscure how radical it was for engineering. Instead of treating noise purely as a defect to be removed by building more perfect components, it showed that intelligent encoding could overcome much of the damage that remained. A real communications link uses physical hardware, energy, bandwidth, delay, and decoding effort. Shannon's theorems do not eliminate those costs. They tell designers what compromises are possible in principle. Subsequent researchers spent decades finding useful codes, algorithms, and systems that moved toward the theoretical limits.

Another mathematician at Bell Labs, Richard Hamming, pursued a related but distinct problem: how to build codes whose structure lets a receiver locate and correct some errors. Shannon's theory concerned limits and existence; Hamming supplied influential explicit constructions. Their work belongs to a shared industrial environment but not to a single discovery attributable to one person. John Tukey suggested the word bit, a shortened form of binary digit, which Shannon used and acknowledged in the nineteen forty-eight paper. The new mathematical language quickly became part of engineering speech. Words now so ordinary that they appear in consumer advertisements were being negotiated among particular people working on concrete problems.

Shannon's life outside formal publication was rich in experimental curiosity. He rode a unicycle, sometimes while juggling, and built devices that seemed designed to amuse as much as instruct. His electromechanical mouse, Theseus, explored a maze and used a simple memory of previous attempts to find its way. A machine with a mechanical strategy for learning a path was not artificial intelligence in the contemporary sense of a large learned model. It nevertheless made a pointed question tangible: how can a mechanism behave adaptively without a human specifying every turn? He also explored chess-playing machinery and other contraptions. The playful work deserves a place in his biography because it reveals how he approached the border between reasoning and mechanism.

At Bell Labs he met Mary Elizabeth Moore, known as Betty, a mathematician and numerical analyst. They married in nineteen forty-nine and raised a family. Accounts of the household describe room for inventions, games, and experiments. Treat Betty as a person with technical training, not merely as the spouse watching a famous inventor play. The presence of skilled colleagues at work and at home makes the image of a solitary scientist especially inadequate here. Shannon could make an idea seem disarmingly simple in part because he had worked among people who understood the actual intricacies of computation and communication.

From the mid-nineteen fifties he increasingly returned to MIT, becoming a professor there after his long Bell Labs career. He published less as time went on. This has sometimes been presented as a mystery, as if a researcher must keep producing major papers on a predictable schedule. But public output is not a measure of the full content of a person's life. He had already opened problems that many others could spend entire careers pursuing. He worked on inventions, followed interests, and participated in a wider community. In later years he lived with Alzheimer's disease; he died in February two thousand one, aged eighty-four.

It is tempting to say that Claude Shannon invented the information age. That sentence is useful only if handled cautiously. He did not build the first telegraph, first telephone, first computer, or first workable error-correcting system. Nor did he erase the contributions of Nyquist, Hartley, Boole, Hamming, Tukey, and the engineers who turned mathematical limits into functioning networks. What Shannon did was give modern communications a common quantitative framework, and show that binary switches could be designed with logical algebra. In those achievements he connected two large worlds: the switching machinery that performs decisions, and the probabilistic theory that explains what can be conveyed through imperfect channels.

Think again of the noisy telephone line. The practical engineer still needs wires, components, power, and human judgment. But the engineer can now ask how much uncertainty the source produces, how much capacity the channel has, and how much redundancy a code must add to make recovery reliable. These questions are abstract enough to apply to radio, fiber optics, storage, and satellite links, yet precise enough to guide actual designs. Shannon's life shows what becomes possible when mathematical curiosity is supported by laboratories that can build things, and when the person asking the question is willing to see the same structure beneath very different machines.

## Historical companion (not narrated)

### Chronology

- **1916–1936:** born in Petoskey, grew up in Gaylord; studied mathematics and electrical engineering at the University of Michigan.
- **1936–1940:** worked at MIT on Vannevar Bush's differential analyzer; switching-circuit thesis research in the late 1930s; 1938 paper; formal MIT degrees in 1940; additional dissertation on genetics.
- **1941–1949:** Bell Laboratories and wartime secure communications; 1948 *A Mathematical Theory of Communication*; 1949 publication of classified secrecy theory; marriage to Mary Elizabeth Moore.
- **1950s–2001:** maze mouse, chess and other experimental machines; MIT professorship, family, later illness and death in 2001.

### Mathematical companion

For a binary source with probability \(p\) of sending one, Shannon entropy in bits is

\[H(p)=-p\log_2 p-(1-p)\log_2(1-p),\]

with the usual limiting convention \(0\log 0=0\). It is one bit at \(p=1/2\) and less otherwise. This is a statement about a **probability distribution** and a chosen logarithmic unit, not the cultural or semantic value of a message. Channel capacity is a maximum information rate for a specified channel model. The coding theorem characterizes achievable arbitrarily low error probability under its assumptions; it neither supplies practical finite-block codes automatically nor guarantees literal zero errors.

For ideal relay circuits, two contacts in **series** give a logical AND; suitable **parallel** paths give OR. Later Boolean gate algebra is a faithful mathematical descendant of the methods used in Shannon's 1938 paper, but physical circuits also require timing and electrical engineering beyond pure truth tables.

**Historical cautions:** The 1938 published switching paper and the MIT repository's 1940 thesis issue date refer to a complicated publication sequence, not contradictory inventions. Shannon credited earlier transmission research and Tukey for the word *bit*. Information entropy measures uncertainty under a model, not meaning or importance. The coding theorem is an existence result with asymptotic assumptions, not a guarantee of error-free finite communication. Betty Shannon was a numerical analyst, and Hamming's constructions were distinct contributions.
