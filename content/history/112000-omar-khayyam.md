# 112000 — Omar Khayyam: Cubic equations, changing stars, and the uncertainty of a poem

**Track:** History — People and ideas. **Focus:** Nishapur, Seljuk patronage, conic constructions for cubic equations, Euclid's parallels and ratios, calendar reform, philosophy, Persian poetry and the disputed manuscript history of the *Rubaiyat*. **Background:** the argument uses elementary equations, intersecting curves, and the difference between a solar year and a calendar year.

A mathematician draws a circle and a parabola. Neither curve is particularly mysterious. Their intersection, however, answers a question that had resisted the familiar tools of arithmetic: which length solves a certain cubic equation? The mathematician is Omar Khayyam. Most people who know his name know it through short poems about wine, fate, and the brevity of life. The man who wrote mathematical treatises and worked on astronomical calendars is less visible. The relationship between those two figures—the geometer and the poet—is a historical problem of its own.

Omar Khayyam was born around 1048 in Nishapur, in the region of Khorasan in northeastern Iran. His name is often linked with tent making, perhaps the trade of his father or an ancestor. The city was part of a wide Persianate and Islamic intellectual world, with traditions of theology, philosophy, mathematics, medicine, and astronomy circulating through teachers, libraries and courts. Nishapur was also affected by shifting political power. Education, religious authority and patronage were not insulated from those changes.

The surviving record does not give us a diary of Khayyam's childhood or a dependable list of his private friendships. Several popular biographies make his life into a story of three schoolboys—Khayyam, the future vizier Nizam al-Mulk, and Hasan-i Sabbah—who promise to support one another. The tale is memorable and has entered popular culture, but its chronology and documentary foundations are inadequate. We can use it as an example of how literary tradition creates a neat origin story, not as an established scene from his youth.

A more secure way to begin is with the mathematical and philosophical education he received or pursued. He engaged deeply with Greek geometry, especially Euclid, and with Arabic-language algebra developed by al-Khwarizmi, Abu Kamil, and subsequent mathematicians. He knew the philosophical tradition associated with Ibn Sina, or Avicenna, but direct personal study with Ibn Sina is chronologically impossible. Intellectual lineage does not require that two scholars meet in a classroom. Books, commentaries and teachers transmit arguments across generations.

The algebraic problem for which he is best remembered begins with a distinction we can still feel. A linear equation asks for an unknown number in a relationship involving its first power: three times a number is twelve. A quadratic adds a square: a number multiplied by itself is five. A cubic includes the third power, as in the volume of a cube. Modern students learn formulas for the first two categories and may hear of a general formula for the third. Khayyam did not have our symbolic language or the later Renaissance algebraic methods for solving every cubic by radicals.

Medieval algebra was often expressed in words and categorized by which positive terms appeared. A typical problem might combine a cube, squares, roots and numbers. The systematic classification was important because the sign conventions and the absence of a general acceptance of negative roots affected what counted as a solution. You cannot simply take a modern cubic with coefficients of either sign and assume every historical classification matches it. Khayyam worked within a geometric account of positive magnitudes and investigated different types separately.

Why do intersecting curves help? Imagine a parabola, the smooth curve traced when one coordinate is proportional to the square of another. Imagine a circle, whose points lie at a fixed distance from its center. An intersection point satisfies two different geometric conditions at once. If those conditions are chosen cleverly, the common coordinates encode the unknown length of a cubic problem. In the language of modern algebra, eliminating one variable from the circle's equation and the parabola's equation may leave an equation of the third degree.

That is not a magic picture that solves any cubic in exactly the same way. It is a family of constructions whose details depend on the equation type.

Here is a more precise modern demonstration, offered only to illuminate the method. Draw a parabola described by y equals x squared. Draw a hyperbola described by y times the quantity x plus one equals two. At any common point, the parabola tells us that y is x squared. Substituting that into the hyperbola gives x cubed plus x squared equals two. The two curves meet at a point with x equal to one and y equal to one, so one is a positive solution of that cubic. The historical conic constructions used different cases and their own geometrical arguments; this is not a claim that Khayyam drew these particular equations. It does show why a geometer might regard the intersection of two familiar curves as a systematic instrument for solving a higher-degree problem.

A modern algebraist asks for all roots, perhaps including negative or complex ones. A medieval geometer often asked for a positive length satisfying a specified relation between cubes, squares and linear magnitudes. The distinction changes the definition of success. A construction can correctly produce the required positive magnitude even if it is not written as a formula for every algebraic root. Historical evaluation should not punish a valid answer for failing to address a question that was not the author's problem.

Khayyam classified and solved classes of cubic equations using intersections of conic sections. He made deliberate use of results from Euclidean geometry and earlier algebraic writers. The work was a substantial advance in the systematic geometric study of cubic problems, while not equivalent to the modern formula taught in higher algebra.

We can make the idea concrete with an invented illustrative equation: a cube plus two times its edge equals three. If the edge has length one, the cube contributes one and the two edges contribute two, so the total is three. The numerical answer is easy to check. The historical challenge was not merely guessing a small root. It was to construct unknown lengths for general classes of relations and explain why the construction corresponded to the original problem. What appears now as plugging a number into an equation could then require a geometric account of different kinds of magnitude.

This concern with the legitimacy of algebra also appears in Khayyam's other writing. He investigated the difficulties of Euclid's theory of parallels and ratios. The fifth postulate, in one familiar form, concerns whether two lines extended indefinitely must eventually meet when a transversal creates certain interior angles. Mathematicians debated whether the rule could be derived from assumptions they regarded as more evident. Khayyam worked within the Euclidean tradition, analyzing alternatives and making arguments through special quadrilaterals.

Later historians have connected one of those quadrilateral constructions with developments in non-Euclidean geometry. That connection can be illuminating, but it must not reverse the direction of history. Khayyam was not knowingly proposing hyperbolic geometry. In his context the effort was to clarify, justify or replace assumptions supporting Euclidean conclusions.

Imagine two sides of a quadrilateral standing perpendicular to a base and having equal length. Join their upper endpoints. The figure seems symmetric, and one wants to know whether the angles at the top must be right angles. In the geometry that later became standard, a statement about parallel lines provides the answer. If that assumption is held in doubt, the construction becomes a test of what other propositions would be needed. Khayyam's discussion of such figures lies in the history of foundational reasoning. It is valuable not because it predicted a nineteenth-century revolution, but because it exposes the dependence of apparently obvious conclusions on assumptions that can be examined. He did not share the nineteenth-century understanding that a logically consistent geometry might deny the parallel postulate. The same construction can have different intellectual meanings in different centuries.

The theory of ratios posed related difficulties. Geometers wanted to compare quantities that might not share a convenient common unit. How can one say two ratios are equal when one involves lengths that are incommensurable, such as the diagonal and side of a square? Euclid's treatment of proportion was powerful but subtle. Khayyam's mathematical writing addressed what justified equalities and comparisons between magnitudes. This is another aspect of his work obscured when he appears only in lists of algebraic solutions.

During the Seljuk era, scholarship required patrons who could command resources on the scale of astronomical observation. In the 1070s Khayyam went to Isfahan, associated with the court of the sultan Malik Shah and the powerful vizier Nizam al-Mulk. He participated in an astronomical project with an observatory and a revised solar calendar, often called the Jalali calendar or, in connection with the patron, the Maliki calendar. He was part of a scholarly enterprise rather than a solitary astronomer adjusting dates by personal decree.

Why reform a calendar? The Earth returns to approximately the same seasonal position relative to the Sun after a tropical year, which is a little longer than 365 days. If a calendar has exactly 365 days every year, its seasons drift. Every four years one might add a day, but a rigid four-year correction is itself slightly too generous. A precise solar calendar requires rules that keep the beginning of the year near a chosen astronomical event, such as the vernal equinox.

The project associated with Khayyam sought to align the calendar with actual solar motion. A calendar is also a compromise between observation and administration. A government needs dates printed or announced in advance so that contracts and obligations can be planned. An astronomer may prefer to determine the equinox by observing where the Sun lies each year. A rule with a repeating leap-day pattern is convenient but approximates a physical cycle that does not fit an exact whole number of days. To judge a reform fairly we must ask what was being regulated. Was it the beginning of a season, the numbering of a year, a method of prediction for officials, or a mathematical model of the Sun? Khayyam's work arose in a setting where these questions overlapped.

Modern summaries sometimes attribute to him a fixed leap-year pattern or a precise number of seconds by which the calendar surpassed all others. The surviving history is more complicated. Observational determination of the year, the administrative form of the reform, and later calendrical cycles should be distinguished. It is safe to say the project took solar precision seriously and that Khayyam participated prominently. It is not safe to claim every later algorithm for the Iranian calendar originated in his own hand.

Astronomy at court had a political dimension. Calendars help regulate taxation, festivals, appointments and the public presentation of royal order. A sultan could sponsor a refined astronomical year because precision also supported legitimacy. Scholars could pursue genuine research under that patronage while depending on its continuation. The year 1092 brought the deaths of Malik Shah and Nizam al-Mulk and serious instability in the Seljuk political world. Khayyam's position and the institutions supporting him were affected. A mathematician's access to instruments and books could be altered by events far removed from a geometry proof.

His later career included travel and study in other cities, and he eventually returned to Nishapur. Sources vary on exact dates and reasons for movement. Some accounts emphasize opposition from religious authorities, others changing patronage or ill health. His philosophical writings certainly took up matters capable of provoking strong disagreement: the relationship between God and the natural world, necessity and contingency, knowledge and causation, and the problem of human freedom. But these treatises should not be flattened into a simple slogan about an atheist poet hiding from clerics.

There is a tension in his philosophical material. In the tradition of Ibn Sina, he engages metaphysical accounts of existence, essence, intellect and divine causation. Poems attributed to him, on the other hand, often sound skeptical, impatient with dogmatic answers, and drawn toward the present moment. A modern reader naturally wants a single personality that explains everything: a rebellious poet who secretly held a consistent philosophical system. The evidence does not oblige us to that tidy portrait. Genre matters.

Suppose a philosopher writes an argument that a cause is necessary, then a poet writes a verse asking why an unpredictable world contains suffering. It may be tempting to choose one sentence as the author's true belief and dismiss the other as a disguise. But the poem's speaker can be a dramatic voice; the treatise's argument may explore a technical position without revealing the writer's whole emotional life. The documentary problem is especially severe when the poem's attribution is uncertain. A responsible portrait should be capable of saying both that Khayyam was a serious participant in philosophical debate and that we cannot reconstruct a private confession from every stanza later ascribed to him. A philosophical treatise argues under explicit assumptions; a quatrain can speak through irony, uncertainty, or a conventional poetic voice.

The poems are also a problem of attribution. Many quatrains associated with Khayyam circulated in Persian manuscripts produced long after his death. Different collections contain different poems, and the total attached to his name expanded over time. Scholars disagree about which verses, if any particular set, can be established as his with strong confidence. One cannot make a reliable psychological biography by taking every poem printed under his name as a literal statement made on a particular day.

This becomes especially important in the English-language world. In 1859 Edward FitzGerald published a version of the *Rubaiyat* that became extraordinarily influential. It was not a line-by-line documentary translation of a securely established authorial collection. FitzGerald selected, reshaped and adapted material into an English poetic sequence with its own artistic and Victorian context. Readers have loved it for good reasons. But the voice of that poem is partly FitzGerald's creation, and it cannot automatically serve as evidence of Khayyam's private beliefs in eleventh-century Nishapur.

The result is one of the stranger afterlives in the history of mathematics. A writer who developed conic solutions of cubic equations and worked on the solar year became famous far outside specialist circles for the melancholy force of short poems. Some readers have met his mathematical work only after meeting the poems; others have treated the poems as merely a colorful anecdote attached to a mathematician. Both responses miss something. He moved through a world in which geometry, philosophy, literature and astronomy were connected forms of learned activity.

The quality of his mathematical reasoning does not depend on whether any particular quatrain is authentic. Nor would an authentic skeptical poem discredit his scientific work. What the combined record tells us is that the labels we now use—poet, mathematician, philosopher, astronomer—divide activities that one medieval scholar could pursue together. A question about proportion might lead to logical foundations; a question about the year might lead to celestial observation; a question about fate could be explored through philosophical argument or compressed into four poetic lines.

Khayyam's work did not end the history of cubic equations. Later mathematicians developed different algebraic methods. In sixteenth-century Italy, new solution formulas opened another chapter; modern algebra eventually described roots through polynomials, complex numbers and field theory. His contributions belong to the earlier history of geometric algebra, and his arguments should be read on their own terms.

The same is true of his work on numerical roots. He discussed methods for extracting roots of different orders and the arithmetic foundations that might justify them. Some historians see in the reported procedures knowledge closely related to binomial coefficients. But the treatise explicitly devoted to those methods has not survived. Claims about the full scope of what he proved should therefore be qualified. We possess enough to recognize a mathematician whose interests were wide and systematic, not enough to reconstruct every missing proof or rank him by a retrospective list of modern theorems.

He died in or near Nishapur, probably in the early decades of the twelfth century; the exact year is disputed between sources. A later literary account describes his burial and words of prophecy about his grave, but it should not be recited as if a contemporary observer had made a reliable recording. His life survives through scholarly works, later biographies, manuscript traditions, and the immense cultural afterlife of his poems. Each source tells something different and has its own limitations.

Return to the circle and parabola. Their intersection has no opinion about the political future of Isfahan or the authenticity of a poem. It simply satisfies two conditions at once. Khayyam's mathematical achievement was to see how that fact could answer problems that arithmetic alone had not yet organized into a general method. His historical achievement is harder to condense. He belonged to a cosmopolitan world of mathematics and philosophy, relied on unstable political patronage, took part in the disciplined correction of a calendar, and left writings later generations read in several incompatible ways. To know him requires the same discipline that good geometry requires: distinguish what has actually been constructed from what one merely wishes the picture would prove.

## Historical companion — not spoken

### Evidentiary chronology

- **c. 1048:** Birth at Nishapur in Khorasan; precise childhood details and the story of three famous school friends are not firmly established.
- **Before the 1070s:** Advanced study of Greek geometry, Arabic algebra and philosophy; treatises on cubic equations and mathematical foundations.
- **c. 1074–1092:** Isfahan period, patronage under Malik Shah and Nizam al-Mulk; astronomical tables and calendar reform.
- **1092 onward:** Seljuk political upheaval changed the circumstances of astronomical and scholarly work.
- **Early twelfth century:** Further work and eventual return to Nishapur; date of death conventionally reported around 1131 but other scholarship places it earlier.
- **1859:** Edward FitzGerald's English *Rubaiyat* begins a new literary afterlife that cannot be conflated with an established authorial Persian text.

### Mathematical companion

- **Modern illustration, not Khayyam's own example:** \(x^3+2x=3\) has positive root \(x=1\). A conic intersection can encode a cubic condition when two equations are eliminated; a particular construction depends on the historical equation class.
- **Geometry and roots:** Khayyam used pairs of conic sections for classes of cubic equations in positive magnitudes; this is not the later general solution in radicals.
- **Parallel reasoning:** The so-called Saccheri-type quadrilateral has equal perpendicular sides on a base. Khayyam used such a figure within attempts to establish Euclidean geometry; he did not formulate modern hyperbolic geometry.
- **Calendar drift:** \(365.2422-365\approx0.2422\) day per solar year. A leap-day rule approximates the accumulation; actual equinox-based reckoning is more nuanced than the attribution of one fixed cycle.

**Historical cautions:** The “three school friends” compact is not established history. Direct study under Ibn Sina is chronologically impossible. The *Rubaiyat* manuscript corpus is heavily layered, and FitzGerald's version is an adaptation. Do not turn Khayyam into an aware precursor of non-Euclidean geometry or attribute a later Iranian calendar rule uniquely to him.
