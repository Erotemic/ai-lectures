# 109000 — Aryabhata: A rotating Earth and a book of numbered verses

**Track:** History — People and ideas. **Focus:** Indian mathematical astronomy around 500 CE; the *Aryabhatiya*, its ingenious numerical language, trigonometry, planetary models, eclipses, institutional setting, criticism and transmission. **Background:** a circle, a shadow, and simple arithmetic are sufficient; each mathematical idea is developed in the narration.

You are standing on the bank of a river. A boat passes, and a passenger aboard it watches the trees slip backward along the shore. Of course the trees are not moving. The passenger is. Now enlarge the argument until the observer stands on Earth and watches the stars travel across the night sky. Could the stars be like those trees? Could the observer be moving while the apparent background moves the other way?

This is one of the questions raised by Aryabhata, a mathematician and astronomer working in India near the beginning of the sixth century. He offered a rotating Earth within a mathematical astronomy that otherwise placed Earth at the center of planetary motions. He did not announce a modern heliocentric solar system. He did something arguably more interesting for a historical portrait: he recognized that motion seen from a moving observer needs interpretation. A seemingly obvious sight could have more than one physical explanation.

The secure personal facts about Aryabhata fit into remarkably little space. From his own text we learn that he was twenty-three when his treatise was composed in the year we call 499 of the common era. That points to a birth around 476. He was associated with Kusumapura, commonly connected with Pataliputra in the Ganges region, though scholars debate exactly how to interpret his geographical indications. Accounts of his birthplace differ; we cannot reconstruct the route of his childhood, identify his parents, or reliably describe his household. Later traditions call him an eminent teacher, but the precise institution where he taught is not firmly established. A proposed association with Nalanda should be understood as a possibility, not an appointment recorded in a surviving employment register.

Those uncertainties are not an obstacle to telling his intellectual life. His work itself is a record of choices: what problems were important, which old methods he trusted, what he wanted students to memorize, and which explanations he was willing to defend against accepted views. We can follow those choices through a text called the *Aryabhatiya*.

To understand why a book like this existed, forget for a moment the idea that astronomy was simply a hobby of watching stars. Mathematical astronomy served calendars, ritual timing, civil reckoning, and the prediction of celestial events. It required arithmetic that could survive the handoff between generations of teachers. Astronomers needed ways to compute the positions of the Sun and Moon, understand planetary irregularities, and distinguish events that could be seen from one location and not another. The mathematical work could be exact in its procedures even when the observational parameters were approximate.

The *Aryabhatiya* is a remarkably compressed composition of about 121 Sanskrit verses grouped into four sections. A modern listener might expect a textbook to unfold a lesson in full sentences, with a diagram, a worked exercise, and an answer key. Aryabhata's text is closer to a storehouse of rules that demands explanation. A verse might encode numerical constants, state an algorithm, or prescribe steps for a calculation in almost no space. To learn it was to enter a tradition of oral recitation, commentary, and expert unpacking. Brevity was not the same as simplicity.

One reason verse could serve as a technical archive is that oral learning and written explanation were not opposites. A student might memorize a compact rule, then hear a teacher unfold its meaning through numerical examples. Later a commentator could preserve that explanation in writing, or offer a different one. This creates an interpretive challenge for historians: an original two-line prescription may say less than a subsequent multi-page exposition. It is tempting to credit the earliest named author with every proof in the later tradition, but that collapses the labor of centuries. Aryabhata's intellectual choices include both what he specified and what he left to the competence of his readers.

The arrangement also reveals the scale of his project. Mathematics appears alongside astronomical constants, methods for reckoning time, and models of celestial position. These topics were not packaged as separate school subjects. A procedure for extracting a square root and a rule for computing a planet's longitude could belong to the same technical training. Their unity was practical: the astronomer needed arithmetic to move from an epoch and a table to the appearance of the sky on a specified night.

Part of that compression depended on a numerical notation that used syllables and letters. The modern expression of a large number as a string of digits was not the form in which Aryabhata presented his constants. His alphabetic system could carry place information and enormous astronomical quantities inside verse.

The numbers in astronomical models are often huge because a long cycle lets fractions be represented as integers. Suppose a planet completes approximately a certain number of turns over thousands of years. A tabulated total for the long interval can be divided into shorter periods when one wants a position for a particular date. The model's accuracy depends on the total number of revolutions, the chosen starting time, and the corrections applied to the mean position. The arithmetic is not just decoration: a small error in a period can accumulate into a much larger disagreement over long spans of time. Compressing these constants into verse allowed a school to preserve a calculational framework, not just a scattered set of figures.

Imagine trying to remember a table of numbers long enough to predict planetary positions, but writing it as something with rhythm and verbal shape. That is the practical challenge his arrangement addressed. It does not mean he invented the number zero or the decimal positional system. Indian numeration had a long and composite history, and Aryabhata's own device needs to be distinguished from the numeral forms that later became familiar across the world.

Consider a simple example of the mathematical style. Aryabhata gives a rule from which the circumference of a circle can be estimated by a numerical factor astonishingly close to pi. In modern arithmetic his rule corresponds to the ratio thirty-one thousand four hundred sixteen divided by ten thousand. The result is three point one four one six. Modern pi begins three point one four one five nine. The two agree through four decimal places. That accuracy did not emerge from someone merely noticing that a round plate has a circumference about three times its diameter. It belongs to sophisticated traditions of geometric calculation and numerical approximation.

Still, we should keep the word approximation. The number three point one four one six is not pi itself. Some descriptions of Aryabhata present him as having proved, in modern terms, that pi is irrational. The evidence does not establish such a modern proof; historians disagree about how much to read into his Sanskrit phrasing. We can honor an impressive computation without assigning to him every later result about its constant.

Why should an astronomer care so much about a circle? Picture the edge of a rotating sphere and the path of an apparent planet. Angles and arcs turn positions in the sky into numbers. A circle of known radius lets you represent the changing height of an object, or the component of a motion in one chosen direction. A sine is one such component. If you move around a circle, the vertical projection of your position rises, reaches a maximum, and falls. That is all the listener needs to recognize the underlying idea.

Aryabhata's tables used what we would describe as sines, not merely the older Greek table of chords between pairs of points on a circle. He supplied a series of sine differences at regular angular intervals. The interval was three degrees and forty-five minutes, dividing a quarter-circle into twenty-four steps. The table allowed someone to move from a known position to the next by using a computed difference. Think of it as a numerical staircase following a smooth curve: the true sine bends continuously, while the table lets an astronomer advance by manageable increments.

The algorithm was subtle. One way to understand why a sine table needs differences rather than simply a handful of landmark values is to imagine a star at an angle of twenty-two degrees above a reference direction. If your table lists values only at intervals of three degrees and forty-five minutes, the desired position may fall between two entries. Interpolation then becomes a mathematical judgment: how smoothly does the quantity vary between known points? The second differences, or the way neighboring differences change, help control the approximation. A numerical astronomer may have a theory of motion but still fail in practice if the interpolation scheme is inaccurate. Table construction belongs to the central mathematics of the project.

The differences themselves vary according to a rule, and that variation contains information about the curvature of the sine function. In modern symbols we might relate nearby samples through a recurrence. Aryabhata did not print our equations. His verse gave a procedure in his own mathematical language. But the procedure embodied the problem every numerical analyst faces: how can finite arithmetic follow a changing quantity with enough precision for a real task?

Now return to the sky. Aryabhata's astronomy used cycles and corrective motions. The Sun, Moon, and planets were represented through mean motions and adjustments, including epicycle-like constructions. The model was geocentric in the sense that Earth remained the spatial reference for the orbits being calculated. To say Earth rotates is not to say Earth travels around the Sun. These are different claims and demand different evidence.

It is also useful to separate physical interpretation from the success of a numerical prediction. An astronomer might use a geocentric computational scheme that predicts an eclipse well without possessing the modern theory of gravitational orbits. Conversely, a bold physical proposal such as terrestrial rotation does not automatically make every parameter in the accompanying tables superior to competing tables. The history becomes much richer when these are independent questions: what does the model say exists, what calculation does it prescribe, how close are its predictions, and what evidence was available to decide among alternatives? Aryabhata's book and later commentaries put all four questions in play.

His striking proposal concerned the daily apparent turning of the celestial sphere. The boat analogy matters because it explains observational relativity in ordinary language. As a boat travels, fixed things on the bank seem to go backward. Similarly, when an observer rotates with Earth, the stars seem to circle overhead. This does not by itself prove Earth's rotation. Competing models may fit some of the same appearances. It does reveal that the sight of a moving star field cannot be taken as a simple physical description without considering the observer's frame.

Other Indian astronomers disagreed. Varahamihira and, later, Brahmagupta defended different accounts and criticized aspects of Aryabhata's system. One argument raised against a rotating Earth was that an object thrown upward ought to be left behind. Today we would answer that the object begins with Earth's rotational motion. But the historical lesson is not to congratulate ourselves for knowing the answer. The question exposed the difficulty of explaining motion when people lacked a fully worked out mechanics of inertia. Aryabhata's proposal belonged to an active argument, not a linear march toward modern astronomy.

Eclipses are a second revealing case. Imagine the Moon passing through the shadow cast by Earth. Its illuminated face darkens because sunlight is blocked. Imagine, in the other configuration, the Moon passing in front of the Sun for an observer on Earth. The apparent disk of the Sun is covered. Astronomers must calculate relative positions, apparent diameters, and timing to predict these events. Aryabhata treated eclipses through geometry and celestial shadow, not merely as the intervention of a mythic being.

That distinction does not require the claim that everyone around him believed one story and he alone invented rational explanation. Indian astronomical traditions already contained mathematical modeling. His work participated in and revised that tradition. His contemporaries could disagree about cosmological interpretation while sharing sophisticated computational methods. There is no reason to imagine a single uniform ancient Indian view of the heavens.

Some of the *Aryabhatiya* also deals with arithmetic, geometry, and methods for whole-number equations. Consider a problem of clocks or cycles: one event repeats every five units, another every seven, and you seek a time at which the two conditions fit together. In simple terms you may be looking for a number that leaves one remainder when divided by five and another when divided by seven. Aryabhata's mathematical tradition developed a technique known as *kuttaka*, often translated as pulverization, to solve such indeterminate problems. It breaks large numerical relationships into smaller ones, reversing the steps when a compatible solution has been found.

To see why that is powerful, suppose we want a number which is one more than a multiple of five and is divisible by seven. Four times five plus one is twenty-one, which works. The small answer is easy to guess; large astronomical periods are not. A reliable algorithm matters more than lucky inspection.

In a more demanding version of the problem, two astronomical periods may be measured in large integers that share no convenient factor. A repeated-division method begins by finding the quotient and remainder of the larger number divided by the smaller. Then the smaller is divided by the remainder, and so on. The numbers shrink rapidly until a final compatibility condition appears. One can then reverse the divisions to find a required whole-number solution. A modern reader will recognize a relationship to the Euclidean algorithm and modular arithmetic; that recognition helps explain the method, but the ancient Indian procedure arose in its own computational setting. Questions about when two cycles will line up made such mathematics worth preserving. We might now speak of congruences and the Euclidean algorithm. Aryabhata's technique belongs to a historical line of numerical reasoning with its own presentation and purposes, not merely a preliminary version of modern symbolic number theory.

His mathematical expressions for progressions, areas, volumes, and calculations illuminate the same ambition: compress practical knowledge into procedures an expert could carry and teach. Some rules were accurate in their intended applications; others were approximate or limited. It is misleading either to call him the inventor of all the mathematics recorded in the book or to dismiss the work because not every rule matches a modern theorem. He inherited a technical culture and made consequential choices within it.

What did it mean for another scholar to read the *Aryabhatiya*? A brief verse can travel far, but it can also be misunderstood. Commentators became essential. Bhaskara the First wrote a substantial commentary in the seventh century, explaining formulas, providing demonstrations, and helping readers reconstruct the calculations. The commentary is not an afterthought. It is part of the evidence that the work was taught, questioned, and made intelligible to people living after its author. Later Kerala astronomers, among them Paramesvara and Nilakantha Somayaji, continued to study Aryabhata's astronomy while developing new ideas of their own.

Another work, the *Aryabhata-siddhanta*, is known through references in later authors but does not survive as a complete independent text. Its existence reminds us that the record is partial. We cannot assume that the one famous work captures everything Aryabhata wrote or every view he held. We should be cautious about smoothing disagreements between the surviving treatise and accounts of the lost one.

His influence did not stay inside one language. Indian astronomy circulated into the Arabic-speaking scholarly world, where translations, adaptations, and numerical tables entered new contexts. Ideas could cross languages and geographical distances even when their authorship became difficult to trace. A later scholar might know a result without knowing exactly which manuscript or individual first framed it.

Even his name eventually became a historical problem. Al-Biruni, who read about Indian mathematical astronomy in the eleventh century, encountered references that he interpreted as belonging to different Aryabhatas. Modern study of the textual record has corrected parts of that confusion, but it is a valuable reminder that a scholar's reputation does not travel as a modern biographical file. Titles are abbreviated; dates are reckoned in different eras; authors are cited through commentaries. The mathematician may remain influential as a calculator for centuries while the details of his life are increasingly obscure.

There is another caution in stories of cultural transmission. Saying that a work influenced scholarship in Baghdad does not mean all its details arrived intact or all later developments were copied from one source. Techniques travel by teaching, translated tables, calculated examples, and adaptation. At each stop someone can preserve, alter, correct, or challenge them. The history of mathematics is partly the history of those transformations.

The apparent simplicity of modern notation makes Aryabhata especially vulnerable to anachronism. A modern textbook can rewrite his numerical schemes as decimal digits, his angular tables as trigonometric functions, and his cycles as equations. The translation helps us test calculations. But if it becomes the whole story, the original achievement disappears. We no longer see a scholar fitting astronomical data into memorized verse, explaining appearances through relative motion, and giving other people methods they could apply without our symbols.

One can learn a great deal from the fact that so little personal biography remains. We cannot tell what Aryabhata ate for breakfast, which teacher encouraged him, or how he reacted when someone rejected his theory of Earth. Those are tempting scenes, and they would be inventions. What survives is a more demanding kind of intimacy: the structure of his work and the evidence of its use. He treated the sky as something that could be calculated. He built a durable language for doing it. He left questions his successors had to answer.

Return to the boat on the river. Its passenger watches the shore slide past. We know why that apparent movement happens. Aryabhata asked people to entertain a similarly unsettling explanation for the night sky. Whether a particular astronomical parameter was right or wrong is only part of the historical achievement. The deeper contribution was the combination of observation, computation, compact mathematical procedure, and the willingness to question which object was actually moving. Five centuries after the beginning of the common era, in a set of tightly packed verses, a mathematician gave his readers an entire system to inspect, remember, criticize, and improve.

## Historical companion — not spoken

### Evidentiary chronology

- **c. 476:** Birth year inferred from Aryabhata's statement that he was twenty-three in the year corresponding to 499; birthplace is not secure.
- **499:** *Aryabhatiya* composed, approximately 121 stanzas in four sections, associated with Kusumapura.
- **Sixth to seventh centuries:** His methods were discussed and contested by later Indian astronomers; the separate *Aryabhata-siddhanta* survives only through references.
- **629:** Bhaskara I wrote a commentary, a crucial witness to the transmission and interpretation of the terse original.
- **Later centuries:** The text and its astronomical procedures influenced Sanskrit commentary and the mathematical astronomy of other language traditions, including Arabic adaptations.

### Mathematical companion

- **Circumference approximation:** A rule equivalent to \(\pi\approx 31416/10000=3.1416\). This is an approximation, not a proof that \(\pi\) is irrational.
- **Trigonometric tabulation:** Twenty-four intervals across a right angle give a spacing of \(90^\circ/24=3^\circ45'\). Aryabhata specified differences between sine values in a convention with a fixed reference radius.
- **Moving observer:** Apparent angular motion depends on the observer's motion. Earth's **rotation** does not imply a heliocentric orbit.
- **Congruence demonstration (modern example, not copied from the text):** Find \(n\) such that \(n\equiv1\pmod5\) and \(n\equiv0\pmod7\). A solution is \(n=21\). *Kuttaka* refers to a historical class of indeterminate-equation procedures.
- **Eclipse geometry:** Solar: Moon obscures Sun from the observer's line of sight. Lunar: Earth obstructs sunlight from reaching Moon. Predictive accuracy depends on orbital parameters and observer position.
