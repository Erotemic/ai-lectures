# 039500 — Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz: Hanover, the republic of letters, and the calculus war

**Track:** History · People and ideas. **Theme:** the historical life of a mathematician, philosopher, and court adviser. **Background:** no calculus assumed; technical examples follow the main historical narrative.

For nearly four decades Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz served the courts of Hanover while writing to scholars and statesmen across Europe. He advised rulers, compiled dynastic history, sought machines that could drain mines, helped organize scientific institutions, and wrote philosophy that could not be separated from the religious politics of his time. He was also one of the architects of differential calculus. The two lives were not parallel careers. They belonged to one person who repeatedly sought patrons and alliances large enough to support work that no employer had explicitly requested.

In Hanover the tasks were concrete. A court required a library to be maintained, family rights to be established, legal opinions to be written, and reports to be produced on proposed improvements. Leibniz had become a librarian and counsellor to Duke Johann Friedrich, then served successors whose enthusiasm for his many projects varied. His employers needed records of the House of Brunswick and its past. Leibniz travelled to libraries and archives to support that history. A dynastic genealogy was political infrastructure: it helped justify claims to territory, titles, and succession. Years of this work did not yield the completed history his later employer wanted. The mismatch between the demands of service and the scope of his plans became a recurring source of trouble.

The Harz mining projects show his ambitions working on a physical landscape. Water entering mines had to be removed so that shafts could be operated. Leibniz proposed schemes involving machinery and wind power. An elegant proposal on paper met the problem of variable winds, mechanical construction, cost, management, and the judgments of people who worked the mines. The projects never achieved the practical success he hoped for. That outcome should not be erased from a celebratory portrait. Engineering is subject to material and institutional constraints that no amount of optimism or symbolic ingenuity can dismiss. It is possible for a proposal to be inventive and still to fail.

For intellectual companionship Leibniz built a network that reached far beyond Hanover. Letters passed among courts, academies, university scholars, and clerics in different languages. They carried mathematical challenges and philosophical disputes, but also news, favors, requests, and personal concerns. His correspondence was not an abstract network diagram. It demanded human labor in an era of costly and uncertain transport. He could make an intellectual introduction between people who would never share a room, then spend days on the obligations that followed. Modern editors have devoted decades to reconstructing only portions of this surviving correspondence. Its scale helps explain both his astonishing range and the mountain of unfinished writing.

His surviving correspondence is immense: tens of thousands of letters and related records across a European network. A letter might carry a mathematical problem, a philosophical objection, a request for patronage, or news of a colleague's illness. Modern editors of these papers describe Leibniz as a mediator who preferred to preserve an intellectual relationship even when the ideas were sharply opposed. Yet the same network consumed time. Each incoming letter offered another task to perform and another promise to answer. The dream of a universal language was being pursued by a man whose ordinary days were filled with different human languages, interests, and obligations.

One of his most important relationships was with Sophie, Electress of Hanover. She had political influence, wide intellectual interests, and a family connection to the future British succession. Leibniz also engaged closely with her daughter Sophie Charlotte, who became queen in Prussia. Their discussions concerned theology, philosophy, government, and education, not only the mathematics for which he is best known. These women were patrons and interlocutors with their own positions and agendas. The correspondence matters because the architecture of European learned life was not confined to universities; court households could sustain discussions that institutions elsewhere might not.

At the turn of the eighteenth century, Sophie Charlotte's interest in an observatory and broader scientific learning created an opening for Leibniz's longstanding academy plans. Working with Daniel Ernst Jablonski and others, he argued that an institution should embrace sciences and useful arts instead of serving astronomy alone. A Berlin scientific society was founded in seventeen hundred, with Leibniz as its first president. Founding a society required charters, money, appointments, a relationship to the ruler, and a program for gathering and sharing knowledge. Leibniz thought institutional design was a scientific problem in its own right. He wanted useful information to circulate rather than remain isolated in someone's cabinet or manuscript collection.

His success had limits. Different patrons could offer titles and sometimes recognition without sufficient resources to realize ambitious plans. He continued work for Hanover even while cultivating projects in Berlin and later Vienna. His employers could regard a request for travel or another position as a failure to complete the work they had already assigned him. His wide range of commitments made him valuable, but it could also make him appear undisciplined. There was no secure modern research professorship into which his career neatly fits.

These relationships were much more than useful names in the margins of his publications. Sophie Charlotte in particular enjoyed long conversations with Leibniz and took his philosophical questions seriously. When she died in 1705, he was deeply affected and his correspondence slowed for months. His later reflections on theodicy and the relation of reason to belief grew in a world where such conversations had formed part of everyday intellectual life. Sophie of Hanover remained another source of friendship and protection. When she died in 1714, Leibniz lost one of the people best placed to advocate for him as her son ascended the British throne.

His mathematical work meanwhile acquired a public life quite different from its manuscript beginnings in Paris. In sixteen eighty-four he published differential calculus in the Leipzig journal *Acta Eruditorum*; in sixteen eighty-six he published on integration. By the end of the century other mathematicians were using, criticizing, and extending the notation. The brothers Jakob and Johann Bernoulli were prominent participants, as were Johann Bernoulli's students and correspondents. Challenges about the quickest path of descent, the shape of a hanging chain, or the solution of differential equations could be circulated publicly and answered by different people. The calculus grew because it ceased to be the property of a single inventor's notebooks.

The publication of his methods also changed the mathematics of a network of correspondents. Jakob and Johann Bernoulli used the new calculus to investigate curves, differential equations, and optimization. Their work, along with contributions from l'Hôpital and others, made the method more systematic and powerful. A celebrated problem asked for the curve along which a bead, sliding without friction under gravity, reaches a lower point in the least time. The answer is a cycloid, not the straight line one might initially expect. The brachistochrone became a proving ground for the new calculus of variations, a subject in which optimal quantities are entire curves rather than single numbers.

Correspondence did not mean constant harmony. The brothers Jakob and Johann Bernoulli were brilliant, competitive, and eventually bitterly divided. Historians studying Leibniz's letters find a man who enjoyed intellectual contest but often tried to prevent a disagreement from becoming a permanent personal breach. That habit is revealing. The inventor of a notation that made relations explicit spent much of his social life managing relations that resisted any simple rule. He had friends he scarcely ever met and collaborators whose letters crossed political borders more easily than their bodies could.

The catenary was another famous challenge. Hang a uniform flexible chain from two fixed points and ask what curve it forms. It resembles a parabola, but the exact curve is a hyperbolic cosine. Huygens, Leibniz, and Johann Bernoulli solved the problem around sixteen ninety. It is an elegant reminder that geometry can arise from force balance and that differential equations can determine shapes in nature. The new symbolic language helped make such problems systematic rather than isolated feats of geometric invention.

A more bitter contest developed around Newton. Newton had worked out fluxional methods before Leibniz's first publications, though much remained in his papers or in selective correspondence. Leibniz developed his own symbolic procedures and published earlier. Accusations of borrowing grew from particular communications and later pamphlets into a partisan campaign. British advocates could argue that manuscripts were evidence of priority; continental advocates could argue that accessible publications and usable notation had changed actual mathematical practice. In seventeen twelve a Royal Society inquiry sided with Newton, its independence compromised by Newton's undisclosed participation. The dispute damaged Leibniz's reputation in Britain and deepened the split between mathematical communities.

The dispute had consequences beyond reputation. In much of continental Europe, Leibniz's notation and the work of the Bernoullis encouraged rapid development of analysis. In Britain, attachment to Newton's fluxional notation and the priority conflict contributed to a different mathematical culture. The symbols a community chooses can affect how easily discoveries spread. Leibniz had supplied notation that made many operations look natural: products, quotients, composites, and integrals could all be handled through a coordinated syntax.

The quarrel cannot be settled by picking a hero and a villain. The archival evidence supports independent foundational work with different publication histories. It also supports the charge that a learned society's authority was used in a dispute where its president had a personal stake. The consequences were not symmetrical: Newton held high official positions in Britain, while Leibniz was trying to preserve standing in an increasingly fraught relationship with the Hanoverian court. Both names survived in the history of calculus; contemporaries experienced real damage, factions, and distrust.

Leibniz's philosophical opposition to Newton's circle took place on a still larger stage. With the rise of the Hanoverian dynasty in Britain, his former student and correspondent Caroline became Princess of Wales. She passed letters between Leibniz and Samuel Clarke, a defender of Newtonian philosophy, in seventeen fifteen and seventeen sixteen. They disputed the status of absolute space and time, the character of divine action, and how to distinguish genuine physical differences from mere changes in description. The exchange is often called the Leibniz–Clarke correspondence. Caroline's work in making it happen belongs to its history, and Clarke's arguments are his own, not simply words dictated by Newton.

In seventeen fourteen Sophie of Hanover died, and her son George Ludwig inherited the British crown as George the First. Leibniz did not move with the court to London. He had failed to finish the dynastic history his employers wanted, and his public quarrel with Newton's supporters further complicated any position at the British court. He had also sought opportunities in Vienna. The last years were filled with writing, ailments, and anxiety about what remained incomplete. He still corresponded widely and advanced philosophical arguments, but the courtly promises of earlier decades had narrowed.

A biographical study by Maria Rosa Antognazza restores some comedy to the ageing Leibniz. His clothes were becoming old-fashioned and flamboyant enough to amuse a young prince at court. In private he wore layers intended to soothe gout and arthritis, including fur-lined garments and heavy socks. The same account describes a man whose conversation could still win people over and whose humor made him pleasant company. One observer remarked on the novelty of a learned man who was clean and good-humored. Beneath the wigs and layers was someone feeling time run short, writing prolifically while his body made long travel and ordinary work harder.

He died in Hanover in November seventeen sixteen. Contemporary accounts present a modest funeral; later retellings sometimes turn it into a theatrical demonstration that everyone had abandoned him. Better to recognize both his isolation at the end and the substantial relationships he had maintained through life. His papers survived in astonishing quantities. Editors and historians have had to reconstruct chains of letters, handwritten corrections, and publication choices to establish what he wrote and when. Leibniz did not leave a clean shelf of completed books summarizing every ambition. He left a record of thinking in motion, distributed among different projects and interlocutors.

The surviving intellectual work now becomes easier to see as a connected project. Leibniz wanted operations that could be reliably repeated, whether they concerned numbers, machines, or arguments. That did not mean he believed all human judgment had already been reduced to symbols. His experiments with mechanisms and notation, and his aspirations for a formal language, have very different degrees of completion. It is worth discussing the mathematics and technology after seeing the man who tried to persuade patrons, train craftsmen, and maintain a republic of letters large enough to make them real.

Leibniz's work on calculation was not confined to pen and paper. During his time in Paris and later in Hanover, he worked on a mechanical calculator that could perform addition, subtraction, multiplication, and division. Its most distinctive feature was the stepped drum, a cylinder with teeth of different lengths, capable of engaging other gears in a controlled way. An operator could turn a handle and have the machine perform a sequence of digit operations. Making this reliable was difficult. Carries had to propagate correctly; precision engineering and mechanical friction limited what the machine could do. Leibniz improved his design over many years, but his devices did not become widely used commercial machines in his lifetime.

The calculating machine gives us a more tactile Leibniz. It required finely made parts, artisans who could manufacture them, money to refine prototypes, and demonstrations before potential patrons. Paper calculations could promise an automatic arithmetic, but gears had tolerances and a mechanism could fail when a carry propagated incorrectly. Leibniz kept returning to improvements over many years. His eagerness to mechanize calculation met the stubbornness of metal. That struggle makes his later interest in symbolic logic easier to imagine: the hope that an operation could be specified exactly had already been tested against a machine that sometimes refused to cooperate.

Why did a philosopher of calculus care about mechanical arithmetic? Consider multiplying thirty-seven by twenty-four. One can compute thirty-seven times four, then thirty-seven times twenty, then add the results. The operation is already an algorithm: a finite prescription using simpler repeated steps and positional notation. If a mechanism can represent digits and carry values between positions, much of the work can be transferred from the human calculator to the device. Leibniz saw that arithmetic has structure sufficiently regular to become a machine process. The difference between a symbolic operation and a physical mechanism is less absolute than it first appears.

A second line of work concerned the representation of numbers. In ordinary base ten, the number thirteen means one ten and three units. In base two, thirteen is written one-one-zero-one. Reading from left to right, those digits represent eight, four, zero twos, and one unit. Add eight, four, and one to recover thirteen. Leibniz studied binary arithmetic extensively and published an explanation in seventeen oh three. Because only zero and one are needed, basic operations can be extremely simple, even though the numeral strings become longer. This is not a direct line from Leibniz's paper to the invention of every modern digital computer. Earlier binary ideas existed, and many later developments intervened. Yet it is an instructive precursor to systems that represent information through two states.

Let us calculate a little in binary. One-one in base two is three. One-zero-one is five. Add them as we add decimal digits, carrying when two ones make one zero in the current column and one in the next. Three plus five is eight, written one-zero-zero-zero. The calculation is as exact as its decimal counterpart. The choice of base changes the representation, not the underlying number. Leibniz was fascinated by this separation between what a quantity is and how it is expressed. Once representation is treated as a variable, arithmetic becomes a field for design.

He linked binary notation to an interpretation of the sixty-four hexagrams of the Chinese Book of Changes, which he learned about through Jesuit correspondence, especially with Joachim Bouvet. The correspondence is historically important as an episode of intellectual exchange across cultures. It is equally important not to turn it into a simplistic story in which Leibniz discovered something unknown elsewhere. The Chinese tradition had its own history and purposes, and positional binary representations had appeared in other mathematical contexts. What Leibniz supplied was one particularly clear European exposition of an arithmetic system using just two symbols, along with theological and philosophical interpretations of its significance.

One can take the binary idea further without claiming Leibniz invented modern electronics. In a digital circuit, two stable physical states can represent zero and one. With suitable gates, combinations of bits can represent numbers, text, images, and instructions. The conceptual bridge is the possibility of encoding different contents into a systematic symbolic representation. The engineering bridge, however, required Boolean algebra, electrical circuits, logical design, and electronic devices developed long after Leibniz. A good historical portrait identifies the seed of an idea without pretending the entire future was already contained in it.

His interest in binary arithmetic also gives an opportunity to understand positional algorithms more deeply. Multiply the binary number one-one, meaning three, by one-zero-one, meaning five. The units bit of the second number is one, so write a copy of one-one. The next bit is zero, so add nothing in the twos column. The third bit is one, so add another copy of one-one shifted two places, meaning twelve. The sum is fifteen, written one-one-one-one in binary. Everything is carried out using shifts, additions, and the digits zero and one. The calculation is exact, and the repeated pattern is easy to mechanize. This is one reason positional notation can support machines, even though the historical stepped reckoner operated in decimal arithmetic.

Leibniz also wrote about more familiar decimal arithmetic and calculations with determinants and equations. To solve two linear equations in two unknowns, one can eliminate a variable; the combination a d minus b c, formed from their coefficients, determines whether a unique solution is possible. This is the two-by-two determinant. Leibniz explored determinant-like constructions in the sixteen nineties, but the modern determinant theory was created through many contributions and later systematic notation. The historical point is that he was interested in formulaic patterns across quite different kinds of calculation.

A third line of work involved logic. Leibniz imagined a characteristica universalis, a universal symbolic language in which concepts could be represented clearly, and a calculus ratiocinator in which arguments could be checked by formal rules. His famous vision that disputants might sit down and calculate was a program, not a completed device. Many questions resist conversion into mechanically settled formal propositions; even where formalization succeeds, the choice of assumptions matters. Still, the ambition anticipates later developments in symbolic logic and automated reasoning. The historical connection is a continuity of problems and aspirations, rather than an uninterrupted chain of practical machines.

Here the early study of combinations returns. Suppose two claims can each be true or false. There are four possible assignments: true and true, true and false, false and true, and false and false. An explicit table can show when a compound statement is true. For the logical word AND, only the first assignment yields true. For OR, any assignment with at least one true input yields true. Modern truth tables were developed much later, but the example captures the attraction of Leibniz's program. Once a statement has been given a formal representation, some questions about it become finite calculations. The hard part is choosing a language that captures the reasoning one cares about.

Leibniz also had a distinctive approach to philosophy. He argued for a principle of sufficient reason: there should be an explanation for why things are as they are rather than otherwise. He defended a principle often called the identity of indiscernibles: if two things share all their properties, there is no genuine distinction left between them. He developed a metaphysical picture of simple substances called monads and tried to reconcile free will, divine order, and the laws of nature. These doctrines are not mathematical theorems and should not be presented as if they were. But they make his scientific ambitions intelligible. He hoped that the world's diversity possessed intelligible structure and that reasoning could reveal it.

His philosophical life involved extensive correspondence with thinkers including Antoine Arnauld, Samuel Clarke, and many others. The exchanges with Clarke, who defended aspects of Newton's position, brought questions about absolute space, time, the vacuum, and the foundations of physics into sharp focus. If two universes differed only by shifting every object to the east, would there be a meaningful physical distinction? Leibniz argued against unnecessary absolute distinctions, using his principle of sufficient reason and a relational view of space. These debates belong to philosophy and physics, but they also concern a mathematical habit: attend to the structure that remains unchanged under a transformation.

Leibniz's work on physics also reveals his independent mathematical taste. In disputes about collisions and mechanical effect, he argued for the importance of a quantity he called vis viva, or living force, proportional to mass times velocity squared. That is close to twice the kinetic energy in modern mechanics, although the conceptual setting and exact physical interpretation were different. Imagine a mass of one unit moving at speed two. Its mass times velocity squared is four. Double the speed to four and the quantity becomes sixteen: four times larger. This squares-with-speed dependence is exactly what makes stopping distance and impact energy behave differently from momentum. Leibniz was challenging some accounts of motion that privileged mass times velocity alone. Later mechanics clarified the distinct conservation laws for momentum and energy.

The disagreement was not just philosophical. It prompted precise experiments and calculations about bodies falling from heights and colliding. Huygens's work on impacts was important to this history. Leibniz sought quantities that remained invariant through physical transformations. Here his mathematical instincts were aligned with his philosophical hope that nature had intelligible structure. Yet one should resist treating vis viva as though it were the final modern theory. The road to energy conservation passed through many contributors and new physical assumptions.

This wider science gives the rivalry with Newton another meaning. Leibniz challenged the usefulness of measuring force solely by quantity of motion, and he asked whether motion should be understood through relations among bodies rather than an independently existing spatial container. He pursued these arguments through equations, metaphysics, and conversation. One need not accept all his conclusions to understand why physics and logic were important to him: he believed that the right representations would distinguish superficial disagreement from genuine difference.

Another mathematical illustration brings these strands together. Leibniz investigated the alternating series one minus one third plus one fifth minus one seventh and so on. Multiply its infinite sum, under suitable convergence reasoning, by four, and one obtains pi. Today this is commonly called the Gregory–Leibniz series, because James Gregory had found the relation earlier. Its appeal is immediate: a famous geometric constant appears as a sequence of simple fractions. Its weakness is also instructive. The sum converges slowly. Adding thousands of terms still leaves a noticeable error. Symbolic elegance and computational efficiency are different properties. A good numerical method needs more than a beautiful exact identity.

Why does that series produce pi? One route begins with the geometric series for one over one plus x squared. For absolute x less than one, expand it as one minus x squared plus x to the fourth minus x to the sixth and so on. Integrate term by term from zero to one, carefully handling the endpoint through a limiting argument. The result is one minus one third plus one fifth and so forth. But the integral of one over one plus x squared from zero to one is arctangent of one, which is pi over four. The calculation combines algebra, infinite series, and integration in a way characteristic of the new analysis.

A historical complication lies underneath this argument. In Leibniz's time, mathematicians did not possess all the later machinery for uniform convergence and rigorous interchange of limits and integrals. They found powerful results with reasoning that later generations needed to clarify. This is not an indictment. It is a useful reminder of what a new mathematical technique initially looks like. Methods are often productive before their most careful foundations have been written down. The challenge is to improve rigor without losing the insight that made the method fruitful.

There is a temptation to read every idea backward from digital computers and modern symbolic logic, as though Leibniz foresaw each mechanism that was later built. That would be a poor way to understand his life. He did not possess electronics, modern formal semantics, or the rigorous nineteenth-century foundations of analysis. He worked with quills, gears, printers, archives, couriers, and people. The central historical question is why those tools suggested to him that human reasoning might be made more effective by designing better signs and better procedures. He repeatedly met the limits of what patrons, craftsmen, rival scholars, and his own time could accomplish.

Leibniz's public reputation after death was shaped by both the spectacular success of his calculus notation and the disorder of his enormous intellectual estate. The latter is not merely a deficiency. It records the life of a person who seldom had a single profession, who was always seeking a more capable institution, and who believed enough in correspondence to keep beginning new projects when earlier ones were unfinished. The mathematician of differentials cannot be separated from the court historian, the legal adviser, the friend of Sophie, the traveller to Paris, or the aging philosopher still answering letters from Hanover.

## Display-only historical and mathematical companion

### Hanover, academies, correspondence, and the last years

- **1676–1716:** Leibniz served successive Hanoverian rulers in legal, historical, library, diplomatic, and engineering capacities. Harz drainage proposals met technical and financial resistance.
- **1684 / 1686:** Publications on differential and integral calculus in *Acta Eruditorum*; Bernoulli and other continental mathematicians helped turn the notation into communal mathematical practice.
- **1690s–1700:** Travel and archival work for Hanover, exchanges with Sophie and Sophie Charlotte, and participation in establishing the Berlin scientific society.
- **1703:** Binary arithmetic publication and dialogue with Jesuit correspondence about the Chinese hexagrams. Earlier non-Leibniz binary work must not be erased.
- **1712–1716:** Controversy over calculus priority, Royal Society inquiry influenced by Newton, Hanoverian accession in Britain, and the correspondence with Samuel Clarke mediated by Caroline, Princess of Wales.
- **1716:** Death in Hanover; posthumous editing of extensive manuscripts and letters remains necessary to reconstruct the full intellectual project.

### Mechanical and positional arithmetic

In base two,
\[
13=8+4+1=(1101)_2,\qquad
3=(11)_2,\quad 5=(101)_2,\quad 3+5=(1000)_2=8.
\]
The stepped reckoner used decimal mechanical arithmetic and a stepped drum; binary representation did not directly power the historical machine.

### Infinite series and analytic hindsight

For \(|x|<1\),
\[
\frac1{1+x^2}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}(-1)^nx^{2n},\qquad
\frac\pi4=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^n}{2n+1}.
\]
The second expression follows by integration with a limiting argument at the endpoint. After \(N\) alternating terms the error in \(\pi/4\) is at most \(1/(2N+1)\). This is often called the Gregory–Leibniz series; James Gregory published the result earlier. Modern convergence theorems and notation postdate this period.

### Logic, physics, priority

The *characteristica universalis* and *calculus ratiocinator* were ambitions for explicit symbolic reasoning, not completed computers or modern formal systems. Leibniz's arguments about sufficient reason, space, and force were philosophical and physical claims, not theorems of modern logic. Newton's earlier unpublished manuscripts and Leibniz's earlier publications reflect separate contributions. The 1715–16 Leibniz–Clarke papers were mediated by Caroline and should not be reduced to direct Newton–Leibniz letters.
