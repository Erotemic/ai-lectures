# 135000 — Lise Meitner: The physicist in exile and the breaking nucleus

**Track:** History — People and ideas. **Focus:** Vienna, Berlin and Stockholm; women's entry into physics; radioactivity; the Meitner–Hahn–Strassmann collaboration; escape from Nazi Germany; the Meitner–Frisch interpretation of nuclear fission; Nobel attribution and moral responsibility. **Background:** Basic atoms and energy are developed in speech.

In late December 1938, a physicist who had recently escaped Nazi Germany received a letter containing a result that seemed impossible. The chemists in Berlin said that after bombarding uranium with neutrons they had found barium. But barium was much lighter than uranium. The calculations and chemical identifications resisted the explanations that had worked for decades. What kind of nuclear event could turn something so heavy into products so much lighter?

Lise Meitner and her nephew Otto Robert Frisch worked through the puzzle during a holiday visit in Sweden. In Frisch's later recollection, they discussed it while walking outdoors in the snow. Their answer was that the uranium nucleus had split. They understood how such a split could release enormous energy, and they named the process nuclear fission. The experiment and the explanation together transformed nuclear physics. Yet the prize most closely associated with the discovery would go to someone else. The reason cannot be understood from the laboratory result alone. It belongs to a story of collaboration, forced migration, scientific institutions and the political destruction of a research community.

Meitner was born in Vienna in 1878, when the city was part of the Austro-Hungarian Empire. Her family was Jewish, and her father was a lawyer who supported the education of his children. The opportunities open to a girl who wanted to study physics were restricted. Universities had been built around male students, male appointments and assumptions about the kind of future a woman should have. Meitner needed extra preparation to qualify for further study. When women were admitted to the University of Vienna, she took advantage of the opening and studied physics with teachers including Ludwig Boltzmann, whose thermodynamics and statistical reasoning helped transform the field. In 1906 she received a doctorate, one of the first women to do so in physics there.

Vienna was not a provincial scientific environment, but having a doctorate did not guarantee a research career. Meitner moved to Berlin in 1907 to study with Max Planck, who was central to the new quantum theory. He initially represented a scientific establishment with restrictive attitudes toward women, yet he came to respect Meitner's abilities. She began working with Otto Hahn, a chemist investigating radioactive substances. Their fields overlapped but demanded different skills. The chemist could isolate substances and identify their properties; the physicist could ask what the behavior of emitted particles revealed about the atom. The arrangement was not free from social inequality. Women faced restrictions on laboratory space, appointments and pay; Meitner's early Berlin work was carried out under conditions that a similarly situated man would not necessarily have accepted.

Radioactivity was a rapidly changing science. The atom, once imagined as an indivisible unit of matter, had become a source of penetrating radiation and transformations into other elements. Marie and Pierre Curie, Ernest Rutherford, Frederick Soddy and many others had shown that radioactive substances could change over time. An element might produce an alpha particle, which consists of two protons and two neutrons, or a beta particle, associated in modern nuclear language with the conversion between a neutron and a proton. By studying how the radiation changed and what new substance appeared, researchers could build a chain of nuclear transformations.

But nuclear physics at the start of Meitner's career did not have the tidy picture found in an introductory textbook today. The neutron was not discovered until 1932. The detailed nuclear forces and quantum description were still being developed. What an experimenter could know depended on detectors, chemical separations, counting methods and a growing theory of what atomic nuclei could contain. A chemist and a physicist looking at the same sample might ask different questions, and both perspectives could be essential.

The partnership between Meitner and Hahn became productive. In the years before the First World War they studied radioactive decay chains and the identities of newly observed substances. During the war Meitner worked as a medical X-ray technician, while Hahn's wartime service took him into the German chemical-weapons system. Their lives were not sheltered from the catastrophe through which Europe was passing. They later helped identify a long-lived isotope of the element protactinium. That result belongs to a history in which earlier researchers had observed a different, short-lived protactinium isotope. Scientific priority was not a single moment when the element suddenly sprang into existence as an idea.

The Kaiser Wilhelm Institute for Chemistry offered them a more durable research setting. Meitner developed an increasingly independent career and eventually held a professorship in Berlin in the 1920s, a major institutional milestone for a woman physicist in Germany. She also made contributions to the study of beta radiation and nuclear processes beyond what is now remembered as her famous discovery. The scientist who explained fission in 1938 had already spent decades learning to turn faint signals into reliable arguments.

In 1933 Adolf Hitler came to power. The Nazi government began expelling Jewish scholars and dismantling the professional rights of people classified under its racial laws. Meitner had converted to Protestantism earlier in life, but under antisemitic policy personal religious practice did not shield someone of Jewish ancestry. Her Austrian citizenship temporarily complicated her status, and she remained in Berlin while many colleagues fled. She later criticized her own delay in leaving. We do not need to decide how brave or imprudent she ought to have been from the comfort of hindsight. She was trying to continue a life's work while the conditions under which it existed were being systematically destroyed.

In March 1938 Germany annexed Austria. Meitner's nationality no longer offered even limited protection. By July she was in immediate danger of being trapped in Germany. Colleagues helped her escape across the Dutch border; among them were Dirk Coster and Adriaan Fokker. She left behind a laboratory, possessions and a scientific position built over decades. She reached Sweden and found a place in Stockholm associated with Manne Siegbahn's institute. The existence of a nominal position did not mean she recovered the resources, collaborators or autonomy she had had in Berlin. Exile preserved her life but fractured the experimental environment in which her work had taken shape.

The nuclear problem that would define the next months had begun earlier. In 1934 Enrico Fermi and collaborators fired neutrons at uranium and observed new radioactive activities. Neutrons carry no electric charge, so they can approach the positive nucleus without the electrical repulsion that hinders an incoming positively charged particle. Physicists hoped that capturing a neutron might create elements heavier than uranium, the heaviest naturally occurring element then known by atomic number. Chemical analysis of the products was hard. Many radioactive species had short half-lives; a small amount of one substance could masquerade as another if its behavior was not isolated cleanly.

Irene Joliot-Curie and Pavel Savić in France reported puzzling results. Hahn, Meitner and the chemist Fritz Strassmann investigated related activities, and in 1938 the Berlin chemists pursued painstaking chemical separations. Even after Meitner fled, she and Hahn continued to exchange letters. The experimenters found a radioactive product behaving like barium. At first the result was so contrary to expectation that they considered alternatives involving radium. A radium nucleus is much closer to uranium in atomic number than barium is, and the prevailing expectations favored relatively modest nuclear changes.

Here is why the barium result was so startling. Uranium has 92 protons in its nucleus; barium has 56. The difference is 36 protons, far larger than could be explained by the familiar emission of one or two alpha particles. If the chemical identification was sound, the assumption that a heavy nucleus could only lose small fragments had failed. Hahn and Strassmann's work provided the crucial chemical evidence, and their skepticism about a straightforward interpretation is part of the story. A laboratory result can be excellent even when the experimenters lack the theory that makes it intelligible.

Meitner's forced separation did not mean she ceased to reason about the experimental work. She and Hahn met and corresponded while the chemists refined the analysis. When the barium evidence arrived at the end of 1938, Meitner discussed it with Frisch, who worked with Niels Bohr in Copenhagen. They drew on a picture of the nucleus as a charged liquid drop. Surface tension tends to keep an ordinary drop together; electric repulsion among the positively charged protons tends to push a nuclear drop apart. For a very heavy nucleus these competing effects can make deformation and splitting possible after a disturbance such as neutron absorption.

Think of a roughly rounded drop that stretches into an elongated shape. As a narrow neck forms, the parts at opposite ends may separate. This is an analogy, not a claim that the atomic nucleus literally consists of continuous water. The nuclear system has quantum behavior, discrete particles and forces different from those acting on a puddle. But the analogy was good enough to suggest the basic possibility: one very heavy object could break into two substantial fragments, rather than shedding only tiny pieces. The chemically observed barium was no longer an impossible visitor. It could be part of a nuclear fragment.

Where did the released energy come from? Meitner and Frisch estimated that the two fragments would have slightly less total mass than the original nuclear system. Einstein's relation between mass and energy tells us that a small reduction in rest mass can appear as a large increase in energy. The calculation suggested roughly two hundred million electron volts per fission, vastly greater than energies typical of a single chemical molecular rearrangement. This does not mean that a piece of uranium spontaneously converts all its mass into energy. Only a small mass difference between reactants and products supplies the released energy, and its division among fragment motion, radiation and other products depends on the reaction.

The conservation laws still hold. Electric charge, total energy and other relevant quantities have to balance. In an actual uranium fission event the fragments are not always identical, and additional neutrons and radiation may be emitted. A simple classroom picture showing two equal halves is an illustration, not a universal reaction equation. The insight of late 1938 was that splitting was physically possible and that the scale of the energy made sense. Soon Frisch performed experiments that supplied further physical confirmation, while the research community rapidly grasped what the explanation meant.

Meitner and Frisch published their interpretation in *Nature* in February 1939. They called it fission, borrowing a word associated with biological division. Their paper explicitly recognized the experimental work on which their argument depended. It appeared amid a rush of investigations by scientists across Europe and the United States. The idea of a neutron-induced chain reaction quickly followed: if fission emits additional neutrons, some may induce further fissions. But the possibility depends on neutron energies, probabilities of capture and escape, the composition and geometry of the material, and many engineering constraints. One dramatic nuclear event is not automatically a sustained reaction or a bomb.

The distinction matters for Meitner's biography. She helped provide the physical explanation of a phenomenon that would make nuclear reactors and nuclear weapons possible. She did not organize the Manhattan Project, design the first bomb or direct wartime weapons laboratories. Invited to join the American atomic-bomb effort, she refused. That refusal should be understood in the context of her actual scientific work and the choices she faced, not turned into a claim that she had somehow been uninvolved with the scientific knowledge on which later weapons rested. Scientists can contribute fundamental discoveries and still reject particular applications of them.

Europe meanwhile entered another catastrophic war. Meitner remained in Sweden rather than returning to Berlin. She continued scientific work under difficult institutional conditions and later received honors that acknowledged her contributions. Yet the attribution of nuclear fission had been altered by the breakup of the Berlin team. When a physicist is driven out of a laboratory, the published record may make an ongoing collaboration appear narrower than it was. The separation of experimental chemistry from theoretical physics became especially damaging when a prize committee tried to attach a single award to the discovery.

The Nobel Prize in Chemistry for 1944, awarded to Otto Hahn and actually conferred in 1945, honored the discovery of nuclear fission. Meitner was not included; neither were Strassmann or Frisch. The omission has been investigated by historians with access to nominations, committee records, letters and later testimony. It is not enough to say merely that Hahn stole the prize, as if one individual alone controlled the committee. Nor is it credible to say that Meitner made no scientific contribution because she was outside the laboratory when the decisive barium separations were completed. Hahn and Strassmann's chemical evidence and Meitner and Frisch's physical interpretation were different, mutually important parts of the discovery. The conditions of Nazi persecution and Meitner's marginal position in Sweden distorted the recognition that followed.

Ruth Lewin Sime's historical work has shown how scientific collaboration, forced migration and postwar memory interacted in the Nobel controversy. Some later accounts minimized the role of physicists in making sense of the experimental results. Other popular accounts compensate by making Meitner the solitary discoverer and reducing everyone else to an obstacle. Neither account does justice to the record. When chemistry produced a result without an acceptable explanation, and physics offered an explanation that depended on difficult chemistry, the discovery crossed a disciplinary boundary. It was precisely the kind of achievement that can be damaged by a reward system designed to identify a small number of winners.

After the war Meitner became a visible public figure and continued to criticize the moral failures of scientists and institutions under Nazism. Her correspondence reveals feelings of anger, disappointment and loyalty that did not always coexist comfortably. She could value old colleagues while challenging the choices they had made. She could appreciate scientific recognition without regarding a missed Nobel Prize as the only thing that defined her life. Later honors included the Max Planck Medal and, in 1966, a shared Enrico Fermi Award with Hahn and Strassmann. Meitner eventually moved to England, where she died in 1968.

A historical life has more than one possible ending. One ending is the Nobel ceremony that excluded Meitner. Another is a formula about energy released by nuclear splitting. Yet another is the long career before 1938: the young woman navigating university restrictions in Vienna, the physicist conducting radiation research in Berlin, the collaborator who learned when to trust or question an unexpected chemical result. If we begin and end only with the prize, we reduce a scientist to the injustice done to her.

Return to the winter conversation in Sweden. The scientist is far from the apparatus, dependent on a letter from a laboratory she cannot safely enter. The chemists are far from a convincing physical interpretation. Frisch brings another route to the relevant theory. No single person holds every piece. A discovery becomes possible when these pieces are made to fit, and its history becomes distorted when the people who made them are separated by borders, institutions and memory. Meitner's life is a scientific story about a nucleus that could split, and a human story about a collaboration that politics forced apart.

## Non-spoken companion: nuclear reasoning and historiography

- **The liquid-drop argument:** Meitner and Frisch used a model associated with Bohr to compare electrostatic repulsion and surface effects, yielding the possibility of division into two large fragments. The model is not a literal continuum-fluid account of the quantum nucleus.
- **Energy:** Roughly 200 MeV per fission is an order-of-magnitude energy estimate. In SI units 200 MeV ≈ 3.2 × 10^-11 joules. It is the change in total energy including binding, not complete conversion of uranium mass.
- **Chemical identification versus physical interpretation:** Hahn and Strassmann's barium identification and Meitner and Frisch's physical account were distinct, mutually necessary achievements. The preceding work of Joliot-Curie, Savić, Fermi and others matters.
- **Prize date:** The 1944 Nobel Chemistry Prize was conferred in 1945. Avoid statements that place an ordinary 1944 ceremony at which Hahn received it.
- **Escape and Stockholm:** Use the documented Dutch escape route and acknowledge inadequate research resources in Swedish exile. Do not infer that a formal appointment meant an uninterrupted research program.
- **Wartime research:** Explaining fission is not the same as designing a nuclear weapon. Chain reactions require further experimental physics and engineering.
