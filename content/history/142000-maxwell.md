# 142000 — James Clerk Maxwell: The invisible field

**Track:** History · People and ideas. **Background:** No specialist physics assumed. The spoken lecture develops fields, induction, molecular motion, and statistical reasoning from concrete examples.

Imagine a glass jar containing nothing more remarkable than air. On one side of the room is a magnet; on another, a prism and a patch of sunlight. Beyond the window, somewhere far above the atmosphere, are the rings of Saturn. There is no obvious reason these things should belong to one person's intellectual life. A jar of gas is full of colliding molecules. A magnet acts without any visible connection to what it attracts. A prism breaks white light into colors. Saturn's rings are a problem of gravity on the scale of a planet.

For James Clerk Maxwell, the connection was not that all four problems had the same answer. They did not. It was that each presented the same kind of challenge: the world shows us a large-scale pattern, but the mechanism behind it may be hidden. How do we reason from what we can measure to what we cannot directly see? And when does an elegant explanation deserve to be called a physical theory?

Maxwell is now most often remembered for a short set of equations named after him. They connect electric and magnetic fields and make a startling prediction: disturbances in those fields can move through empty space as waves, at the speed of light. The prediction placed light itself within the theory of electromagnetism. But the familiar four equations are a modern presentation of a more intricate nineteenth-century achievement. They came out of experiments by other people, Maxwell's mathematical constructions, arguments about measurement, and an extended attempt to take Michael Faraday's picture of the world seriously. Their author also devised a fundamental description of the velocities of gas molecules, helped establish color photography, analyzed the stability of Saturn's rings, and helped create an experimental physics laboratory at Cambridge.

It is tempting to flatten this life into the word genius. The actual story is more informative. Maxwell was a child absorbed by doors and watercourses, a student who made friends through mathematics, a professor whose most successful period was shaped by institutional upheaval, a husband whose home was also a site of research, and a physicist who kept asking what a mathematical pattern meant in the laboratory. He lived only forty-eight years. Much of the technological world that later claimed his equations was built after his death.

He was born in Edinburgh on 13 June 1831. His father, John Clerk Maxwell, was a lawyer with family property in southwest Scotland. His mother, Frances Cay Maxwell, was closely involved in his early upbringing. The family moved from Edinburgh to Glenlair, a country home in Kirkcudbrightshire. Young James encountered tools, paths, drains, agricultural machinery, and the hidden arrangements that made household objects work. A family letter from 1834 describes his fascination with locks, keys, bell wires, and the ways water traveled from one place to another. The words commonly recalled from his childhood are a request to be shown how something worked. The letter is more useful than a legend of a child anticipating his future discoveries: it shows the kind of curiosity the adults around him actually noticed.

His mother died in 1839, when he was eight. The loss made his father an especially important presence. His formal education was not effortless. Sent to the Edinburgh Academy in 1841, Maxwell was a country child entering a socially demanding school. Later biographies describe teasing, awkward clothes, and an early impression among some classmates that he was slow. These episodes need not become a comforting fable in which every later achievement was hidden beneath mockery. He made enduring friendships, among them with Peter Guthrie Tait, who would become a prominent physicist. His interests extended beyond school marks to geometry, poems, sketches, and mechanical devices.

At fourteen, he studied how a loop of string and a set of fixed points could generate different oval curves. With two pins and a taut loop of string you can draw an ellipse. What happens when you change the number of fixed points, their positions, or the relation between the string and the drawing point? Maxwell developed a geometric treatment of such curves. James David Forbes, the natural philosopher at the University of Edinburgh, presented his paper to the Royal Society of Edinburgh in 1846 because the young author was not himself presenting it. There is no need to imagine an adult mathematician appearing fully formed in a schoolboy. The paper shows that Maxwell was already skilled at moving between a physical construction and the mathematics that described it.

He entered the University of Edinburgh in 1847, studying subjects including mathematics and natural philosophy. Forbes was an important teacher, particularly for experimental science and its connection to mathematical reasoning. Maxwell also learned about the mathematical work then developing in Britain and on the Continent. In 1850 he went to Cambridge, beginning at Peterhouse before moving to Trinity College. Cambridge mathematical training was famously competitive. The final examinations ranked students by their performance in problems solved under intense pressure. In 1854 Maxwell finished as second wrangler, behind Edward Routh, and the two shared first place in the Smith's Prize examination. The rankings help locate him in a demanding institution, but they are not a measurement of the scientific importance of their later work.

Cambridge also broadened his circle of collaborators and friends. William Thomson, later Lord Kelvin, was already shaping the theory of electricity and heat. George Gabriel Stokes worked on fluid motion and light. Maxwell found an intellectual world in which a mathematical question could become an experiment, an experiment could challenge a theory, and a theory might propose a new kind of measurement. These were not all Maxwell's inventions. They were the working habits of a remarkable scientific community, and he became one of its most original participants.

An early research problem drew him far from electrical circuits. Saturn possesses a broad system of rings, clearly visible in telescopes by the nineteenth century but difficult to interpret. Are the rings rigid solids? Are they continuous fluids? Or are they composed of many smaller objects in orbit? A solid ring seems easy to imagine: put a hoop around a planet. Gravity, however, pulls different portions of the hoop in different directions, and the ring must respond to even a small displacement. A fluid ring can deform, but fluid behavior introduces other instabilities. A vast collection of separate orbiting bodies behaves differently because each can follow an orbit appropriate to its distance from the planet.

The question was posed for Cambridge's Adams Prize. Maxwell worked through stability conditions, including the effect of disturbances that could grow rather than die away. The essay won the prize associated with the 1856 competition and appeared as a book in 1859. His conclusion favored rings formed from many independent particles or small satellites, rather than one continuous solid or fluid band. This was not a telescope image of separate particles; it was reasoning about what could remain stable. Later astronomical observations supplied additional evidence. The point is not that a mathematical proof, by itself, told astronomers every feature of the actual rings. It is that dynamics narrowed the physically possible answers before anyone could look closely enough to see the structure directly.

The calculation also makes a useful contrast with the mechanics of a single planet. A planet circling a star can sometimes be treated as one moving body. A ring made of countless pieces cannot be understood by following one piece in isolation. The pieces interact. They can collide and exchange energy. The stability of the whole is a collective question. In later work on gases Maxwell would confront another collective problem, but with an astonishing change in scale: not countless particles orbiting a planet, but molecules racing through ordinary air.

In 1856, shortly after his father's death, Maxwell became professor of natural philosophy at Marischal College in Aberdeen. It was his first professorship. He was in his twenties, teaching undergraduates while developing research and making his place in a university community. In Aberdeen he met Katherine Mary Dewar, daughter of the college principal, Daniel Dewar. They married in 1858. Katherine was not simply a spectator in the years that followed. Surviving scientific papers and historical research record her as a participant in investigations of vision and of gas viscosity, though the full division of experimental labor at home is not recoverable from the published record. To describe every experiment as the achievement of one isolated mind would miss how research was actually conducted.

The Aberdeen appointment did not last. Marischal College and King's College in Aberdeen were consolidated into a new university, and Maxwell lost his chair in the reorganization. In 1860 he took up the professorship of natural philosophy at King's College London. It is often easier to tell a scientific biography as a chain of promotions. In reality his route included competition for posts, institutional decisions he could not control, and a move that proved scientifically productive. He was in London until 1865, close to the Royal Institution, the Royal Society, and an active community of experimenters.

Before we reach the most famous work, consider an experiment in color. Shine red, green, and blue light onto the same area of a screen. By adjusting their intensities, one can reproduce a wide range of color sensations. That is strange if color is thought of simply as a label attached to an object's surface. Our eyes respond to light, but they do not deliver a direct catalog of every wavelength present. Different mixtures of wavelengths can appear alike. Maxwell's investigations of color matching provided quantitative evidence for the idea that human color vision depends on three kinds of response, later understood in terms of cone photoreceptors.

A color match is not the same as a claim that every physical spectrum consists of three wavelengths. You can mix different distributions of light and obtain the same visual experience because the eye reduces a rich signal to a small number of responses. Maxwell used rotating color discs and color-matching measurements to study this relation. He also paid attention to observers whose responses differed, including people with color-vision deficiencies. In surviving tables of observations, one observer is identified as J, meaning Maxwell himself, and another as K, understood by historians as Katherine. The result is a glimpse of experiments in which a human observer and a measuring procedure had to be treated together.

There was an audacious practical consequence. If color can be reconstructed from three carefully chosen components, might photography do the same? Conventional photographic plates registered light intensity, not a full color image. Maxwell proposed making three separate exposures through different colored filters and projecting them together through corresponding filters. At an 1861 demonstration, the photographer Thomas Sutton, working with Maxwell's plan, made three photographs of a tartan ribbon for this purpose. The projected color image is a landmark in photographic history. It was also partly a lucky experiment. The photographic materials of the day were scarcely sensitive to red light; later examination indicates that ultraviolet sensitivity helped register portions of the ribbon that the intended red exposure should not have recorded well. Thomas Sutton's photographic skill belongs in the account, and so does the limitation of the demonstration. A sound theoretical proposal and a successful demonstration do not always work together in exactly the way their authors initially believe.

Now turn from light received by the eye to light in space. Faraday had transformed the understanding of electricity and magnetism through experiments. He showed that a changing magnetic situation can induce an electrical effect in a circuit. Move a magnet near a loop of wire, for example, and an induced current can appear. The essential fact is not simply that magnets pull on iron or that electrical charges attract and repel. The magnetic state of a region can change, and a change can produce a new electrical effect elsewhere.

Faraday found it useful to imagine lines of force passing through space. Iron filings around a magnet can make a pattern, but those filings are not the magnetic field itself. The lines help represent something that exists throughout a region, not only at the surfaces of objects. In many earlier theories, two bodies influenced each other across a distance, and the principal question was to calculate the force between them. Faraday's view invited a different question. What is happening at each place in between? Maxwell admired Faraday's experimental insight and set himself the task of finding mathematics capable of expressing it.

That changed what counted as the object of study. An electric field tells us what electrical force a test charge would experience at a given point, taking account of the charge used to probe it. A magnetic field describes magnetic effects at a point, including how a moving charge would respond. The fields are not material strings drawn between objects. They are physical quantities assigned to positions in space, and the values can change with time. A map of wind gives a partial analogy: there is a direction and strength at different locations. But an electromagnetic field is not ordinary wind or a stream of air. The analogy is useful for thinking about a varying quantity distributed through space, not for identifying its substance.

Maxwell's earliest major treatment, 'On Faraday's Lines of Force,' appeared in the middle 1850s. He used mathematical analogies, including aspects of fluid flow, to represent field behavior. He later developed intricate mechanical models involving rotating elements and intermediary particles. To modern eyes, the model can look bizarre: invisible wheels and vortices turning in a material medium. But in a time before the electrical properties of space were understood, the machinery gave him a way to explore what a field could do. Crucially, it was a modeling device, not a set of microscopic components later discovered inside a magnet. Maxwell was willing to use a mechanical picture for its mathematical fertility and then pursue more general relationships that did not depend on that picture being literally true.

Here is one of the decisive puzzles. Take a battery, wires, and a capacitor, which is simply a pair of conducting plates separated by an insulating gap. When the circuit is connected, charge accumulates on the plates. For a short time, a current flows in the wires. In the gap between the plates, however, no electrons need to travel from one metal plate to the other. If you measure the changing electric field in that gap, it grows as the capacitor charges.

There is an apparent inconsistency if we treat magnetic effects as produced only by conduction current. Imagine tracing a closed loop around one of the wires and choosing a surface stretched across that loop. If the surface cuts through the wire, it encounters current. If we bulge the surface so it passes through the capacitor gap instead, it does not cut through the metal current. The same closed boundary would apparently give two answers for the circulation of the magnetic field. A workable field law cannot depend on which imaginary surface we choose to span that boundary.

Maxwell's resolution was to include the contribution of a changing electric field, what he called electric displacement. In modern language, a time-varying electric field contributes to the magnetic-field law alongside ordinary electric current. In an insulating capacitor gap it is not a stream of charged particles crossing the space. It is the changing state of the electric field that supplies the missing term. With this term, the field equations fit together with the conservation of electric charge: charge cannot mysteriously accumulate or disappear because we chose a different mathematical surface.

That addition matters even when there are no wires, plates, or moving charges nearby. Imagine a changing magnetic field in otherwise empty space. Faraday's induction law says such change is accompanied by a circulating electric field. Maxwell's extended magnetic law says a changing electric field is in turn accompanied by a magnetic field. The two kinds of change can sustain a traveling disturbance. It is not that an electric field creates a magnetic field and waits, then the magnetic field creates electricity and waits, in a sequence of isolated little pushes. The coupled equations describe one continuous wave, with electric and magnetic variations interrelated across space and time.

The equations also determine how fast such a wave would travel. The relevant constants describe the electrical and magnetic response of empty space. In today's units, the speed is one divided by the square root of the product of the electric constant and the magnetic constant. You do not need to memorize that expression to hear the significance. Constants established through electrical and magnetic measurements turn out to give a speed near the measured speed of light. A quantity discovered by comparing units of electrical force and current had the dimensions of velocity. Maxwell used results from earlier electrical measurements, including work by Wilhelm Weber and Rudolf Kohlrausch, and compared the resulting speed with optical determinations.

Here is the intellectual leap: perhaps light is itself an electromagnetic wave. The theory did not merely supply a story for one familiar laboratory phenomenon. It connected what had appeared to be separate domains. Optics had its experiments with prisms, mirrors, interference, and polarization. Electricity had batteries and sparks. Magnetism had compasses and coils. Maxwell showed that the mathematics of the field could accommodate traveling waves whose speed matched light and whose vibrations were transverse to the direction of travel, as light's polarization required. It was a unification with testable content, not a similarity of words.

Maxwell described the field in papers published between 1861 and 1862, and in a more general work, 'A Dynamical Theory of the Electromagnetic Field,' received by the Royal Society in October 1864, read in December, and published in 1865. This historical timing matters because textbooks sometimes place the entire invention under one date. The conceptual work developed across years. The 1864 paper did not contain the neat four vector equations that commonly appear on a classroom poster. Maxwell set out a larger group of equations in component form, including relationships needed to define his physical quantities. Later mathematicians and physicists, notably Oliver Heaviside and Josiah Willard Gibbs, helped bring the theory into its familiar compact notation. Calling the modern equations Maxwell's recognizes his central achievement without pretending that the typography of today's textbooks appeared unchanged on his desk.

A careful explanation should also distinguish prediction from confirmation. Maxwell did not stand beside an antenna and demonstrate radio transmission. Heinrich Hertz produced and detected electromagnetic waves experimentally in the late 1880s, after Maxwell had died. Further work by researchers and inventors made wireless communication possible. Maxwell's equations supplied an account of why waves of electromagnetic fields should exist; the generation, detection, engineering, and social adoption of radio were later achievements by many people. Credit for a theory is neither the same as credit for an experiment nor ownership of every technology built from it.

During these years Maxwell was working on a very different question as well. What is temperature, viewed from inside a gas? In everyday experience, a gas has a pressure and a temperature. A container of air has these properties as a whole. The microscopic picture proposes that its molecules move in many directions, bounce from walls, and collide with one another. Pressure is linked to momentum transferred to the walls. Temperature is connected to average molecular energy. But one cannot assume every molecule moves at one standard speed. Collisions continually redistribute the motion.

Rudolf Clausius and other investigators had already developed important parts of this kinetic theory. Maxwell took a further step in his 1860 paper on the dynamical theory of gases. Instead of seeking a single representative speed, he asked for a distribution: what fraction of molecules have speeds within a certain range? Picture a crowded road where not every vehicle moves at the same pace. A mean speed tells you something, but it cannot answer how many vehicles are creeping or racing. For molecules the differences are not minor details; they are part of the state of the gas.

Maxwell considered the three components of a molecule's velocity: forward or backward along one direction, and similarly along two perpendicular directions. Under assumptions of equilibrium and isotropy, the probability pattern for one component has the bell-shaped form familiar from the theory of errors. Combining the components produces a distribution of speeds. There are relatively few molecules moving almost not at all, relatively few moving extremely fast, and many moving in an intermediate range. The exact curve is not merely a colorful picture of randomness. It makes quantitative predictions. From temperature and molecular mass, one can calculate characteristic speeds and ask how changing the gas or its temperature should affect the distribution.

This is an important kind of scientific explanation. A microscopic law need not say exactly which molecule will be here next Tuesday. For a gas containing an astronomical number of molecules, a statistical statement can be more illuminating than an impossible list of trajectories. Maxwell was not announcing that mechanics had stopped applying to individual molecules. He was showing how probability could make mechanics useful in describing collectives. Later Ludwig Boltzmann developed this approach further. The familiar name Maxwell–Boltzmann distribution honors a history of connected, partly independent contributions, not the work of one person at one instant.

The molecular picture also led to an initially counterintuitive claim about viscosity, the resistance a fluid offers to layers sliding past one another. Think of stirring a thick liquid and then a thin gas. It seems plausible that a denser gas would always be more viscous because there are more molecules to interfere with motion. But in a sufficiently dilute gas, increasing density increases the number of molecules carrying momentum while decreasing the average distance each molecule travels before a collision. These effects roughly compensate. Kinetic theory predicts that viscosity can be largely independent of pressure over a range where the gas remains dilute and other conditions are held fixed.

This does not mean every gas has the same viscosity under every condition, nor that the result holds at arbitrary densities. It is a prediction about a definite regime. Maxwell tested related effects using carefully designed apparatus, including suspended discs whose motion was damped by gas. Katherine's participation in this experimental work is recorded, although domestic and laboratory records do not permit a precise accounting of every task. Maxwell refined the gas theory in later papers, correcting weaknesses in earlier assumptions. The striking part of the story is that a theoretical argument about invisible molecular motion could tell an experimenter what a macroscopic instrument ought to register.

If molecular motion is statistical, what becomes of the laws of heat? The second law of thermodynamics says, in one common formulation, that heat does not spontaneously flow from a colder body to a hotter one in an isolated ordinary process. Yet individual molecules sometimes move against an average trend. In a letter to his friend Peter Guthrie Tait in 1867, Maxwell imagined a tiny, extraordinarily observant being controlling a door between two regions of gas. The being admits fast molecules in one direction and slow molecules in the other, eventually producing a temperature difference without the usual expenditure of work.

This was the ancestor of what later became known as Maxwell's demon. It is not evidence that Maxwell believed an invisible creature inhabited boilers. It is a disciplined thought experiment. Suppose we can distinguish individual molecules and selectively open a gate. Does a statistical law retain the absolute character that a simple textbook formulation might suggest? The question exposed a tension between the microscopic description of a system and the macroscopic direction of thermal change. Later analysis would bring information, measurement, and physical control into the problem. The experiment's lasting value lies partly in forcing physicists to say exactly which processes and assumptions they mean when stating the second law.

Another of Maxwell's interests can be understood by looking at a steam engine. A mechanical governor adjusts a valve as the engine's speed changes. If the engine runs too quickly, the mechanism reduces the flow of steam; if it slows, the mechanism permits more power. Feedback sounds straightforward. But a badly designed governor can overcorrect, making the machine surge and oscillate instead of settling. In 'On Governors,' published in 1868, Maxwell analyzed the stability of such regulating systems mathematically. The work helped establish ways of thinking about feedback and control that would later become central to engineering. It belongs to the same mind that studied orbiting particles and oscillating fields: understanding a system often means understanding how it responds to a disturbance.

After five years at King's College London, Maxwell resigned his professorship in 1865 and returned for much of the following period to Glenlair. He did not abandon research. He revised ideas, wrote, corresponded, investigated, and worked on the monumental 'Treatise on Electricity and Magnetism,' which appeared in 1873. The book is not simply a collection of four equations with examples. It is a wide-ranging effort to create a coherent mathematical language for experiments, circuits, potential, magnetism, dielectrics, and the electromagnetic field. Some material now seems laborious beside modern formulations. That is partly because Maxwell was building relationships that later generations learned to take as given.

His private circumstances mattered to the work without giving us access to every private feeling. The Maxwell home could accommodate the apparatus and patient observations of physics; correspondence reveals humor, poetry, and friendships; the boundaries between teaching, research, home, and scientific society were not rigid. Maxwell's wife Katherine was an intellectual and practical participant in parts of his investigations, but the surviving record has tended to make her visible only through references in his papers and through memories collected by others. It is responsible to recover that participation. It would not be responsible to invent her precise words in a laboratory conversation or to assign her a mathematical result without evidence.

In 1871 Maxwell accepted a new role at the University of Cambridge: the first Cavendish Professorship of Experimental Physics. The university had a formidable mathematical tradition, but laboratory-based physics teaching did not yet have the institutional home that its advocates wanted. The Cavendish Laboratory, funded through a gift associated with William Cavendish, Duke of Devonshire and Chancellor of the university, opened in 1874 under Maxwell's direction. Its name also evoked Henry Cavendish, an earlier physicist and experimenter. Maxwell planned facilities, supervised their formation, and advocated training the eye and hand as well as the mathematical mind.

Imagine what it means for an institution to learn experimental physics. It needs instruments that can be calibrated. It needs students who know what an electrical measurement is and how uncertainty enters. It needs a place where a result can be repeated, criticized, and improved. Cambridge mathematics was already capable of extraordinary abstraction. The laboratory made a claim that such abstraction should be joined to disciplined practical investigation. Maxwell's inaugural lecture defended that connection. The laboratory would later become associated with discoveries made by scientists Maxwell never met, including major developments in atomic and nuclear physics. His institutional achievement was to help establish the conditions under which that work could take place, not to prefigure every discovery himself.

One of his final historical projects concerned Henry Cavendish's unpublished electrical experiments from the eighteenth century. Maxwell obtained manuscript material, reconstructed methods and calculations, and prepared it for publication. In some cases he performed tests rather than merely transcribing documents. The resulting 'Electrical Researches of the Honourable Henry Cavendish' was published in 1879. A scholar editing a predecessor's laboratory notes might appear to be taking time from more original research. But the work shows Maxwell's conviction that precision, experimental reasoning, and historical understanding mattered. Scientific discoveries do not become more secure because we erase the people who arrived earlier at related ideas.

Maxwell's own health was worsening. Contemporary accounts describe digestive trouble becoming more serious during the late 1870s. He died in Cambridge on 5 November 1879, aged forty-eight, from abdominal cancer. The illness had also claimed his mother decades earlier, according to early biographical accounts. Katherine survived him. Their marriage had not produced children. After his death, colleagues and former students helped preserve his scientific papers and his reputation; the meaning and applications of his work continued to unfold beyond his lifetime.

The electromagnetic field theory faced questions when Maxwell died. Could waves of the kind he predicted be created and detected independently of ordinary light? Hertz's experiments supplied a compelling answer in the 1880s. Other scientists recast and extended the equations. The ether, which nineteenth-century physicists commonly invoked when interpreting waves, would not remain a necessary mechanical medium in twentieth-century physics. Special relativity would give the speed of light a central role, and quantum theory would transform the account of matter and radiation. Maxwell's work survived these revisions not as an untouched mechanical story about vortices but as a set of extraordinarily successful relations among measurable physical quantities.

His other work had similarly long lives. The statistical description of gas velocities became a foundation of kinetic theory. The color-matching approach helped shape the science of perception and image reproduction. Analysis of Saturn's rings showed how stability could decide between models before detailed observation became possible. Feedback analysis suggested how to recognize instability in machines built to regulate themselves. These achievements should not be presented as accidental side quests on the way to electromagnetism. They reveal how Maxwell thought: use mathematics to expose what a model commits you to; use measurements to decide what the model is worth; and be prepared to revise the mechanism while retaining the relationship that experiment has established.

Return to the beginning: a jar of gas, a magnet, a prism, and the rings of Saturn. In one case, you cannot see individual molecular trajectories. In another, you cannot see a field directly. In the third, you cannot read the physical spectrum from the color the eye reports. In the fourth, the particles making up a ring are too small or distant to resolve. Maxwell treated such limitations not as an invitation to guess freely but as an opportunity to ask what observable consequences would follow from a precise account of the unseen.

His best-known achievement was to show that electricity, magnetism, and light could be understood as aspects of one electromagnetic field. Yet the larger lesson of his life is about the practice of science. A theory earns its standing by doing more than naming a mystery. It organizes experiments already known, corrects inconsistencies, and makes room for phenomena nobody has yet measured. Maxwell's most durable question was not only what the invisible world might contain, but how we could know.

## Non-spoken companion: historical and scientific reference

### Concepts worth keeping separate

- **Fields versus illustrations:** Lines of force visualize a direction and magnitude field; they are not material filaments. Maxwell's 1861–62 mechanical vortex models helped him develop relationships but are not the literal microscopic ontology of the modern equations.
- **The capacitor puzzle:** Modern form of Ampère–Maxwell law is \(\nabla\times\mathbf B=\mu_0\mathbf J+\mu_0\varepsilon_0\,\partial\mathbf E/\partial t\). The changing-electric-field contribution resolves the surface-choice inconsistency and charge continuity. In a dielectric gap it is not a conduction current through the dielectric.
- **Induction and waves:** \(\nabla\times\mathbf E=-\partial\mathbf B/\partial t\). Combining this with the magnetic equation in charge-free vacuum gives a wave equation with speed \(c=1/\sqrt{\mu_0\varepsilon_0}\) in standard SI notation. This is explanatory modern notation; Maxwell's historical formulation used different quantities and conventions.
- **The original versus modern Maxwell equations:** The 1864 paper, published in 1865, presented twenty component equations among its generalized field relations, not the familiar four-equation Heaviside/Gibbs vector packaging. The modern format retains Maxwell's physical content but represents subsequent reformulation.
- **Gas speed statistics:** Under equilibrium assumptions, velocity components have a symmetric Gaussian distribution and the molecular *speed* has a Maxwell distribution, weighted by the number of velocity vectors with a given length. For fixed temperature, lighter molecules have higher characteristic speeds. Boltzmann made additional major contributions.
- **Gas viscosity:** In a dilute gas, molecular number density and mean free path oppose each other in the momentum-transport calculation; viscosity can be largely pressure-independent under stated conditions. This is not universal across arbitrarily high pressures or near phase transitions.
- **The demon:** A conceptual challenge to the implications of a statistical second law, not a demonstrated perpetual-motion device. Later information-thermodynamic analyses require their own assumptions and should not be retroactively attributed to Maxwell.

### Historical chronology and attribution

- **1831–1850:** Born in Edinburgh on **13 June 1831**, family home at Glenlair, mother Frances dies in 1839; Edinburgh Academy from 1841, oval-curve paper presented by James David Forbes in 1846, University of Edinburgh from 1847, Cambridge from 1850. A few nineteenth- and twentieth-century sources print **13 November** as his birth date, apparently following one college record; the Royal Society's archival person record and the James Clerk Maxwell Foundation give June 13. The disagreement is not evidence for two separate people.
- **1854–1860:** Second wrangler and co-first Smith's Prizeman with Edward Routh; professorship in Aberdeen from 1856; Adams Prize essay on Saturn's rings completed for the **1856 competition**, awarded in 1857 according to some institutional timelines, published in 1859. Marriage to **Katherine Mary Dewar** in 1858. The Aberdeen university merger ended the professorship.
- **1860–1865:** Professor at King's College London. **Thomas Sutton** made the filter-separated ribbon photographs for Maxwell's 1861 demonstration; contemporary plate sensitivity made their apparent success partly serendipitous. Papers of 1861–62 and the **1864** Royal Society reading, published **1865**, developed field theory. Maxwell's 1860 gas work and later experimental investigations included Katherine's assistance.
- **1867–1873:** Private letter to Peter Guthrie Tait in 1867 anticipates the demon; 1868 paper on governors; 1871 Cambridge experimental physics professorship, 1873 *Treatise on Electricity and Magnetism*. Later label 'Maxwell's demon' should not be treated as his own original name for the being.
- **1874–1879:** Cavendish Laboratory opens under his leadership in 1874; he edits Henry Cavendish's experiments (published 1879); Maxwell dies in Cambridge on **5 November 1879**. Hertz's laboratory evidence for electromagnetic waves came later in the 1880s.
