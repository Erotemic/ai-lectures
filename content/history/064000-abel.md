# 064000 - Niels Henrik Abel: A Norwegian life beyond the fifth degree

**Track:** History - People and ideas. **Focus:** Denmark-Norway and the union with Sweden, family hardship, Holmboe, the quintic, European mathematical publishing, Crelle, Paris, elliptic functions, and an early death. **Background:** elementary algebra is enough.

A mathematical paper can arrive too late for its author. In eighteen twenty-nine, August Leopold Crelle was trying to secure a position in Berlin for a young Norwegian whose work he had helped publish. The appointment would have offered the scholar what he had rarely possessed: a stable income and the time to devote himself to mathematics. Niels Henrik Abel was already dying. He died in April, aged twenty-six. News of his prospective appointment reached Norway only after his death. It is tempting to make the episode a tidy legend about a genius whom the world recognized too late. The reality is more precise and more interesting. Abel was noticed by particular people who worked hard on his behalf. Those efforts were not enough to overcome the distances, money, illnesses, and institutional delays of early nineteenth-century Europe.

Abel was born in August eighteen oh two, when Norway was still joined to Denmark under the Danish crown. His father, Soren Georg Abel, was a Lutheran minister; his mother, Ane Marie Simonsen, came from a merchant family. He grew up mainly in Gjerstad, where his father succeeded the boy's grandfather as parish minister. The rectory was a place where religion, teaching, politics, and household finances met. His father participated in the political life of Norway during the years in which the old union with Denmark broke apart. He did not live in a country with the secure university system or the scientific travel networks that a scholar in Paris might take for granted.

The Napoleonic wars brought particular hardship to Norway. British actions against the Danish fleet, followed by blockades and shortages, disrupted Norwegian trade and food supplies. Timber exports and grain imports were affected. In eighteen fourteen, Norway passed from Danish rule into a new union with Sweden, while retaining a constitution and substantial institutions of its own. Abel was a child and adolescent during these upheavals. They were not decorative background events. A poorer country meant fewer scholarships, a smaller mathematical profession, and much less ability to support a precocious student through years of specialized work.

Abel attended the Cathedral School in Christiania, now Oslo. Accounts of his schooldays often turn the story into a sudden discovery of talent. But his mathematical life developed through teaching and access to books. The decisive teacher was Bernt Michael Holmboe, who recognized that Abel could go far beyond the prescribed exercises. Holmboe introduced him to advanced mathematical literature and encouraged independent study. Abel read the writings of Euler, Lagrange, Gauss, and other mathematicians while still very young. Their work was not an inheritance he could simply absorb without effort. He had to learn its language, reconstruct arguments, and try problems that those authors themselves had not settled.

There was a special intimacy to his education. Holmboe was not merely a name on a diploma. Later he would edit the collected writings of the pupil he had encouraged. The astronomer Christopher Hansteen and other university figures also helped Abel find ways to continue. In a country whose university was young, personal interest could substitute for a formal research apparatus, but only imperfectly. Letters, borrowed books, and conversations became an informal seminar. The arrangement demanded generosity from older scholars and persistence from the student. It is a reminder that self-education, so often celebrated in biographies, almost never means education without other people.

An early journey to Copenhagen in eighteen twenty-three gave Abel contact with a wider Scandinavian scientific world. It also brought him into contact with Christine Kemp. The scientific and the personal parts of the trip were intertwined. Later, when he was offered travel abroad, he faced a difficult decision about whether to follow a prescribed itinerary or remain with companions. His letters make clear that he did not enjoy traveling alone. Those feelings shaped where the mathematics took him. The route to Crelle was not a perfect plan executed by a calculating genius. It was a human itinerary made through friendships, anxieties, letters of introduction, and the wish for company.


The family's circumstances became more difficult. Abel's father lost standing through political controversy and died in eighteen twenty. A large family remained, and the responsibility of providing for relatives could not be separated from Niels's efforts to study. When he entered the new university at Christiania in eighteen twenty-one, support came largely through the intervention and personal contributions of teachers and professors. Think about what that means for a young mathematician. He needed paper, books, food, rent, and the means to correspond with colleagues; at the same time he was attempting work at the edge of what European mathematics knew. It is impossible to measure how much research never began because other students lacked that support.

His first famous problem concerned polynomial equations. For a quadratic equation, a familiar formula gives the answers by taking sums, differences, products, quotients, and square roots of expressions involving the coefficients. Formulas for cubic and quartic equations had been found centuries earlier, using more complicated combinations of radicals. The obvious next challenge was an equation of degree five, a quintic. Could there be one general formula of the same kind, perhaps very complicated, but still built from arithmetic operations and taking roots? Abel initially believed he had a solution; he found his error. That failed attempt was part of the mathematical education that produced the eventual result.

The distinction between finding a particular solution and proving that a whole class of formulas is impossible matters. A particular quintic may be easy to solve. Some fifth-degree equations can be factored, and others have roots that can be expressed using radicals. Abel's claim was about the general quintic: no universal expression using only a finite combination of the usual arithmetic operations and radical extractions will solve every such equation in terms of its coefficients. He was not proving that fifth-degree equations have no roots, or that one cannot approximate them numerically. The theorem locates a limit of an entire style of symbolic calculation.

Paolo Ruffini had argued earlier for an impossibility result, but his work was not widely accepted as a complete proof. Abel supplied the decisive proof that became the standard landmark in the subject. Later, Evariste Galois provided a more systematic explanation of which polynomial equations are solvable by radicals, using the transformations of their roots. The three histories should not be collapsed into a single lonely discovery. Ruffini, Abel, and Galois addressed connected but different questions with different methods, and their work was assessed and transmitted through different channels. Abel belongs to the history of modern algebra not only because he reached a negative answer, but because he understood how to prove a result about all possible formulas of a specified kind.

Publishing that answer was itself difficult. An early version appeared in a brief privately printed pamphlet in eighteen twenty-four, written in French because French was an international language of mathematics. Abel had little money for printing and made the presentation extremely compressed. A short proof of an impossible task is only useful if readers can follow it and recognize what has been established. His explanation was revised and amplified, but reputation did not arrive automatically. A mathematician without an established position, working far from the major academies, needed both correctness and an audience.

In eighteen twenty-five, with a travel grant obtained through Norwegian institutional support, Abel set out for the Continent. His projected itinerary included visits to leading centers of mathematics, but he changed his route and went to Berlin. That decision transformed his professional life. He met August Leopold Crelle, a Prussian engineer and administrator with a commitment to making mathematics circulate beyond existing centers. Crelle was establishing the *Journal for Pure and Applied Mathematics*, soon known informally as Crelle's Journal. Abel became one of its earliest and most important contributors. The friendship gave him something that a solitary proof could not: a publisher, an editor, a correspondent, and a person prepared to recommend him to institutions.

He had originally been encouraged to go toward Goettingen and perhaps to meet Gauss. The famous Gauss was not an easy figure for a young foreign mathematician to approach, and Abel heard discouraging reports about his accessibility. We should not turn those reports into a dramatic confrontation between two men; an encounter did not take place in the form later legend might wish. Word of a mathematician's temperament could travel as widely as his printed proofs. The young scholar needed to decide where his limited time and money would give him the best chance of conversation. Berlin, a city with a new journal and a welcoming editor, could be more valuable to him than a city with the most celebrated mathematician alive.


The journal began publishing in eighteen twenty-six, and Abel's papers helped give it standing. He did not merely become famous through its pages; the journal and its young contributor strengthened one another. Crelle's achievement is easy to hide in a history told only through the names of theorems. Founding a journal meant arranging printers, handling manuscripts, cultivating correspondents, and persuading readers that new work deserved attention. Those activities are part of why nineteenth-century mathematics could move across borders. A brilliant Norwegian and a Prussian publisher were building an international research community by exchanging paper.

From Berlin, Abel traveled on to Paris. Paris was the place where much of the mathematics he had studied was being produced and judged. The French Academy counted figures such as Cauchy and Legendre among its leading members. Abel prepared a substantial memoir concerning a new class of relations among integrals of algebraic functions and submitted it to the Academy in eighteen twenty-six. Cauchy and Legendre were named as reviewers. The manuscript was not promptly published. Abel remained poor and isolated, and he returned toward Norway without receiving the recognition that a successful Academy memoir might have brought.

That Paris episode has often been retold as if a great mathematician simply lost a great manuscript through carelessness. The documentary history is more complicated. A submitted paper could be assigned to busy reviewers; editorial procedures could be slow; different manuscripts could be mistaken for one another in later accounts. It is fair to say that Abel's major Paris memoir did not receive timely publication or effective assessment. It is not fair to treat every detail of the manuscript's journey as certain or to use the failure as proof of one reviewer's personal hostility. The harm remains real even when the anecdote is disentangled. For a young scholar in debt, a delayed report had consequences that differed sharply from those faced by an established professor.

Abel's name is associated with two branches of mathematics that can be difficult to distinguish on a first encounter: elliptic functions and Abelian functions. The name elliptic arose in part from the study of integrals connected with arc lengths of ellipses. One begins with integrals that resist expression using elementary functions such as polynomials, exponentials, and logarithms. Instead of treating the integral only as a problem in finding an antiderivative, Abel, like Carl Gustav Jacobi, studied new functions obtained by inverting these integrals. If an integral sends a value of a variable to a new value, one may ask whether the inverse relationship defines a rich kind of function, with addition laws, periodic behavior, and its own theory.

This was not a change in the spelling of old formulas. It was a change in the objects of study. The inverses of certain elliptic integrals have a striking double periodicity when considered as complex functions: shifting the input by either of two independent complex periods leaves the function unchanged. On the real line, one thinks of a sine wave repeating after a single period. In the complex plane, the geometry becomes a two-dimensional lattice of repeating values. The subject connects algebraic equations, complex analysis, and the geometry of surfaces. Jacobi independently built a powerful theory in the same years, and the relation between their work included mutual respect as well as the pressure of rapid publication.

The Paris memoir pointed beyond the elliptic case toward more general integrals and addition theorems. Modern terms such as Abelian variety and Abelian integral grew from this wider program, but they were shaped by generations of later mathematicians. Abel should not be pictured as working with all of the modern apparatus already assembled in front of him. He was finding relationships that made future apparatus necessary. His work helped demonstrate that a problem previously expressed in terms of elaborate integration could be reorganized around the algebraic and geometric properties of new functions.

Abel also investigated infinite series. A finite sum is a well-defined arithmetic object. An infinite series is a proposed limit of partial sums, and it need not behave like a finite sum merely because its notation looks similar. Which operations preserve convergence? When may one pass to a limit while changing another variable? Abel studied these questions before the later standardized language of uniform convergence became common. One result now bearing his name describes how a power series approaches a boundary point under suitable hypotheses. It is one part of a broader nineteenth-century effort to replace plausible manipulations of endless expressions by statements with clear conditions.

A listener can see the difficulty without any complicated notation. Imagine adding one half, then one quarter, then one eighth, and so on. The partial sums approach one, and the infinite sum is well behaved. Now imagine that the terms also depend on another variable, and that the variable approaches a special point. It is tempting to interchange the two operations: take infinitely many terms, and let the variable change. That interchange can be valid or invalid depending on how the terms behave. Analysis was full of such questions because the new functions of the era were represented by series and integrals. Abel's attention to convergence shows that his achievement was not confined to one spectacular impossibility theorem. He worked on the habits of proof that allowed new mathematics to be trusted.


The scientific labor was sustained in the face of very ordinary anxieties. Abel continued to worry about employment, the people he supported, and debts incurred by travel. At times he worked as an instructor and sought university assistance. He had become engaged to Christine Kemp, who worked as a governess. Their relationship and plans for a household were subject to the same uncertainty as his academic career. Describing his final years solely as a sequence of theorems leaves out the simple desire to be able to live with someone and earn a dependable living.

By eighteen twenty-eight, Abel was seriously ill. Tuberculosis was widespread, and there was no effective antibiotic treatment. The journey to Froland to spend Christmas with Christine was physically difficult; soon afterward he suffered severe illness and remained there. Crelle continued trying to arrange a position in Berlin. The appointment prospects could not rescue him. Abel died in Froland on the sixth of April, eighteen twenty-nine. A career that appears large from a later library shelf was, from its author's point of view, only a few years long.

The response after his death shows how belated recognition is made, not simply bestowed. Crelle wrote about Abel's mathematical achievement and character. Holmboe edited his collected works. Jacobi's work and Abel's work were placed in relation by mathematicians trying to understand the new functions. The French Academy awarded its Grand Prize in eighteen thirty jointly to Abel and Jacobi. The famous Paris memoir was eventually published years after its submission. These actions could preserve a result and strengthen a reputation, but they could not change the conditions in which the research had been done.

More than a century later, Norway established an international mathematics prize bearing Abel's name; awards began in two thousand three. That legacy brings attention to a mathematician who had almost no stable professional platform. It also carries a risk. When an early death becomes an emblem of genius, the living people who helped that genius work can disappear. Holmboe's teaching, fellow professors' financial help, Crelle's journal, international readers, and Christine Kemp's presence are not incidental decorations around a quintic theorem. They are evidence of the network in which his mathematics had a chance to exist.

The fifth-degree equation is a useful final image. It asks whether more ingenuity will eventually produce an ever more intricate formula. Abel's answer says that ingenuity sometimes consists in proving that the entire proposed kind of answer cannot exist. From a small and financially strained Norwegian university to the mathematical capitals of Europe, he made that distinction lasting. His life invites admiration, but also a more demanding thought: how much of the history of mathematics depends on whether a young person can afford the journey, find an editor, receive a reply, and remain alive long enough to be heard?

## Historical companion (not narrated)

### Chronology and evidence cautions
- **1802-1821:** Born in Norway within Denmark-Norway; childhood in Gjerstad during wartime scarcity and the 1814 political settlement; Cathedral School in Christiania, Holmboe's teaching, father's death and family financial difficulties; admission to university.
- **1824-1827:** Compressed early quintic publication; overseas grant, Berlin meeting with Crelle, beginning of *Crelle's Journal* (1826), Paris submission of a major memoir in October 1826; return to Christiania in 1827.
- **1827-1829:** Research on elliptic functions and infinite series; economic difficulties, engagement to Christine Kemp; tuberculosis and death in Froland, 6 April 1829.
- **1830 and later:** Paris Academy prize shared with Jacobi; collected works edited by Holmboe; later publication of Paris memoir; Abel Prize first awarded in 2003.

The general quintic is **not** the claim that every quintic is individually unsolvable by radicals. Ruffini attempted earlier impossibility arguments; Galois developed a general solvability criterion afterward. Treat the Paris manuscript's handling as an institutional history with documentary uncertainty, not a single confirmed story of deliberate suppression. Both Abel and Jacobi transformed elliptic-function theory; their work was independent and sometimes concurrent.

### Mathematical display companion
- General polynomial: \(x^5+a_4 x^4+a_3 x^3+a_2 x^2+a_1 x+a_0=0\). No universal formula in the coefficients by arithmetic and radicals solves all such quintics.
- Inversion perspective: after defining \(u=\int_0^x dt/\sqrt{(1-t^2)(1-k^2t^2)}\), study \(x\) as a function of \(u\), rather than only the integral.
- Elliptic functions are meromorphic functions on the complex plane admitting two independent periods. This is a later standard formulation, not a transcription of Abel's notation.
- Abelian limit example: if \(\sum_{n\ge 0} a_n\) converges to \(S\), then under the usual one-sided approach \(\lim_{r\uparrow1}\sum a_n r^n=S\). Converse statements require extra hypotheses.
