# 091000 - Oscar Zariski: From a borderland childhood to the foundations of algebraic geometry

**Track:** History - People and ideas. **Focus:** Kobrin and Kyiv during war and revolution, the Roman school of algebraic geometry, name and migration, Johns Hopkins, the transformation of the subject, São Paulo, Harvard, family, and students. **Background:** an intuitive understanding of equations; algebraic concepts are explained in speech.

What does it mean to say that a picture has a rigorous foundation? A mathematician might draw a curve, notice where it crosses itself, make a clever geometric argument, and reach a convincing conclusion. But can the argument survive when the curve has more dimensions than one can picture, when a surface develops a singularity, or when its equations are studied over a field very different from the real numbers? Oscar Zariski spent much of his career asking those questions. He was not a person who had inherited a neat place within the mathematical establishment. He had crossed a continent shaken by war and revolution, changed his name, and moved again when a European political climate deteriorated. His new language of algebraic geometry grew from both an admiration for the old geometric imagination and a conviction that the old proofs could not support everything mathematicians wanted them to do.

He was born in eighteen ninety-nine, in the town of Kobrin, then part of the Russian Empire, now in Belarus. The correction matters because the political map of his childhood was not the map of Europe familiar today. His family was Jewish. His parents named him Ascher Zaritsky. His father was Bezalel, and his mother Hannah took particular care with the boy's education. A tutor helped him develop an early command of language and arithmetic. The family was not simply living in an abstract borderland called Eastern Europe. They belonged to a community exposed to the upheavals of imperial rule, nationalist conflict, revolution, and anti-Jewish violence.

The First World War brought fighting into the region and forced the family away from Kobrin toward Chernigov, in what is now Ukraine. Ascher went to Kyiv for university study. The city underwent repeated changes of military and political control during the revolutionary years. These events made ordinary academic plans precarious. He had hoped to study mathematics directly but entered philosophy when the mathematics places were unavailable, while continuing to pursue mathematical ideas. Such a decision is easy to read backward as a charming sign of independence. In the moment it was a practical adjustment made within a city where universities and everyday life were repeatedly disrupted.

By nineteen twenty, the political instability had convinced him to leave. He continued his education in Italy, moving through the university world of Pisa and Rome. In Rome he entered a famous school of algebraic geometry associated with Guido Castelnuovo, Federigo Enriques, and Francesco Severi. Italian algebraic geometers possessed a remarkable repertoire of geometric intuition. They could reason about algebraic curves and surfaces using families of lines, intersections, transformations, and delicate diagrams. They often saw relationships long before the available algebraic foundations could justify every step. To a young researcher, this intellectual culture was a kind of apprenticeship in mathematical imagination.

Zariski completed a doctorate at the University of Rome in nineteen twenty-four on a question connected with Galois theory. That same year he married Yole Cagli, whom he had met in Italy; they traveled back to Kobrin for the marriage. The personal journey intersected with a change of public identity. Enriques encouraged the young Ascher Zaritsky to adopt the Italian-sounding professional name Oscar Zariski. The name appeared on a joint publication and remained with him throughout his career. The new name is often told as a biographical curiosity. It also says something about how academic belonging, migration, language, and the wish to be recognized in a new country intersected in the life of a Jewish scholar.

Zariski's relationship with his Italian teachers was affectionate and intellectually complicated. He learned much from them and later praised their inventiveness. Yet even during his Roman years, Castelnuovo recognized what he called Zariski's algebraic tendency. The young mathematician was interested not only in whether a geometric statement appeared true, but in which algebraic facts made it true. He would eventually try to reconstruct the subject on foundations that worked beyond the geometric examples from which its classical methods had grown. This was not contempt for his teachers. It was an argument over how to preserve the best parts of their work by making them dependable.

Italian political life was also changing. Mussolini took power in the early nineteen twenties, gradually destroying representative institutions and imposing fascist control. Zariski's decision to leave Italy in nineteen twenty-seven had several causes, including the opportunity to work with American mathematicians and the worsening political environment. We should not insert the later Italian racial laws of nineteen thirty-eight into a departure that occurred eleven years earlier. That would make the chronology false. The young scholar had already experienced the instability and violence of Eastern Europe; another authoritarian setting offered little reason to build a permanent future there.

Solomon Lefschetz helped him reach the United States. Zariski joined Johns Hopkins University in Baltimore, where he would spend much of the next two decades. Lefschetz, based at Princeton, became an important mentor and interlocutor. His methods in topology connected algebraic varieties to the study of shapes and their holes. Zariski traveled to discuss such work with him, learning how geometric questions could be recast by studying invariants that remain unchanged under continuous deformation. The Baltimore years were intellectually productive, though the economic depression made academic security a complicated matter. He and Yole also built a family, eventually raising a daughter and a son. The question of belonging had moved from visas and university appointments into the everyday work of sustaining a household.

A key moment came with Zariski's book Algebraic Surfaces, published in nineteen thirty-five. Writing a systematic account forced him to examine claims that had circulated within the Italian school. He did not decide that its mathematics was worthless. He came to believe that its most powerful results demanded a reconstruction. In later recollections he described his discomfort with gaps in arguments, even while maintaining his admiration for the geometric spirit behind them. The decision changed the next phase of his career. He would employ the machinery of modern algebra, including ideas associated with Emmy Noether and Bartel van der Waerden, to make the geometry of polynomial equations more exact.

Consider a simple plane curve given by an equation such as y squared equals x cubed. Near the origin, that curve has a cusp, a point where ordinary tangent-line intuition becomes delicate. A smooth circle and a cusped curve may both be described by one polynomial equation, but the local algebra around a point behaves differently. A nonsingular point has local coordinates that vary regularly; at a singular point the equations can lose rank or develop multiple branches. Algebraic geometry needs a way to recognize such differences without relying on a hand-drawn picture. Local rings, ideals, valuations, and integral dependence provide that language. The particular example is modern exposition, not a reconstruction of Zariski's lecture notes.

Zariski worked in the late nineteen thirties and early nineteen forties on these algebraic foundations. He investigated local uniformization and problems of resolving singularities. Roughly, to resolve a singularity is to replace a difficult geometric object by a related one that is smoother, while retaining enough of the original geometry to answer questions about it. One important technique is a blow-up: instead of treating all directions through a point as indistinguishable, the construction separates directional information. In an elementary picture, what had been a single troublesome crossing can be replaced by a configuration where the branches are easier to study. In higher dimensions and over general fields the actual mathematics becomes far more demanding. Zariski established major results, including work on three-dimensional varieties in characteristic zero, but he did not solve every resolution problem. Subsequent mathematicians, including Heisuke Hironaka, made further decisive advances.

The symbolic language can seem to have displaced the picture. In fact, it supplied a way to ask geometric questions with greater reach. Suppose a polynomial vanishes at a set of points. The equations that vanish on the same set form an ideal. Studying inclusion among such ideals gives a means of describing which algebraic sets sit inside others. In what is now called the Zariski topology, closed sets are defined by polynomial equations. This topology behaves very differently from ordinary distance-based topology: it is generally much coarser. Yet it records exactly the algebraic structure that matters for many questions about varieties. The name honors his role in developing the approach, while the intellectual movement included many others.

War entered his life again through the news from Europe. Nazi persecution and mass murder affected his extended family in Kobrin. A Harvard account of his life records that he learned during his nineteen forty-five visit to São Paulo that relatives had been killed. Family trauma should not be manufactured into an explanation of a proof, but it belongs in the biography. He had left Europe to study and build a career; people he knew had remained in places that the war later engulfed. The safety of the United States did not cancel the losses across the Atlantic.

São Paulo also provided an episode that captures Zariski's mathematical style. He gave a course whose regular audience, according to later accounts, consisted essentially of André Weil. Lecturer and listener argued over the material and learned from their exchanges. To an administrator counting attendance, the event might have appeared unsuccessful. To the two mathematicians, it created an extended conversation between different approaches to the same field. Weil had his own program for foundations in arithmetic and geometry. Zariski had his program for local algebra and singularities. Their exchanges illustrate how high-level mathematics can advance through sustained disagreement rather than through consensus at the beginning.

After a period at the University of Illinois, Zariski joined Harvard in nineteen forty-seven. There he helped transform algebraic geometry in the United States through research, graduate training, seminars, editing, and academic appointments. He served as mathematics department chair for part of the late nineteen fifties. His students included Hironaka and David Mumford, who would produce major work of their own. The line of influence did not run one way. The younger researchers developed new methods and questions, and their achievements should not be treated as if they were merely delayed consequences of Zariski's instructions.

Together with Pierre Samuel, Zariski wrote the two-volume Commutative Algebra, with volumes appearing in nineteen fifty-eight and nineteen sixty. It helped make abstract algebra a practical instrument for geometers. The word commutative matters here. In a commutative ring, multiplying a by b gives the same result as multiplying b by a. Polynomial rings have that property, and ideals within them encode systems of equations. Localizing a ring allows us to examine behavior near a chosen point or prime ideal. These technical moves turn questions about curves, surfaces, and varieties into questions that can be solved using algebra. In the new language, intuition and proof could work together rather than compete as rival philosophies.

Zariski received major honors, including a Cole Prize, the United States National Medal of Science, and later distinctions from mathematical societies. He remained active in professional service and publishing. His later work included questions of equisingularity: how a family of geometric objects may change without changing essential features of its singularities. His final years were affected by declining health. He died in Brookline, Massachusetts, in July nineteen eighty-six, with Yole and their family surviving him. By then the field he had helped reconstruct was central to number theory, geometry, and the mathematical ideas that would follow.

The word 'foundations' can sound like a rejection of everything that came before. Zariski's life offers a different interpretation. He learned from Italian geometers, from Lefschetz, from the modern algebra of Noether and van der Waerden, and from arguments with colleagues and students. He wanted classical geometric insight to become more reliable and more portable. His migration across languages and institutions was not a metaphor he invented for this mathematics; it was the real route his life took. At the end, the achievement was neither a perfect picture nor a page of symbols divorced from space. It was a new way for geometry and algebra to answer one another's questions.

## Historical companion (not narrated)

### Historical chronology

- **1899–1924:** born Ascher Zaritsky in Kobrin; wartime family flight; Kyiv studies amid revolutions and war; emigrated to Italy; doctorate and marriage to Yole Cagli in Rome in 1924.
- **1927–1944:** moved to Johns Hopkins with help from Solomon Lefschetz; *Algebraic Surfaces* in 1935; modern algebraic reconstruction and singularity results.
- **1945–1969:** São Paulo discussions with André Weil; University of Illinois; Harvard professor from 1947, chair in 1958–60; school of algebraic geometry.
- **1969–1986:** retired from Harvard; later mathematical work and honors; died in Brookline in 1986.

### Mathematical companion

For an algebraically closed field \(k\), an affine algebraic set in \(k^n\) is the common zero locus \(V(I)\) of an ideal \(I\subseteq k[x_1,\dots,x_n]\). The Zariski-closed subsets are polynomial zero sets. The algebraic geometry of \(V(I)\) is connected to the quotient ring \(k[x_1,\dots,x_n]/I\), with the radical of \(I\) important when distinguishing equations that define the same point set. Schemes and non-algebraically closed fields require a more general treatment.

A simple singular curve is \(y^2=x^3\). Its defining polynomial has both first partial derivatives zero at the origin; the origin is not a smooth point. Resolution methods study controlled modifications of a variety and the behavior of its local rings. Zariski's contributions to local uniformization and resolution should not be equated with the later, broader theorem of Hironaka.
