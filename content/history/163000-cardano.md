# 163000 — Girolamo Cardano: A secret, a wager, and the price of a reputation

**Track:** History — People and ideas. **Focus:** Renaissance medical practice, games of chance, the cubic equation, the Tartaglia–Cardano dispute, Lodovico Ferrari's quartic, family catastrophe, and the Roman Inquisition. **Background:** Basic algebra; every historical mathematical problem is explained in speech.

In March 1539, a mathematics teacher from Venice arrived at the house of a physician in Milan carrying something more valuable to him than a book or a purse of coins. He carried a rule. It could solve a kind of cubic equation that had defeated generations of European algebraists. He had used the rule to win a public contest, and he meant to publish it himself one day. The physician, Girolamo Cardano, wanted to learn it now.

The visitor was Niccolò Tartaglia. By his account, Cardano swore on the Gospels that he would not publish the secret. Tartaglia then revealed the procedure in the form of a poem: instructions for splitting a number into two parts, extracting cube roots, and reconstructing the unknown. Six years later, the rule appeared in Cardano's book, the Ars Magna. The book credited Tartaglia and another mathematician who had found part of the method earlier, but the credit did not erase the disagreement about Cardano's promise. Tartaglia was furious, and the quarrel would entangle a third mathematician, Lodovico Ferrari, in years of public challenges. The story is often compressed to a single question: did Cardano steal a formula? There is a more revealing question. What happened when a mathematical discovery could be held as a professional secret, and one of Europe's most ambitious writers believed that publishing it mattered more than keeping a promise?

Before deciding what to make of Cardano, we should meet the man who invited Tartaglia inside. He was born in Pavia in 1501, to Fazio Cardano and Chiara Micheri. His parents were not yet married. In Renaissance Italy, legitimacy could shape admission to professional bodies, the standing of a household, and the opportunities available to a child. His father practiced law in Milan and taught mathematics. His mother had suffered losses during an epidemic. Cardano later wrote about his childhood, illnesses, family conflicts, ambitions, and failings with an autobiographer's extraordinary appetite for detail. These pages offer rare access to his own voice, but they are not a transparent window into every event. Cardano was making a record of his life, and sometimes a defense of it, at the same time.

His father wanted him to study law. Girolamo chose medicine. He began at Pavia in 1520, then moved to Padua after war disrupted university life. We should picture a Renaissance medical student not as someone memorizing modern pharmacology, but as someone studying ancient medical authorities, anatomy, diagnosis, philosophical accounts of nature, and the habits of learned argument. A university degree might qualify a physician to address wealthy clients, but permission to practice in a city depended on powerful professional institutions. Cardano completed his medical education in the mid-1520s, married Lucia Bandareni in 1531, and struggled for years to establish himself.

The College of Physicians in Milan repeatedly refused to admit him. Cardano's birth outside marriage was one impediment; his combative personality and relations with the city's medical establishment were others. From a distance, this can sound like a minor professional dispute. For a physician trying to support a family, it meant access to patients, income, status, and a reliable career. He practiced near Padua, moved back toward Milan, and taught mathematics at the Piatti school, a charitable institution that his father had also served. The man later celebrated for the great algebra book was for a time a physician whose formal standing was in question and a mathematics instructor whose income was precarious.

Cardano also gambled. He played dice, cards, and chess. In some periods, by his own telling and the accounts built from it, play consumed money and time he could not easily spare. The point is not to diagnose him from five centuries away. It is to notice that gambling forced an intensely practical mathematical problem onto his attention. At a gaming table, knowing which outcomes are possible is not the same as knowing which outcomes are likely. If there is money at stake, the difference matters immediately.

Take two ordinary six-sided dice. There are thirty-six ordered combinations if the dice are fair and distinguishable. The total seven can occur in six ways: one and six, two and five, three and four, and their reversals. The total two occurs in just one way. Both totals are possible, but they are not equally likely. To a practiced gambler, the difference is a matter of winnings and losses. To a mathematician, it suggests a systematic way to count chances: list equally possible elementary outcomes, then count how many produce the event of interest. Cardano calculated distributions of dice totals, and he left a treatise on games of chance that would be printed long after his death, in 1663.

There is an instructive limit here. It is tempting to make him the sole founder of probability and say that modern probability theory started at his gaming table. Problems about chance, gambling, and dividing stakes already circulated in different traditions. Cardano's work also contains mistakes, and its late publication meant it did not guide the better-known seventeenth-century development of the subject as a published textbook might have done. Still, the attempt is striking. A man who hoped to win money by playing games also treated the game's uncertain outcomes as things that could be counted and reasoned about. His mathematics grew out of a life he was not always able to control.

There is an irony in the way we remember the two men who would dispute the cubic. Tartaglia was the poorer teacher who kept a mathematical secret partly because it could bring him income. Cardano was the physician and gambler who became the author of an influential public book. Neither was dealing with pure knowledge sealed away from social pressures. They lived among examinations, wagers, patronage, contracts, printers, quarrels, and careers. To understand the cubic controversy, we need to understand why solving the equation was so difficult, and why possessing the solution could change a mathematician's prospects.

A quadratic equation, such as a square plus three times the unknown equals ten, can be solved by completing a square. Medieval and Renaissance algebraists knew powerful rules for second-degree equations, though they often organized cases differently from modern textbooks. The cubic adds a third power: the unknown multiplied by itself three times. Take the equation, a cube plus three times the unknown equals four. In modern symbols it would be written x cubed plus three x equals four. Here the answer happens to be one. But discovering that one works is not the same as having a general rule for solving any equation of that form. Change the four to an inconvenient number and guessing rapidly becomes inadequate.

The notation also matters. Sixteenth-century algebraists did not typically write a single equation with arbitrary positive and negative coefficients and treat it as one unified object. A cube and a certain number of unknowns equaling a number was a distinct case from a cube equaling a certain number of unknowns plus a number. Negative roots and negative coefficients did not have the same ready status that they have in modern algebra. A procedure for one case did not automatically count as a procedure for all the others. The mathematical breakthrough was therefore both a new calculation and a new way of seeing related cases.

Earlier in the century, the Bolognese mathematician Scipione del Ferro had found a method for one important kind of cubic. He did not publish it in the form later associated with Cardano. Information about the method passed to Antonio Maria Fior. Tartaglia, working in Venice, had been challenged to a public mathematical contest with Fior in 1535. The participants exchanged thirty problems each, with reputations and agreed stakes at issue. Fior depended heavily on the kind of equation whose solution had come to him from del Ferro. Tartaglia developed a general method for that case in time, and contemporary accounts of the contest say that he answered all thirty of Fior's questions. The public performance made his reputation. His discovery was not merely the ability to find a root. It was the ability to produce a rule that worked again and again.

Cardano heard of the contest and pursued Tartaglia. By 1539 he was an ambitious physician, teacher, and author, beginning to find recognition in Milan. He proposed publishing the rule under Tartaglia's name, according to the correspondence preserved in Tartaglia's later account. Tartaglia declined. He planned to publish his own discovery himself. Cardano kept asking. He also dangled the possibility of an introduction to Alfonso d'Avalos, a powerful governor and patron. For a Venetian mathematics teacher, this mattered. Court connections could be worth much more than an admiring comment in a learned book.

The eventual visit took place in Milan in March. The governor was away, and Tartaglia found himself negotiating directly with Cardano. The wording of their conversation comes to us chiefly through Tartaglia, who later printed his side of the dispute. That does not make it worthless; it means we should distinguish an attested, partisan report from a recording of an objective conversation. Tartaglia said that Cardano swore not to publish his discoveries and would even encode the notes to preserve the secret. The teaching followed in a poem. Cardano now possessed the rule, but possession was only the beginning of the next mathematical struggle.

Let us work through a modern reconstruction of the insight. It will explain why a handful of rhymed instructions could be so valuable. Return to our equation: a cube plus three times the unknown equals four. Pretend the unknown can be expressed as one number minus another, call them u and v. Expand the cube of u minus v. You obtain u cubed minus v cubed, minus three times u v times u minus v. That last difference is the original unknown. Now require u times v to equal one. Then the subtraction of three u v times the unknown cancels the added three times the unknown. Everything difficult collapses into a simpler condition: u cubed minus v cubed must equal four.

We therefore need two numbers whose product is one and whose cubes differ by four. Instead of seeking u and v directly, look at their cubes. Call those two cubes A and B. Their difference must be four, and their product must be one, because u v equals one. From the familiar identity that the square of a sum equals the square of a difference plus four times the product, we discover that the sum of A and B must be the square root of twenty. So A is two plus the square root of five, and B is the square root of five minus two. Take their cube roots and subtract. The result is exactly one.

If you follow that arithmetic closely, you will see why the formula feels like a magician's trick when it is first encountered. We changed the unknown into a difference. That created an extra product term. We chose the product so that the troublesome linear term disappeared. Then two equations about the cubes of the new numbers reduced the problem to a square root followed by cube roots. The solution is not an inspired guess that happens to work for one equation. It is a general strategy: transform an equation into a form where a hidden symmetry makes it solvable.

For a general equation in which x cubed plus p times x equals q, the same approach sets x equal to u minus v and chooses three times u v equal to p. We then require u cubed minus v cubed equal to q. The two cubes have a known difference and a known product, so they can be found by a quadratic calculation. Our spoken example used friendly numbers, but the mechanism extends to any positive p and q in the relevant case. Other signs require different algebraic handling. It is possible to work from the same insight to a broad solution of the depressed cubic, meaning a cubic with no square term.

A general cubic can also contain a square term. Why is its absence not fatal to a general theory? Because we can shift the unknown. If x is replaced by a new unknown plus or minus a suitable constant, the cube expansion produces a square term whose coefficient we can arrange to cancel the original square term. In today's notation, for a cubic with a squared coefficient a, the substitution x equals y minus a divided by three removes the square term. The result is a depressed cubic, which the new methods can attack. Cardano and Ferrari developed the theory of these reductions and cases in ways not contained in the original secret poem. This is one reason the Ars Magna was more than a transcript of an overheard rule.

But the rule did not work smoothly in every case that mattered. Cardano tried applying it to equations with several real solutions, and sometimes his calculation demanded the square root of a negative number. In August 1539, he wrote to Tartaglia describing precisely this kind of obstruction. Tartaglia did not simply supply the missing theory. Their correspondence was already strained. To a modern mathematician, a square root of a negative number invites the complex numbers. To these algebraists, it was a demand that the current understanding of permissible magnitudes had not yet learned how to satisfy.

Consider a small example different from our first one. The equation x cubed equals fifteen times x plus four has the real solution four. You can verify it: four cubed is sixty-four, and fifteen times four plus four is also sixty-four. In fact the equation has three real solutions. Yet the standard radical expression, when arranged in the usual way, asks for a square root of negative one hundred twenty-one. How can the route to a real number pass through a non-real expression? Neither Tartaglia nor Cardano possessed the mature language of complex arithmetic that we now use to answer this. Later in the century Rafael Bombelli would advance the calculations of such expressions. With modern notation, two plus eleven times the imaginary unit and two minus eleven times the imaginary unit are cubes of two plus the imaginary unit and two minus the imaginary unit. Their cube roots add to four. A real result emerges from a pair of complex intermediate terms.

That example is not a claim that Cardano wrote down Bombelli's later solution or understood the modern complex plane. It shows the unfinished territory opened by the cubic. The formula was a breakthrough, but it was also a challenge to the existing conception of number. Algebra was moving toward methods that would be useful before their meaning was fully settled. Cardano's book is valuable partly because it exposes that frontier instead of hiding every awkward case.

We have not yet met the third man properly. Lodovico Ferrari entered Cardano's household as a young servant and became his assistant, student, and formidable mathematical collaborator. Ferrari is often reduced to a name in the cubic dispute, but he solved a problem that is in some respects even more surprising: the general fourth-degree equation, or quartic. In outline, his method rearranged a quartic into something that could be made into two squares, with a cleverly chosen additional quantity. The condition that makes the squares fit turns out to be a cubic equation. Solving the quartic therefore depends on the cubic. Once the cubic became accessible, the fourth degree came within reach.

This connection gives the ethical dispute its sharpest edge. Cardano could not publish Ferrari's achievement as a complete, intelligible method without also making the cubic method available. A private promise about one rule now stood in the way of a much larger public mathematical exposition. That does not dissolve the promise. It explains why the decision was not merely whether to copy a line of verse. Cardano was choosing how to assemble a network of results into a book that other mathematicians could study, extend, and criticize.

In 1543, Cardano and Ferrari visited Bologna and encountered evidence of del Ferro's earlier solution, preserved through his successor and relative Annibale della Nave. Ferrari later described seeing a written account in del Ferro's hand. The two visitors took this as evidence that Tartaglia was not the first discoverer of the method for that kind of cubic. Cardano treated the finding as grounds for publication. From Tartaglia's perspective, the source of the idea was not the only issue: Cardano had made a promise to him. We can understand why Cardano believed the history of discovery had changed the matter, and also why Tartaglia regarded the obligation as unaltered.

When the Ars Magna appeared in 1545, it presented procedures for cubic and quartic equations with named acknowledgments. Credit was given to del Ferro and Tartaglia for the cubic and to Ferrari for the quartic. That is a crucial fact, because the conventional phrase 'Cardano stole Tartaglia's formula' can suggest that the book erased the discovery and claimed it as Cardano's own. It did not. Another crucial fact is that giving credit in print does not necessarily excuse the publication of an entrusted secret. Modern research norms make public knowledge a central ideal, but those norms were not fully in place in the world of mathematical challenges, private teaching, and patronage. The harm in Tartaglia's account lay in the broken undertaking and the loss of control over when and how his result would appear.

Tartaglia published his grievance in 1546. Ferrari replied through a sequence of printed challenges, and the controversy eventually produced a face-to-face disputation in Milan in August 1548. Cardano did not take Tartaglia's proposed place as opponent. Ferrari did. Accounts of the contest make Ferrari the winner and describe Tartaglia's departure after the first day; the surviving records of the challenges and replies are not the simple transcript of a modern scored examination. The debate was one more event in which mathematical skill, personal reputation, patronage, and public theatre became impossible to separate.

The book meanwhile became part of the mathematical infrastructure. Mathematicians could work from rules in a printed text rather than depend on an expert willing to reveal a secret. This is an important turning point without requiring us to call Cardano a hero. The legitimate grievance and the wider dissemination of knowledge can both be real. The Ars Magna did not make all cubics painless, settle the meaning of complex numbers, or end algebraic controversy. It made a body of discoveries available for a different kind of collective work.

Cardano's own life did not remain centered on algebra. He was becoming celebrated as a physician. In 1552 he traveled to Scotland to treat John Hamilton, the Archbishop of St Andrews, who had suffered serious respiratory illness. The physician who had once struggled to join Milan's College of Physicians could now be called across Europe. He stayed in Edinburgh for months and returned with substantial payment and greater international reputation. The medical details belong to the sixteenth century; we should not translate a reported treatment into a modern clinical trial or assert a cure by present-day standards. The point is the magnitude of the social reversal. His authority had become valuable enough to carry him beyond the Italian states.

The same mind that sought algebraic formulas wrote on natural philosophy, medicine, astrology, mechanical devices, and the problem of how a human being should live with chance. Astrology was not a quaint hobby tacked onto an otherwise modern scientist. It belonged to the intellectual system through which Cardano, like many Renaissance scholars, tried to relate celestial motions, natural causes, medicine, and personal fate. A biography that deletes it produces a cleaner mathematician than the one who lived. A biography that treats it as if it were reliable scientific prediction produces a distorted picture in the opposite direction. Cardano inhabited a time in which the boundary between legitimate natural philosophy and forbidden divination was contested, and that contest would eventually threaten his career.

In his private life, notoriety did not protect him from catastrophe. His eldest son, Giovanni Battista, had trained as a physician. He married Brandona Seroni despite his father's opposition. The marriage became a crisis; Giovanni was charged with murdering his wife and was executed in 1560. Accounts of the family's disputes survive through sources with strong biases, including Cardano's own writings. We can say that the murder case and execution shattered his family and standing. We cannot responsibly reproduce every accusation made against Giovanni's wife as a verified explanation for the crime. The historical record contains people whom the famous mathematician described angrily and who did not have equal power to leave an account of their own.

Cardano moved to Bologna and became professor of medicine. The appointment looks like a professional triumph, but the years were marked by conflict. His son Aldo caused further trouble, including theft and debts in the accounts Cardano left. Relationships with other professors deteriorated. It would be easy to make these events into a moral formula: a man who risked too much lost everything. Real lives are not equations of that sort. Illness, institutional jealousy, family choices, legal judgments, and the workings of state and church authority cannot be reduced to a single defect of character.

In October 1570, the Inquisition arrested Cardano in Bologna. For a long time a common explanation of the trial was that he had recklessly prepared a horoscope for Jesus Christ. The surviving inquisitorial documents, studied in recent scholarship, show a more complex investigation. His encyclopedic writing about natural causes, celestial influence, miracles, and the boundaries of divine action was at issue. The Roman Inquisition was increasingly policing religious thought in the aftermath of the Council of Trent, and Cardano's public fame did not make him immune. He remained imprisoned until December and was required in 1571 to abjure views for which he was judged vehemently suspect of heresy. He lost his professorship and faced restrictions on teaching and publishing.

Imagine what this meant to someone whose life's work had included placing ideas into print. Algebraic rules, medical arguments, natural philosophy, astrology, autobiography: his professional identity had been assembled through writing, teaching, and disputation. After the trial, publishing was no longer simply a matter of persuading a printer or satisfying his critics. The institutions with coercive power over him could determine what he was permitted to say. That is a different kind of limit from the one faced by a mathematician working on an equation. You cannot prove a doctrine acceptable to an inquisitor by showing that an identity is algebraically true.

Cardano went to Rome and spent his final years there. He wrote the autobiography through which much of his personality has come down to us: a record crowded with misfortunes, professional battles, achievements, disappointments, calculations, and judgments of his own character. He died in 1576. Sources disagree over the precise day in September, a distinction that does not change this account. The autobiography itself would only be published in 1643, long after he could answer readers' questions about it.

What, then, is his legacy? There are several, and they do not fit into one moral verdict. He helped turn the solution of higher-degree equations from a collection of guarded techniques into a shared printed mathematical subject. His collaboration with Ferrari moved from the cubic to the quartic. His investigations of chance revealed how a gambling problem could invite mathematical enumeration while also showing the limits of early probability. His medical career demonstrates how learned expertise and social permission could be separated. His arrest shows a writer and thinker confronting a political and religious authority that mathematics by itself could not disarm.

Return to the visit in Milan. Tartaglia was not wrong to care about a promise, and Cardano was not wrong to see that mathematics could become greater when ideas were written down and made accessible. The historical choice was more tangled: one person's trusted disclosure, another person's plan to publish, a newly revealed predecessor, a brilliant assistant, and a professional culture in which a rule could mean the difference between obscurity and appointment. The cubic formula survived the quarrel because other people were eventually able to read it, verify it, and use it. The quarrel survived because the people involved could not be replaced by symbols. Cardano's life holds both truths at once. An equation can be solved by finding the right transformation. The human question of what one owes to the person who first told you how to solve it remains harder.

## Historical and mathematical companion — not spoken

### A chronology that separates testimony from reconstruction

- **1501, Pavia:** Cardano is born to Fazio Cardano and Chiara Micheri. His parents marry much later.
- **1520s, Pavia and Padua:** He studies medicine; sources place the final doctorate in 1525 or 1526. War disrupts the university at Pavia.
- **1531–1539, Saccolongo, Gallarate, Milan:** Marriage to Lucia, practice, Piatti mathematics teaching, and eventual admission to Milan's College of Physicians in 1539.
- **25 March 1539, Milan:** Tartaglia discloses the cubic method to Cardano. The secrecy oath is preserved in Tartaglia's retrospective printed account; this is not a neutral stenographic record.
- **1540–1543, Milan and Bologna:** Cardano and Ferrari develop the cubic/quartic material; in 1543 Ferrari's later account places their inspection of del Ferro's prior method in Bologna.
- **1545, Italy:** *Ars Magna* is published and explicitly credits del Ferro, Tartaglia and Ferrari while making the methods public.
- **1552, Edinburgh:** Cardano treats John Hamilton, Archbishop of St Andrews; the journey is a documented change of geography, not simply a patronage connection.
- **1560, northern Italy:** His son Giovanni Battista is executed following a murder conviction.
- **1562–1570, Bologna:** Cardano holds a medical professorship and becomes the subject of an inquisitorial investigation and imprisonment in 1570.
- **1571–1576, Rome:** After a formal abjuration and restrictions on teaching/publishing, he resides in Rome; his autobiography is written near the end of his life and published posthumously in 1643.

### Cubic mechanics in modern notation

For a depressed cubic of the form

\[
x^3+px=q,\quad p>0,\ q>0,
\]

put \(x=u-v\) and demand \(uv=p/3\). The expansion gives

\[
(u-v)^3+p(u-v)=u^3-v^3+(p-3uv)(u-v)=u^3-v^3=q.
\]

Let \(A=u^3, B=v^3\). Then \(A-B=q\) and \(AB=(p/3)^3\). Since \((A+B)^2=q^2+4AB\),

\[
A=\frac q2+\sqrt{\left(\frac q2\right)^2+\left(\frac p3\right)^3},\qquad
B=-\frac q2+\sqrt{\left(\frac q2\right)^2+\left(\frac p3\right)^3}.
\]

The real solution in this case is \(x=\sqrt[3]{A}-\sqrt[3]{B}\). In the narrated example \(p=3,q=4\), so \(A=2+\sqrt5\), \(B=\sqrt5-2\), and \(x=1\).

For a full cubic \(x^3+ax^2+bx+c=0\), set \(x=y-a/3\). The quadratic term vanishes; the reduced equation is \(y^3+py+q=0\), where \(p=b-a^2/3\) and \(q=2a^3/27-ab/3+c\).

**The irreducible case:** \(x^3=15x+4\) has roots \(4,-2+\sqrt3,-2-\sqrt3\), all real. The formal radical expression \(\sqrt[3]{2+11i}+\sqrt[3]{2-11i}\) equals \(4\), because \((2+i)^3=2+11i\). This is a **modern reconstruction using Bombelli's later complex arithmetic**, not a solution method Cardano possessed in this form.

### Probability and its limits

Two distinguishable fair dice have 36 equiprobable ordered outcomes, of which six sum to seven. This is an explanatory example of enumeration, not a claim that Cardano introduced the modern sample-space formalism. *Liber de ludo aleae*, printed in 1663, contains both correct combinatorial results and errors; because it circulated publicly so late, historians distinguish its originality from its direct influence on Pascal, Fermat, and Huygens.

### Source disputes and reliability

- **Oath and accusation:** Tartaglia's *Quesiti* and the correspondence as preserved in historical transcriptions provide his position. The promise matters; *Ars Magna* nevertheless names its mathematical predecessors.
- **Date conflicts:** The doctorate is placed in **1525** by MacTutor and **1526** by the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy. The day of Cardano's death differs between those sources (**21** versus **20 September 1576**). The narration states only the well-established years.
- **Inquisition:** The Stanford Encyclopedia's investigation of archival research rejects the once-standard one-cause explanation that the trial was simply punishment for a horoscope of Jesus. It describes issues arising from *De rerum varietate*, natural causation, and theology.
- **Family testimony:** Cardano's allegations against his daughter-in-law, members of his household, and adversaries are testimony from a party to those disputes, not independent verdicts.
- **Chance treatise:** Scholarship disagrees about its date of drafting and revision; the date of **posthumous publication, 1663**, is secure.
