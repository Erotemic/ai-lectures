# 053500 — Leonhard Euler II: Berlin, late Petersburg, and the machinery of mathematics

**Track:** History · People and ideas. **Focus:** royal patronage, academy administration, education, scientific disputes, named collaborators and mature analysis. **Background:** historical curiosity; examples introduced as needed.

In 1741 Leonhard Euler arrived in Berlin with a reputation already built through the Swiss Bernoulli circle, work for the Saint Petersburg Academy, and results in mechanics and the theory of numbers. He was thirty-four years old and entering an unfamiliar court. He brought not only methods of calculation but a wife, children, institutional obligations, and established relationships with Russian colleagues. His next quarter-century would show how productive mathematical research could be both encouraged and constrained by a royal academy. His final years would reveal another side of the same story: science made by a severely visually impaired scholar working within a household and an international team.

Frederick the Second had invited him to Berlin. Political insecurity for foreign scholars in Russia was one factor in his willingness to leave. Euler arrived in Berlin in seventeen forty-one. The king intended to strengthen the academy as an emblem of Prussia's place among European powers. Pierre-Louis Moreau de Maupertuis became its president; Euler led its mathematical work. The two men had an important working friendship, though academic alliances could also become political liabilities when disputes reached the royal court.

An academy post in Berlin was not a modern research-only position. Euler supervised an observatory, arranged publications, assisted with maps and calendars that helped finance the institution, and worked on practical questions involving pumps, waterways, navigation, ballistics, insurance, and pensions. A king who supported mathematics expected useful advice. Euler could therefore be summoned to answer a question about the hydraulics of a royal residence and then return to a problem about the motion of the moon. His extensive correspondence and published work were sustained by this surprisingly administrative career.

Frederick the Great wanted learning to help symbolize Prussia's ambitions. He also liked polished conversation, French literature, philosophical dispute, and the appearance of enlightened monarchy. Euler was a deeply committed Protestant, an analyst absorbed in precise technical work, and an administrator with definite opinions about how an academy should operate. It is too simple to make the resulting friction a cartoon about a rational king and an unworldly mathematician. Both men had practical interests, but they valued different ways of demonstrating intellectual authority. Court patronage could grant security, and court preference could withdraw it without the kind of transparent governance later researchers might expect.

The academy depended partly on enterprises that do not resemble research grants: the issuing of calendars and maps, supervision of instruments, and ordinary accounting. These publications were financial infrastructure. When accounts had to be balanced, Euler could not simply announce that a new result in analysis made bookkeeping irrelevant. The Berlin-Brandenburg Academy's archive records conflicts over precisely such administrative responsibilities. These disputes are evidence of the institutional role he occupied; they were not footnotes interrupting a pure mathematical career.

Berlin was also where Euler wrote some of his most influential textbooks. The Introductio in analysin infinitorum, published in seventeen forty-eight, helped organize the study of functions in a way later generations could learn. His books on differential calculus and the calculus of variations extended that educational enterprise. He developed arguments through series, curves, algebraic transformations, and carefully chosen examples. The symbols now associated with him were not all his inventions, and their modern meanings were not always fixed in his day. What mattered was the way he stabilized and spread a usable mathematical language. Students who never entered his academy could nevertheless learn to reason in a style that owed much to his books.

Euler's teaching did not stop with specialist readers. He wrote a long sequence of explanatory letters to a German princess about the physical world, astronomy, philosophy, and the principles by which knowledge is acquired. They were later published as the Letters to a German Princess. The titles of his technical papers alone would conceal this side of his career. He was not only producing solutions; he was addressing different audiences, from the university-trained analyst to a curious reader at court. His religious convictions and philosophical disagreements were also part of that teaching voice, even when modern readers find their conclusions disputable.

Those letters to a German princess deserve a closer look. Beginning in 1760, Euler addressed Princess Friederike Charlotte through a sustained correspondence, written in French, that eventually comprised 234 letters. He explained the movement of bodies, sound and light, the shape of the Earth, and the grounds on which one might trust a physical explanation. Later editors printed them in several volumes, and they found readers in multiple languages. The audience and teaching format were different from a calculus treatise. Euler could be both an inventive technical writer for specialists and a clear, persuasive teacher for people outside the academy. The princess was not merely a decorative dedication on a book; the exchange was part of a specific courtly educational relationship.

He also shared a public intellectual world with Maupertuis and d'Alembert. Disputes over principles of motion and how to state them grew into arguments about credit, methods, and authority. Maupertuis's strained position after the controversy over least action left Euler with difficult administrative duties. To credit Euler for the calculus of variations does not require saying that he invented the principle of least action out of nothing. The debate took place among researchers making different claims about physical explanation and the correct mathematical expression of mechanical laws. Euler's texts helped convert the question into a systematic subject, while Lagrange later developed powerful new techniques that changed it again.

Consider the optimization problem that links this scholarship to ordinary reasoning. A straight line connects two points with the shortest route in a flat plane. But a bent rod, a hanging chain, or a ray of light moving through different media can be governed by other quantities: elastic energy, gravitational energy, or travel time. The path that is selected need not minimize geometric distance. One can write an expression for the quantity being optimized by adding up contributions along a curve. Then imagine changing the curve slightly and ask whether the change in that total is zero to first order. The resulting differential equation is commonly called the Euler-Lagrange equation. The version taught today owes its name to the development of the subject across both men's work, rather than to one afternoon's discovery. The example is a modern explanation of why the subject matters, not a claim about a particular court engineering task.

Euler's fluid work offered a different kind of generality. A flowing fluid must be described at many positions at once; its pressure and velocity can change in space and time. An equation for a single particle is not enough to express how neighboring portions push on one another. In the 1750s Euler formulated equations for fluid motion that underlie the modern theory of ideal fluids. These equations omit viscosity and do not solve all the problems of turbulence, but they made precise the connection between local acceleration, pressure forces, and body forces. Their descendants appear in weather modeling, hydrodynamics, and aerodynamic calculation. This was a leap from his early study of mechanical systems, but it still reflected the eighteenth-century drive to express physical change with general mathematical relations.

The title of a scholar at court could hide several jobs. Euler inspected or advised on technical designs, administered people and publications, and helped manage the calendar privilege that brought money to the Berlin academy. Some papers were prompted by prize contests, others by requests from officials or by questions arriving in letters. The resulting work circulated among readers who might never meet him. An institutional appointment was not simply a seal of approval on completed mathematics; it could determine which questions were asked and which manuscripts could reach print.

His published treatments of optics and mechanics also crossed a boundary that had not yet hardened between physics and mathematics. On questions of light, Euler's preference for wave-like explanations stood against influential corpuscular pictures. He did not settle all of optics; later experiments and theories altered the debate. He was part of a European conversation in which experimental reports, mathematical models, and philosophical commitments pulled in different directions. The point is not to turn every eighteenth-century disagreement into a competition with a modern winner. It is to see why the same scholar who organized infinite series also cared intensely about natural philosophy.

Relationships with rulers and fellow scholars were not always easy. Frederick admired French Enlightenment culture and sometimes preferred the conversation of figures such as d'Alembert. Euler disagreed with d'Alembert on scientific questions. When Maupertuis died, Euler handled important academy business without receiving the presidential title; royal interference increasingly restricted the position. His departure from Berlin in seventeen sixty-six followed years of tension, not a sudden abandonment of a flourishing life. He accepted a new invitation from Catherine the Great and returned to Saint Petersburg, while maintaining the learned contacts that transcended both empires.

The move back to Petersburg in 1766 was not a return to the same institution he had left a quarter-century earlier. Russia was ruled by Catherine II, who sought European scholarly prestige and had reason to welcome a famous mathematical figure. Euler was now a veteran with an international publishing reputation; he could negotiate his work with different kinds of authority than the young immigrant of 1727. Yet he could not restore his eyesight or recover an uncomplicated domestic life. After his cataract operation in 1771, his vision became very limited. The fire that same year destroyed his house, but evidence about how specific papers were rescued differs among later accounts. A biography should not make an individual rescuer's heroism into a certain fact if the underlying sources are in tension.

The people around Euler require names and work, not a single label of assistants. Johann Albrecht Euler held an academy position of his own. Anders Johan Lexell was a capable astronomer and mathematician. Nicolas Fuss joined the household as an assistant, helped sustain the late research output, became part of the extended family, and later wrote an influential eulogy. Describing such work only as taking dictation would erase calculations, corrections, and the organization of publication. Describing it as proof that Euler himself contributed nothing after losing sight would be equally wrong. Scientific credit can acknowledge the central role of one researcher while making visible the collaborators who allowed a difficult project to continue.

Katharina Gsell died in 1773. Euler married Salome Abigail Gsell, her half-sister, in 1776. The second date is sometimes confused with the first wife's death, but the family records and the historical eulogy distinguish them. The family records show that only a few of the thirteen children reached adulthood; the register also preserves the children who died young. That pattern of family loss had been with him long before his final blindness. We cannot know all the feelings of a household from the tidy dates of a genealogy, but the record is enough to keep family and science in view together.

The third example concerns prime numbers. Every integer greater than one can be factored into primes. Euler used that structure to relate a sum over all positive integers to a product over primes: for a real exponent greater than one, the sum of the reciprocals of integers raised to that exponent equals a product, over primes, of one divided by one minus the reciprocal of that prime raised to the same exponent. To see why, imagine expanding each factor as a geometric series. A term in the resulting product selects a power of two, a power of three, and so on. Unique prime factorization makes every positive integer appear once. The identity joins analysis, with its convergent infinite sums, to arithmetic, with its indivisible building blocks. More than a century later, Riemann would carry related ideas into the complex plane and ask new questions about where a certain function becomes zero. The later questions were not already answered by Euler, but they grew in part from a language he helped develop.

The prime-number identity is worth working through in more detail. Start with the geometric progression one plus one half to a power, plus one quarter to the same power, and so on. It can be written as one divided by one minus two to the negative power, provided the exponent is large enough for the sum to converge. Repeat the construction for three, five, seven, and every other prime. Multiplying these expressions means making one choice of a power for each prime. Because an integer has one prime factorization, each possible reciprocal power of a positive integer appears exactly once in the formal expansion. The astonishing result is a product over primes that equals a sum over all positive integers. For real exponents greater than one, both sides converge in the ordinary sense. It is a clue that prime numbers are not merely isolated curiosities; together, they determine the multiplicative structure of the entire number system.

Euler also treated functions as objects one could transform and compare. In eighteenth-century analysis, a function often meant an analytic expression; the wider modern definition had not yet settled. His handling of exponential functions, logarithms, trigonometry and complex numbers created a durable working language. A familiar modern identity relates a complex exponential to cosine and sine. One can glimpse its meaning by expanding both as power series: the even powers of the imaginary unit produce alternating real terms, while the odd powers produce imaginary terms. The identity brings several separate techniques into a single statement. Yet the importance of Euler's analysis cannot be reduced to a memorable equation that is now printed on posters. It lay in building methods and books that generations of mathematicians could extend and repair where greater rigor was needed.

There were many other sides to the work: polyhedra and their vertices, edges, and faces; fluid motion and the equations governing it; differential equations describing vibrations; methods for finding curves that optimize a quantity; and new ways to teach trigonometry through functions. Each belonged to a different problem and a different scholarly conversation. It is misleading to treat them as ornaments attached to an isolated mind. Euler's mathematics developed within a network of Bernoullis, academy correspondents, skilled assistants, printers, patrons, students, and competitors. Even his notation and textbooks were forms of communication designed to outlive a particular calculation.

The archive of Euler's work makes the scale of that legacy concrete. Hundreds of works are catalogued under his name, and the posthumous publication history is substantial. Those counts do not measure his greatness by weight of paper, nor do they mean he worked without colleagues. They indicate how widely eighteenth-century mathematical knowledge moved through institutional presses, prize competitions, teaching texts, and correspondence. Euler helped build that system even while its finances and politics often frustrated him. To remember the scholar who crossed Basel, Petersburg, and Berlin is to remember both the extraordinary range of mathematical results and the human machinery that made it possible for those results to be written, read, and kept.

When Euler died, he had served two courts, crossed several political borders, survived serious illness and private losses, and worked through decades in which European science was being organized around increasingly permanent institutions. What joined his achievements was not a single theorem but a method of thinking: represent a changing physical or mathematical situation clearly enough that relationships can be calculated, tested, and taught. The next time an equation carries Euler's name, it is worth remembering the family in Saint Petersburg, the academy calendars in Berlin, the distant correspondents, and the people who helped his later manuscripts reach print. They were part of how that equation came to exist.

## Historical companion (not narrated)

### Historical chronology and interpretation
- **1741:** Euler reaches Berlin; the scientific academy grows under Frederick II and Maupertuis.
- **1740s–1760s:** Directs mathematical work and carries administrative responsibilities; produces the *Introductio* (1748), work on fluid dynamics and variation, and substantial educational writings.
- **1760–1762:** Writes the *Letters to a German Princess* to Friederike Charlotte, later issued in book form.
- **1766:** Returns to St Petersburg; mathematics continues through a collaboration involving Johann Albrecht Euler, Lexell and Fuss.
- **1771:** House fire and cataract operation, followed by severe visual disability.
- **1773 and 1776:** First wife Katharina dies; Euler marries Salome Abigail Gsell.
- **1783:** Dies in St Petersburg; manuscript publication continues afterward.

### Mathematics for the display companion
For a smooth functional \(J[y]=\int_a^b L(x,y,y')\,dx\), a fixed-endpoint stationary curve formally satisfies \(\frac{d}{dx}(\partial L/\partial y')-\partial L/\partial y=0\). A stationary path need not be a minimum. This formulation is modern, not a transcript of Euler's notation.

For an inviscid fluid with density \(\rho\), velocity \(v\), pressure \(p\) and body-force acceleration \(f\), the modern Euler momentum equation is \(\rho(\partial_t v+(v\cdot\nabla)v)=-\nabla p+\rho f\). This is not a Navier–Stokes equation with viscosity.

For real \(s>1\), \(\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}n^{-s}=\prod_{p\text{ prime}}(1-p^{-s})^{-1}\). The product requires the stated convergence range in this direct argument.
