# 049500 — Johannes Kepler II: Family, faith, witchcraft trial, and war

**Track:** History · People and ideas. **Theme:** Linz, Katharina Kepler’s legal defense, religious exclusion, the Thirty Years’ War, late publications and the third law. **Background:** Explains orbital periods, proportions, geometry, and a later Newtonian interpretation.

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

In sixteen eleven Johannes Kepler, who had revised the accepted picture of planetary orbits through a discrepancy of only eight minutes of arc in Tycho Brahe's observations, faced a succession of losses. His wife Barbara died, and one of their children died in the same period. The Habsburg court that had employed him was changing, and he soon left Prague for Linz. To tell the rest of Kepler's story only as the discovery of a third planetary law would be to conceal the demands of earning a salary, raising children, practicing a contested faith and protecting family members from prosecution. Those demands occupied a man who continued to write important books about the heavens.

Meanwhile Kepler had a life that could not be separated from war, family and institutional insecurity. He married Barbara Müller in fifteen ninety-seven; several of their children died young. Barbara died in sixteen eleven, and Kepler moved to Linz. He married Susanna Reuttinger in sixteen thirteen, and their household again grew. His positions demanded calendar making, horoscopes, official tables and correspondence as well as research. These were part of how a working mathematician secured an income in a period when astronomy was not a salaried research profession in the modern sense.

In Linz, Kepler worked for the Protestant estates of Upper Austria while Catholic dynastic authority pressed on the region. He remarried in sixteen thirteen, to Susanna Reuttinger. He was not merely a professor living safely in a sympathetic Lutheran community. His own pastors challenged his understanding of the Lord's Supper, and the church authorities excluded him from communion because he would not unreservedly accept the Formula of Concord. Scholars of his religious life describe sustained correspondence and appeals. The dispute mattered to him as a believer, and it narrowed the range of places in which he might find a secure position. It also resists an easy simplification: Kepler was not made irreligious by scientific inquiry. He remained committed to Christian faith while contesting the coercive boundaries drawn by particular churches.

His research in this period spread well beyond the modern list of three planetary laws. He developed methods for measuring the volumes of casks and other solids, wrote for readers who needed usable mathematical techniques, investigated logarithms, and worked on an extensive exposition of Copernican astronomy. A question about the volume of a wine barrel might sound minor beside the orbit of Mars; it was not minor for merchants, builders or those interested in how mathematics could describe variable shapes. Kepler's curiosity crossed the modern boundary between fundamental astronomy and practical calculation.

His interests also complicate the notion of a simple battle between science and superstition. Kepler cast horoscopes and discussed astrology in the intellectual and economic setting of his time. He criticized some ordinary astrological practices while pursuing other claims that do not belong to modern empirical astronomy. His search for cosmic harmony included musical ratios, geometrical patterns, and theological conviction. We can take the mathematical insight in his planetary laws seriously without endorsing every proposed cause or symbolic interpretation. That selective judgment is not a condescension to the past. It is what we do when we distinguish successful quantitative models from the larger philosophical programs in which they were developed.

From sixteen fifteen onward, another kind of calculation occupied him: assembling evidence to defend his mother, Katharina. She lived in Württemberg and had been accused of witchcraft. Accusations of this kind were entangled with neighborhood quarrels, social position, the policing of reputation and local legal procedures. Ulinka Rublack's research into the surviving trial records corrects stories that turn Katharina into an eccentric caricature or treat an aunt supposedly executed for witchcraft as an established family fact. Those features were among the accusations, not facts historians can safely repeat. The danger to Katharina was real regardless of the rumors' unreliability.

One of the gravest crises concerned his mother, Katharina, who was accused of witchcraft in a Württemberg legal proceeding. Her case unfolded over several years, not in a single heroic afternoon. Kepler undertook substantial legal and intellectual work on her behalf, drawing on the assistance of lawyers and university scholars. She was eventually released and not convicted. The surviving records show the frightening material consequences of religious accusation, social suspicion and judicial power. The case also reminds us that early modern scientific thinkers inhabited communities with laws and fears very different from ours. Kepler's work on planetary theory did not protect his family from those pressures.

The case moved from accusation to a long imprisonment. Katharina was arrested in August sixteen twenty and held for more than a year. Kepler came from his own responsibilities to assist her and worked with legal counsel on a detailed defense. His surviving argument, now available in translation through a Cambridge historical project, disputes the meaning of testimony and presses officials about delays and procedure. It shows a son working through the rules and language of a legal system, not a scientist making a single dramatic speech that instantly released his mother. She was freed in the autumn of sixteen twenty-one; she died the following year. The outcome was neither proof that the institutions were just nor a story of an isolated intellect defeating an entire society by brilliance alone. Lawyers, university assessors, family support, written petitions and official discretion all belonged to the result.

During these same years the Thirty Years' War spread across central Europe. The conflict grew from struggles over confessional and political power and drew in rulers and armies beyond the lands where Kepler lived. For a working mathematician, the consequences could arrive as suspended salaries, displaced patrons, insecurity on the roads, difficulty arranging a printer and renewed pressure on religious minorities. War did not merely supply the dark backdrop to a peaceful study. It altered the practical routes by which books, money and letters could reach him.

This was also a century of religious and political instability across central Europe. Kepler's own Lutheran convictions made positions in some Catholic territories insecure. When writing a biography of a mathematically gifted person, it is tempting to treat every move between cities as a step in a clean professional ladder. For Kepler, moves were often responses to expulsion, unpaid salaries, confessional restrictions, or the search for a patron. His correspondence and the histories of his workplaces show someone repeatedly obliged to secure a living while pursuing a demanding project. This is a different experience from the modern image of a researcher with a permanent institute, a salary, and a research grant.

In sixteen nineteen, amid these responsibilities, Kepler published The Harmony of the World. Its argument joined astronomy with proportions and music, looking for numerical relations in a cosmos understood to be orderly. The book included the relationship between orbital period and orbital size now called his third law. Its presence in a work devoted to harmony matters. Kepler did not simply write down a detached equation from a modern physics textbook; he found the relationship within a wider inquiry whose symbolic and theological elements were essential to him, even where later astronomy retained only some results.

Kepler's search for harmony was neither merely numerical nor identical with modern scientific method. He connected the geometry of the planets to musical intervals, proportional relationships and a theological interpretation of nature. Many of those connections did not become accepted physics. Still, the third law emerged from the search, and it became one of the clues Newton used when formulating gravitational theory. We can acknowledge the productive role of a framework without treating everything in that framework as correct.

He also struggled to publish astronomical tables based on Tycho's observations. Logarithms, recently introduced by John Napier, promised to reduce enormous computational labor by turning multiplication into addition. Kepler recognized their usefulness and worked to understand and produce his own tables. In sixteen twenty-seven he published the Rudolphine Tables. These were not a glamorous alternative to his planetary laws; they represented the painstaking business of making predictions that other astronomers could check. Their usefulness helped secure the new astronomy beyond theoretical argument.

The Rudolphine Tables are an example of the practical consequence of Kepler's theoretical achievement. An astronomical table is a tool: it lets a person calculate predicted planetary positions from parameters, rather than reworking an entire theory from the beginning each time. The tables incorporated Tycho's observations and Kepler's methods and appeared in sixteen twenty-seven. Making them required painstaking computation and attention to the presentation of numerical results. Long before electronic calculators, a miscopied digit or a mistaken logarithm could propagate through a prediction. The work depended on people willing to do hard arithmetic as well as people willing to propose bold geometrical changes.

Logarithms were transformative because they converted multiplication into addition and division into subtraction in suitable tables. If two quantities must be multiplied, one could look up their logarithms, add the tabulated values, and use a reverse lookup. The method still required care and could introduce numerical error, but it reduced the burden of many large calculations. Kepler recognized their potential for astronomy soon after Napier's work became known. This is not a separate footnote about the history of calculators. It helps explain how a new mathematical account of planetary motion became a practical method for forecasting the sky.

Printing the Rudolphine Tables in sixteen twenty-seven was another victory of persistence, though one supported by Tycho's long observational labor and the work of calculators and printers. Kepler went to Ulm to see the publication through. The new tables made his astronomical methods usable by others, not merely persuasive to readers of his theoretical books. In sixteen twenty-eight he entered the service of the military commander Albrecht von Wallenstein at Sagan. That move placed an astronomer yet again in dependence on an influential patron during war. Kepler died in Regensburg in sixteen thirty while pursuing unpaid financial claims. His career had never settled into the comfortable permanence that later biographies sometimes assign to a celebrated scientist.

Only after seeing the family, religious and political history in which this last work took place should we turn to the mathematics. The third law tells us how the timescale of planetary movement changes from one orbit to another. The law is simple in its modern form, but that simplicity should not erase the enormous labor of comparing orbits, checking observations and finding an order Kepler himself understood as harmony.

The third law came a decade later, in The Harmony of the World, published in sixteen nineteen. It relates a planet's orbital period, the time it takes to make one complete journey, to the size of its ellipse. The square of the period is proportional to the cube of the ellipse's semimajor axis. Semimajor axis means half the length of the long axis. The law allows us to compare two planets without solving every detail of their positions on every day.

Take Earth as a reference. Use one year as its period and one astronomical unit as its semimajor axis. Mars has a semimajor axis about one point five two astronomical units. Cube one point five two, and you get about three point five. Take the square root, and you get about one point eight eight. So the third law predicts a Martian year of roughly one point eight eight Earth years. That agrees with observation. The calculation does not require us to memorize the exact value of the gravitational constant, because the comparison is between bodies orbiting the same central mass in a good approximation.

The third law also gives a good test of what it means for a scientific relationship to have explanatory reach. Suppose a planet travels on an orbit with a semimajor axis four times that of Earth's orbit, in the ideal two-body approximation around the same Sun. The cube of four is sixty-four. The law says the square of its orbital period, measured in Earth years, is sixty-four, so its period is eight years. This calculation does not require knowing where the planet is on a particular night. It relates two broad properties of an orbit, and can therefore help constrain a whole family of predictions.

But there is a catch in how we apply it. We have to use the semimajor axis, not just the planet's distance from the Sun at one observation. The distance varies along an ellipse. We also have to compare orbits around the same central body, or use a generalized version involving the masses. And the ideal law is exact only within an appropriate two-body model; other planets create perturbations. These assumptions explain why a formula can be extremely valuable even when high-precision astronomers still need extensive numerical calculations. Kepler found a relation strong enough to survive these later refinements, yet the modern mathematical explanation for its form belonged to a subsequent generation. His search for harmony found a numerical law whose power went beyond the harmonic philosophy that helped motivate the search.

Today we explain the area law using conservation of angular momentum under a central force. In modern notation the quantity distance squared multiplied by the rate at which the direction changes is constant for a particle acted on by a force aimed at a fixed center. But that is a later explanation. Kepler reasoned within his own physical and philosophical picture of forces and solar influence. It would be anachronistic to say he applied Newtonian gravitation, which was developed decades after Kepler died.

The area law can be made more concrete with a simple approximation. Draw the Sun at one vertex of a long, narrow triangular wedge and the planet at the far end. For a tiny change of position perpendicular to the Sun-planet line, the triangular area is roughly one half the distance to the planet times that sideways displacement. If the planet is twice as far from the Sun, it needs only half as much sideways displacement in the same interval to sweep the same area. That does not fully describe the orbit, because the line also changes direction and the planet may move radially, but it shows why equal area is not the same as equal speed along the orbit. A planet near the Sun generally moves faster along its path than it does far from the Sun.

Modern mechanics provides a mathematical explanation unavailable to Kepler in its later form. Under a central force, the acceleration points along the radius toward or away from the force center. Such a force produces no torque about that center. The angular momentum therefore remains constant. For a point mass moving in a plane, the swept-area rate is proportional to that angular momentum divided by twice the mass. That is the hidden conservation rule behind the area law. But chronology matters. Newton's mechanics and inverse-square gravitational explanation came later; we should not make Kepler sound as though he derived the law from Newton's equations. Kepler sought causes and used geometrical and physical analogies of his own period.

In Kepler's lifetime none of these examples supplied a Newtonian theory of gravitational attraction. The modern central-force derivation explains why the laws fit together under particular physical assumptions. It is a valuable way to understand Kepler's achievement, provided we do not insert Newton's later explanation into Kepler's own head. The orbit models changed what astronomers could calculate; the subsequent physical theory changed why those patterns made sense.

Kepler died in sixteen thirty, before the later Newtonian synthesis that would give his laws a new mechanical setting. What survives is neither a flawless autobiography of scientific reason nor a pile of numbers that happened to fit. It is a record of persistence under political pressure, sometimes mistaken metaphysical expectations, inventive geometry, and an insistence that a coherent account should confront measured discrepancies. We can appreciate that achievement without claiming that Kepler anticipated every later explanation or that all the beliefs accompanying his discoveries were scientifically vindicated.

There is another lesson in Kepler's success. Astronomical observations are not pure truths delivered without interpretation. They are made by people with instruments, procedures and uncertainties. Tycho's data were accurate enough to challenge Kepler's models because of sustained observational practice. Kepler's courage was not simply trusting a number. It was testing whether the discrepancy persisted across a serious model and whether alternative mathematical descriptions could account for it without arbitrary repairs.

Return once more to the little eight-minute disagreement. If Kepler had been satisfied with an approximate circular model, he might have produced predictions adequate for some purposes and moved on. Instead the difference became a reason to rethink what could count as a planetary path. That is why his laws belong in the history of mathematics as well as astronomy. An ellipse is a geometric object; an area rule is a relation between geometry and time; the period law relates measurements across distinct orbits. All three are simple to state. Their discovery required decades of work, the labor of multiple observers, and a life lived amid pressures that a formula does not reveal.

## Family, faith, courts, and late career (not spoken)

### Selected chronology

- **1611–1613:** Barbara's death, move to Linz, marriage to Susanna. Kepler's dispute with Lutheran authorities over communion made his professional and religious position difficult.
- **1615–1621:** Accusations against Katharina Kepler; imprisonment in 1620–1621; release in autumn 1621. Historians should not repeat unsubstantiated prosecution rumors as biographical facts.
- **1619:** Publication of *Harmonice mundi*, including what is now called the third planetary law.
- **1627:** *Rudolphine Tables* printed, using Tycho's observations and Kepler's theory, with logarithmic computation.
- **1628–1630:** Service under Wallenstein at Sagan and death in Regensburg while seeking unpaid sums.

### Evidence and historiography

Ulinka Rublack's archival work studies Katharina's case through surviving records. The University of Cambridge makes translated extracts from Kepler's legal defense available. The conflict over communion is documented by historians of his religious commitments. Newtonian central-force explanations in the mathematical companion must remain identified as later reconstructions, not Kepler's own proof.

## Mathematical and historical companion (not spoken)

### Geometry of an elliptic orbit

For an ellipse with semimajor axis \(a\), eccentricity \(0\le e<1\), and the Sun at a focus:

\[
r_{\mathrm{peri}}=a(1-e),\qquad r_{\mathrm{aphe}}=a(1+e).
\]

For an idealized case \(a=10, e=0.2\), the distances are 8 and 12 units. The **orbital position in time** requires the area law as well as the ellipse geometry.

### Equal areas and angular momentum (modern explanation)

\[
\frac{dA}{dt}=\frac12 r^2\dot\theta=\mathrm{constant},
\]

for motion under a central force. Kepler arrived at the law centuries before this compact Newtonian interpretation.

### Period scaling

For two planets about the same dominant central mass, in the Keplerian approximation,

\[
\frac{T_1^2}{T_2^2}=\frac{a_1^3}{a_2^3}.
\]

With Earth at \(a=1\) AU and \(T=1\) year, Mars at \(a\approx1.524\) AU gives \(T\approx\sqrt{1.524^3}\approx1.881\) years.

### What the primary text establishes

A digitized 1609 edition of *Astronomia nova* is available through the Smithsonian Libraries. It is evidence for what Kepler actually published, but it should not be read as a modern presentation of Newtonian central-force dynamics. For the relation between Kepler's own derivation, Tycho's observations, and a later angular-momentum explanation, compare the primary text with the historical and modern expositions below.
