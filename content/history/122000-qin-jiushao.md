# 122000 — Qin Jiushao: War, government, and arithmetic in the Southern Song

**Track:** History — People and ideas. **Focus:** Southern Song public administration, Qin's autobiographical preface and hostile contemporary reports, military service, the 1247 *Mathematical Treatise in Nine Sections*, simultaneous remainders, polynomial root finding, practical surveying, and historical character. **Background:** congruences and equations are explained without modern number theory prerequisites.

Imagine a person who knows the calendar, keeps official records, organizes military resources, studies polynomial equations, and attracts serious accusations of misconduct in public office. Qin Jiushao belonged to all of these worlds. His mathematical book, completed in 1247, is among the greatest works of thirteenth-century Chinese mathematics. The evidence about his conduct is much less flattering. A historical portrait has to hold both facts in view. Intelligence does not erase political responsibility, and a hostile source does not automatically reveal every motive or private act.

Qin was born around 1202 in what is now Sichuan. His father, Qin Jiuyu, was an educated official serving the Southern Song government. The dynasty ruled southern China after losing its northern territories to earlier invasions. Its capital at Lin'an, present-day Hangzhou, supported sophisticated administrative and scholarly institutions, even as northern frontiers remained under military pressure. The world into which Qin entered was not a peaceful laboratory of pure mathematics. It was a society in which astronomy, taxation, transport, land surveys, military defense and official appointments depended on numerical techniques.

At about seventeen, while his father held an administrative post in the west, Qin took part in military service connected with suppressing a rebellion. His early life involved travel with his father's official assignments. In the 1220s he reached the Southern Song capital and gained access to the Board of Astronomy. Astronomical and mathematical knowledge was embedded in state institutions, not confined to private scholars. A calendar was not simply a list of holidays: calculating celestial cycles meant prestige, authority and practical scheduling. A young man with access to astronomical professionals could encounter methods unavailable to an ordinary reader.

We possess a rare biographical witness in Qin's own preface to his famous mathematical treatise. There he recalls opportunities to study at the astronomical bureau and instruction by a reclusive scholar whose identity is not preserved. Those statements tell us what Qin wanted his readers to know about his education. They do not independently verify every episode of his youth. A preface has a purpose. It establishes the author's competence, signals a debt to teachers, and places a book within a scholarly lineage. Its first-person voice brings us close to Qin, but it should be read with the same criticism we bring to an autobiographical account written today.

The older mathematical authority was the *Nine Chapters on the Mathematical Art*. Liu Hui had supplied a landmark commentary many centuries earlier, and subsequent generations developed computation with counting rods, algorithms for solving equations, and applications to measurement. Qin inherited the practice of arranging coefficients on a counting board. Imagine making numbers meaningful not only through their value but through their positions. An array can distinguish constant terms from coefficients of higher powers. The rods can be moved as the computation proceeds. This is an organized symbolic technique, even if no letter x appears on the board.

His education was interrupted by the political and military history of the Song state. Mongol forces pressed into southwestern China in the 1230s. Qin later wrote that he spent years in frontier troubles, enduring danger and displacement. The precise chronology and rhetoric of this statement belong to his preface, not to an impartial military log. Yet conflict in Sichuan was real. When we ask why a mathematician was moving between cities, holding posts and returning to problems at uncertain intervals, the answer partly concerns the violent transformation of the region around him.

Qin entered government service and held a series of local administrative positions. The historical record contains allegations of extortion, corruption, aggressive conduct and private enrichment. Some reports came from contemporaries writing in the political world that Qin inhabited. Their force should not be concealed. Nor should they be repeated as though every colorful accusation were a verdict based on complete surviving evidence. We can report that the allegations were made, that appointments and dismissals followed, and that the pattern mattered to his career. We cannot reconstruct every legal proceeding or witness statement from the material available to us.

One particularly revealing detail is that poetry, riding, archery and administrative skills belonged alongside mathematics in accounts of his abilities. He was not a university specialist who happened to have a hobby in astronomy. He was a literate official capable of operating in several elite professional cultures. His mathematical writing reflects that range. Some problems concern celestial phenomena, others money, land, construction and military affairs. The ambition was to show how a common set of numerical tools could handle situations that an administrator might encounter, whether in practice or in stylized textbook form.

In 1244 Qin's mother died. Following the mourning conventions of his society, he left office for a period. During this interval he worked on the treatise now known as *Shushu Jiuzhang*, or *Mathematical Treatise in Nine Sections*, completed in 1247. The coincidence between mourning, interruption of official duties and intellectual production is significant. It does not mean mathematics appeared as an uncomplicated refuge from grief. We do not know how he felt. But the chronology identifies an unusual period in which a person whose career depended on appointments could devote sustained effort to organizing a demanding text.

The book divides eighty-one problems into nine sections. The echo of the ancient *Nine Chapters* is deliberate in structure, but Qin's methods reach well beyond a reproduction of the older classic. He develops calculations involving congruences, high-degree polynomial equations, surveying, taxes, grain, fortifications, and commercial matters. A reader can move from a numerical puzzle to a general algorithm, then see the same procedure working in a different setting. A system of teaching emerges through the problem collection rather than through modern abstract theorem headings.

The opening section's best-known mathematics concerns simultaneous remainders. Suppose a number leaves remainder two when divided by three, remainder three when divided by five, and remainder two when divided by seven. If we only know one condition, there are infinitely many possibilities. The first condition permits two, five, eight, eleven and so on. The second permits three, eight, thirteen, eighteen and so on. Their intersection includes eight, twenty-three, thirty-eight and many others. Among those, twenty-three has remainder two when divided by seven. So twenty-three is one answer. Add one hundred and five and all three remainders remain the same.

Modern mathematicians call this a Chinese remainder problem, although versions of remainder puzzles existed in earlier Chinese mathematics. Qin did not discover that a handful of small remainder conditions can sometimes be satisfied. His achievement was a systematic way to combine conditions, including difficult cases, with a technique that could be taught and reused. Historians commonly connect his procedure with a method called the great expansion and unification. The precise calculations reflect the structure of the moduli: are they relatively prime, and what happens when they are not?

There is a useful qualification. Three, five and seven have no common factors, so every consistent triple of remainders picks out one answer within a cycle of one hundred and five. If the divisors share a factor, the conditions can contradict each other. A number cannot be both even and odd, for instance; more subtly, a number cannot have remainder one upon division by four and remainder two upon division by six, because one condition forces it to be odd and the other even. A good algorithm does not merely announce a large integer. It must recognize which data are compatible before promising a solution.

Why would a government official care about such abstract relations? One reason is that calendars and periodic events naturally create remainder constraints. But we should not assert that every problem in Qin's text describes a real administrative case. Textbook writers often design contrived numbers so that a procedure can be demonstrated. The historical significance is the organization of mathematical technique in a society where the calendar, bureaucracy, military and trade supplied a familiar language for asking numerical questions.

The opening remainder method also reveals how an algorithm can depend on number-theoretic compatibility. In our example, begin with every number leaving remainder two after division by three. Write it as two plus three times another integer. Requiring a remainder of three after division by five turns this into a condition on that new integer. In fact the smallest useful value is two, which gives eight. Now the numbers satisfying both conditions can be written as eight plus fifteen times any integer. Requiring remainder two when divided by seven selects twenty-three. At each step the original problem becomes one smaller congruence. That is a modern presentation of the logic, not a transcription of Qin's counting-board instructions.

More sophisticated systems do not always involve pairwise coprime divisors. Imagine an astronomical cycle repeating every twelve days and another repeating every eighteen days. Their joint pattern repeats every thirty-six days, not every two hundred sixteen. If both schedules specify a condition on the same day, those conditions must agree modulo their common factor, six. A general procedure needs to track that fact. Qin's account is historically important because it gives sustained attention to the machinery of combining remainder conditions, not merely a single recreational puzzle. The fact that those conditions look elementary is one reason modern presentations can underestimate their difficulty in a computational setting without our notation.

The *Nine Sections* also deals with polynomial equations of high degree. Consider a square garden whose side we do not know. From some measurements we derive an equation for that side. Perhaps the unknown is hidden beneath repeated powers: the square, the fourth power, and higher even powers. The next step is not to recite a quadratic formula, because a tenth-degree equation generally offers no such elementary shortcut. Instead, arrange coefficients and systematically test or refine candidate roots, using positional calculation that avoids repeatedly expanding the entire polynomial from the beginning.

One of Qin's celebrated surveying problems describes a circular walled city, gates, an observer and a tree. The geometry leads to a tenth-degree polynomial in a modern transcription. Its relevant positive root gives a diameter of nine units in the problem's measurement scheme. This is not an ordinary numerical sum disguised in fancy language. It is an example of how a geometrical relation can be transformed into an algebraic object, then attacked computationally. The city and tree make the question vivid, but their historical purpose is to demonstrate a method.

Modern histories sometimes label the procedure Horner's method because a related technique became familiar through later European work. That name can be convenient if it describes an algorithm. It is misleading if it suggests that Qin borrowed a method from someone who lived centuries after him. Moreover, Qin built on earlier Chinese work on polynomial coefficients and root extraction; the lineage includes Jia Xian and other predecessors. The accurate claim is neither that Qin invented polynomial computation from nothing nor that a later European label explains its origin.

The practical chapters also ask how to measure a boundary or an inaccessible point. Imagine a riverbank where direct travel to a landmark is impossible. By taking accessible distances and constructing similar triangles, a surveyor can recover an otherwise unknown length. When relationships become more complex, the resulting equation may be nonlinear. The ability to reduce measurements to algebra was valuable even before modern symbolic notation. For Qin, geometry and arithmetic were not isolated disciplines: each could be a stage of the same problem-solving process.

His other problem classes make the overlap between mathematical and administrative imagination especially clear. The work includes divisions concerning grain and taxation, money, construction, military affairs and trade. Such headings make it easy to assume he was writing a manual for use at a specific government desk. In fact, a mathematical text can borrow familiar terms while selecting numerical conditions for the sake of its calculations. The right historical approach is to ask how a problem is framed, what procedure the author offers, and which part is a stylized exercise. A convincing scene of an actual tax collector calculating one of Qin's examples requires evidence that the manuscript alone does not supply.

Take the mathematical side of a land survey. A boundary can be irregular, measurements can be indirect, and the desired area can depend on an unknown length. In an elementary problem, triangles supply an area formula immediately. In a more complicated layout, the surveyor may have to solve a polynomial equation before the length becomes known. The book's organization invites the reader to see surveying as an interplay of geometry and calculation rather than memorizing one formula for every kind of field. The same underlying algebra can be repurposed for construction or trade, though the wording of the problem changes.

Qin's official career resumed after the treatise. He received and lost posts in different regions. One late appointment as governor of Qiongzhou, on Hainan, ended in dismissal amid serious charges of exploitation and corruption. We should resist the temptation to romanticize this as the persecution of a mathematician whose genius threatened officials. The accusations concerned his conduct in office. We should also resist using the reported misconduct as an excuse to pretend his mathematics was less sophisticated. Historical judgment requires the ability to describe achievement and wrongdoing without making one cancel the evidence for the other.

There is a danger in borrowing Qin's practical problem headings too literally. We might imagine every piece of official finance was calculated by a person who knew sophisticated remainder arithmetic. Real administrations combine rules of thumb, experienced judgment, written forms, and specialized mathematics. An advanced treatise tells us what its author was able to formulate and teach, not what every office actually performed. This is especially important in the Southern Song, a dynasty with elaborate institutions but substantial regional variation. The prestige of a mathematical solution and its routine adoption are different historical claims.

His text also contains an intellectual tension between choosing problems whose answers are exact and dealing with the imperfect measurements of the world. A city can be represented by a perfect circle for the purpose of solving an equation, while an actual city wall is never an ideal circumference. Rainfall measured in a vessel may give a neat formula, but real rain, container shape and measuring error complicate practice. Qin's examples show an ambition to connect idealized calculation to familiar activities; they do not eliminate the gap between model and reality. That gap makes the mathematical text a particularly interesting witness to the habits of numerical reasoning in public life.

The hostile accounts of Qin present another kind of gap between model and reality. If one chose to write only the mathematics, the reader would meet a disembodied master of algorithms. If one chose only accusations, the treatise would nearly disappear beneath a moral portrait. Neither would be adequate history. His literary and professional reputations are evidence of the contradictions of an educated official whose mathematical sophistication did not guarantee sound conduct in office. The unresolved details ought to remain unresolved.

He died around 1261, after a career that had traversed a vulnerable dynasty and a changing set of political jurisdictions. Within two decades, the Southern Song would fall to the Yuan. The mathematical tradition did not disappear with the dynasty. Later scholars, including Li Ye, Yang Hui and Zhu Shijie, worked with related algebraic and computational approaches. Their methods were not all copied from Qin, but his text belongs to the extraordinary cluster of surviving thirteenth-century Chinese mathematics. Much of what we know of that school exists because books were copied, annotated, lost from some libraries, and preserved or rediscovered through others.

His personal story is more sharply documented than that of some earlier Chinese mathematicians, though it is less certain than a modern archive would make possible. He left a preface; other people left complaints; appointments and military events give us chronological markers. These sources do not make him a sympathetic protagonist by default. What they reveal is a brilliant computational thinker operating within war, official rank, social ambition and disputed conduct. The history matters because it makes the mathematics legible as human work rather than an isolated national trophy.

Consider the remainder puzzle one last time. Twenty-three obeys three distinct numerical conditions, and a method can uncover it without guessing forever. Qin's professional life seems less likely to fit one clean equation. Scholar, soldier, official, teacher of procedures, accused exploiter: none is a remainder that can simply be discarded. His 1247 treatise shows mathematical reasoning of striking generality, expressed through the tools and problems of Southern Song China. Its survival allows us to admire that reasoning while retaining a clear view of the complicated person and political world from which it came.

## Historical companion — not spoken

### Chronology and evidence

- **c. 1202:** Birth in Sichuan under the Southern Song; father Qin Jiuyu served in administration.
- **1219:** Military participation against a rebellion, according to biographical sources.
- **1220s:** Access to the Board of Astronomy at Lin'an/Hangzhou; Qin's own preface recalls a reclusive mathematics teacher.
- **1230s–1240s:** Military conflict with Mongol forces, frequent changes in his administrative posts, and allegations by contemporary critics.
- **1244:** Mother's death and mourning leave from office.
- **1247:** *Shushu Jiuzhang*, *Mathematical Treatise in Nine Sections*, eighty-one problems.
- **1250s:** Further offices, including dismissal after appointment on Hainan; accusations of corruption and exploitation.
- **c. 1261:** Death; date and precise circumstances from later biographical reconstruction.

### Mathematical companion

- **A Chinese remainder example:** \(x\equiv2\pmod3\), \(x\equiv3\pmod5\), \(x\equiv2\pmod7\). Solutions are \(x\equiv23\pmod{105}\).
- **Compatibility:** \(x\equiv1\pmod4\) and \(x\equiv2\pmod6\) are incompatible by parity. A modern CRT generalization requires that the two residues agree modulo the gcd of their divisors.
- **Polynomial example:** A city-tree problem described by Qin yields a modern transcription \(z^{10}+15z^8+72z^6-864z^4-11664z^2-34992=0\); the positive solution \(z=3\) corresponds to a city diameter of \(z^2=9\) units. This is a mathematical reconstruction in modern notation.
- **Algorithmic attribution:** Qin's remainder combination and high-degree polynomial computations drew on earlier Chinese calculation traditions; avoid assigning invention of all remainder arithmetic or all positional polynomial evaluation to him.
