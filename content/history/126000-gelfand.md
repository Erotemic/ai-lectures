# 126000 — Israel Gelfand: A Moscow seminar and the unity of mathematics

**Track:** History — People and ideas. **Focus:** Poverty and limited schooling in Soviet Ukraine, mathematical self-education and Kolmogorov, functional analysis, normed algebras and operator theory, the Moscow seminar, Soviet institutions and discrimination, representations, biology, education, and the Rutgers years. **Background:** functions, operators and algebraic structures are explained in speech.

A university seminar is not usually remembered as an institution of its own. A professor speaks, students ask questions, and the next week's lecture replaces the last. In Moscow, one weekly seminar acquired a reputation across the mathematical world. Israel Gelfand organized discussions in which a speaker might be interrupted, challenged to explain a simple example, or asked to begin again from the underlying idea. The meetings could be intellectually exhilarating and difficult for presenters. For decades they brought together researchers whose subjects looked unrelated. Their organizer became one of the most influential mathematicians of the twentieth century, despite having started without a completed secondary education.

Israel Moiseevich Gelfand was born in 1913 in Okny, a town then in the Russian Empire and now in Ukraine. His family was Jewish. Schooling and family circumstances placed limits on the ordinary path to higher education. In later recollections he spoke warmly of an encouraging mathematics teacher; family biographical accounts also describe his exclusion from a vocational school because of his father's small business. That exclusion belonged to the Soviet class politics of the time. It is a reminder that the constraints on entry to education could be ideological as well as financial, and that Gelfand's later academic ascent did not begin with an uninterrupted institutional welcome.

In 1930, still a teenager, he went to Moscow. He worked in odd jobs, including at the Lenin Library, and used access to books to pursue mathematics. The library story is often retold as a romance of pure self-teaching. It is more concrete than that. Gelfand was working while trying to learn what a university education would normally provide. A library offered texts and a place for intellectual contact, but it did not pay his living expenses or automatically grant him a degree. His opportunities depended on individual encouragement and on a mathematical community willing, at crucial moments, to recognize ability outside the standard curriculum.

A family account recounts an encounter at the library with Andrey Kolmogorov, who gave him problems and was impressed by his solutions. The precise form of the exchange depends on recollection rather than a contemporary transcript. What is independently clear is that Gelfand began attending Moscow university lectures, taught in evening institutes, and became a graduate student under Kolmogorov in 1932. It was an extraordinary transition, but it was not a transition from knowing nothing to suddenly inventing advanced mathematics. He had read intensively, solved problems, and begun teaching others.

Teaching people older than himself became part of his early professional training. In evening classes, the instructor might face workers who had spent the day at their jobs and wanted a practical explanation of mathematics. Gelfand later valued the experience. A mathematician who can repeat a theorem without explaining what it means has not necessarily learned how to teach. He developed an interest in finding the illuminating question, the simple example, and the line of argument that exposes the central structure. Those habits later became famous in his seminar, though the seminar's demanding culture did not always feel gentle to its participants.

The Moscow mathematical environment he entered was exceptionally strong. Kolmogorov had worked on probability and analysis; Aleksandr Plessner, Lazar Lyusternik and others contributed to functional analysis; Soviet institutions were supporting important mathematical research even as political control created serious dangers. Gelfand's early work involved functions taking values in abstract spaces and linear operators acting on those functions. His 1935 dissertation and later research joined a wider effort to understand infinite-dimensional mathematics in a rigorous and flexible way.

To grasp the subject, start with an ordinary vector. You might add two arrows, multiply an arrow by a number, or measure its length. Now replace the arrows with functions. Adding two functions means adding their values point by point; multiplying by a number rescales the entire function. A linear operator takes one function and produces another, preserving addition and scalar multiplication. Differentiation and integration are familiar examples when the relevant function spaces are chosen carefully. The space may have infinitely many independent directions, but algebraic and geometric ideas still apply.

Gelfand wanted more than a catalog of operators. He sought structural ways to understand whole classes of problems. In his work on commutative normed rings, now usually discussed through Banach algebras, he found a striking bridge to classical analysis. In a suitable commutative algebra, an element can be studied through the values assigned to it by multiplicative linear functionals, often called characters. Instead of staring only at an abstract algebraic symbol, one can turn it into a function on a space of characters. This is the idea behind the Gelfand transform.

There is an accessible analogy. Consider all continuous functions on a compact space, with ordinary pointwise addition and multiplication. You can learn something about a function by evaluating it at each point. Evaluation at a point turns a function into a number, and it respects multiplication. Gelfand's insight was that for broad classes of commutative algebras the possible multiplicative evaluations themselves form a meaningful space. The algebra and the geometry of that space illuminate each other. The most familiar full representation theorem for commutative C-star algebras makes this relationship exact.

For a very small example, take an algebra consisting of pairs of real or complex numbers. Add pairs component by component, and multiply them component by component. There are two obvious ways to evaluate a pair as a single number: keep its first entry, or keep its second. Both evaluations respect multiplication. The two evaluations therefore behave like two points of a space. A pair in the algebra can be viewed as a function on that two-point space, assigning its first number to one point and its second number to the other. The real representation theory is far richer, but this example gives the key change of viewpoint: abstract algebraic operations can encode the ordinary operations of functions on a space.

Now consider an algebra where multiplication does not commute. Multiplying two matrices in opposite orders can give different results. The simple picture of functions on an ordinary set is no longer adequate, because ordinary numerical functions commute under pointwise multiplication. Gelfand and his colleagues did not respond by pretending that this difference was irrelevant. They asked what kind of operator model could represent the richer algebra. That question led toward one of the defining bridges between functional analysis and mathematical physics. It also helps explain why the distinction between commutative and noncommutative mathematics was so significant in Gelfand's work.

Gelfand did not establish every representation theorem in this area alone. His collaboration with Mark Naimark, especially in the theory of operator algebras, was essential. In the 1940s their work connected abstract normed *-algebras to concrete algebras of operators on Hilbert space. When we say Gelfand–Naimark theorem, the pair of names is not incidental. It records a research collaboration, and it recognizes that abstract algebra and analytic models could be brought into the same framework. Such connections were characteristic of Gelfand's style.

In 1941 he became a professor at Moscow State University. During the Second World War, scientific institutions operated under the immense pressure of invasion, evacuation, industrial work and political oversight. Soviet mathematics did not cease, but the conditions of work changed. Gelfand's career continued across these disruptions. In 1943 he began the weekly mathematical seminar that became internationally famous. It was a place to test mathematical ideas, learn from visiting speakers and connect seemingly distant fields. The duration of the seminar mattered: it became a continuing community rather than a one-time public lecture.

Accounts of the seminar often emphasize his interruptions. A speaker might present a technical result and Gelfand would ask what it meant in the smallest useful example. That could be a constructive demand for clarity. It could also be intimidating. These recollections come from different participants with different experiences, so they should not be assembled into a fictional transcript of one typical afternoon. The documented significance is that many researchers passed through a common intellectual forum, and some learned to think about their problems in a broader way because of the questions asked there.

The seminar was only one part of a remarkable network of research. Gelfand worked on representations of noncompact groups, generalized functions, differential equations, integral geometry, and many other subjects. In representation theory, one tries to understand a group by the ways it can act linearly on vector spaces. A group may describe symmetries, but studying all its elements directly can be difficult. A representation converts symmetry into matrices or operators. For compact groups, a mature theory already existed through the work of Frobenius, Schur, Weyl and others. Noncompact groups introduced new analytic difficulties, and Gelfand helped develop methods to meet them.

Generalized functions, sometimes called distributions, extend the notion of a function so that objects like a point impulse can be handled rigorously. A classical function assigns a number to each point. A distribution instead acts on suitable test functions and returns numbers. This makes sense of operations that are awkward for sharp impulses, discontinuities or weak solutions of differential equations. Gelfand and his collaborators developed and presented these tools in influential books, including the multivolume *Generalized Functions* series associated with Georgii Shilov and others. Again, mathematical influence traveled through pedagogy as well as original papers.

His books often favored conceptual explanation and revealing examples. That approach had an international audience, including readers who never attended the Moscow seminar. For a student learning about calculus of variations or linear algebra, the highest value of such a book may be that it encourages a particular kind of question: what happens if we change this assumption; can we find a counterexample; what is the simplest case in which the phenomenon appears? The student receives not just formulas but a practice of inquiry.

It would be incomplete to describe Soviet mathematical life as a meritocracy based solely on talent. Gelfand faced restrictions connected with antisemitism and Soviet institutions, while also receiving important official appointments and honors. He became a corresponding member of the Soviet Academy in 1953 but was not elected a full member until 1984. The long gap has been discussed in accounts of the political and discriminatory environment surrounding his career. We should distinguish documented exclusion and institutional history from overconfident explanations of the motive behind each administrative decision. His career included privilege and obstacles, not an uncomplicated trajectory of either support or persecution.

From the late 1950s Gelfand broadened his attention to biology and medicine. With collaborators including Mikhail Tsetlin he organized discussions that drew physiologists and mathematicians together. In biology, the challenge is often not to write a perfect equation but to decide which structures in a living system can be modeled at all. Cells communicate, proteins fold, physiological processes depend on many scales. Gelfand's move into these questions reflected his long-standing conviction that mathematical methods could illuminate very different phenomena. But it would be mistaken to claim that he single-handedly created modern mathematical biology.

There was a personal dimension to some of these interests, documented in family accounts and obituaries. Illness in his family affected the way he thought about medical research. We should acknowledge that connection without inventing a private causal chain that would reduce a many-year scientific program to one tragedy. The important historical fact is that he devoted sustained attention and institutional effort to biological research, encouraged interdisciplinary seminars, and worked with scientists whose expertise lay outside mathematics.

Gelfand's later work also reached algebraic combinatorics and geometry. Results and constructions associated with the names of collaborators—such as Bernstein–Gelfand–Gelfand in representation theory—became tools in fields far from the original problem. Some research involved understanding how symmetries of algebraic objects can be encoded in sequences of modules and maps. To a listener without advanced algebra, the central lesson is structural: instead of attempting to solve a representation problem one matrix at a time, organize the relations among many representations in a pattern that can be studied systematically. The technical results have many authors and precise hypotheses; the biography should not present every theorem in their vicinity as Gelfand's individual work.

The breadth of his interests could generate a tension in the seminar. Specialists need detailed calculations, but a leader asking for a small example may interrupt a delicate line of proof. Some participants remembered the difficulty of presenting to him; others considered it indispensable intellectual training. A thoughtful account does not have to decide that every interruption was generous or every interruption rude. It can describe a mathematical culture that prized exposing fundamental questions while recognizing that such a culture imposed real demands on speakers and students. In this respect the seminar was not merely a series of lectures; it was a school of mathematical conversation with its own strengths and costs.

His family life included marriage to Zorya Shapiro and later to Tatiana Alekseyevskaya, herself a mathematician; he had children from both marriages. As with other scientific families, personal and professional networks sometimes overlapped, and later family members helped preserve memories and documents. Some of our evidence for his early years comes from such later reminiscence. That is valuable evidence if it is identified correctly, but it should not be confused with a school record or a contemporaneous diary.

Near the end of the Soviet period, Gelfand moved to the United States. After visiting positions around 1989–1990, he joined Rutgers University in New Jersey in 1990. At an age when many people would have retired, he continued publishing, organizing seminars and discussing new problems with collaborators. Rutgers colleagues remembered that several mathematical conversations could be underway in his office at once. This is a recollection, not a quantitative measure of scientific output, but it describes the social style of a scholar who treated mathematics as an active exchange rather than a finished monument.

His work in America included algebraic structures, geometry, combinatorics and biological questions. He also supported mathematical education, including correspondence opportunities intended for students who lacked easy access to advanced instruction. That interest was consistent with the early part of his life: a boy without the standard school credentials had found a path into research because a few people and institutions made room for his work. One should not turn this parallel into a claim that he planned every later educational activity as repayment for his youth. It is enough to see the continuity of a concern for how people learn to think.

The diversity of his textbooks also tells us something about the audience he imagined. Some were aimed at researchers, full of advanced operator theory; others were written to bring serious mathematical thinking to young students. His collaborations on books did not remove all difficulty. A clear explanation of an abstraction can be demanding because it asks the learner to understand the structure rather than merely memorize a recipe. His interest in correspondence teaching extended that ambition to students geographically distant from major academic centers. A person could submit solutions and receive feedback without first moving to Moscow or another university city.

The move to Rutgers did not mean his scientific identity became detached from Soviet mathematics. He carried a set of questions, collaborators, books and seminar habits across a political border. At the same time, a new generation of American colleagues encountered a scholar who had spent decades developing that network under conditions different from their own. The connection was not always simple. Language, institutional expectations and styles of communication can make even familiar mathematical ideas seem new. The success of the move was partly the continued ability to ask questions in a community willing to engage them.

Some mathematical biographies end with the list of graduate students, as though influence were only a genealogical diagram. For Gelfand that would be particularly inadequate. A great many researchers attended his seminars, read his books or were drawn into a project without being his formal doctoral student. The professional world of a mathematician is larger than the names on a university supervision record. Teaching can be diffuse, carried through a conversation, a carefully chosen counterexample or a book sent to a student. Much of his impact passed through those channels.

Gelfand received major international honors, including the Wolf and Kyoto prizes and recognition from mathematical societies. He died in 2009 at the age of ninety-six. The geographical span of his life ran from a small town under the Russian Empire to Soviet Moscow and finally to the United States. The intellectual span was even broader: from basic arithmetic teaching to operator algebras, representations, generalized functions, medical models and the training of younger mathematicians. His biography contains institutional achievement, political restriction, deep collaborations, and a distinctive pedagogical culture.

What unified that range? Not the assertion that every field is secretly identical. Rather, he looked for mathematical structures that could migrate. An abstract algebra might become a space of functions; a group might become a family of operators; a biological question might suggest a new mathematical model. The famous seminar embodied a practical version of that vision. Someone proposed a technical result; other people asked for its meaning, its examples, and its relation to something else. That could be demanding, even uncomfortable, but it also created possibilities that no solitary specialist would necessarily see.

A theorem named for Gelfand may now appear on a page without the story of the young reader in the Lenin Library, the Soviet institutions that both supported and restricted him, the Monday seminar, or the Rutgers office. The theorem is part of the record. So is the life around it. Israel Gelfand became an influential mathematician by making connections: between algebra and analysis, between scholars, between abstract problems and the habits of teaching. The unity he sought was not a single formula for all mathematics. It was the capacity to recognize a fruitful question when it crossed a boundary.

## Historical companion — not spoken

### Chronology and evidence

- **1913:** Born in Okny/Krasnye Okny, now Ukraine; limited access to secondary and vocational schooling. Family memories describe early encouragement and later vocational-school exclusion under Soviet class policies.
- **1930:** Went to Moscow, worked odd jobs including at the Lenin Library, studied independently.
- **1932:** Became Kolmogorov's graduate student; evening teaching and Moscow university lectures.
- **1935:** Research thesis in functional analysis.
- **1938–1940:** Normed-ring work and advanced Soviet academic degree; chronologies vary between submission and award.
- **1941:** Professor at Moscow State University.
- **1943:** Began the long-running Moscow seminar.
- **1940s onward:** Work with Naimark on operator algebras; representation theory and generalized functions with many collaborators.
- **1953 / 1984:** Corresponding and full membership respectively in the Soviet Academy, amid the politically and socially restrictive Soviet scientific system.
- **Late 1950s onward:** Mathematical biology, interdisciplinary seminars and research groups.
- **1989–1990:** Visiting appointments followed by move to Rutgers University.
- **2009:** Died in New Brunswick, New Jersey.

### Mathematical companion

- **Gelfand transform:** For a commutative unital Banach algebra \(A\), the transform of \(a\in A\) is the function \(\widehat a(\varphi)=\varphi(a)\) on its character space. An isometric *-isomorphism with \(C(X)\) requires the commutative C*-algebra hypotheses.
- **Gelfand–Naimark:** Every C*-algebra admits a faithful representation as a norm-closed *-algebra of bounded operators on Hilbert space; jointly attributed to Gelfand and Naimark.
- **Representation theory:** A group homomorphism into invertible linear operators; analytic subtleties are especially significant for noncompact groups.
- **Distributions:** Continuous linear functionals on a test-function space; the Dirac delta maps \(\varphi\) to \(\varphi(0)\). Avoid calling delta an ordinary real-valued function.
- **Attribution:** Gelfand's seminars, textbooks and collaborations were collective work; do not absorb Naimark, Shilov, Tsetlin or other contributors into a single-author story.
