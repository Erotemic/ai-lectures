# 121000 — Bhāskara II: A teacher of numbers and the moving heavens

**Track:** History — People and ideas. **Focus:** Twelfth-century Indian astronomy, intellectual inheritance from Brahmagupta, mathematical verse and pedagogy, the *Līlāvatī* legend, algebraic problems, the cyclic method, the Ujjain astronomical tradition, and the transmission of a scholarly family. **Background:** arithmetic, elementary equations and periodic astronomical motion are developed in speech.

A mathematician living in twelfth-century India could not separate the motion of the heavens from the practice of calculation. The calendar required dates; an eclipse required a prediction; a student needed rules that could be memorized and then understood. An equation might be an intellectual puzzle, but it could also be part of the machinery of astronomy. Bhāskara, often called Bhāskarācārya, or Bhāskara the teacher, worked at this intersection. The name Bhāskara the Second is a modern way of distinguishing him from an earlier astronomer called Bhāskara. He did not call himself the second member of a numbered historical series.

He was born in 1114, conventionally associated with the Vijayapura region of what is now India. We know far less about his household than about his mathematical writing. His father, Maheśvara, was himself an astronomer or practitioner of the mathematical sciences. This was not a world in which a boy had to encounter arithmetic for the first time in a university classroom. Knowledge could be transmitted within families, through oral instruction, commentary, verse, and the apprenticeship of students to working scholars. But no source gives us license to invent the conversation between father and son. We know that Bhāskara inherited a tradition and that his books display an extraordinarily careful understanding of it. We do not have a diary of the moment he learned to count.

A useful geographic center is Ujjain. The city was associated with astronomical calculation and with scholars including Varāhamihira and Brahmagupta, whose work preceded Bhāskara by centuries. Later biographies often describe Bhāskara as heading the astronomical center there. Such descriptions identify an important scholarly milieu; they should not be made to sound like a modern observatory with an archived organizational chart. The medieval institution of astronomical learning could involve teachers, patrons, local calendars, inherited tables, and professional reputations, without the administrative structure that the word observatory might suggest today.

The country around him was not governed by one timeless, unified Indian state. In the twelfth century, different dynasties, courts, regional languages, and forms of patronage shaped scholarly life. A mathematical author could write in Sanskrit for readers far beyond one city, and still depend on students or supporters whose identities are now only partly visible. Practical astronomy was valuable for calendrical and religious purposes, but such utility did not reduce all its practitioners to clerks calculating dates. They also studied difficult number-theoretic problems and mathematical relations that could be enjoyed for their own intricacy.

Bhāskara's principal major composition, the *Siddhāntaśiromaṇi*, is dated to 1150. Its familiar four-part organization comprises the *Līlāvatī*, concerned especially with arithmetic and elementary mathematics; the *Bījagaṇita*, on algebra; and the astronomical sections on planetary computations and the celestial sphere. The exact classification of volumes and constituent works varies among catalogs and translations. That is a bibliographical problem, not evidence that Bhāskara failed to organize his mathematics. His program reached from numbers and geometric measurements to the apparent movements of planets.

Why use verse for mathematics? Consider a rule that must be remembered in a culture where an instructor speaks a text and students reproduce it. A well-composed verse can preserve an algorithm. But a verse can also be so compressed that it is nearly unreadable without a teacher. That is one reason commentaries matter. Bhāskara wrote explanations of his astronomical work, and later generations supplied further commentaries. A statement that looks like a neat modern formula in an English translation may originally have appeared as a short Sanskrit instruction whose technical interpretation depended on a long scholarly tradition.

The *Līlāvatī* acquired one of the most famous anecdotes in mathematical biography. In a version circulated much later, Bhāskara calculated an auspicious wedding time for a daughter named Līlāvatī. A water clock was to mark the hour; a small pearl accidentally blocked the apparatus; the moment passed; and her father supposedly composed a mathematics book to comfort her. The story appears in accounts associated with a Persian translation of the book in the late sixteenth century, long after Bhāskara's life. The surviving evidence does not establish that Līlāvatī was his daughter, that this wedding happened, or that the book was written for that reason. The tale is a part of the book's later cultural life, not a documented scene from its author's household.

The work itself is sufficient to explain its fame. It asks learners to think about proportions, purchases, mixtures, geometrical measurements, progressions and combinations. Take a problem of direct proportion. If four measures of grain cost twelve coins, what do seven measures cost, assuming a constant unit price? First find the price of one measure, three coins; then multiply by seven, obtaining twenty-one. A student who can recite the rule but cannot recognize when proportionality fails is not yet an expert. The instructional value lies in seeing why the operation matches the relation between quantities. Medieval computational texts built that competence through problem after problem, even when their styles of proof differed from a modern school textbook.

In another kind of problem, a merchant gains and loses money. We now write a negative number with a minus sign. Indian algebraists had long used a language of debts and fortunes to manage signed quantities. Bhāskara inherited rules from an established tradition rather than inventing negative numbers out of nothing. His treatment includes calculations with zero, and it also exposes a persistent difficulty: what can it mean to divide by zero? Some historical formulations use an infinite or unchanged quantity in contexts where a modern mathematician would reject the operation as undefined. It would be misleading both to claim he possessed the modern theory of limits and to pretend he had never confronted the problem. He was working with a coherent computational language that did not have all of today's restrictions.

One reason the Sanskrit mathematical text has to be read as a teaching artifact is that its exercises do not all describe transactions the author witnessed. A problem involving a swarm of bees or a merchant's purchase can be a device for training in ratios, just as a modern book invents a train timetable to teach simultaneous equations. The mathematical point survives even if the insect or merchant was imaginary. The student is invited to identify what must remain constant when the circumstances change. Bhāskara's skill as a writer lies partly in making such questions memorable without making their solution depend on the remembered image alone.

Combinations supply another window onto his teaching. Suppose a student is asked how many different pairs can be chosen from five objects. If order does not matter, there are ten pairs. One can count them by taking four choices for the first object, three new pairs for the second, then two and one, yielding four plus three plus two plus one. A compact combinatorial rule can encode that reasoning. Such problems were not a new invention in twelfth-century India; they belonged to a long combinatorial and literary tradition. Their presence in a mathematical teaching book shows how extensively the curriculum ranged beyond day-to-day accounting.

The *Bījagaṇita* takes us further into algebra. Suppose we seek a number whose square is forty-nine. There are two signed solutions in modern arithmetic: seven and negative seven. Accepting negative quantities enlarges the set of answers and changes which manipulations must be considered legitimate. Now imagine an equation with two unknowns, constrained to integer solutions. Such problems resist an answer that can be written down as a single fraction. They require search, reduction, and the recognition of number-theoretic structure.

Consider the equation three times the square of one integer, plus one, equals the square of another. One solution is one and two, since three times one squared plus one equals four. But for some coefficients the smallest nontrivial solutions become unexpectedly large. In a famous example connected with the Indian cyclic method, multiplying a square by sixty-one and adding one must produce another square. A correct answer can be so large that guessing is no reasonable strategy. For sixty-one, one solution is: take the first integer as two hundred twenty-six million, one hundred fifty-three thousand, nine hundred eighty; the second as one billion, seven hundred sixty-six million, three hundred nineteen thousand, forty-nine. Their squares satisfy the relation exactly. The sizes are part of the achievement, not an ornament to it.

A method that reaches such answers cannot simply test every positive integer in sequence. The Indian *cakravāla*, or cyclic procedure, works by transforming related equations, using carefully chosen intermediate values until a difficult problem becomes tractable. Brahmagupta had developed powerful composition identities, and a related method appears in later Indian work. Bhāskara's algebraic treatment is an important source for the mature procedure. Calling the problem a Pell equation refers to a much later European naming tradition. It is not evidence that the relevant European mathematician discovered the Indian calculation, nor that Bhāskara was solving a modern equation after reading a modern algebra textbook.

Here is the idea of composition without the full algorithm. If the product of a coefficient and one square differs from another square by a small amount, combining two such relations can produce a third relation. The new numbers may be larger, yet the discrepancy can be more useful. In the hands of a skilled calculator, the sequence of transformations is guided rather than random. This kind of insight links algebra to algorithmic thinking: a correct procedure can be repeated, taught, audited, and applied to many instances. The mathematical creativity lies not only in announcing one large numerical pair but in making a reproducible path to it.

The astronomical sections demand a different discipline. An apparent planet moves relative to the fixed stars; the Sun's observed position affects the calendar; an eclipse depends on geometric alignments. Astronomers needed numerical tables and methods for converting between angles and lengths. Trigonometric relations were not arbitrary school exercises. They helped transform angular observations into quantities a calculator could handle. Bhāskara worked with identities concerning sines of sums and differences of angles, within an Indian trigonometric tradition with its own terminology and computational conventions.

The Earth turns, celestial bodies seem to travel, and an astronomer must decide how to describe what is seen. A modern reader may identify a statement that sounds like a derivative or instantaneous motion and proclaim that Bhāskara invented calculus. That claim can obscure both his achievement and the later development of calculus. He analyzed changing quantities and rates of motion in astronomical contexts; he did not leave Newton's or Leibniz's systematic symbolic calculus. More usefully, we can ask what quantities he was comparing, how the calculation worked, and what prediction it made possible. In that narrower, evidenced sense, his mathematical astronomy contains important reasoning about change.

There is a second lesson in his astronomical writing: a calculation does not end when a relation is recognized. An astronomical procedure has to be usable repeatedly, at different dates and for different locations. A student might need to correct a mean planetary position, then determine whether the result changes the predicted time of a conjunction. Modern calculations can conceal that labor behind a computer. For a scholar using inherited tables, verse instructions and handwritten numbers, the choice and order of intermediate steps are essential. A mistake of one place value can spoil an otherwise sophisticated model. The difference between a theoretical relation and a working calendar is the arithmetic that connects them.

His treatment of the sphere also required moving between the observer's ground and the geometry of the sky. A rising time depends on latitude; day length varies with season and place. If a theoretical model describes the sky in general, the observer still needs a way to insert local circumstances. This helps explain the enduring role of spherical and trigonometric methods in Indian astronomical texts. They were not detached geometric puzzles. They supplied tools for making celestial theories answer concrete questions from different places on Earth.

The astronomical books were not merely theoretical declarations. They concerned rising and setting, conjunctions, eclipses, latitude, and the practical consequences of moving from a geometric scheme to a numerical prediction. Even a good computational model has to deal with the difference between an idealized circle and an observation made from a particular location. One can teach mathematical astronomy as a chain of adjustments: decide which motion is the baseline, determine where the observed position departs from it, apply a correction, and check the result against the phenomenon. Bhāskara's works belonged to centuries of refinement of this practice.

An astronomer also inherits errors. Tables are copied; parameters are updated or retained; a new observation can reveal that a familiar rule predicts a wrong time. A medieval writer's claims of accuracy must be read within the observational and computational possibilities of the day. Bhāskara was alert to technical mistakes in inherited methods and wrote his own explanatory and computational works. In 1183, near the end of his life, he composed the *Karaṇakutūhala*, a work intended for astronomical computation. The late date matters: he was not a brilliant young author who vanished after a single book. We can trace a career of continued scholarship across decades.

The family transmission of his mathematics continued beyond his death in 1185. An inscription records an educational endowment connected with his grandson Caṅgadeva in 1207, supporting study of the *Siddhāntaśiromaṇi*. This is stronger evidence for an actual teaching tradition than the pearl-and-water-clock romance. It tells us that the books remained valuable to people willing to support their study after their author was gone. A learned lineage could preserve mathematical techniques not by a single dramatic moment of discovery but by maintaining teachers, manuscripts and audiences.

There is a revealing historical difference between saying that a formula was known and saying that the formula's use was understood. A sine addition identity can be placed in a list, but an astronomical calculator has to know when to apply it, what angle conventions the table uses, and how much accuracy the available numerals can support. Likewise, a rule for manipulating negative numbers can be correctly stated while its geometric interpretation remains unfamiliar to a student. Bhāskara's corpus is evidence not just of isolated correct results but of a curriculum in which varied techniques were made to work together.

The division between practical astronomy and theoretical computation was porous. A planetary table could contain inherited numerical constants that were not easily justified from first principles, while the algebra used to manipulate those constants could be exceedingly sophisticated. It is misleading to judge such a text by sorting its sentences into modern categories labeled scientific, religious, practical and pure. Medieval astronomical calculation could serve ritual scheduling and still contain genuine abstract mathematics. The same scholar could be conservative about inherited cosmological assumptions and creative about the arithmetic used inside a model.

This is also why translation creates historical dangers. A nineteenth- or twentieth-century translator may express a concise Sanskrit computational rule with notation developed long after Bhāskara. That notation is often the right pedagogical tool for a modern reader, as long as we acknowledge it. Trouble begins when the ease of reading a modern expression is mistaken for evidence that the original author conceived exactly the same abstract structures. Historical precision protects the complexity of the original work rather than diminishing it.

His works would travel farther still. The *Līlāvatī* was read and commented on in India for centuries and circulated in Persian and other translations. A rule could acquire new readers without the original author having met them; a problem could be reinterpreted in another mathematical vocabulary. Translation was an act of scholarship. It chose equivalents for technical terms, explained unfamiliar examples, and sometimes introduced stories to make the work attractive. This is why the history of the mathematics and the history of the book cannot be separated. A biography based only on the earliest surviving text misses much of its later influence, while one based only on late legends risks turning scholarship into folklore.

There is a tempting way to tell Bhāskara's life as a list of firsts: the first negative numbers, the first calculus, the first solution of this or that equation. Most of those slogans collapse several centuries and several mathematical traditions into one headline. A better account observes the sophistication of a teacher who knew Brahmagupta's arithmetic, extended the handling of indeterminate equations, transmitted methods through memorable verses, and worked within astronomy's persistent demands. One achievement is intellectual; another is pedagogical; another is institutional, visible in the transmission of the books through his descendants and readers.

We cannot reconstruct his daily timetable, whether he preferred to teach at dawn, or how he reacted when a student finally solved a problem. We can say that his work required the patient management of complicated calculations and that he supplied material intended to help others learn them. He lived between worlds we now separate into arithmetic, algebra, astronomy and education. The boundaries were not the boundaries of a modern university department. Numbers told an astronomer about the heavens; the heavens gave students reasons to calculate; and a poem could carry an algorithm from one generation to another. That combination is the enduring substance of Bhāskara the teacher.

## Historical companion — not spoken

### Chronology and documentary limits

- **1114:** Traditional birth year; associated with Vijayapura in the Deccan. His father Maheśvara practiced mathematical astronomy.
- **1150:** *Siddhāntaśiromaṇi*, including the famous arithmetic, algebra and astronomical portions, conventionally dated to this year.
- **Late twelfth century:** Mathematical astronomy in the Ujjain tradition, sometimes described in later histories as leadership of an observatory; the exact institutional form is not a modern archive-backed position.
- **1183:** *Karaṇakutūhala*, computational astronomy.
- **1185:** Traditional death year.
- **1207:** Endowment associated with grandson Caṅgadeva for study of his work; strong evidence of posthumous transmission.
- **1587:** Persian translation by Fayzi. The Līlāvatī daughter's wedding and water-clock story is a much later legend, not contemporary biography.

### Exact mathematical companions

- **A model quadratic:** \(x^2=49\), so \(x=\pm7\) when negative values are permitted.
- **Cyclic equation:** \(61x^2+1=y^2\), with \(x=226153980\), \(y=1766319049\) (this is the accepted numerical solution; read the spoken verbalization with care).
- **Check:** \(61(226153980)^2+1=(1766319049)^2\).
- **Trigonometric relation:** \(\sin(a+b)=\sin a\cos b+\cos a\sin b\), stated here in modern notation for comparison, not as a claim of historical notation.
- **Division by zero:** Bhāskara's historical rules require contextual interpretation; ordinary real-number division by zero is undefined, and his language should not be equated with a fully modern limit.
