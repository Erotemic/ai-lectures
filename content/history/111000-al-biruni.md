# 111000 — Al-Biruni: Measuring the world across languages and empires

**Track:** History — People and ideas. **Focus:** Khwarazm, the political ruptures of 995 and 1017, measurement and error, comparative calendars, Sanskrit learning and *India*, mathematics, astronomy, geography and scientific patronage. **Background:** ratios, angles, shadows and calendars are explained in the spoken narrative.

Imagine two astronomers in different cities, looking at the same eclipse of the Moon. The eclipse is a shared event, but each astronomer records its time using a local clock. The difference between their local times contains information about how far apart their cities lie east and west.

There is an important complication. The eclipse is visible over a broad region, but the recorded time of its beginning depends on how a particular observer defines and detects contact between shadow and lunar disk. Local time itself must be related to the Sun's position. Thus the same event can provide a shared reference only if the observers agree about what has been timed and convert the observations carefully. In a modern laboratory we might demand synchronized clocks; in al-Biruni's setting astronomy itself offered the synchronization. The method captures an astonishing idea: celestial phenomena can help coordinate measurements made by people who have never stood side by side.

If the Earth rotates once in twenty-four hours, an hour of time difference corresponds to one twenty-fourth of a full turn, fifteen degrees of longitude. In principle, a celestial event becomes a surveying instrument large enough to span a continent.

Around the year 997, al-Biruni in Khwarazm and Abu'l-Wafa in Baghdad arranged observations of a lunar eclipse to help compare their positions. The instruments, clocks and observations were not perfect. That is precisely why the episode matters. Al-Biruni did not merely celebrate the idea that mathematics could measure the world. He worried about how a measurement was made, how an instrument might distort it, what assumptions an observer had silently inserted, and how to compare records produced by people far apart.

Abu Rayhan Muhammad ibn Ahmad al-Biruni was born in 973 in Khwarazm, the region south of the Aral Sea, in territory now associated with Uzbekistan. The Arabic term attached to his name is sometimes connected with the outer district of the city of Kath. He grew up at a crossroads of Iranian and Central Asian political and scholarly cultures. The names of his parents and the full conditions of his household are not securely documented. What we know instead is that he was able to study with learned people and gain access to the mathematics and astronomy used in the region's courts.

His early teacher Abu Nasr Mansur was a mathematician and astronomer connected with the ruling family of Khwarazm. That relationship mattered. Learning advanced astronomy required books, skilled teachers, and access to instruments. It also required enough time to do calculations that might not yield immediate political advantage. Courts could supply those resources, while their rivalries could remove them without warning. Al-Biruni's career was repeatedly redirected by that bargain.

As a young man he measured the latitude of Kath from the Sun's height in the sky. Latitude is your north-south position on Earth. Imagine the Sun at its highest point on a particular day. If you know its declination, meaning its angular displacement north or south of the celestial equator, then the observed noon altitude constrains your latitude. The relationship is simple in idea but technically demanding in practice. Your instrument must read small angular differences reliably, the horizon and vertical reference must be known, and the calendar date must be correct.

Before he was twenty-five, al-Biruni was also interested in map projections. There is no perfectly faithful method for laying a curved surface flat. If you preserve one property, another may be distorted. In a projection, the size or shape of a region can change depending on where you place it.

If you try to peel a globe's surface and lay it on a table, something must stretch or tear. A mathematical projection chooses a rule for assigning each point on the globe a point on the map. Different rules serve different purposes. A traveler may want reliable bearings, a geographer may wish to compare areas, while an astronomer may need accurate positions relative to known circles on the sphere. No simple choice preserves every distance between every pair of places. Al-Biruni's interest in these problems reveals that he was not merely accumulating coordinates; he was studying how a numerical representation alters the thing represented.

Al-Biruni examined earlier projection methods and proposed alternatives. A map is not merely a picture of territory: it is a mathematical contract between the geometry of the Earth and the geometry of a page.

Then politics intervened. In 995 the ruling order in Khwarazm changed, and al-Biruni left the region. He spent time around Rayy, near modern Tehran, where he lacked reliable patronage and experienced material difficulty. The details of some travels have to be reconstructed from references and astronomical observations, not from an uninterrupted diary. His record is unusually rich because eclipses and observations can be dated. He could leave a trace of his whereabouts by noting where and when he saw the Moon darken.

At Rayy he examined the work of the astronomer al-Khujandi, whose monumental sextant had been built to observe the Sun's position. Al-Biruni argued that some of the instrument's error arose from the physical structure settling under its own weight. The precision of a number cannot exceed the stability of the device that produced it. A massive, carefully graduated instrument can still be wrong if the thing holding it moves. That observation sounds familiar to anyone who has ever calibrated a modern laboratory instrument, but in a tenth-century astronomical setting it shows attention to the material mechanics of measurement.

Al-Biruni's movements took him to different courts. In Gurgan, under the patronage of Qabus, he composed the work often called the *Chronology of Ancient Nations*, around the year 1000. This book is not simply a collection of dates. Different communities count years from different epochs, use different lengths of month, insert leap days or intercalary months, and commemorate events according to religious or political traditions. To compare historical statements across cultures, the scholar must first determine which date system each speaker is using.

Imagine two records which say a king died in the fifth year of a reign and during a named festival. Without the rules of the relevant calendars, these clues cannot be mapped onto one modern date. Some calendars follow lunar months; others track the solar year; still others combine both. A twelve-month lunar year is roughly eleven days shorter than a solar year, so a festival can drift through the seasons unless a system has a means of correction. Al-Biruni treated such differences not as curiosities but as calculable structures. Comparative history needed mathematical conversion.

This demand made the historian something like a careful accountant of epochs. A date can be precise within one culture's system and still be ambiguous to a reader who does not know when the year begins, whether months are inserted, or how an accession year is counted. A ruler's inscription and a religious festival might give mutually inconsistent dates until the conventions are reconstructed. Al-Biruni's collection of calendar systems was therefore an argument against a lazy kind of universal chronology. The world was not obliged to arrange all its past events along the same scale without a translation rule.

The work also demanded languages and the interpretation of texts. An astronomer could compare the timing of a celestial event with a documentary claim. A historian could ask whether a month name, once translated, referred to the same season for two writers. Al-Biruni's range of scholarship therefore grew from a practical recognition: to measure the world accurately, one must often understand the people who describe it. Numerical error and cultural misunderstanding can produce the same false result.

He returned to Khwarazm, where the Ma'munid court sponsored astronomical research. He and Abu Nasr Mansur again worked in an environment rich in scientific contact. With patronage he could build or use instruments for repeated solar observations. It would be easy to imagine this as the happy restoration of a stable scholarly home. But the politics of eastern Iran and Central Asia were shifting toward the expanding Ghaznavid power, centered in Ghazna.

In 1017 Mahmud of Ghazna conquered Khwarazm. Al-Biruni and other scholars were brought into the Ghaznavid sphere. Describing the change as a pleasant move to a new research institute would misrepresent the historical situation. His freedom of movement and relationship with Mahmud are contested; some evidence suggests he was effectively compelled to accompany the conqueror. The same political power that supported large scholarly undertakings could be responsible for military coercion and destructive campaigns. These are not unrelated footnotes. They shaped where al-Biruni could travel, whom he could meet, and what he could write.

Mahmud's campaigns reached into the Indian subcontinent. Al-Biruni came into contact with Indian intellectual traditions and learned Sanskrit well enough to study significant texts. The exact number of trips, the places he visited, and the degree of official support he received are not always clear. What is clear is the ambition of the work that followed. Instead of collecting only exotic anecdotes, he attempted systematic explanation of Indian doctrines, customs, calendars, mathematics, astronomy and religious thought, often comparing them with traditions already familiar to him.

The major book conventionally known as al-Biruni's *India* was completed around 1030. It asks, over and over, what people in one culture mean by a technical term that a reader from another culture might misinterpret. He discusses Sanskrit authorities, including mathematical astronomers such as Aryabhata and Brahmagupta. He tried to make their calculations intelligible in Arabic, a language of scientific writing across much of the Islamic scholarly world. Yet he also evaluated claims critically and was capable of misunderstanding the people he studied. The text is evidence of intellectual effort, not of perfect modern neutrality.

He sometimes described the distances between intellectual communities in severe language, and his own religious and social standpoint shaped what he noticed. Yet he could also distinguish what Indian scholars said from what critics alleged about them. That habit matters in a historical setting where translation could easily become caricature. A mathematician examining a foreign astronomical table might reject a number because of measurement, reject a model because of physical theory, or misunderstand a computation because of a different starting epoch. These are three very different kinds of disagreement. Al-Biruni worked hard to tell them apart, even when he did not always succeed.

Here is an example of why language matters in mathematics. In one tradition, a sine is the length of a specific segment in a circle of chosen radius. In another, the same trigonometric relationship might be represented as a ratio with no dimensions. Translating the word alone is insufficient. One must track which circle is assumed, what unit counts the arc, and how the numerical table was scaled. Al-Biruni's encounters with Indian mathematics required such attention to conventions, alongside the study of astronomical and philosophical arguments.

He was not the first scholar to link Sanskrit and Arabic scientific cultures. Translation and adaptation had been underway for centuries, and knowledge traveled through many intermediaries. Al-Biruni's distinction lies in his sustained comparative project and the detail of the surviving record. He did not invent Indian numerals, Indian astronomy, or the scientific exchange between languages. He treated the exchange itself as a subject worth studying.

There was also the problem of the Earth's dimensions. Al-Biruni described procedures for determining Earth's radius using angular measurements, including a celebrated method based on viewing the horizon from an elevated place such as a mountain. The geometric idea is elegant. From the peak, sight a line that just grazes the spherical Earth at the horizon. The line of sight is tangent to the sphere, so the radius drawn to the tangent point meets it at a right angle. The observer, Earth's center, and the horizon point form a right triangle.

Suppose the mountain's height above the surrounding level is known. Suppose you can accurately measure how far below a horizontal direction the visible horizon lies. That is a very small angle, called the dip. Geometry links the dip, the mountain height, and the Earth's radius. The calculation is straightforward in a modern companion equation, but the measurement is perilous.

Take an illustrative mountain one hundred meters high above the surrounding plain and an Earth radius of about six thousand three hundred seventy kilometers. The dip of the horizon in the ideal spherical model is only a little more than three tenths of a degree. An angular error of even a few hundredths of a degree can matter greatly when the desired result is millions of meters. The larger the scale you infer from a small observable difference, the more carefully you must understand instrument resolution and local conditions. We should never judge an ancient geodesist only by whether a modern computer, using selected numbers, reproduces a remarkably close final radius.

A tiny error in a small angle can change a large inferred radius. The atmosphere bends light, sea or plain horizons may be indistinct, and mountain heights are not trivial to determine. A plausible historical calculation is not automatically a modern geodetic survey.

The famous radius estimate attributed to al-Biruni has often been praised for its closeness to modern values. Historians have examined the methods, manuscript traditions, and units behind the reported number. We can say he understood and analyzed a powerful geometrical procedure without pretending every published modern conversion is equally secure. His achievement involved relating local measurements to a global object and explaining the assumptions connecting them.

In another class of problems he addressed the distance between cities. Knowing their latitudes is not enough: points on the same latitude can be separated by an enormous east-west distance. A difference in longitude adds another angular relation. When you have both coordinates, spherical geometry tells you the angular separation along the Earth's surface. Multiply the angle, expressed in radians, by a suitable Earth radius, and you obtain the distance along a great circle. In the historical form, calculations used trigonometric tables and conventions different from our notation. The governing insight is the same: longitude, latitude, and distance live on a sphere, not on a sheet of graph paper.

Astronomical practice repeatedly brought him back to the distinction between theory and instrument. A computed planetary position assumes some geometry of the heavens, a value for a period, an epoch from which time is counted, and a way of interpolating tabular values. If an observation disagrees with a prediction, which assumption failed? Perhaps the instrument shifted. Perhaps the table uses a different calendar. Perhaps the observer's geographic coordinates were wrong. Or perhaps the astronomical model needs improvement. Separating these possibilities is scientific work of a very high order.

Al-Biruni corresponded and argued with other major scholars, among them Ibn Sina, known in Latin as Avicenna. Their surviving exchange addressed questions in natural philosophy, not just arithmetic. They did not share every assumption about matter, space, and the heavens. Intellectual disagreement required access to the other person's argument in a transmissible form: letters, treatises, and the vocabulary of a scholarly language. We can see an intellectual community spread across political boundaries even while those boundaries were being redrawn by war.

Under later Ghaznavid rulers, including Mas'ud, al-Biruni pursued major astronomical writing. The work known as the *Canon of Mas'ud* synthesized advanced mathematical astronomy. It was joined by treatises on mathematical instruction, minerals, pharmacology, geography, and related subjects. The sheer range sometimes tempts biographers to call him the inventor of every science mentioned in his books. That is not what broad learning means. He drew on Greek, Iranian, Indian, and Arabic-language scholarship; he compared, criticized, calculated, and sometimes broke new ground.

His treatment of inherited knowledge was not a simple opposition between tradition and innovation. A Greek geometrical theorem could provide a proof technique. An Indian astronomical table could offer parameters worth checking against observations. A Persian historical calendar could supply chronological data no Greek source contained. The scholar's task was to ask which claims could be combined, which needed correction, and which had been translated incorrectly. By that standard the most impressive feature of his range is not the number of disciplines named on a list. It is the recurrent method of comparative scrutiny.

His lifetime also raises hard questions about scientific patronage. A court may commission the very measurements used to extend administrative control. A scholar may carry sophisticated methods into a conquered region under conditions that offer him little freedom. A work of comparative scholarship may be intellectually generous while being inseparable from an imperial encounter. Treating al-Biruni only as a model of tolerance would erase the violence around him. Treating him only as a servant of conquerors would erase the intellectual independence visible in his methods and criticism.

The final years of his life are not documented with the precision that some modern biographical tables suggest. The conventional death date is around 1048, although some scholarly discussions point to notices that complicate this chronology. The discrepancy is worth recording. Even for a scientist who used eclipses as chronological anchors, a modern historian must distinguish explicit evidence from a date copied through reference works.

What kind of life have we been able to recover? Not a continuous domestic story. We do not know his everyday routine in the way we know that of some modern scholars. But we know a great deal about his working obligations: changing courts, forced political movement, study across languages, observational records, collaboration with teachers, debates with philosophers, and the difficulty of making measurements trustworthy. There is no sharp division between the human part and the mathematical part. The politics explains the travels; the travels explain the languages; the languages explain the comparative texts; and the texts explain why precise astronomy remained a problem of interpretation.

Think again of the eclipse observed in two cities. It is a natural event that nobody at either court controlled. Each observer could record it from a different place, with a different instrument and a different local time. They could then compare observations and recover something about the Earth between them. That is the pattern of al-Biruni's career. He worked among different governments, religions, calendars, and scientific traditions, each offering a partial description of the world. His most lasting habit was to ask what could be compared, what had to be translated, and what the numbers actually justified. He wanted more than a large collection of facts. He wanted a disciplined way to put them into relation.

## Historical companion — not spoken

### Evidentiary chronology

- **973:** Born in or near Kath in Khwarazm. Abu Nasr Mansur becomes a key early teacher and collaborator.
- **c. 990:** Made latitude observations near Kath as a young scholar; later examined instruments and map projections.
- **995:** Dynastic upheaval forced him away from Khwarazm; time in Rayy and other courts involved uncertain patronage.
- **997:** Coordinated lunar-eclipse comparison with Abu'l-Wafa in Baghdad for longitude determination.
- **c. 1000:** Composed the *Chronology of Ancient Nations* under the patronage of Qabus at Gurgan.
- **1017:** Conquest of Khwarazm by Mahmud of Ghazna; he entered the Ghaznavid sphere under circumstances that should not be assumed voluntary.
- **c. 1030:** Completed *India* and pursued major work in astronomy, mathematics and geography under later Ghaznavid rulers.
- **c. 1048 or later:** Death, with some disputes among modern chronologies.

### Mathematical companion

- **Longitude via common eclipse:** A difference of one hour in local timing corresponds to \(360^\circ/24=15^\circ\) of Earth rotation, before correction for calendar standards and observational error.
- **Latitude:** Solar altitude at meridian transit, together with declination, constrains an observer's terrestrial latitude; the relevant geometry changes by hemisphere and season.
- **Radius from mountain horizon dip:** For mountain height \(h\), radius \(R\), and dip below the local horizontal \(\delta\), tangent geometry yields \(\cos\delta=R/(R+h)\), hence \(R=h\cos\delta/(1-\cos\delta)\). Ignore atmospheric refraction only for this idealized demonstration.
- **Great-circle distance:** In a spherical model \(d=R\arccos(\sin\phi_1\sin\phi_2+\cos\phi_1\cos\phi_2\cos(\lambda_1-\lambda_2))\); latitude \(\phi\), longitude \(\lambda\), and angles in radians. This is modern compact notation for relations addressed through historical trigonometry.
- **Comparative calendars:** A lunar year of twelve synodic months is roughly 354 days; a tropical solar year is about 365.24 days. Comparison requires identifying epoch, month rules and intercalation.
