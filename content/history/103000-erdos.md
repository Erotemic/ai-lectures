# 103000 - Paul Erdős: A mathematical life without borders

**Track:** History - People and ideas. **Focus:** Budapest, family, persecution and exile, elementary and probabilistic number theory, collaborative networks, the probabilistic method, combinatorics, friendships, students and late career. **Background:** primes, elementary counting and probability are explained in speech.

Paul Erdős spent much of his life traveling with a small amount of luggage, arriving at the homes and offices of mathematicians and inviting them to solve problems. This image is so memorable that it threatens to swallow the mathematician himself. A man living out of a suitcase, asking for coffee, assigning numbers to his collaborators and cash prizes to his problems: it sounds like a collection of stories rather than an account of mathematical work. But Erdős helped change what mathematicians considered a fruitful problem, how they used probability, and how they worked together. He did not build a single grand theory. He opened doors in number theory, combinatorics, graph theory and the study of random structures, and other people walked through them.

The mathematician was born Pál Erdős in Budapest on March twenty-sixth, nineteen thirteen, in a city that was then part of the Austro-Hungarian Empire. His parents, Anna and Lajos, were both mathematics teachers. A terrible loss had just come to the family: his two older sisters died of scarlet fever in the days surrounding his birth. Anna became intensely protective of her surviving child. The family's experience is worth knowing not because tragedy somehow created a mathematical talent, but because it helps explain the unusual childhood that followed. Erdős was not the child of strangers to mathematics. He grew up among people for whom numbers, proofs, and teaching were daily work.

The First World War soon disrupted the household again. Lajos was called into the army, taken prisoner by Russian forces, and spent years in Siberia. Anna taught while a German-speaking caregiver helped look after Paul. When his father returned in nineteen twenty, he brought languages acquired in captivity. In a short autobiographical account written near the end of his life, Erdős remembered playing with the calendar because he wanted to know when school holidays would bring his mother home. Arithmetic was woven into an ordinary child's desire to have his parent nearby. He also recalled that, around the age of four, he reasoned about what happens when one subtracts a larger number from a smaller one. The negative-number story comes from his own later memory, not an independently recorded childhood experiment.

For much of his early education Erdős studied at home. His parents could teach him at a level few elementary teachers were prepared to offer. Lajos showed him proofs about the prime numbers, those whole numbers greater than one that have no positive divisors except one and themselves. An argument that there are infinitely many primes could be understood without sophisticated machinery. Suppose the list of primes were complete. Multiply them all together and add one. The result is not divisible by any prime on the list, because division by each leaves remainder one. Yet every integer greater than one must have a prime divisor. So the supposed complete list cannot be complete. The proof is ancient, but for a child it can produce the thrilling sensation that reason can reach infinitely far.

Budapest also offered a culture of mathematical problems outside the family. A remarkable Hungarian journal for secondary-school students, often called KöMaL, published challenges and solutions, connecting students who might otherwise never have met. Mathematics competitions and university seminars gave ambitious young people reasons to test one another's ideas. This culture mattered to Erdős's style. A good problem was not merely an examination question to be marked right or wrong. It was a way to start a friendship, find a new method, or expose something that existing theory could not explain.

The political setting was not benign. Hungary after the First World War experienced upheaval, counterrevolution, antisemitic violence, and the numerus clausus law restricting Jewish access to higher education. Erdős was from a Jewish family, though his parents were not religious observers. He later said that, as a young boy, he resisted a suggestion that the family convert under social pressure. It is tempting to quote the episode as proof of a fully formed adult political philosophy. He was remembering a child's response decades later. What can be said with confidence is that exclusion and fear formed part of his education in the larger world, while his family and mathematical friends gave him access to another world in which a well-posed question could earn attention.

The young circle included Paul Turán and Tibor Gallai, and later a remarkable network of Hungarian mathematicians who would themselves influence number theory and combinatorics. Leopold Fejér, famous for work in mathematical analysis, mentored a generation at the University of Budapest. Dénes König had made graph theory a serious subject of study. Erdős took mathematics courses and soon began doing research. A graph, here, means a collection of points and links between them. We can model the friendships in a class by putting a point for each child and drawing a line whenever two children know each other. Questions about such diagrams seem easy to state. Their answers can become unexpectedly difficult.

One of Erdős's early achievements was a short proof of a theorem associated with Joseph Bertrand and Pafnuty Chebyshev. For every whole number greater than one, there is a prime strictly between that number and twice that number. Take ten: eleven, thirteen, seventeen and nineteen all lie between ten and twenty. The question is not whether there are primes in the examples we can check, but whether there must be a prime for every size of number. Chebyshev had proved the result decades earlier. Erdős did not discover the theorem. As a teenager he found a strikingly elementary argument that made the result accessible by a different route.

This distinction is central to his style. Erdős often wanted an elegant route through a problem that many people knew was true. For him a short proof was not a mere shortcut; it could reveal an unsuspected relationship among quantities. But elegance is partly an aesthetic judgment. Someone who has spent years developing an abstract framework may see beauty in the framework itself, while Erdős might see beauty in a clever use of a binomial coefficient. Both are mathematical creativity. His appetite for specific problems made him highly productive and occasionally placed him at odds with colleagues who wanted a more unified theory.

At the university, Erdős wrote a doctoral dissertation in number theory under Fejér's formal supervision. He completed his degree in nineteen thirty-four. By then the political situation across Europe was growing more threatening. The mathematician Louis Mordell arranged a fellowship for him at Manchester in Britain. Erdős traveled there, but rarely confined himself to one campus. He visited Cambridge, London and other centers, seeking out mathematicians whose questions interested him. The habit later described as wandering was already visible before the Second World War, but at this point it had practical reasons too. A young researcher without secure long-term academic employment needed patrons, invitations and friendships.

In Britain he worked with Richard Rado, another scholar who had fled the Nazis, and developed what became important results in combinatorics. He also remained connected to George Szekeres, Esther Klein, Turán and other friends from Budapest. The Erdős–Szekeres theorem describes a surprising inevitability in ordered sequences: in a sufficiently long list of distinct numbers, one can always find a sizable subsequence that rises throughout or falls throughout. The order need not be consecutive. For instance, a chaotic list of nine distinct numbers must contain a rising or falling subsequence of at least three numbers, though a particular set may contain longer ones. The general guarantee says that a sequence longer than the product of two thresholds forces a rising subsequence of one length or a falling subsequence of the other.

Why care about that? Because the theorem transforms disorder into a structured pattern that must be present, even when no deliberate structure was put into the list. It belongs to the same family of ideas as Ramsey theory, which asks how large a complicated system must become before some regular configuration is unavoidable. Such theorems suit a mathematical imagination interested in small local conditions and startling global conclusions. They also suit collaboration. One person poses a geometric puzzle about points; another translates it into an ordered sequence; a third sees the right counting argument.

The Szekeres collaboration belonged to a recognizable human story. In a famous problem about convex polygons among points in the plane, Esther Klein posed the question that led to the joint work of Erdős and George Szekeres. Erdős later called it the happy-ending problem because Klein and Szekeres married. The nickname has lasted, but it can obscure Klein's role if the story names only the two men whose names are attached to the published theorem. It is better to remember that the mathematics emerged from a social group in which ideas passed among several people.

In nineteen thirty-eight, as Europe approached war, Erdős left the Continent and went to the United States. He held a fellowship at the Institute for Advanced Study in Princeton. From the security of America he worried about those who remained in Hungary. His mother survived the war, but many relatives and acquaintances did not. Mathematics continued; mourning and uncertainty continued alongside it. Accounts written after the war sometimes turn his later mobility into a carefree personal preference. That leaves out the fact that a Jewish scholar's movement across borders in the nineteen thirties and forties was partly a matter of survival.

America offered powerful mathematical institutions but not a guaranteed professional home. He spent time at Princeton, the University of Pennsylvania, Notre Dame, and other places, working with people who welcomed him. He was a brilliant visitor and not always easy to fit into a conventional faculty structure. A university appointment involves teaching, committees, and obligations to students over years. Erdős wanted the ability to follow a problem wherever it led. The tension between institutional stability and intellectual freedom became a feature of his adult life, not merely a comic anecdote about somebody who forgot to own a house.

The war years produced important mathematics. With Mark Kac, Erdős developed a theorem showing that the number of distinct prime factors of a large integer has a kind of statistical regularity. The primes are the indivisible building blocks of ordinary multiplication, yet the way many different primes occur among large integers can be described by probability. Imagine choosing a whole number uniformly from all numbers up to a very large limit. Count how many different primes divide it. The typical count is around the logarithm of the logarithm of the limit, and its fluctuations, after suitable rescaling, approach the familiar bell-shaped normal distribution. This is the Erdős–Kac theorem.

The result is surprising because prime factorization is exact and arithmetic, while a bell curve suggests repeated random noise. A particular integer is not random in its factorization; its prime factors are fixed. Randomness enters when we select an integer from a large collection and ask how a statistic varies. Erdős and Kac showed that ideas usually associated with probability could illuminate number theory without changing the underlying arithmetic facts. One of Erdős's strengths was noticing when a technique from one subject would pay off in another, especially if the definitions could be kept plain.

By the late nineteen forties the distribution of prime numbers led him into a celebrated episode. The prime number theorem says, roughly, that the number of primes at most a very large number x is close to x divided by the natural logarithm of x. Gauss and Legendre had investigated such patterns, and Jacques Hadamard and Charles-Jean de la Vallée Poussin proved the theorem independently in eighteen ninety-six by methods using complex analysis. For decades mathematicians asked whether the result could be shown without that machinery. An elementary proof, in this context, did not mean easy. It meant a proof avoiding certain higher analytic tools.

Erdős and the Norwegian mathematician Atle Selberg contributed decisive work to such a proof in nineteen forty-eight. Selberg had developed a crucial identity, while Erdős supplied arguments about the spacing of primes that helped complete the route. A disagreement followed over whether the results should be presented jointly or in separate papers, and about how to assign credit. The correspondence and later historical studies document more than one perspective. A popular retelling in which one mathematician simply stole the other's proof does not adequately describe the mathematics or the publication history. Selberg's contribution was substantial, and so was Erdős's. The elementary proof and the dispute are both real; the two men should not be reduced to moral characters in a legend.

The argument's appeal to Erdős is clear. A theorem that seemed to require formidable tools could be reached by a combination of identities, inequalities and reasoning about how primes can be distributed. But there is a lesson about collaboration here too. Shared ideas are not always neatly divisible into ownership. A proof may become possible only after one participant's insight makes another participant's insight usable. The very speed of mathematical conversation can outrun conventions about who will publish first and what form a joint result should take. The episode was an uncomfortable exception to Erdős's broadly generous attitude toward mathematical credit and joint work, but it also shows that his career was not free of rivalry.

Another strand of his work was already gathering force: the thought that randomness can prove the existence of something completely definite. Suppose we want to establish that a large graph has a property but cannot find a simple explicit example. If a randomly chosen graph has a positive chance of having the property, then at least one such graph must exist. It sounds almost too easy. The hard part is choosing the random model and estimating the chance of failure. In nineteen forty-seven Erdős used this idea to obtain lower bounds in Ramsey theory. We will return to the method, because its influence extends into computer science, information theory and combinatorial geometry.

The world that Erdős had left behind in Budapest had been damaged by war and transformed by new political borders. The world he entered in America gave him mathematical colleagues but not complete security. Neither experience stopped him from carrying questions from one place to another. What changed in the next decades was the scale. People began to expect that he might appear with an idea, a problem, or an unexpected suggestion; young researchers found that a short conversation with him could lead to a publication. The traveler became an institution of his own, one that had no fixed address.

Erdős's childhood in a home of mathematics teachers and his adulthood among migrating colleagues were not separate stories. Together they made him attentive to what one person could learn from another. The most important thing he carried from city to city was not a theorem printed on paper, but a practice: begin with a question simple enough to share, refuse to accept that a subject has no more surprises, and discover who else might see what you cannot. The next part of his life reveals what happened when that practice was carried out across an entire mathematical world.

The early career had already taught Erdős to move among people and questions. Now that habit would turn into a global network, and the probabilistic method would become one of his most powerful tools. The rest of his life is not a separate story. It is what happened when those early ways of working were carried into an increasingly international mathematical world.

By the nineteen fifties Erdős was known across number theory and combinatorics as a restless and highly productive problem solver. He had lived and worked in several countries, many of them connected by the forced movement of scholars during the Second World War. The war had ended, but movement remained politically charged. In nineteen fifty-four the United States refused him a re-entry visa after travel involving Hungary, then governed by a communist regime. Questions about his political associations, and the often rigid administrative thinking of the Cold War, put restrictions on a scholar who had already escaped antisemitic persecution in Europe. He worked for years from Israel and elsewhere and was eventually able to return to the United States during the nineteen sixties. This was not the freedom of someone gliding effortlessly above states and borders. A traveler can have friends everywhere and still be stopped at the frontier.

Erdős did not create a normal permanent household when the visa problem ended. Instead he continued to circulate through the homes of mathematicians and universities, usually with few possessions and a rapidly changing set of plans. Some descriptions make this sound like a performance staged for admirers. It was more practical than that. He wanted to maximize the time he could spend thinking with others. Friends such as Ronald Graham helped him keep papers, correspondence, and other necessities organized. The generosity was real, but so was the labor behind it. A free-roaming academic depended on people who had homes, families, offices and obligations of their own. The traveler and the host did not carry equal logistical burdens.

Ronald Graham became especially important. They met at a mathematical meeting in the nineteen sixties and worked together on number theory and combinatorics. Graham and his wife became part of Erdős's extended practical support network, providing space and help with correspondence and arrangements. Graham was also a major mathematician in his own right, not a mere administrator for a celebrated friend. The two exchanged problems for decades. One lesson of their friendship is that mathematical collaboration need not mean two people contributing identical types of insight. Someone can pose a sharp question, someone else can discover an approach, and the value of each may become visible only after a solution has been found.

Erdős's mother, Anna, survived the Holocaust and lived to see his international career. During the nineteen sixties, when he could travel more freely, she sometimes accompanied him, despite her advanced age, and continued doing so until her death in nineteen seventy-one. The image complicates the familiar story of the solitary mathematical wanderer. He had a close family bond. The household of his childhood, where both parents taught mathematics, continued to matter to him decades later. Losing Anna was a profound change. Friends recall his intensified work after her death, but it would be reductive to say that one event mechanically caused a level of productivity that had been extraordinary for many years.

To see what he offered his collaborators, consider a problem about the ways people can be connected. Imagine six people at a party. Draw a line between any two who know each other, and color the line blue. Draw a red line between any two who do not know each other. Can one arrange all fifteen lines so that there is no blue triangle and no red triangle? No. Every way of coloring those edges forces a triangle of one color. This is a small example of a Ramsey theorem: as a system gets large enough, complete disorder becomes impossible in a precise combinatorial sense.

For large groups, though, finding the exact thresholds can be very hard. Instead of constructing a clever coloring by hand, Erdős asked what would happen if every edge were colored independently, with each color equally likely. For any particular set of k people, the chance that all the edges among them share a single color is small: there are only two monochromatic choices among a much larger number of possible colorings. There are many different groups of k people, but we can add upper bounds on their failure probabilities. If the total is less than one, then there must be at least one coloring with no monochromatic group of that size. We have proved the existence of an arrangement without ever exhibiting which arrangement it is.

This is a simple form of the probabilistic method. The randomness is in the reasoning, not necessarily in the final object. The object whose existence we establish can be a completely ordinary, fixed, finite graph. Before Erdős, probability had appeared in combinatorics, and he was not the first mathematician to use a random choice in an existence argument. What he did was make this way of thinking powerful and systematic, especially in work beginning with his nineteen forty-seven bounds on Ramsey numbers. Later researchers, including Joel Spencer and many others, would develop the method into a wide set of techniques with applications well beyond graph coloring.

The result can feel unsatisfying. If the argument says there is a coloring with a desired property, why not tell us what the coloring is? In some applications one does need an explicit construction. But an existence proof is still a theorem, and it may show that our failure to find an example is a limitation of our search method, not a proof that the example cannot exist. It can also suggest which features a constructive search should pursue. Erdős's thought was often to cross this boundary first: show that an object has to be there, then leave an invitation to build it.

A related surprise appeared in his work on graphs with two apparently conflicting properties. The girth of a graph is the length of its shortest cycle. A graph with very large girth has no short loops, so when viewed in small neighborhoods it resembles a tree. Trees are easy to color so that adjacent vertices have different colors: two colors suffice. The chromatic number of a graph is the minimum number of colors needed to color all its vertices while ensuring that linked vertices receive different colors. Erdős proved that there are finite graphs with both arbitrarily large girth and arbitrarily large chromatic number. Locally, they can look simple; globally, they are hard to color.

Why does that matter? It warns us that examining only small patches may fail to reveal an object's global difficulty. A graph can have no triangles, no squares, and no other short cycles up to a chosen length, yet still force a great many colors. The proof again uses carefully chosen random graphs and estimates. Its conclusion is a precise existence theorem, not a claim that every graph with few short cycles is difficult to color. The gap between local appearance and global structure became a recurring theme in modern combinatorics and theoretical computer science.

There is an instructive contrast between this probabilistic style and the tradition of giving an explicit construction. If someone hands us a complicated graph, we can check a property by examining edges and vertices. The probabilistic argument may prove that a graph with the required property exists without saying which graph to look at. This frustrates people who want to see the object, yet it can open questions that seemed inaccessible by construction alone. Later researchers sometimes found efficient randomized algorithms, and sometimes found entirely deterministic arguments suggested by probabilistic proofs. Erdős's contribution was not to declare randomness superior to construction. It was to show that carefully controlled uncertainty could be an instrument of certainty.

This also explains the importance of sharp examples. Suppose we prove that every sufficiently large graph has a certain pattern. A counterexample below the threshold is not merely an annoyance. It tells us whether the theorem's numerical estimate can be improved. Erdős was famous for asking such questions in forms that other mathematicians could understand quickly and then spend years trying to answer. His problems had a social life: a visitor could take one home, adapt it to a new subject, and return with a proof or a stronger conjecture.

With Alfred Rényi, Erdős also helped make random graphs a central subject in their own right. Instead of using randomness only to prove that some difficult graph exists, one can ask what a typical graph looks like when its edges are chosen by a specified random rule. How many links must we expect before a giant connected component appears? At what stage does a small graph change from scattered islands into a network dominated by one connected cluster? How likely are cycles, isolated vertices or other configurations? Their work in the late nineteen fifties and early nineteen sixties explored such questions and gave language to thresholds and sudden structural changes. Other researchers, including Edgar Gilbert, studied related random graph models; it is important not to compress the whole history into one pair of names.

A mathematical network can change abruptly even when we add edges one by one. Early on, new links join small groups. Later, at certain ranges of connection density, a large connected component becomes likely. This is the kind of phenomenon that now appears in discussions of communication networks, epidemics and the internet. The models have limits: real human relationships are not usually independent random coin flips, and real networks have communities, incentives and geography. The value is that a minimal model makes a structural question answerable, giving researchers a baseline against which the complications of the real world can be understood.

Erdős's working range was wider than random graphs. In number theory he asked about how primes are spaced, how integers can be represented as sums, which sets of numbers can avoid certain patterns, and what happens for almost all large integers. In extremal combinatorics he asked how much structure one can fit into a finite system without forcing a forbidden configuration. The Erdős–Ko–Rado theorem, developed with Chao Ko and Richard Rado, is a beautiful example. Suppose we collect many subsets of the same size from a fixed universe, and require that every pair of chosen subsets has at least one element in common. How large can the collection be? Under the relevant size conditions, the extremal family consists of all subsets containing one designated element. It feels natural after hearing it, but proving that no more elaborate intersecting family can be larger is the mathematical achievement.

The theorem illustrates another feature of Erdős's talent: he looked for sharp thresholds and extremal examples. What is the maximum number of edges a graph can have without creating a certain pattern? What is the smallest collection that guarantees an intersection? How far can primes be apart? Sometimes a single example sets a lower bound, while a general counting argument proves an upper bound; when the two meet, the problem is solved. He could take these seemingly separate questions into a common conversation about how large a configuration can become before it must contain structure.

Other collaborators enlarged the range further. With Mark Kac he had connected number theory to a normal distribution; with Rényi he explored random graphs; with Turán, Szekeres, Rado, Graham and many others he pursued arguments of very different kinds. Some later work used advanced tools and long technical reasoning. The stereotype that he did only tiny elementary puzzles is false. Short problem statements can be gateways to formidable proofs. Likewise, the claim that he single-handedly invented discrete mathematics erases predecessors such as König and many contemporary research communities. Erdős helped reorganize and invigorate those communities, partly by treating combinatorial problems as central rather than peripheral.

For a younger researcher, an invitation from Erdős could change a professional path. A well-posed problem is a compact form of trust: it says that the person being addressed may have something new to contribute. Erdős worked across differences of age and institutional status, but it is important not to exaggerate that openness into a claim that everyone enjoyed equal access to his network. Travel, visas, language, and funding still determined who could meet whom. He moved through a world in which mathematical internationalism had to coexist with Cold War restrictions and the historical scars left by fascism and war.

His mother Anna became an especially important traveling companion in her later years. Their closeness was not a footnote to the eccentricity of a man without a conventional home; it was part of the life that made constant travel meaningful to him. When she died in nineteen seventy-one, he lost a person who had been present from the first pages of his mathematical education. Friends remembered changes in his mood and habits after that loss. The temptation to diagnose a whole career through one family relationship is as misleading as the temptation to omit family altogether. Both mathematics and human attachment remained continuous threads.

He was also known for assigning monetary prizes to open questions. The amounts varied with the difficulty and his sense of the problem. A prize could turn a question into a challenge that outlived an individual conversation. He often gave away substantial parts of his lecture fees and awards, helped younger mathematicians, and spent relatively little on himself. There are memorable stories of an envelope being passed to someone who needed support. We should not treat every retold anecdote as documented fact, nor assume that cash was the sole motive of those who took up his problems. The real mechanism was social attention: a problem with a name, a reward and a clear formulation could find a new solver far from the person who first posed it.

The most playful numerical monument to Erdős is not one of his theorems. A mathematician's Erdős number is defined through coauthorship: Erdős himself has number zero; anyone who published a joint paper with him has number one; anyone who published with one of those people has number two, and so on by the shortest chain of coauthored publications. It began as a joke among mathematicians but became a way to talk about the connectedness of research. A low Erdős number does not make someone a better mathematician. Nor is the measure a complete picture of influence. A lecturer can change a student's future without writing a paper with them; an editor can improve an argument without appearing as coauthor. What the number captures is the size and reach of a remarkable publication network.

Erdős's paper count climbed past fifteen hundred. He had hundreds of coauthors. These figures are relevant because they describe an exceptional form of collective productivity, but they can mislead if treated as a ranking of human worth or mathematical depth. A paper may contain a small but elegant observation or a landmark theorem, and counting them equally is an arbitrary choice. Some of his most lasting effects cannot be counted at all: problems proposed at conferences, techniques passed between fields, and the courage a young researcher gained from being taken seriously by a famous mathematician.

For Erdős, mathematical talent was not restricted to those who had already acquired position or prestige. He enjoyed talking to children with a taste for mathematical puzzles and worked with younger researchers on terms that could sometimes surprise senior academics. His language had a private playfulness. Children were sometimes called epsilons, borrowing the Greek letter used for a very small positive quantity in analysis. He described beautiful proofs as if they came from a perfect, imaginary book of mathematical arguments. The image of the Book later gave its name to a celebrated collection of elegant proofs by Martin Aigner and Günter Ziegler. These phrases matter because they reveal what he valued: a mathematical argument should sometimes have the clarity and inevitability of a discovery that seems, once seen, impossible to improve.

Yet the language of a perfect proof should not hide the imperfection of mathematical life. Erdős could be impatient, absorbed to the exclusion of other responsibilities, and dependent on friends for practical matters. The use of stimulant drugs and caffeine is discussed in memoirs and reporting, but sensational portraits often make chemistry stand in for an explanation of decades of achievement. There is no good basis for treating a particular habit as a recipe for mathematical insight. The serious record is the work and the people who participated in it. Admirers sometimes found him demanding, even exhausting. He was nevertheless welcomed in many places because he could turn the time together into something intellectually valuable.

His relationship to mathematical institutions remained complex. Universities supplied salaries, students, seminars, travel support and publication venues; he benefited from all of them even when he resisted a conventional long-term appointment. His mobility worked because a distributed academic community trusted him, shared resources, and accepted an extraordinary exchange: a bed and meals for a chance to work on problems with someone who might see a new approach. It is a mistake to imagine this as a structure that anyone could simply copy. His friends had commitments of their own, and Erdős was able to contribute at a level that made such an arrangement attractive to them.

Late in life Erdős continued to work with people much younger than himself, travel among conferences and correspond with an enormous circle of colleagues. He received major honors, including the Wolf Prize in nineteen eighty-three, but did not settle into the conventional public role of a laureate looking back at a finished career. He was still asking questions and working on proofs. He died in September nineteen ninety-six during a mathematical gathering in Warsaw. The location matters. He had spent his life in contact with a European mathematical community fractured by war and borders, and he died among the people with whom he shared its rebuilding and expansion.

What, then, is the right way to remember Erdős? There is a temptation to choose between two images. In one, he is a peculiar man who traveled endlessly and could scarcely manage daily life. In the other, he is an almost supernatural source of proofs. Neither image is enough. The first is a set of entertaining anecdotes that understates the mathematics. The second disguises the genuine effort of hundreds of collaborators and the historical conditions in which ideas moved. Erdős's achievement was a style of problem solving joined to a way of organizing intellectual friendship. Those two things amplified each other.

He taught generations of mathematicians to ask whether an apparent impossibility could be overcome by counting differently, choosing randomly, studying a sharp extreme, or bringing in a colleague with another perspective. His work helped make discrete mathematics central to the modern mathematical landscape. And he showed that the social shape of a subject can be altered by one person's sustained generosity with problems and attention. The answer to a good mathematical question can belong to everyone who learns the proof. So can the question itself. That was a form of mathematics Erdős carried wherever he went.

### Chronology and intellectual networks

and relationships

- **1913–1920:** born in Budapest to mathematics teachers Anna and Lajos; sisters died of scarlet fever; father's Russian captivity and return after the First World War.
- **1920s–1934:** studied in an intellectually active Hungarian student problem culture; friendships with Turán, Gallai and Szekeres; Fejér's supervision; elementary proof of Bertrand's theorem; doctorate.
- **1934–1938:** Mordell's Manchester fellowship; research travel in Britain; Rado and other collaborations; antisemitic regimes reshape research mobility.
- **1938–1945:** Institute for Advanced Study and US appointments; wartime separation from family; Erdős–Kac work in probabilistic number theory.
- **1947–1949:** probabilistic bounds in Ramsey theory; elementary prime-number-theorem results and dispute over publication with Selberg.
- **1954–1963:** US re-entry restricted in Cold War circumstances; continuing travel and work in Europe and Israel; subsequent return to US mathematics.
- **1960s–1971:** continuing collaboration with Ronald Graham and others; mother Anna accompanied some travels until her death in 1971.
- **1947–1961, and beyond:** probabilistic method, Ramsey bounds, high-girth high-chromatic graphs, random graph theory with Rényi, extremal set systems, and wider collaborative programs.
- **1983–1996:** Wolf Prize, continued travel and problem posing, death in Warsaw at a mathematical conference.

### Mathematical companion

**Bertrand's postulate.** For every integer \(n>1\), some prime \(p\) satisfies \(n<p<2n\). Chebyshev proved the result first; Erdős gave an influential elementary proof as a teenager.

**Erdős–Szekeres monotone subsequence theorem.** Any sequence of \(ab+1\) *distinct* real numbers contains an increasing subsequence of length \(a+1\) or a decreasing subsequence of length \(b+1\). The spoken nine-number example is intentionally conservative: nine distinct numbers force an increasing or decreasing subsequence of length **three** (in fact five numbers suffice for the three-term guarantee), but nine **do not** guarantee a four-term monotone subsequence: taking \(a=b=3\) requires \(ab+1=10\) terms. The sharp threshold for a guaranteed length-four subsequence is ten.

**Erdős–Kac theorem.** If \(\omega(n)\) counts distinct prime factors, then for a uniformly chosen integer \(n\le x\), \((\omega(n)-\log\log x)/\sqrt{\log\log x}\) converges in distribution to a standard normal as \(x\to\infty\). The probabilistic selection is across integers; the factorization of any fixed integer is deterministic.

**Prime number theorem.** \(\pi(x)\sim x/\log x\). Both Selberg and Erdős supplied important work in the elementary-proof development; publication and priority are matters for documented history, not slogans.

**Ramsey lower bound by random coloring.** Independently color all \(\binom n2\) edges of the complete graph on \(n\) vertices red or blue. For a specified set of \(k\) vertices the probability that all \(\binom k2\) edges agree in color is \(2^{1-\binom k2}\). By the union bound the chance of at least one monochromatic \(K_k\) is at most \(\binom nk 2^{1-\binom k2}\). If this expression is strictly below 1, then some coloring avoids both colors of \(K_k\), so \(R(k,k)>n\). This is existence without explicit construction.

**Large girth and chromatic number.** For every positive integers \(g\) and \(r\), there exists a finite graph with girth greater than \(g\) and chromatic number greater than \(r\). The proof uses random constructions and deletion arguments. Do not confuse having no *short* cycle with being acyclic.

**Random graphs.** Erdős and Rényi studied random graph processes and threshold behavior, especially versions with a fixed number of possible edges selected. Gilbert's independent-edge model is closely related but historically distinct.

**Erdős–Ko–Rado theorem.** In a family of \(k\)-subsets of an \(n\)-element set with pairwise nonempty intersections, if \(n\ge 2k\) then at most \(\binom{n-1}{k-1}\) subsets occur; the family of all \(k\)-sets containing a fixed element attains equality. Equality uniqueness has boundary caveats (for example \(n=2k\)), so do not claim it unconditionally.

**Erdős number.** Graph distance in the network whose vertices are research authors and whose edges connect coauthors. A collaboration metric, not an index of ability or causal influence.

**Historical cautions:** Erdős’s 1996 autobiographical memory is retrospective. The childhood losses and the Holocaust must not be treated as a causal account of genius. The prime-number-theorem proof credits both Selberg and Erdős and requires documentary care in its publication history. Esther Klein's role in the happy-ending problem is substantive. Random graph research has several predecessors and contributors. Coauthorship networks measure a particular relationship, not intellectual merit. Beware colorful anecdotes repeated without records, and avoid romanticizing stimulant use.
