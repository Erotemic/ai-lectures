# 097000 - Johann Benedict Listing: The shape of a field called topology

**Track:** History - People and ideas. **Focus:** Frankfurt schooling and art, Städel sponsorship, Gauss's Göttingen circle, Italian volcanology and magnetic observation, physiological optics, university appointments and finances, the naming of topology, and the Möbius-band priority story. **Background:** geometry and elementary graphs are introduced through concrete examples.

When Johann Benedict Listing studied a surface, he wanted to know things that a ruler could not tell him. How many pieces did it have? How were its parts connected? Could a loop be pulled tight without cutting it? Would a line traveling around the surface return with its orientation reversed? These questions are so familiar in modern mathematics that it is difficult to recover how much conceptual work went into separating them from ordinary measurements of length, angle, and area. Listing was one of the people who gave that work a name: topology. Yet the mathematics of connections was only one part of his life. He also drew, studied languages, measured the Earth's magnetic field, investigated human vision, and navigated the administrative and personal pressures of a nineteenth-century university.

Listing was born in Frankfurt am Main in July eighteen oh eight. His father was a brushmaker, and the family had little money. His mother, Caroline Friederike Listing, came from a poor rural background. The young Johann displayed talent in both science and drawing, and from his early teens he earned some money through calligraphy and other graphic work. For Listing, drawing began as paid work and became a way of thinking about spatial relations. It also helped him enter the patronage networks that supported his education. He needed other people's assistance to study beyond what the family could afford.

At Frankfurt's Musterschule, a teacher named Müller encouraged his interests. Listing proceeded to a Gymnasium, studied languages and mathematics, and received support from the Städel foundation, associated with art and museums. The foundation's remit led to an education that mixed mathematics with architecture. From the standpoint of a modern university department, the combination might seem awkward. For Listing it was productive. He could imagine geometry as a study of spatial organization, while keeping in view the practical and visual problems faced by designers, surveyors, and natural scientists.

He arrived at the University of Göttingen in eighteen thirty. Göttingen had become one of the most important scientific centers in Europe, and Carl Friedrich Gauss was its most distinguished mathematician. Listing studied with extraordinary breadth: astronomy, mineralogy, geology, botany, anatomy, physics, and chemistry as well as mathematics. Gauss noticed his ability and invited him into a circle that included the physicist Wilhelm Weber. It is easy to picture the student learning only a new mathematical theorem from Gauss. The actual research culture was far less compartmentalized. Magnetism, astronomy, land measurement, and abstract geometry were closely connected through instruments, calculations, and questions about how things occupy space.

Listing's doctoral work concerned second-degree surfaces, and Gauss supervised it. He received the doctorate in eighteen thirty-four. Soon afterward he joined his friend Wolfgang Sartorius von Waltershausen on a scientific journey to Sicily, where Waltershausen was studying volcanic geology and collecting magnetic observations of interest to Gauss. The journey required skill in measurement and patience with the practical realities of travel. It also gave Listing a setting in which to think about the geometry of connections. While in Italy he wrote to his former teacher Müller about a developing subject concerned not chiefly with sizes but with relative position, order, and continuity. The word topology appears in this correspondence before it appeared in a published title.

The choice of the word had a history. Earlier writers, including Leibniz, had imagined a geometry of position, sometimes called analysis situs. Euler had analyzed the bridges of Königsberg by treating landmasses as points and bridges as connections, without needing their exact physical lengths. Gauss discussed geometrical questions in which global arrangements mattered. Listing was not inventing the whole field from nothing. He was trying to give a coherent identity and vocabulary to a family of problems that had appeared in different parts of mathematics. The term topology helped signal a change in attention: from how big a figure is to how its components are arranged and joined.

Imagine a network of islands linked by footbridges. You could draw the islands farther apart or closer together without changing which bridges connect them. If the question is whether a walk can cross every bridge exactly once, the relevant facts are the pattern of connections. A beautifully accurate map may be unnecessary; a few dots and lines can contain all the essential information. That is a topological way of thinking. It does not mean metric geometry is useless. It means certain questions remain unchanged under movements and distortions that preserve the underlying pattern.

Listing published an early account of the field under the title Vorstudien zur Topologie, or Preliminary Studies in Topology, in the late eighteen forties. Bibliographic sources differ slightly in how they date its appearance, commonly giving eighteen forty-seven or eighteen forty-eight, and the distinction need not bear a larger historical argument. The important point is that the subject was becoming publicly identifiable. The pamphlet was not a complete modern textbook with groups, manifolds, homology, and all the machinery now called algebraic topology. Listing was gathering examples, language, and modes of classification. A name can prepare the ground for work that its creator will never see.

In the meantime his professional life was complicated. A Göttingen position in mathematical physics placed him within a community whose university politics were entangled with German constitutional struggles. In eighteen thirty-seven, several Göttingen professors had protested the Hanoverian king's reversal of the constitution and lost their positions. Wilhelm Weber was among them. When the revolutions of eighteen forty-eight changed the political climate, Weber returned. Listing had been appointed in the intervening years, and the institutions negotiated a reorganization. Listing became ordinary professor of mathematical physics, while Weber took the chair of experimental physics. Such appointments were not determined only by the merits of scientific papers. Universities were political institutions, bound to monarchies, budgets, public controversy, and the reputations of their faculty.

Listing remained deeply engaged in experiments. He studied physiological optics and the behavior of light in the eye. A question about gaze might initially sound unrelated to topology. Yet both fields invite attention to transformations and constraints. If the eye turns from one direction to another, its motion cannot be described by saying only where its line of sight ends up. The eye has an orientation, and rotations in three-dimensional space must be understood carefully. Listing's law, a principle associated with the orientation of the eye during certain movements, belongs to the physiological side of his work. Modern eye science includes complexities and qualifications beyond its original formulation. Still, the work shows the same researcher moving between spatial mathematics and physical observation.

He married Pauline Listing, and the couple had daughters. The household had persistent financial difficulties. Historical biographies record debts, disputes, and difficult relations with landlords and servants. It would be unfair to turn these records into comic relief or to make a mathematical claim depend on an imagined domestic scene. The material circumstances nevertheless mattered. Lack of stable household management and reliance on loans could damage a person's standing with colleagues and officials. Listing's friend Waltershausen helped secure assistance when the family approached a serious financial crisis. Their friendship had been formed decades earlier through fieldwork in Sicily. The episode makes patronage more complicated than a simple story of a rich benefactor helping a poor student: personal obligations developed over years and sometimes crossed from scientific cooperation into practical rescue.

One of Listing's most famous discoveries is often introduced under another mathematician's name. If you take a paper strip, give one end a half-turn, and join the ends, the result is a Möbius band. A pencil can travel along what looks like one side and eventually reach what initially looked like the other without crossing an edge. The band has only one side in the relevant local-orientation sense and a single boundary component. August Ferdinand Möbius independently investigated this one-sided surface in eighteen fifty-eight. Listing also investigated it independently around that time and described it in print before Möbius's eighteen sixty-five publication. The chronology is more involved than simply assigning sole invention to the person named by the object.

What makes the band surprising is not merely the trick of twisting paper. On an ordinary cylinder made from an untwisted strip, you can consistently distinguish one side from the other. On a Möbius band, the local choice of a side cannot be continued consistently around the whole surface. Mathematicians call such a surface non-orientable. The explanation uses global behavior. Every tiny patch looks ordinary, yet tracing the patches around a closed route defeats an attempt to give the entire surface one consistent orientation. This is a perfect example of the kind of fact Listing wanted topology to capture: a property arising from how local pieces are connected, rather than from exact length or curvature.

A second topological theme came from Euler's formula for polyhedra. For a convex polyhedron, count its vertices, subtract its edges, and add its faces. The result is two. A cube has eight vertices, twelve edges, and six faces; eight minus twelve plus six gives two. You can change the shape of the cube without affecting this relationship, as long as the relevant connectivity is preserved. But what happens to more complicated spaces, or to objects formed from many cells of different dimensions? Listing's eighteen sixty-two work on spatial complexes explored extensions of Euler-style counting. Its historical details are more technical than the paper-strip example, but the ambition was similar: discover quantities that remain stable when geometry changes without changing the essential organization.

This work did not become today's algebraic topology fully formed. Riemann, later Poincaré, and many other mathematicians developed ideas of connectedness, dimension, homology, and invariants along different lines. Listing's contribution belongs to that broader history. He proposed terminology, studied specific surfaces and complexes, and helped make the geometry of connection into an explicit research program. He also continued research in geodesy and the shape of the Earth, maintaining the older Göttingen tradition of close ties between mathematics and the physical sciences.

Listing died in Göttingen in December eighteen eighty-two. He had spent decades in the university while working across an extraordinary range of subjects. His name is less familiar to modern students than that of Gauss or Möbius, although the field name topology became permanent. His career also resists the stereotype of the mathematician sealed away from ordinary life. The young man who drew to help support his parents became a scientist of magnetism and vision, a university professor, a sometimes financially troubled family member, a loyal traveling companion, and a geometer trying to name a new kind of question.

If you hold a twisted paper band in your hands, you can approach it in two ways. One is to measure it: how long, how wide, how curved? The other is to ask what remains true if you stretch it without tearing or gluing. Listing's history is a history of the second question becoming a discipline. The name he offered now covers mathematics he never imagined, and the surface he studied carries another man's name. Neither fact diminishes his work. They tell us that a field is made collectively, through language, examples, institutions, and people whose contributions are remembered in different ways.

## Historical companion (not narrated)

### Chronology

- **1808–1830:** born in Frankfurt; father a brushmaker; financial help through drawing and calligraphy; Städel sponsorship for mathematics and architecture.
- **1830–1834:** studied at Göttingen under Gauss and in a broad scientific curriculum; doctorate on quadric surfaces.
- **1834–1848:** travel to Sicily with Sartorius von Waltershausen; terrestrial magnetism and geology; correspondence using the word *topology*; early pamphlet *Vorstudien zur Topologie*, dated 1847 or 1848 in reference sources; professorial reorganization after Weber's return.
- **1840s–1882:** physiological optics and Listing's law, family difficulties and assistance from Waltershausen; independent study of one-sided band around 1858; 1862 work on spatial complexes; later geodesy; death in Göttingen.

### Mathematical companion

For a finite cell decomposition of a sphere-like polyhedral surface, the Euler characteristic is

\[\chi=V-E+F=2.\]

For a torus, \(\chi=0\). Generalizations use alternating counts of cells in further dimensions, subject to appropriate definitions and hypotheses. A Möbius band is a compact surface with one boundary component and is **non-orientable**. One can build a physical model by identifying the two short edges of a rectangle after reversing the transverse direction; this is a global identification property, not a statement that one can never draw a local normal vector.

**Historical cautions:** Do not claim that Möbius alone discovered the one-sided band. Separate Listing's early word use in correspondence, printed pamphlet, and later work on complexes. Secondary sources differ on whether the pamphlet is dated 1847 or 1848; the narrative uses the late 1840s. His household finances are described from biographies, not invented dramatic scenes. Gauss influenced, but did not publish, many of the early topology ideas attributed to his circle.
