# 090000 - Henri Cartan: A family, a collective, and the rebuilding of mathematics

**Track:** History - People and ideas. **Focus:** French education between the wars, several complex variables, the Bourbaki collaboration, the Occupation and the Cartan family, postwar seminars, sheaves and homological algebra, and campaigning for persecuted mathematicians. **Background:** basic familiarity with functions; geometric and algebraic ideas introduced in speech.

In the middle of the nineteen thirties, a group of young French mathematicians tried to write a textbook. Their ambition was not modest: they wanted a new account of mathematics that would be systematic, modern, and clear about every assumption. They disagreed over chapters, rewrote the prose again and again, and eventually published under a name that belonged to none of them, Nicolas Bourbaki. Henri Cartan was a central participant. But he was also a mathematician of several complex variables, a teacher who helped form the postwar French school, a brother whose family suffered Nazi persecution, and later a campaigner for colleagues whose governments imprisoned them. His life makes the word 'collective' more than a description of an authorship convention. Mathematics, for him, was repeatedly a shared responsibility.

Henri Paul Cartan was born in Nancy, France, in July nineteen oh four. His father, Élie Cartan, would become one of the most influential geometers of the century. His mother was Marie-Louise Bianconi. When Henri was a young child the family moved to Paris because his father had obtained an academic appointment there. The house was musical: the children studied instruments, and music remained part of family life. Henri's younger brother Jean pursued composition; another younger brother, Louis, became a physicist. There was also a sister. It would be easy to imagine Élie Cartan arranging the children's education to produce a successor. Henri later gave a more ordinary account: his father answered questions but did not insist that mathematics was the only worthwhile life. Henri chose his own path toward the subject.

French schooling gave him access to rigorous mathematics, but it also placed him in a country whose academic institutions had been permanently affected by the First World War. A large part of a generation of students and teachers had been killed. French mathematics did not stop, and older figures such as Émile Borel and Paul Montel continued important work; still, the loss of young scholars had consequences for recruitment, teaching, and intellectual continuity. When Cartan entered the École Normale Supérieure in Paris, he joined a community that would be preoccupied with repairing those losses. Among the students he came to know was André Weil. Their friendship would eventually connect a department classroom to a sweeping collaborative project.

Cartan studied with Montel and worked in complex analysis, the mathematics of functions whose variables can take complex-number values. A complex number has a real part and an imaginary part; in a single complex variable, differentiability turns out to be much more restrictive and powerful than it is for ordinary real functions. Such functions have local power-series descriptions and a rich geometric theory. Henri's interests moved toward functions of several complex variables. The phrase sounds like a small change, but the geometry becomes harder. Domains may have complicated boundaries; conditions that guarantee the solution of a problem in one variable may fail or require deeper tools in several. Cartan received his doctorate in nineteen twenty-eight, began teaching at Caen and Lille, and pursued this growing research program.

The intellectual network extended outside France. At Weil's suggestion he had studied work on several complex variables, and in nineteen thirty-one he visited Heinrich Behnke in Münster, Germany. There he met Peter Thullen. The two collaborated on questions about singularities, and a joint paper appeared the following year. In nineteen thirty-one Henri also wrote a paper with his father, Élie, drawing on the elder Cartan's expertise with Lie groups. Those collaborations deserve separate credit. 'The Cartans' were not one mathematician, and Henri's work was not merely an inheritance from his father. They had different programs and, on this occasion, found a productive point of contact.

Henri moved to Strasbourg in nineteen thirty-one and married Nicole Weiss in nineteen thirty-five. They would have five children. Strasbourg was a French city close to the German border, with a university and an intellectual history shaped by changing sovereignty after the Franco-Prussian War and then World War One. Its location was an advantage for mathematical exchange with German-speaking researchers. Soon that same geography would become a source of danger. Cartan's friendship with Behnke and Thullen belonged to a Europe in which academic conversations could cross borders; the Nazi seizure of power in nineteen thirty-three made such exchange politically fraught. Thullen left Germany, and institutions that had supported mathematics were increasingly subject to racial and ideological control.

The Bourbaki project took shape while these circumstances were developing. In January nineteen thirty-five, Cartan and several colleagues met to discuss how to write better teaching materials. By that summer a founding group had emerged, including Weil, Jean Dieudonné, Claude Chevalley, Szolem Mandelbrojt, René de Possel, and Jean Delsarte. Their chosen pseudonym, Nicolas Bourbaki, made the enterprise look like the work of an individual. It was the opposite. Members debated the smallest assumptions, circulated drafts, and replaced chapters with new versions when the result did not satisfy them. Cartan later remembered both friendship and exhausting disagreement. Dieudonné was often able to turn collective argument into a final, readable draft. A book that looks perfectly unified may conceal many voices and much editorial labor.

The project had a mathematical philosophy as well as a social one. Rather than teach a list of specialized recipes, the authors organized subjects around structures: groups, rings, fields, topological spaces, and the maps connecting them. A group, for example, is not defined by a picture of rotating a triangle or by the arithmetic of numbers. It is an operation satisfying a few laws. Once those laws are stated, a conclusion proved from them applies to every instance of the structure. That abstraction can be liberating. It can also be forbidding if the reader never sees what concrete problem the structure clarifies. Bourbaki's influence on twentieth-century mathematics was immense and contested. Cartan was a participant in that argument, not a spokesman whose judgments need to become the judgments of this lecture.

The Second World War interrupted the project and the family life behind it. Strasbourg's population and university were evacuated in nineteen thirty-nine, and Cartan taught at Clermont-Ferrand before taking up duties in Paris. Under the Occupation he could not simply return to his Strasbourg home or recover his papers. In one account, Behnke, an old German colleague, helped retrieve some documents that eventually came back after the war. That small incident is important because it shows the difference between a hostile regime and the choices of particular scholars, while never excusing the regime's violence.

The Cartan family suffered a far greater loss. Henri's brother Louis became involved in the French Resistance, was arrested by German authorities, deported, and killed. The family endured a long period without reliable news. Only after liberation could they learn more about his fate. Henri's brother Jean had already died young, of tuberculosis. These were not abstract examples of European tragedy offered to make a mathematical lecture seem dramatic. They were losses within a household of parents, siblings, spouses, and children. Henri continued teaching and research during these years, but any account of a smoothly advancing research career would misrepresent the conditions under which he lived.

After the war he resumed contact with Strasbourg and colleagues across Europe. In nineteen forty-six he visited the new research setting at Oberwolfach. In memories of the occasion he recalled the cold and the pianos as well as the people. He later traveled to the United States, where he met Samuel Eilenberg. That meeting developed into a major collaboration. Eilenberg was already prominent in algebraic topology, a subject that studies spaces through algebraic invariants. Cartan brought his interests in analysis and structures. Their book Homological Algebra, published in nineteen fifty-six, helped give mathematicians a shared language for kernels, images, exact sequences, and derived constructions. The book was collaborative, and its title named a body of methods that many others also contributed to.

An elementary example conveys the kind of organization involved. Suppose we have three collections of objects and maps passing from one collection to the next. The image of the first map consists of what actually arrives at the middle. The kernel of the second map consists of what can go no further. When the two are equal, the sequence is exact at the middle. That condition measures a precise fit between what comes in and what becomes invisible. In modern mathematics, chains of such maps can describe holes in a space, relations among equations, and obstructions to solving a problem. The technical versions require definitions that this biography cannot supply in full, but the general lesson is clear: by keeping track of what passes through a map and what disappears, one can turn a difficult global question into an algebraic pattern.

Cartan's research on analytic functions led to another powerful language, the theory of sheaves. Imagine a landscape covered by overlapping maps. On every small patch, we know how to draw a function or solve an equation. But can the local answers be fitted into one answer over the whole landscape? A sheaf records local data and specifies when it agrees on overlaps. The central problem is not simply to pile up local solutions. It is to discover the obstruction to joining them. Cartan's theorems on coherent analytic sheaves and Stein spaces provided deep existence and vanishing results. In carefully specified settings, local holomorphic information can be controlled globally. The words theorem A and theorem B conceal substantial analytic and geometric work, including major contributions by others such as Kiyoshi Oka and Jean-Pierre Serre.

Teaching and seminars carried these methods into the next generation. Cartan established a seminar at the École Normale Supérieure in Paris, and its written proceedings circulated from the late nineteen forties through the nineteen sixties. Students including Serre, René Thom, and Adrien Douady helped extend the style of problem-solving and exposition. The classroom was not a sealed audience observing a master. Research questions moved among speakers, notes, and letters; new tools were tested in public. In some sense the seminar offered a second version of the Bourbaki workshop: mathematics developed through collective criticism, although the projects and people were not identical.

Cartan's public responsibilities did not end at the university. In the nineteen seventies he joined other mathematicians in protesting political repression of scholars. A particularly urgent case involved Leonid Plyushch, a Ukrainian mathematician held in a Soviet psychiatric institution because of his dissident activity. At the International Congress of Mathematicians in Vancouver in nineteen seventy-four, Cartan helped assemble support for a campaign. He worked with others, including Laurent Schwartz and Lipman Bers, on the Comité des Mathématiciens. Plyushch was released in nineteen seventy-six. The committee also supported persecuted scholars in other countries, including José Luis Massera in Uruguay. Its work was international rather than a claim that only one political system could violate academic freedom.

There is no simple line from the Nazi killing of Louis Cartan to Henri's later campaigning, and we should not invent a private psychological explanation. The historical record does show that he knew what political violence could do to families, that he remembered the vulnerability of intellectual networks, and that he acted publicly when other mathematicians were threatened. His activism gives a concrete meaning to the idea of an international research community. A community is not made only by invitations to conferences. It can also be tested by whether its members speak when a colleague is prevented from working or is punished for a political view.

Henri Cartan was honored widely and continued to influence mathematics after his retirement in nineteen seventy-five. He died in Paris in August two thousand eight, aged one hundred and four. Over that long life he saw mathematics move from classical analysis to a language shaped by topology, algebraic structures, sheaves, and categories. He played a part in that change, but no responsible account will make him the sole inventor of each method. The names of colleagues, students, and predecessors are part of the work itself.

We began with a room full of mathematicians arguing over a textbook. The disagreement was not a defect to be removed from the story. It was part of the intellectual process. Cartan's own achievements in analysis, his collaboration with Eilenberg, his seminars, his family history during occupation, and his public defense of persecuted mathematicians belong to one biography but should not be flattened into a single moral. They show a person working within institutions he helped create and sometimes had to defend. Much of his mathematics asks when pieces of information fit together into a global structure. His life reminds us that people and institutions never fit together so cleanly. Keeping a research community alive demands work of a different kind, and he took that work seriously.

## Historical companion (not narrated)

### Historical chronology

- **1904–1935:** born in Nancy; moved to Paris; École Normale Supérieure; PhD in 1928; Lille and Strasbourg; collaboration with Thullen and with his father; married Nicole Weiss in 1935; helped found Bourbaki.
- **1939–1945:** Strasbourg university displaced to Clermont-Ferrand; Paris appointment in 1940; brother Louis arrested, deported and killed during the Occupation.
- **1946–1975:** postwar international travel; collaboration with Samuel Eilenberg; École Normale seminar; analytical sheaf theory and algebraic topology; teaching in Paris and Orsay.
- **1974–2008:** helped organize mathematicians' international human-rights campaigns; retired 1975; died 2008.

### Mathematical companion

A sheaf \(\mathcal F\) assigns local data to open sets, with compatible restriction maps. Data on overlapping open sets can be glued when it agrees on overlaps. For a coherent analytic sheaf on a Stein space, Cartan's theorem A asserts that germs are generated by global sections; theorem B says \(H^q(X,\mathcal F)=0\) for \(q\ge1\). These statements require the coherence and Stein hypotheses.

An exact sequence of abelian groups \(A\xrightarrow{f}B\xrightarrow{g}C\) satisfies \(\operatorname{im}(f)=\ker(g)\) at \(B\). Homological algebra systematically studies such configurations through functors, chain complexes, and derived invariants. The 1956 Cartan-Eilenberg book was a major synthesis, not sole discovery of the entire field.

**Historical caution:** Do not conflate Henri Cartan with his father Élie Cartan. Bourbaki was collective; do not assign it a single founder.
