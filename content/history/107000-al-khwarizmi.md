# 107000 - Al-Khwarizmi: Algebra in the city of translation

**Track:** History — People and ideas. **Focus:** Abbasid Baghdad under al-Ma'mun; the limited biographical record of Muhammad ibn Musa al-Khwarizmi; algebra as a system of equation types and geometric justification; arithmetic with Indian numerals; astronomical and geographical work; manuscripts, translators and the meanings of algebra and algorithm. **Background:** simple equations and squares are introduced in speech.

Suppose a person comes to you with an inheritance problem. A sum of money is to be divided according to specified obligations. Some shares are known, others are not, and several relations must hold at the same time. The question is not how to memorize one answer. It is how to turn a tangle of statements into a problem with a method of solution. That kind of work occupies a central place in the book of Muhammad ibn Musa al-Khwarizmi, a mathematician working in Baghdad in the ninth century. His name, changed by centuries of translation, eventually entered English as algorithm. A word from the title of his algebra book gave us algebra. Yet neither word is a simple certificate that he invented everything now included under it.

We know few secure facts about al-Khwarizmi's private life. The dates often listed, roughly the late eighth century to the middle of the ninth, are estimates. His name suggests a connection with Khwarazm, the region south of the Aral Sea in Central Asia, but it does not settle his birthplace or the place where he grew up. Historians have even disagreed over the interpretation of names attached to him in later biographical notices. This is a case in which the uncertainty is part of the biography, not an inconvenience to be hidden. We should resist inventing a childhood journey from Central Asia to Baghdad merely because it would make a satisfying opening scene.

What can be placed more securely is his scholarly activity in Abbasid Baghdad during the reign of the caliph al-Ma'mun, from eight hundred thirteen to eight hundred thirty-three. The city was a political and intellectual center where Greek, Persian, Indian and Arabic-language learning met. The caliph's patronage supported work in astronomy, translation, mathematical calculation and geography. Al-Khwarizmi is associated with the scholarly environment commonly called the House of Wisdom and with other researchers, including the brothers known as the Banu Musa. But popular depictions of one magnificent building in which every important scientist of the era sat at a desk together make institutional history too tidy. Different books, scholars, libraries, patrons and working arrangements belonged to the broader environment.

We should also resist the opposite simplification, that translation was only passive copying. To translate an astronomical table is to choose a vocabulary and coordinate conventions. To reproduce a proof is to decide which relation must be made explicit for a new reader. To combine methods from different traditions is to compare their assumptions. Baghdad's scholars did not receive a sealed container of finished ancient knowledge. They participated in a continuing process of mathematical selection, criticism and invention.

Al-Khwarizmi's famous work bears a title often rendered The Compendious Book on Calculation by Completion and Balancing. The central Arabic terms are al-jabr and al-muqabala. Their historical meanings are more specific than the sweeping modern word algebra. In the book, al-jabr concerns restoring or completing an expression by moving a deficient term so the relationship is preserved; al-muqabala concerns balancing or cancelling comparable terms. His equations are written mostly in words, using categories such as numbers, roots and squares. An unknown raised to the second power is not printed as a little two above a letter, and the unknown is not generally a letter at all.

The book's organization is revealing. Rather than start with one all-purpose modern quadratic formula containing positive and negative coefficients, al-Khwarizmi identifies six basic kinds of equation. That makes sense in a setting where quantities such as a square, a root and a positive number are handled separately and the routine use of negative coefficients is limited. A square may equal roots, a square may equal numbers, or roots may equal numbers. More complicated cases put two of the categories together against the third. For each type there is a rule. The aim is to classify the problem, reduce it to a standard form, and know how to solve it.

Consider one of his well-known examples. A square together with ten roots equals thirty-nine. The word root here represents the unknown number, and the square means that number multiplied by itself. Let us try to understand the method without pretending the author used our notation. Begin with an unknown square. Imagine adding four rectangular strips around it, with their combined area equal to ten times the unknown side length. Each strip has width two and a half. We now have an almost complete larger square, missing four tiny corner squares. Each corner has side two and a half, so together those corners have area twenty-five. The original square and strips had area thirty-nine. Add the missing twenty-five, and the complete large square has area sixty-four. Its side is eight. Take away the five units contributed by the two strips across the width, and the original side is three.

Check it: three squared is nine, ten times three is thirty, and together they make thirty-nine. The arithmetic answer is not the most remarkable part. The construction explains why a rule works for an entire type of quadratic equation. Its pieces transform a verbal problem into the geometry of a completed square. The relationship between equation and diagram becomes evidence, not merely a mnemonic. The same logic is still taught in modern algebra, though a modern equation would allow algebraic signs and solutions that the original presentation did not handle in the same manner.

Al-Khwarizmi was not the first human being to solve quadratic problems. Babylonian mathematical texts had long contained sophisticated procedures, Greek geometers reasoned about related figures, and Indian traditions included their own methods for unknown quantities. Historians debate detailed lines of influence among these bodies of work. His achievement was to present algebra as a systematic subject with named equation types, stated procedures, examples and geometrical justification, in a text that later scholars could study and develop. A claim of priority is meaningful only after we specify what activity is being claimed: solving an equation, organizing a discipline, naming a method, or transmitting a book.

The practical examples are part of the subject. The work refers to inheritance, property, trade and land measurement. An account of how a fortune is divided may require more than ordinary arithmetic because one share depends on an unknown whole. A surveyor may know an area and a relationship among the sides of a piece of land but not the sides themselves. Algebra brings the unknown into a relation governed by a rule. The real setting matters, though the problem can often be treated mathematically without deciding which legal distribution is just or which commercial arrangement is desirable. The text supplies procedures for solving specified relations, not a complete theory of social life.

The algebra book belongs beside al-Khwarizmi's arithmetic, where another enormous historical process becomes visible. The numerals now called Hindu-Arabic grew out of Indian place-value traditions and were studied in the Arabic-speaking world. Al-Khwarizmi composed an account of calculation with these numerals, although the original Arabic form of this arithmetic treatise has not survived in the way the algebra book has. Latin adaptations, later copies and related literature preserve an influential part of its reception. This manuscript history should make us cautious about quoting a convenient Latin phrasing as if it must have been his original sentence.

A place-value system makes multiplication and long division easier to organize because the value of a digit depends on its position. Put a one in the hundreds place and a two in the units place, and you have one hundred two rather than twelve. A zero between digits can mark a position not occupied by another digit. But the history of these symbols and procedures is much older and geographically broader than one ninth-century text. Al-Khwarizmi helped communicate and systematize methods; he did not create the Indian numerals from nothing.

The Latinized version of his name became associated with calculation using the new numerals. Medieval terms such as algorismus referred to this practice. Across time the word transformed into algorithm, now used for a finite, specified computational procedure. Modern algorithms include methods for sorting data, finding paths in a network, solving equations and carrying out cryptographic operations. It would be anachronistic to say that al-Khwarizmi invented the modern theory of algorithms or computer science. The etymology of a word records part of a reception history, not exclusive ownership of every concept later attached to it.

His astronomy is less famous in popular accounts but was central to the same scholarly world. Astronomical tables supplied numerical values for predicting positions and movements in the sky. Calculations for calendars and observations depended on timekeeping, angles and numerical methods. Al-Khwarizmi wrote tables influenced by Indian astronomy, while the wider Abbasid tradition compared methods drawn from several sources. It is useful to imagine a scholar switching from an equation about an estate to the problem of a planet's position. The surface applications differ. Both require a reliable path from known quantities to an unknown quantity.

Geography was another field in which mathematical organization mattered. Al-Khwarizmi produced a geographical work that organized places by coordinates and drew on the earlier tradition associated with Ptolemy. A list of longitudes and latitudes is not only a catalog of names. It represents a claim that places can be related quantitatively on a common framework. The framework can be corrected when measurements improve, but that requires deciding where the coordinate origin lies, how distances are projected and which observations are trusted. The historical record does not support reducing the work to a claim that he was the first mapmaker; many traditions of cartography existed before and around him.

What did daily life look like for al-Khwarizmi? We cannot describe the furniture of his room or the conversation at his breakfast. Yet the books themselves let us infer the practices required for their making. Someone had to collect written problems and judge which examples were representative. Figures had to be drawn carefully enough to support a geometrical argument. Tables needed consistency checks. Manuscripts had to be copied and compared. Patrons could help fund scholarship, but their interests also influenced which kinds of work received attention and which books were dedicated to them. The specific organization of a scholar's day is hidden; the intellectual labor is not.

Later scholars in the Islamic world did more than preserve al-Khwarizmi. Mathematicians including Abu Kamil extended equation-solving, developed the handling of irrational quantities, and introduced new problems. Across the twelfth century, Latin translators such as Robert of Chester and Gerard of Cremona made versions of the algebra available to readers in Europe. Their texts did not always contain the same parts, and different versions could reflect different stages in the book's transmission. Modern philological studies have argued that the surviving Arabic and Latin witnesses do not all preserve the same exact original wording. It is therefore better to talk about a tradition of texts than to treat a single printed translation as a transparent window into ninth-century Baghdad.

The surviving manuscript material offers another lesson about evidence. A copy written many centuries after an author may contain notes added by a teacher, adjustments made by a scribe, or corrections from another reader. Some of those changes can clarify the mathematical argument. Others can distort it. The historian's task is to identify the witness, compare versions and state which reading supports the claim. Mathematical texts have an additional resource: a formula can be checked against its own proof. But a mathematically correct reconstruction is not automatically the exact wording the original author used.

Al-Khwarizmi's historical importance is sometimes packaged into a pair of slogans: father of algebra and father of algorithms. Both are too broad unless qualified. The book on completion and balancing organized a significant part of equation-solving as a discipline in an influential form. The arithmetic connected a name to computational procedures that later spread through Latin schools. His astronomy and geography show a scholar who worked across several practical and theoretical sciences. His predecessors and successors matter because his work belonged to active international networks of calculation, not to an empty field awaiting a solitary founder.

It is tempting to picture the progress of mathematics as a story about symbols becoming shorter. Greek geometrical prose becomes Arabic rules, then symbolic equations, then a machine program. But this straight line misleads. Different traditions solved different problems, sometimes with techniques suited to their own notation and institutions. A geometrical proof in words may communicate an insight that compressed symbols conceal. An astronomical table can bring theoretical mathematics into practical use. The choice of representation is part of the thinking, and a scholar may need several representations for the same problem.

Return to the inheritance problem. A person wants to find an unknown quantity subject to several known constraints. Al-Khwarizmi offers a route: identify the kind of relation, transform it without changing what it means, and carry out a rule whose validity can be explained. Today the same basic intention appears in many mathematical settings, though the systems are far more extensive. The surviving life does not tell us the color of his childhood home. It tells us something more directly useful to the history of ideas: in Baghdad, a mathematician made calculation into a subject that could be organized, justified, taught and transported across languages. The words algebra and algorithm are traces of that history, not substitutes for understanding it.

## Historical companion (not narrated)

### Chronology and evidence

- **Late eighth century:** conventional period for al-Khwarizmi's birth. Birthplace and family history are not firmly documented; the regional name is not proof of birthplace.
- **813–833:** research under caliph al-Ma'mun's patronage, associated with scholarly Baghdad and the milieu of the House of Wisdom.
- **c. 825:** conventional approximate date of the treatise on *al-jabr wa'l-muqabala*.
- **Ninth century:** arithmetic on Indian numerals, astronomical tables and geographic coordinates also associated with his corpus.
- **Twelfth century onward:** Latin translations and adaptations; textual witnesses differ. The original autograph is not extant.

### Mathematical companion

**Completing the square.** A typical worked example is \(x^2+10x=39\) for a positive unknown. Adding \(25\) yields \((x+5)^2=64\), so \(x=3\) in the stated domain. The diagram with four strips around a square is a *geometric explanation*, not evidence of modern symbolic notation in the source manuscript.

**Six canonical classes.** With positive coefficients and no general use of negative terms, root, square and number categories lead to different standard equations, rather than one signed-coefficient quadratic formula. *Al-jabr* and *al-muqabala* refer to transformations used to reduce and balance expressions.

**Etymology.** *Algebra* descends from *al-jabr* in the title of the book; *algorithm* follows later Latin forms of the author's name used for Indian-numeral arithmetic. Neither etymology assigns exclusive invention of every modern associated idea.

**Textual evidence.** Al-Khwarizmi's original manuscripts are not extant. Medieval Latin versions and later Arabic copies require comparison; a valid modern mathematical reconstruction is not always identical to a historical reading.

**Historical cautions:** His geographical epithet does not settle his birthplace. The House of Wisdom was part of a larger intellectual environment, not a single modern institution whose staff list we can reconstruct. Quadratic methods and positional numerals existed before al-Khwarizmi. The Latin and Arabic manuscript traditions differ; scholarship on direct influences among Indian, Greek and Arabic algebra is contested and should not be turned into a single line of copying.
