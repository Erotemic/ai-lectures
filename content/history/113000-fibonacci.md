# 113000 — Fibonacci: A merchant's mathematics for a changing Mediterranean

**Track:** History — People and ideas. **Focus:** Leonardo of Pisa and his North African education, commercial arithmetic, *Liber abaci* (1202, revised 1228), practical geometry, number problems, Frederick II's court, manuscript transmission, and the much later fame of the rabbit sequence. **Background:** place-value arithmetic and elementary equations are explained in speech.

Imagine a merchant standing in a Mediterranean port with three lists of quantities. One list records grain in one city's unit of weight, another lists a price in a different currency, and a third tracks shares owed to several partners after the cargo is sold. No one problem is difficult in isolation. The trouble is conversion. Each new unit or fraction creates an opportunity to make a mistake, and a mistake in a large shipment can cost someone a great deal of money. A reliable method of written arithmetic is an economic instrument.

Leonardo of Pisa, later called Fibonacci, became one of the most important mathematical writers to explain such methods to Latin-reading Europe. His fame today rests overwhelmingly on a sequence of numbers whose neighboring terms add to make the next. In his own age, the more consequential work was a book that taught the calculation needed by merchants, surveyors, and mathematical specialists. The biography of Leonardo is therefore also a biography of how knowledge crosses linguistic, political and commercial frontiers.

He was born around 1170, probably in Pisa, though exact birth and death dates are not secured by contemporary registers. Pisa was a maritime republic with trading connections throughout the Mediterranean. Its merchants traveled to North Africa, Sicily and other centers of commerce. Ports were not homogeneous places: languages, measures, coinages and legal customs changed as goods moved across them. A person who could translate between calculation systems had a practical advantage, particularly when dealing with fractions and multiple currencies.

Leonardo's father Guglielmo represented Pisan merchants at Bugia, now Bejaia in Algeria. In a preface to *Liber abaci*, Leonardo described being brought there as a boy and introduced to techniques of calculation using the nine Indian numerals. This is one of the best personal witnesses we possess to his education. We do not know the name of every teacher or the details of the classroom, but we know that he connected his mathematics to what he learned in North Africa and subsequent travels.

The basic system was positional notation. Its symbols, today commonly called Hindu-Arabic digits, can stand for ones, tens, hundreds and larger powers of ten depending on position. Zero holds an empty place and participates in numerical notation. The number two hundred five differs decisively from twenty-five because the middle zero records no tens. A reader accustomed to the system may barely notice this, but in a calculation based on a mixture of Roman numeral conventions, an abacus, and local written shorthand, the ease of recording and carrying across positions was not trivial.

Suppose a trader needs to multiply two hundred five by thirty-seven. Using positional notation we can do it in stages: two hundred five times thirty is six thousand one hundred fifty; two hundred five times seven is one thousand four hundred thirty-five. Adding gives seven thousand five hundred eighty-five. The algorithm can be written in a compact layout that makes place positions explicit. Leonardo's historical book described many such calculations, along with division, fractions and the procedures required for real exchange. The example is ours, not his, but the value is the same: a reusable method independent of the particular goods being counted.

Leonardo did not invent the numerals, the place-value system, or the elementary operations.

The word 'Arabic' in a later European description of the numerals should not erase their South Asian history, just as the phrase 'Indian numerals' should not erase the scholars who developed and transmitted algorithms in Arabic. The movement of knowledge was not a package mailed directly from one country to another. Merchants carried practices; teachers demonstrated procedures; scholars copied and criticized texts; translators chose new words for old operations. A technique could change during the journey and acquire uses unknown to its earlier author. Leonardo explicitly acknowledged learning from the mathematical practices of the places he visited. That acknowledgment is historical evidence against the simplified tale that a lone Italian invented the modern digits.

They had developed over many generations in South Asia and passed into Arabic-language mathematical and administrative cultures, including the works of al-Khwarizmi and later authors. Nor was he the first person in Europe ever to encounter them. Other routes of transmission existed, especially through contact with the Islamic world. His importance lies in producing a powerful, detailed Latin exposition that helped extend their practical and scholarly use.

After years of travel, Leonardo returned to Pisa around the turn of the thirteenth century. In 1202 he composed the *Liber abaci*, often translated as the *Book of Calculation*. It was revised in 1228. The title can mislead a modern reader: it does not mean he was writing a textbook exclusively about counting on a physical abacus. Its subject was computational practice in a broad sense. The book treats arithmetic methods, commercial transactions, proportional reasoning, geometry and algebraic problem solving. It draws on mathematical traditions he had encountered through Mediterranean contacts.

In one problem a merchant's goods change hands under several barter ratios. In another, money is exchanged at different rates. In another, profit must be divided among investors according to their shares. What makes these problems instructive is that there may be no single natural unit. If a length is measured in one city's cubits and a price is listed in another city's coins, the computation must respect both conversions. The mathematical skill is to preserve the relationships while changing the language of quantity.

Consider a hypothetical partnership. One merchant contributes two shares of capital and another contributes three. They earn a profit of seventy-five coins. If the agreement distributes gains in proportion to investment, the five total shares make fifteen coins per share. The partners receive thirty and forty-five. Now suppose one city prices gold by a different weight unit and another charges a fee of one part in twenty. The commercial reality demands that each unit conversion and fraction be applied at the right step. These are elementary calculations individually, but a reliable written exposition turns them into a portable professional skill. Leonardo's book addressed this landscape of problems in far more elaborate detail than a modern five-line example can show.

Fractions deserve particular attention. A merchant might price an item at three and a half units, take a commission of one eighth, and divide the remainder among partners in a ratio of two to three. It is possible to carry the whole problem in exact rational arithmetic. The answer need not be rounded in the middle of the work. The representation of fractions is as important as the decimal digits used for integers. Medieval commercial calculation was not just the story of replacing an old numeral shape with a modern one.

Leonardo's book also taught arithmetic in dialogue with geometry. A practical measurement problem might ask how to determine the area of a field or the volume of a container. A commercial problem might turn into an equation whose unknown is a price. Students could see that the same proportional reasoning applied to money, land and mixtures of material. In this sense *Liber abaci* was encyclopedic, connecting a broad range of topics rather than merely offering a single invention.

The famous rabbits appear as one problem among many. Suppose rabbits mature and reproduce according to a simplified schedule, with each mature pair producing another pair at each step, and the offspring maturing after a specified delay. Count the pairs month by month under the artificial assumption that none dies. The totals follow a recurrence in which each new count is the sum of the two preceding counts: one, one, two, three, five, eight, thirteen, and so on, depending on where the counting begins.

Why does addition of neighboring counts appear? Separate the rabbits into those already mature and those too young to reproduce. Next month, the existing pairs remain, while the mature group adds offspring. The mature group is related to the total from a preceding month. Therefore the new total can be expressed through the last two totals. It is a short, clear example of how a recurrence describes a population with memory. The example is mathematically durable even though no real rabbit population obeys such neat rules indefinitely.

The recurrence gives a second lesson that the problem's popular fame often conceals. The connection between a stock and its age structure drives the dynamics. Newborn pairs cannot reproduce immediately, so the next generation depends on earlier generations as well as the present one. The same mathematical pattern can emerge in counts of sequences, branching choices or ways of arranging short and long units. It does not mean that every spiral or plant has secretly read Fibonacci's book. Later accounts of the golden ratio, phyllotaxis and natural design must be tested separately. The rabbit model is a useful mathematical toy, not a universal law of living things.

Leonardo did not discover this numerical pattern from nothing. Related recurrences and combinatorial counts appeared in earlier Indian mathematics, including traditions concerned with poetic rhythms. Nor did medieval Europeans immediately call it the Fibonacci sequence. That name became standard much later, especially through nineteenth-century mathematical writing. The rabbit problem is interesting as an example within *Liber abaci*, not as the crowning achievement that explains his historical importance.

His other works show why he should not be reduced to a popular sequence. The *Practica geometriae*, composed around 1220, assembled methods for geometrical measurement. It addressed surveying and other practical problems while drawing on classical geometry. The word practical did not mean a departure from serious reasoning. It meant theorems could be connected to tasks of land measurement and construction. One can move from surveying a plot to considering the properties of triangles without changing the mathematical principles being used.

He also wrote *Liber quadratorum*, the *Book of Squares*, a sophisticated study of square numbers and indeterminate problems. Here his interests extended beyond immediate trade. Think of the task of finding numbers that satisfy several square-related conditions at once. A modern algebraist might write down equations and seek rational or integer solutions. Leonardo developed arguments about squares, sums and differences using the techniques available to him. The work reveals a mathematician deeply interested in number relationships for their own sake.

Three perfect squares can sometimes form an arithmetic progression: one, twenty-five and forty-nine, for example, differ by twenty-four each time. The pattern immediately asks for a general method. What conditions allow three squares to have equal differences? How many families of solutions can be constructed? Such questions lead beyond ordinary shopping arithmetic to a theory of whole-number and rational relations. The example is a modern illustration of the subject, not a quotation from his manuscript. The important point is that an arithmetic technique can be pushed until it asks questions about the structure of numbers themselves.

An example of the general pattern is the difference between consecutive squares. Nine squared is eighty-one; eight squared is sixty-four; the difference is seventeen. In general, the square of one more than a number differs from that number's square by twice the number plus one. This can be visualized as a border added to a square arrangement of dots. The rule is elementary; the indeterminate problems that occupy *Liber quadratorum* can be far subtler. The example shows why one might investigate squares as structured objects rather than isolated arithmetic facts.

Leonardo came to the attention of scholars associated with Frederick the Second, Holy Roman Emperor and ruler in Sicily and Italy. Frederick's court supported scholars from different intellectual traditions, and mathematical challenges could be part of courtly exchange. Around 1225, when Frederick's court was in Pisa, Leonardo encountered problems presented through people including John of Palermo. He addressed some in the work called *Flos*, or *Flower*. Here the mathematician's reputation was recognized among learned courtiers, not confined to the world of merchant account books.

The role of a court did not replace the value of mercantile settings. Rather, it shows that Leonardo could work across both. A problem about exchange rates and a difficult number-theoretic challenge might belong to different social worlds, but both valued the capacity to manipulate numerical relationships. Leonardo's texts moved between these audiences by combining clear procedures and technically advanced arguments.

His written works survived through manuscript copying. This fact is easy to forget when a modern reader downloads a scan or reads a printed English translation. The original 1202 version of *Liber abaci* and the revised 1228 text did not circulate as thousands of identical printed pages. Scribes copied material, manuscripts changed hands, and editors much later compared surviving witnesses. The very existence of a modern standardized book called *Liber abaci* depends on centuries of textual preservation and scholarly work.

The scholarship of Baldassarre Boncompagni in the nineteenth century and later translators, especially Laurence Sigler, allowed modern readers to examine the substantial work behind the familiar name. An English edition includes hundreds of pages of prose and calculation. It is dense because the book really does much more than introduce ten numeral symbols. It is both a document of mathematical transmission and a work of original synthesis.

In 1240 the city of Pisa granted Leonardo a stipend in recognition of his service in teaching and advising on mathematical matters. This municipal document is a valuable biographical anchor. It places the mathematician in relation to a city that could materially recognize expertise in calculation. It also warns us against telling the story entirely as the triumph of a misunderstood solitary genius. He was useful to public institutions; his abilities were recognized by people who had reasons to value them.

After that, records of his life are scarce. The common death date around 1250 is approximate. We cannot reconstruct his family relations in the detail modern biographies often supply for famous scholars. Nor can we trace every channel through which the arithmetic described in his books spread. The adoption of Hindu-Arabic numerals in Europe was gradual. It involved merchants, schools, notaries, governments, scribes, and later printed arithmetic manuals; obstacles included habit, recordkeeping conventions, and worries about fraud. Leonardo was an influential participant in that transformation, not a man who walked into Europe one day and replaced all the numerals at once.

Consider what a change in notation requires of an institution. A clerk must trust that a zero cannot be inserted fraudulently; a merchant must read numbers written by people trained elsewhere; teachers must explain the new procedures; legal documents must continue to be intelligible across generations. An abacus can remain useful even after written place-value notation becomes common. For a long time multiple methods may coexist. The history of numerical notation is not a referendum in which an entire continent chooses one system on a single day. Leonardo's books mattered because they made calculation with the new notation explicit, teachable and demonstrably useful.

There is also a danger in treating mathematics as a series of civilizational handoffs in which one people owns an idea until another takes it away. The actual record is more entangled. Indian decimal notation, Arabic-language scholarly calculation, North African commerce, Latin manuscript culture and Italian city governments all enter Leonardo's story. Each made a different contribution. Naming the communities he learned from does not diminish his work; it explains why his book could be so useful.

The next time you see the string one, one, two, three, five, eight, it is worth remembering how small a fraction of Leonardo's writing it represents. He spent years learning across the Mediterranean and wrote a book that tried to make difficult calculation teachable. He studied squares and geometry; he engaged with an imperial court; he was rewarded for service to his city. His legacy is not just that certain numbers recur in leaves, shells, or growth patterns, claims that themselves often need careful qualification. It is that the ability to calculate could be made more portable. A merchant in one port and a scholar in another could work with the same numerical structures even while the coins, languages and measures around them differed.

The imagined merchant at the beginning can now finish his accounts. If the units are clear, the fractions exact, and the conversion rules reliable, then mathematics permits the trade to proceed. Leonardo's importance lies partly in making such procedures available to a new audience, and partly in seeing how the same operations led to deeper questions. The life of Fibonacci is a history of translation in the largest sense: not merely translating words, but translating methods from one intellectual setting into another without losing the relations that made them work.

## Historical companion — not spoken

### Evidentiary chronology

- **c. 1170:** Birth probably in Pisa; precise dates are uncertain.
- **Childhood and youth:** Learned Indian-derived decimal calculation in Bugia (Bejaia), where his father Guglielmo represented Pisan merchants; subsequent Mediterranean travels.
- **1202:** First edition of *Liber abaci*.
- **c. 1220–1225:** *Practica geometriae*, *Liber quadratorum*, and the courtly mathematical setting surrounding *Flos* and Frederick II.
- **1228:** Expanded revision of *Liber abaci*.
- **1240:** Pisan civic stipend recognizing mathematical service; later personal record sparse.
- **Nineteenth–twenty-first centuries:** Scholarly editing, translation, and popular reorientation toward the rabbit recurrence.

### Mathematical companion

- **Positional arithmetic:** \(205\times37=205(30+7)=6150+1435=7585\). Not a historical quotation.
- **Rabbit recurrence:** \(F_{n+2}=F_{n+1}+F_n\). Initial conventions vary with the counting of months. The toy problem ignores death and resource limits.
- **Squares:** \((n+1)^2-n^2=2n+1\). Elementary identity illustrating the structured study of squares; *Liber quadratorum* contains much more demanding indeterminate problems.
- **Transmission caution:** The numeral system originated before Leonardo, and he was not the first European user. His contribution was a substantial exposition and synthesis of commercial and theoretical arithmetic.
