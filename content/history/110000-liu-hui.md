# 110000 — Liu Hui: The commentator who asked why the rules work

**Track:** History — People and ideas. **Focus:** Third-century Chinese mathematics; the layered history of the *Nine Chapters*, proofs by rearrangement and exhaustion, positive and negative numbers, rods and elimination, the sphere problem, and *Sea Island* surveying. **Background:** arithmetic, simple area and volume, and the idea of comparing similar triangles are introduced in speech.

Suppose someone hands you a book of rules. The rule for a trapezoid says to add the lengths of its parallel sides, multiply by the distance between them, and take half. You try it on a few examples, and every answer is right. Would you be satisfied? You might be. The rule is useful. But a second kind of reader asks why half the sum, and why it works for every trapezoid. That question—why a procedure is justified—is the doorway to Liu Hui.

He lived in China in the third century of the common era, during the age called the Three Kingdoms. Historians cannot tell us reliably when he was born or died, which city raised him, whom he married, or what he did for a living. A commentary is conventionally dated to 263, although even the date comes from a later textual witness rather than from a complete contemporary biography. There is very little of the ordinary evidence from which an intimate life story could be built. If we want to know his character, we must begin with how he treated mathematical arguments.

The political world around him was fractured. The Han dynasty had ended, and rival regimes fought for territory, personnel, and legitimacy. The state of Wei held much of northern China, while Shu and Wu controlled other regions. Armies moved, courts reorganized, and officials faced competing demands. None of this proves that Liu Hui served in a particular bureau or witnessed a particular battle. It tells us what kind of society contained his work. Agricultural land had to be measured, grain distributed, labor assigned, taxes calculated, canals built, and the dimensions of objects estimated. Calculation was woven into government and practical administration.

A book called the *Nine Chapters on the Mathematical Art* gave readers hundreds of problems with procedures for solving them. It was not written all at once by Liu Hui. It was a compilation with roots in earlier Han-era mathematics and probably an even more complicated prehistory. Its problems involve land measurement, grain exchange, distribution, taxation, engineering, square and cube roots, simultaneous equations, and right triangles.

The chapters are organized around classes of problems rather than a single chain of definitions followed by propositions. A reader can encounter a mathematical idea first through the demand to share goods fairly, then encounter a related numerical structure in work on construction. That arrangement makes sense in a society where people could learn mathematics as a craft of administration. But the abstraction is real. Once a method for proportion is understood, it can operate whether the measured thing is grain, labor, money, or distance. Changing the story changes the units, not the mathematical relationship. Liu Hui's explanations helped make that transfer visible. Some readers imagine that its practical character makes it less mathematical than a Greek geometry book. That is a false contrast. A problem about grain can embody the same abstraction as an algebraic equation; a problem about earthworks can require sophisticated geometry.

Liu Hui's contribution was commentary: the act of going through an inherited mathematical text and explaining, correcting, extending, and sometimes challenging it. That sounds modest if commentary means merely giving definitions in the margins. It was not modest in his hands. Again and again he asked what principle justified a rule. He took recipes and turned them into arguments. He also found places where received procedures were not correct, and he did not conceal the difficulty.

Take the trapezoid. Imagine making a second, identical trapezoid, turning it upside down, and placing it beside the first. Their slanting sides fit to make a parallelogram. The parallelogram has the same height as the original trapezoid. Its base is the sum of the trapezoid's two parallel sides. The two shapes together have area equal to that base times the height, so one trapezoid has half as much. You can understand the proof without a symbolic equation. A measured rule becomes a statement about how pieces of area behave when rearranged.

Consider what the argument depends on. If two figures can be cut and reassembled without overlap or gaps, they have equal area. If a figure is made of two congruent copies, it has twice the area of one copy. Neither premise requires that the answer first be written as a formula. Now think of a field bounded by two parallel roads of different lengths. The proof tells you that the effective width is their average, as long as the separation is measured perpendicularly. It also tells you when the rule is not applicable. A random four-sided field with no pair of parallel sides cannot simply be forced into the same formula. Proof gives the formula a domain as well as an answer.

This way of reasoning expands to more elaborate solids. Why is a pyramid's volume one third of the corresponding prism? How can a strangely cut wedge be compared with a simpler block? A diagram can suggest an answer, but a trustworthy argument must explain what happens as pieces are divided or transferred. Liu Hui developed geometric decompositions and procedures of finer subdivision that historians often compare with exhaustion arguments. It is useful to recognize the family resemblance to later limit reasoning without pretending his notation or foundations were identical to ours.

Circle measurement provides another extraordinary case. The inherited text often relied on a rough value for the circumference-to-diameter ratio, effectively treating pi as three in suitable calculations. Liu Hui did not merely swap in a better number. He asked why polygons inscribed in a circle should approach the circle's area and what happens to the regions left over between straight edges and the curved boundary. Start with a hexagon inside a circle. Each side cuts off a curved segment. Double the number of sides, and each side becomes shorter. Repeat. The polygon fills more of the circle, and the unfilled pieces become smaller.

One can carry out a toy version of the process without historical counting rods. A regular hexagon in a unit circle has six sides of length one, so its perimeter is six. The circle's true circumference is a little more than six, and an inscribed twelve-sided polygon has a longer perimeter that comes closer. Doubling again improves the estimate. The mathematical work is to calculate each new side, compare the polygon with the circle, and bound what the straight edges leave out. Repetition creates better numerical data; an argument about the remaining pieces turns the repetition into controlled approximation. The second part is what separates a useful table from an explanation of why it improves.

There is a calculation hidden in that picture. An inscribed regular polygon is made of triangles whose vertices meet at the circle's center. As the sides become numerous and short, each triangle has an altitude close to the circle's radius. Sum their areas and you get approximately one half of the radius times the polygon's perimeter. In the limit suggested by the procedure, the perimeter approaches the circumference, giving the area of the circle as half the radius times the circumference. In modern letters this is the familiar relation between pi times radius squared and half of two pi radius times the radius. Liu Hui's reasoning approached that result through geometric dissection rather than through our algebraic notation.

He also used successive polygons to improve numerical approximations to pi. Traditional accounts connect his calculation with three point one four and, through further subdivision, a value close to three point one four one five nine. Different reconstructions of the details rely on text, calculations, and editorial history, so a precise number of polygons should always be tied to a specified account. The historically important point is the structure of the method: repeated doubling, quantified leftover regions, and improving bounds.

Not every difficult shape yielded to him. The volume of a sphere defeated the argument he had available. The *Nine Chapters* contained a procedure whose assumed volume relation was incorrect. Liu Hui examined a special shape formed from intersecting cylindrical regions, often described through a box-lid construction, and could expose the error in the inherited reasoning. But he could not complete the derivation of the correct sphere-volume formula. Rather than certify a false proof, he left the problem unresolved.

This is a revealing moment. Textbooks often present mathematical history as a procession of victorious discoveries. A scholar in such a procession never fails; at worst he takes one step that a successor completes. Liu Hui's unfinished sphere problem is more informative. It shows which analogies were available to him, which comparisons he trusted, and where those comparisons stopped working. Several centuries later, Zu Gengzhi, following work associated with his father Zu Chongzhi and later transmitted through commentaries, supplied a different geometrical argument for the sphere's volume. Modern scholarship, including Donald Wagner's and Daiwie Fu's analyses, examines why the methods differed. Liu Hui's failure was itself an argument about the limits of a method.

Now move to a less visual part of the *Nine Chapters*: simultaneous equations. Think of two kinds of grain sold in combinations. Two baskets of the first grain and one of the second cost eleven units. One basket of the first and two of the second cost ten. In modern symbols we might write two x plus y equals eleven, and x plus two y equals ten. Multiply the second relation by two and subtract the first: three y equals nine, so y is three. Then x must be four. The example is invented here to show the structure; it is not a quotation from Liu Hui's problem text.

Elimination also depends on what operations preserve a set of solutions. If two equations are both true for the quantities sought, subtracting one equation from a multiple of the other produces another relation that the same quantities must satisfy. The new relation may contain fewer unknowns. With three or more variables, the process can be repeated systematically. Counting-rod layouts can support those transformations even when the textual exposition is entirely different from a modern linear-algebra textbook. A representation designed for practical calculation can embody a powerful general algorithm. That is one reason the history of matrices begins long before the word matrix was coined.

Chinese calculators could express coefficients and numerical quantities using counting rods arranged on a board. Columns represented the contributions of different unknowns or equations; moving and combining the rods performed the arithmetic. What we now call elimination could be conducted as a sequence of operations on those columns. Some modern accounts call the procedure Gaussian elimination. The name is a convenience for comparison, not evidence that Gauss invented the Chinese algorithm centuries later or that Liu Hui wrote matrices with square brackets. A mathematical operation may have multiple histories before later notation makes it appear to be a single invention.

The computations required positive and negative quantities. The *Nine Chapters* supplied operational rules, sometimes represented through different-colored counting rods. Liu Hui explained and justified aspects of these rules in his commentary. If a deficiency is represented by a negative quantity and an excess by a positive one, then adding and subtracting them makes practical sense. A calculation can have more negative than positive contributions even when nobody owns a negative basket of grain. The abstract quantity records the difference between a reference amount and what is present. That idea permits operations far beyond any single economic problem.

Imagine balancing two obligations. You owe seven units and receive ten. The net result is three units in your favor. Or you receive four but owe nine; the balance is five against you. Such arithmetic might feel obvious now, but a systematic theory of signed numbers was not inevitable. It had to be embedded in reliable procedures that could be taught and checked. The rules in the Chinese tradition did that without waiting for European algebraic notation to arrive.

There are equally instructive calculations involving roots and fractions. The *Nine Chapters* contains routines for extracting square and cube roots, comparing quantities with different measures, and allocating shares according to proportional contribution. To understand the mathematics one must attend to the unit: length, area, grain volume, coin weight, labor time. A transformation that is legitimate for numbers can be illegitimate for physical quantities if the units are forgotten. The commentary helps readers see the relationships behind the operations. It is not merely a technical patch on an older text.

Liu Hui was also interested in measurement when the object could not be approached directly. Suppose an island rises beyond the water, or a mountain peak is too far away to reach with a measuring pole. You can place markers at known positions, line up your sight with the top of a distant object, then move to a second position and repeat. Two lines of sight, together with measured distances, permit the remote height or distance to be calculated through similar triangles. The shape of the triangle, not the accessibility of its far vertex, becomes the instrument.

A simple invented land-survey problem illustrates the principle. Suppose a tower stands beyond a wall. From two positions on level ground fifty paces apart, you measure how sharply you must look upward to see the top. The nearer angle is steeper than the farther one. The angles define two right triangles with the same unknown vertical side, but with horizontal sides that differ by fifty paces. Geometric proportion can determine the missing height and the first horizontal distance. The historical *Sea Island* methods use poles, alignments and differences in measured distances rather than the trigonometric notation just suggested. But both approaches exploit a known baseline to reveal an inaccessible dimension.

These problems became associated with the *Sea Island Mathematical Manual*. It consists of nine surveying problems and is linked historically with Liu Hui's commentary on the final chapter of the *Nine Chapters*. Later editors separated the material into an independent text. That editorial separation matters: readers looking at two modern book titles might imagine two discrete writing projects separated by years. The surviving textual arrangement is less straightforward. It records a mathematical continuation that later transmission gave a new identity.

The first sea-island problem is particularly apt. One cannot lay a measuring tape from the shore to the summit of an inaccessible island. But a known distance between two observation points and a known measuring pole can produce ratios from which the unseen height is determined. The mathematics turns a physical barrier into a construction of intersecting sight lines. Similar triangles are often taught now through a diagram of a person measuring a tree's shadow. In Liu Hui's world they were part of a practical mathematics of terrain, work, and administration.

When we read his words today, another layer enters the story. The ancient manuscript that Liu Hui wrote is not sitting before us in its original form. The *Nine Chapters* survived through copying, editorial decisions, and later commentaries, among them work by Li Chunfeng and others. Printed editions appeared long after Liu Hui's lifetime. Modern translations require decisions about technical Chinese terminology, diagrams, the relationship between the base text and the commentary, and what a short passage assumes its reader already knows.

A translator must also decide where the old algorithm ends and an explanatory remark begins. Did the original compiler intend a rule as an empirical recipe? Did Liu Hui offer a proof, an illustrative manipulation, or a correction? A footnote that silently turns an ancient word into a modern symbol can make the mathematics easier to read but the historical distinction harder to see. Critical editions help by separating textual witnesses and showing where the wording has changed. We do not need to understand every philological detail to appreciate that a mathematical theorem has a history as a document as well as a history as an idea.

This is why a history of mathematics should name translators and editors as carefully as it names discoverers. Karine Chemla and Guo Shuchun have studied the mathematical reasoning expressed through the textual tradition; Shen Kangshen, John Crossley, and Anthony Lun prepared a major English translation and study. Their work permits readers from other linguistic traditions to inspect Liu Hui's arguments rather than relying on a short list of discoveries. Translation is an intellectual activity here: even the choice between saying a rule 'calculates' and saying it 'proves' can alter the reader's understanding of the commentary.

It would be a mistake to turn the comparison between Chinese and Greek geometry into a contest. One tradition made certain forms of explicit axiomatic proof central; another organized large bodies of knowledge around problems, algorithms, explanatory commentary, and geometric dissection. Neither was internally uniform. Mathematical reasoning crosses these categories: a procedure can carry a proof, and a proof can instruct a calculation. Liu Hui's work makes that plain because he cared about the legitimacy of a rule whether its subject was a circle, a pyramid, a field, or an exchange of goods.

The gaps in the biography remain real. We do not know whether Liu Hui taught pupils in a formal school, how he obtained the manuscripts he studied, or what he hoped a particular patron would think of his work. We should not fabricate a solitary genius sitting by lantern light during a military campaign. What survives instead is a sustained conversation with a book. Its anonymous compilers said, in effect, here is how to calculate. Liu Hui answered: show me what the numbers mean, and why the procedure is true.

There is a form of courage in writing down where a method breaks. A wrong sphere formula could have been accepted by authority. He explored it and found a limit. A rough estimate of pi could have remained adequate. He improved it through repeated geometry. A book of algorithms could have stayed an instrument of expert calculation. He enriched it with explanations that others could contest and strengthen. The man is difficult to see through the political history of the Three Kingdoms; the mathematical mind is easier to recognize. He was a reader who would not let successful arithmetic substitute for understanding.

## Historical companion — not spoken

### Evidentiary chronology

- **Third century CE:** Liu Hui was active in the Chinese mathematical tradition during the Three Kingdoms period. Dates often given for birth and death are approximate conjectures, not biographical records.
- **Traditionally 263:** Commentary on the *Jiuzhang suanshu* (*Nine Chapters on the Mathematical Art*), date supplied by a later text.
- **Subsequent textual history:** *Sea Island Mathematical Manual* circulated as an independent work following separation from the *Nine Chapters* tradition; later commentators including Li Chunfeng transmitted and extended the material.
- **Fifth–seventh centuries:** Work of Zu Gengzhi and other mathematical scholars addressed the sphere-volume problem that Liu Hui had left unresolved.
- **Modern era:** Critical editions and translations make it possible to distinguish inherited rules, Liu Hui's explanations, later comments, and modern notation.

### Mathematical companion

- **Trapezoid by rearrangement:** \(A=\tfrac12(a+b)h\). Two congruent trapezoids form a parallelogram of base \(a+b\) and height \(h\).
- **Circle by exhaustion:** Inscribed regular polygons with increasing side counts give a geometric approximation to the area. Triangle decomposition suggests \(A=\frac12 r C\), or \(\pi r^2\) when \(C=2\pi r\).
- **Elimination (new illustrative problem):** \(2x+y=11\), \(x+2y=10\), giving \(x=4\), \(y=3\). Modern letters are not the counting-rod notation of the original.
- **Sphere problem:** Liu Hui rejected the inadequate inherited volume formula but did not derive the correct \(V=\tfrac43\pi r^3\); a later proof associated with Zu Gengzhi supplied the result.
- **Remote survey:** Compare similar triangles formed by known marker heights and baseline distances with an unknown distant object. The measured distances and sightlines substitute for direct access.

The later sphere proof belongs to Zu Gengzhi rather than to Liu Hui.
