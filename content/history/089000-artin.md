# 089000 - Emil Artin: Music, reciprocity, exile, and the making of modern algebra

**Track:** History - People and ideas. **Focus:** Vienna and Bohemia, the First World War, Hamburg's mathematical community, class field theory, family and migration under National Socialism, American classrooms, and his return to Hamburg. **Background:** curiosity about numbers and algebra; no advanced algebra assumed.

A mathematical theorem can carry a name for a century while the person behind the name becomes difficult to see. Artin reciprocity is one of the great organizing results of number theory. Artin groups, Artinian rings, Artin-Schreier extensions, and a conjecture about primitive roots have all kept Emil Artin's name in circulation. But his life is not well described by a list of these objects. He was a musician as well as a mathematician, a teacher whose students remembered the experience of thinking in his presence, an Austrian veteran of the First World War, a central figure in a young German university, and a father who left the country because Nazi policy made his family's future untenable. His mathematical habit was to discover a structure inside several apparently different problems. The story of his career is also the story of twentieth-century institutions being built, broken, and rebuilt.

Emil Artin was born in Vienna in March eighteen ninety-eight. His father worked as an art dealer; his mother had been an opera singer. Much of his childhood was spent in Reichenberg, now Liberec in the Czech Republic, then a German-speaking industrial city in the Habsburg lands. This was not a timeless, culturally uniform Germany. Political borders, languages, national movements, and family loyalties overlapped. Artin later remembered his schooling as lonely. In accounts of his early development, he did not present himself as a child who had always been consumed by mathematics. Chemistry interested him first. He also spent a memorable year studying in France. The later mathematician's quickness should not erase the person who discovered his subject gradually.

By the time Artin finished school in nineteen sixteen, the First World War had transformed the paths available to young men across Europe. He enrolled at the University of Vienna, but military service interrupted his studies after a semester. He served in the Austrian army until the war ended. The young scholar returning to civilian mathematics in nineteen nineteen had lived through the collapse of an empire and the destruction of the political world in which he had grown up. His training continued at Leipzig with Gustav Herglotz. In nineteen twenty-one he completed a doctorate on number-theoretic methods applied to function fields. The subject would prove important: instead of asking only about ordinary integers, one could study similar arithmetic questions when the 'numbers' were functions and their coefficients came from finite fields.

Artin spent the next academic year at Göttingen, where mathematics was taught through lectures, informal discussion, and the constant movement of ideas among students and visitors. Then he moved to Hamburg. The University of Hamburg had been founded only in nineteen nineteen. It did not have centuries of prestige to rely on; its seminar culture was being made by the scholars working there. Artin, Erich Hecke, and Wilhelm Blaschke helped establish an active mathematical environment. Artin qualified to teach independently in nineteen twenty-three, became an extraordinary professor in nineteen twenty-five, and held a full professorship in nineteen twenty-six. In his twenties he was already helping shape an institution and a field.

Music mattered throughout these years. Artin's family background had given him familiarity with performance, and his own piano playing remained a serious interest. Later recollections of the Artin household describe instruments, conversation, and children learning music. This is not evidence that music somehow caused his theorems. It shows the range of work and pleasure that occupied his life, and it helps explain why former students remembered his teaching as an expressive act rather than as the delivery of a finished set of symbols.

Hamburg was where Artin's astonishing mathematical range became visible. Number theorists had long studied reciprocity laws. Gauss's quadratic reciprocity connected the behavior of primes in a manner that initially looked almost magical. In the nineteenth century, arithmetic was increasingly recast using algebraic number fields, their ideals, and their extensions. A prime number might split into several prime ideals when the field was enlarged. Different patterns of splitting held information about the extension. People such as Hilbert, Takagi, and others developed class field theory to understand these relationships. Artin entered an already sophisticated subject, not an empty territory waiting for a single founder.

To feel the central question, imagine that we enlarge the ordinary rational numbers by adjoining a square root. Some primes behave one way in the new number system; some behave another way. In more complicated extensions, the pattern is not just a yes-or-no distinction. There can be a whole group of symmetries, a Galois group, describing how the new field can be rearranged while its original numbers remain fixed. Artin's reciprocity law, proved in nineteen twenty-seven, attached to arithmetic data a way of reaching that symmetry group. In an appropriate abelian extension, a map built from ideals and their behavior gives the precise quotient and correspondence predicted by class field theory. One must state hypotheses carefully; it is not a magic formula for every imaginable extension. The conceptual achievement was to turn scattered rules about residues and splitting into the expression of a common structure.

The history of the proof is a history of correspondence and mutual influence. Helmut Hasse was writing a major account of class field theory and discussed problems with Artin. Their surviving letters allow historians to follow some of the work as it was taking shape. We should not picture a solitary man writing a definitive answer while the rest of Europe waited. Artin's breakthrough was his own, but its meaning depended on a community of researchers, on existing theorems, and on the circulation of drafts and objections. This was exactly the kind of mathematical world that a seminar could support.

His research moved in several directions at once. Artin studied braid groups, inspired by the motion of strands that wind around one another without passing through each other. Imagine several marked points moving inside a disk and finally returning to the same set of positions. Their trajectories trace strands through space and time. Two motions can be performed one after another, and this gives an algebraic multiplication. The order matters: exchanging the order of two crossings can change the braid. What looks like a craft object becomes a noncommutative group. Artin helped give the subject an algebraic form in the nineteen twenties, decades before braid groups became important in topology and other areas of mathematics.

He also worked on the structure of rings: systems with addition and multiplication that can behave very differently from ordinary integers. In one influential class, descending chains of certain ideals must eventually stop. The modern name Artinian rings commemorates his role, but should not conceal the work of Emmy Noether and other algebraists who were changing the language of the field. The value of a chain condition is that it prevents infinite descent inside an algebraic structure and makes finite decomposition theorems possible. Artin's research provided tools for a mathematics that increasingly treated an algebraic object by its internal relations rather than by a particular formula.

In collaboration with Otto Schreier, he studied formally real and real-closed fields. There is a remarkably concrete question behind the terminology: when does an algebraic field admit a sensible notion of positive and negative? One test asks whether minus one can be written as a sum of squares. In an ordered field it cannot, because squares are nonnegative. That elementary observation leads into the algebraic theory of order. In nineteen twenty-seven Artin solved Hilbert's seventeenth problem: a rational function that never takes negative values, wherever it is defined over the real numbers, can be expressed as a sum of squares of rational functions. Notice the qualification. The statement is not that every such polynomial must be a sum of squares of polynomials. The denominators matter. The distinction is part of the theorem, not a pedantic footnote.

Such diversity of topics can make Artin appear restless. Another interpretation is that he recognized a common opportunity: a broad mathematical situation could become intelligible once the right structural language was found. A prime's splitting behavior, a set of moving strands, a ring's ideals, and the ordering of a field are very different objects. Yet all can be studied by finding transformations, invariant properties, and conditions under which complicated behavior reduces to simpler pieces. Artin's contributions did not amount to one universal theory. Their family resemblance helps explain his influence on how algebra came to be taught.

Private life and public life were also developing. In nineteen twenty-nine Artin married Natascha Jasny, sometimes identified in sources as Natalie. Her family background became consequential under Nazi racial law. They had children, including the future mathematician Michael Artin. His wife and children were not minor characters added to the end of a mathematical biography. Their safety shaped what decisions were possible when Germany changed. After Hitler took power in nineteen thirty-three, the exclusion of Jews and people with Jewish family ties from universities and public life steadily intensified. Artin's own background did not protect a family made vulnerable by his wife's ancestry.

In nineteen thirty-seven the Artins left Hamburg for the United States. The family arrived into a transatlantic community of displaced scholars, with Richard Courant and Hermann Weyl among those who helped receive and support them. A first appointment took Artin to Notre Dame. He then taught at Indiana University, in Bloomington, from nineteen thirty-eight to nineteen forty-six. Later came Princeton, where he held a professorship from nineteen forty-six until nineteen fifty-eight. These appointments were not simply a tale of American academic advancement. They marked the forced transfer of a European research culture through a migration that separated families, colleagues, and students.

At Indiana and Princeton, Artin's classroom presence became an important part of his legacy. Students recalled lectures that developed a subject as if it were being discovered, moving through a carefully chosen example toward an idea that now seemed inevitable. His books and courses helped shape the presentation of algebra, Galois theory, gamma functions, and number theory. A student could encounter abstract concepts not as a dictionary of definitions but as tools introduced because a problem required them. This pedagogical influence is difficult to measure in published theorems. Yet it is one reason Artin's mathematics traveled so far.

The American years also brought other investigations. He continued interests in algebra and number theory, developed his exposition, and participated in an international community that included former refugees and younger American mathematicians. Serge Lang and John Tate were among students influenced by his work and teaching. His children grew up in a household connecting European and American languages and culture. Artin did not cease to be attached to Hamburg. In the nineteen fifties he visited Germany again, and in nineteen fifty-eight he returned to a professorship at his former university. The return was not a restoration of the world that had existed before nineteen thirty-three. Some colleagues were gone; institutions and politics had changed. It was nevertheless a decision to teach again in the place where his career had flourished.

Artin died in Hamburg in December nineteen sixty-two. He was sixty-four. His life had spanned the Habsburg Empire, two world wars, the destruction of German academic life under Nazism, migration to the United States, and the rebuilding of international mathematics after nineteen forty-five. None of those events can be deduced from the reciprocity law. Yet removing them would produce a false sense of what mathematical work requires from the societies that support it. Seminars, libraries, jobs, friendships, family security, travel, and political protection were not external scenery. They were conditions under which people could think and teach.

To conclude, return to a prime number that splits inside a larger field. The phenomenon looks local and particular: this prime behaves one way, that prime another. Artin found a description that exposes the symmetry beneath many such particular events. His career shows a similar movement between particular lives and larger structures, although history is never governed by a mathematical theorem. The scholar in Hamburg was also the father on an emigrant ship, the musician at home, and the teacher inviting students to see an argument take shape. The enduring significance of his mathematics lies partly in the results themselves and partly in the generous, exacting style of thought that others carried away from his classrooms.

## Historical companion (not narrated)

### Historical chronology

- **1898–1921:** born in Vienna; raised mainly in Reichenberg; studies interrupted by Austro-Hungarian military service; doctoral degree at Leipzig in 1921.
- **1922–1937:** Hamburg appointment and seminar; braid theory, reciprocity law, formally real fields, Artin-Schreier work; married Natascha Jasny in 1929; Nazi persecution made emigration necessary.
- **1937–1958:** emigration with family; Notre Dame, Indiana University, and Princeton; major influence through teaching and writing.
- **1958–1962:** returned to the University of Hamburg; died there in 1962.

### Mathematical companion

In an abelian extension of number fields, Artin reciprocity connects ideal-class information with the Galois group via an Artin map. A precise formulation uses a modulus and ray class group: there is a surjective homomorphism from an appropriate ray class group onto the Galois group, with a kernel specified by class field theory. This is not the same as claiming all number-field extensions are abelian.

A braid group on \(n\) strands has standard generators \(\sigma_1,\ldots,\sigma_{n-1}\). Far-apart crossings commute, while adjacent crossings satisfy \(\sigma_i\sigma_{i+1}\sigma_i=\sigma_{i+1}\sigma_i\sigma_{i+1}\). The group remembers crossing order rather than the material properties of actual rope.

Hilbert's seventeenth problem is solved by a rational-function sum of squares, not a universal polynomial sum of squares assertion. A real-closed field is a formally real field with no proper algebraic extension that is formally real.

**Historical caution:** Spellings Natascha/Natasha and Natalie vary in biographical records; avoid implying a different spouse. His wife's ancestry, not Artin's own identity, was central to the Nazi persecution affecting the family. A private retrospective recollection, a contemporaneous letter, and a later mathematical exposition are different grades of evidence. The Artin reciprocity law had substantial precursors; the 1927 theorem must not be confused with a proof of all class field theory.
