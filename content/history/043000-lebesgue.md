# 043000 — Henri Lebesgue: A schoolteacher and the reshaping of analysis

**Track:** History · People and ideas. **Theme:** measure, integration, convergence, and the changing profession of analysis. **Background:** elementary limits, areas, sums, and the idea of a function.
<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

Around nineteen hundred, a teacher at a secondary school in Nancy was carrying out work that would transform mathematical analysis. Henri Lebesgue did not yet hold a celebrated Paris professorship. He worked in a French educational system that trained teachers in demanding mathematics, and he pursued research while earning his living in a classroom. The problem that made him famous grew out of arguments over what a function could be and what it meant to measure something. That achievement had a human and institutional history before it became a standard part of calculus.

Henri Léon Lebesgue was born in Beauvais, France, in eighteen seventy-five. His father worked as a printer, not as a university mathematician. Lebesgue attended school in Beauvais and then in Paris, and in eighteen ninety-four entered the École Normale Supérieure, an institution that educated many of France's teachers and researchers. He qualified as a mathematics teacher in eighteen ninety-seven. What matters about the next phase of his life is how much of it was spent reading, teaching, and trying to understand problems that the usual calculus no longer resolved.

He worked in the library of the École Normale and read René Baire's investigations of discontinuous functions. When calculus is first learned, the functions tend to be smooth curves. By the late nineteenth century, mathematicians knew that a function could be discontinuous at enormously many points, and that pointwise limits of ordinary functions could behave very differently from the functions used to approximate them. Analysis had become a study of the boundary between a formula one could write down and a quantity one could legitimately calculate from it.

Lebesgue taught at a lycée in Nancy between eighteen ninety-nine and nineteen hundred two, while producing work that would alter the subject. A teacher preparing lessons and marking papers might seem far from the research centers of Paris. Yet this was a productive interval. He built on the work of Baire, Émile Borel, Camille Jordan, and others who were trying to make sense of sets of points, their size, and the pathologies of limits. The historical credit is shared across those predecessors. Lebesgue's achievement was to assemble a theory of measurable sets and integration powerful enough to make the new questions answerable.

In nineteen hundred one Lebesgue announced his generalization of the integral, and in nineteen hundred two defended the doctoral dissertation *Intégrale, longueur, aire*: Integral, Length, Area. This was the work that gave his theory a broad foundation. The dissertation title is revealing. It joins three things that had sometimes been studied separately: integration, the length of curves, and the measurement of geometric sets. Lebesgue was not simply trying to rescue an eccentric counterexample. He was asking what it means to assign a reliable numerical size to a complicated object.

The research traveled through his teaching career. Lebesgue went to Rennes as a university lecturer in nineteen hundred two and later to positions in Paris. He married Louise-Marguerite Vallet in nineteen hundred three; they had two children, and the marriage ended in divorce in nineteen sixteen. Biographical summaries record these circumstances but tell us much less about his domestic conversations than about his professional disagreements. It would be misleading to invent an intimate temperament to fill that gap. What is well documented is an intellectually forceful mathematician whose work developed amid rivalries over priority and prestige as well as genuine exchanges of ideas.

His relations with Baire, for instance, were not a simple story of teacher and grateful student. Both investigated the behavior of functions, but their professional relationship became contentious, including an early dispute around invitations to prestigious lectures at the Collège de France. Borel, too, was both an intellectual predecessor and a figure with whom attribution and methodological differences mattered. This is why the mathematical lineage is important. Borel had developed ways to measure sets; Baire had investigated classes of functions; Lebesgue made these ideas interact in a general integral. The achievement was his, but it did not emerge from an empty landscape.

During the First World War Lebesgue worked on problems connected with French national defense. His relationship with Borel, already shaped by differences in method and standing, worsened in that period. The war did not suspend university and scientific work: it redirected people's labor and subjected friendships and professional disagreements to new pressures.

Lebesgue's professional life continued to reflect the relationship between teaching and research. He became a member of the French Academy of Sciences and held a chair at the Collège de France. His lectures and books transmitted the new theory to generations of mathematicians. The measure-theoretic language that students now meet early in graduate analysis was once an intellectual wager made by people trying to clarify functions that violated the habits of nineteenth-century calculus. It was criticized, defended, extended, and eventually incorporated into the standard toolkit.

That route, from schoolteacher to major French research institution, gives the work its human setting. To see what Lebesgue and his contemporaries were trying to repair, let us work through the mathematical problem carefully. The details are worth preserving, but they arise from the history rather than replacing it.

Imagine a light switch that is on at every rational number between zero and one, and off at every irrational number. Rational numbers are fractions of integers, such as one half or seven tenths. Irrational numbers are the other points on the number line, including the square root of two divided by two. Both kinds of points are everywhere: no matter how closely we zoom into a stretch of the line, it contains rational numbers and irrational numbers. If the switch's position is described by a function, what is the total amount of light, measured over the interval from zero to one?

This is an artificial question, but it exposes a real weakness in an older mathematical method. If we divide the interval into small pieces and estimate area by choosing a sample point in each piece, every piece permits the value one, and every piece also permits the value zero. No amount of making the pieces narrower settles the disagreement. An upper estimate gives one. A lower estimate gives zero. In the sense of Riemann integration, the function has no integral. Yet another intuition says the rationals, though dense, are individually scattered and countable. If we can assign a total length to the set on which the switch is on, perhaps that length should be zero.

At the beginning of the twentieth century, Henri Lebesgue developed a way of making that second intuition rigorous. The important shift was not merely a larger table of integration rules. It was a new order of operations. Instead of organizing the domain into short intervals and asking how high the function stands there, one can ask which points have a given range of heights, measure the size of those sets, and assemble the total from their contributions. It is a change of viewpoint that makes many discontinuous functions manageable and makes passing to limits far more reliable.

Begin with the size of a set. A single point on a line has length zero. Two points still have length zero, and so does any fixed finite collection. What about infinitely many points? Here we must avoid the tempting but invalid rule that an arbitrary infinite collection of zeros automatically has size zero. There are uncountably many points in a whole interval, and that interval has positive length. But for a countable collection of individual points, there is a beautiful argument.

List the points as the first, second, third, and so on. Cover the first by a very short open interval, the second by an interval half as long, the third by one half as long again, and continue. The total length of all these coverings can be made smaller than any prescribed positive number. We have not said that the points are separated, or that the intervals do not overlap. We only need an upper estimate for the total length of the covering. Because that estimate can be made as small as we please, the countable set has measure zero. The rational numbers between zero and one are countable, even though they are dense. So the set of positions at which our artificial light is on has measure zero.

There is an important logical point hidden inside the covering argument. We are measuring length using countably many coverings and countable combinations of sets. If two measurable sets do not overlap, we want the measure of their union to be the sum of their measures. The same property should hold for a sequence of pairwise disjoint measurable sets. This is countable additivity. It distinguishes a genuinely countably additive measure from a merely intuitive guess about how size ought to behave. A set can have infinitely many components and still have a definite size, but we must know which sets are measurable and which rules of combination we are allowed to use.

Consider a second example. Start with the interval from zero to one and remove its middle third. Then remove the middle thirds of the two pieces that remain. Continue indefinitely. The surviving set is the familiar middle-third Cantor set. It contains uncountably many points: each survives by making an endless left-or-right choice. Yet its length is zero, because the combined length of the removed open intervals approaches one. This destroys another easy but misleading intuition. A set with uncountably many points need not occupy positive length. Countability explains why the rational numbers have measure zero, but uncountability alone does not tell us the measure of a set.

The example also helps clarify a word from Lebesgue's title: area. In ordinary geometry we measure rectangles by multiplying side lengths, and we use approximations by rectangles for less regular regions. Lebesgue's measure theory extends this impulse while allowing complicated countable operations. It does not say that every imaginable collection of points can be assigned a length consistent with all the expected rules. With the usual axioms of mathematics there are nonmeasurable subsets of the line. The proper lesson is therefore not that every subset has an intuitively obvious size, but that a carefully defined collection of measurable sets is rich enough for an enormous range of analysis.

For the original light-switch function, the corresponding Lebesgue integral of the indicator of the rationals is zero. That is not because the function is almost always zero in some everyday sense of sampling finite decimals. It is because the set on which the value is one has measure zero. The phrase almost everywhere means everywhere except on a set of measure zero. It is a technical phrase, and it depends on having specified a measure. Here our measure is ordinary length extended to an appropriate collection of subsets of the line.

We should pause over what the new theory has and has not done. It has not made every imaginable subset measurable. It has not claimed that dense sets are necessarily negligible. The rationals are both dense and negligible in length, while the entire interval is dense in itself and has length one. Density answers a question about where points can be found. Measure answers a question about the size assigned to a set. Those are different structures, and mathematics becomes clearer when we keep them separate.

For a more ordinary function, take one that equals two on the first quarter of the interval and five on the remaining three quarters. A Riemann sum gives the area of two rectangles: two times one quarter, plus five times three quarters. The result is four and one quarter. The new integral can be described in the opposite order. Which points have height two? A set of length one quarter. Which have height five? A set of length three quarters. Multiply each height by the measure of its level set and add. The number is the same. On simple functions, both pictures coincide, so the new theory is a genuine extension rather than an unrelated notion of area.

Lebesgue's insight was to use these simple, step-like functions as building blocks. A nonnegative measurable function can be approximated from below by nonnegative simple functions. Their integrals approach a limiting value, which defines the integral of the original function. For signed functions, we separate positive and negative parts and require that the appropriate quantities be finite before subtracting them. These conditions are not bookkeeping. Without them, the expression infinity minus infinity is not a meaningful answer.

One of the largest dividends came from a question any analyst has to ask: may we integrate a limit by integrating the approximating functions? In basic calculus, it is easy to be lulled into the idea that limits and integrals commute. Sometimes they do. Sometimes the answer is dramatically wrong. Consider a sequence of narrow spikes. For the first spike, let the function have height one on the first unit interval. For the next, height two on the first half. Next, height three on the first third. In general, the height is the number n and the width is one over n. Each spike has area one. Yet at every fixed positive point, the spikes eventually miss that point, so the function values eventually become zero. Set the value at zero to zero as well. The pointwise limit is zero everywhere, whose integral is zero. But the integrals of the spikes remain one.

A new integral cannot change this fact; the counterexample works for Lebesgue integration too. What the new theory provides is a disciplined account of when the interchange is justified. For increasing sequences of nonnegative measurable functions, the monotone convergence theorem says the integral of the pointwise limit equals the limit of their integrals, allowing an infinite value. For sequences dominated in absolute value by one fixed integrable function, the dominated convergence theorem says the same in the more general signed case. The spike sequence evades that second theorem: its heights grow without bound near zero, and there is no integrable function that dominates every spike at once.

A less extreme example shows what the convergence theorem gives us. Suppose you measure a landscape at finer and finer resolutions. A series of approximating functions may differ at a dense scattering of isolated points, and those discrepancies may prevent old-style partitions from giving stable answers. But if the functions are measurable, converge almost everywhere, and all lie under a common integrable envelope, their integrals converge. It is a remarkably useful conclusion. In probability, a random variable is a measurable function, and its expected value is an integral. In Fourier analysis, where a signal is decomposed into waves, square-integrable functions provide a natural setting. In partial differential equations, the convergence of approximate solutions often depends on understanding how integrals behave under limits.

Lebesgue's theory also forces us to distinguish two notions that can coincide for nice functions. A Riemann-integrable bounded function on a closed interval is Lebesgue integrable with the same value. The reverse is false: our indicator of rational numbers is Lebesgue integrable but not Riemann integrable. An even more important difference emerges when approximations converge in one sense but not another. Pointwise convergence, convergence in integral, and convergence in mean square each answer their own question. Lebesgue's framework makes it possible to formulate and compare them precisely rather than treating every limit as the same kind of approach.

Think of how much conceptual distance the new measure travels. To measure the rationals in an interval, we covered individual points by tiny neighborhoods and kept track of the total length. To integrate a simple function, we measured the sets on which each height occurred. To handle a general nonnegative function, we approached it by simple functions. To justify passing to a limit, we imposed the right convergence condition. Each construction solves a specific problem the earlier one revealed. None depends on a mysterious new arithmetic operation. The revolution lies in reorganizing familiar estimates around sets and their size.

He died in Paris in nineteen forty-one, during the Second World War. By then the new integral was not a specialized alternative known only to a circle in Paris. It had become a basic instrument of modern analysis. One should not mistake the historical sequence for a heroic story in which a single author supplied all the modern axioms and theorems at once. Later mathematicians refined the theory and proved extensions. Lebesgue's contribution is central because he made the new way of integrating concrete, general, and mathematically productive.

Return to the light switch that is on at every rational point. A Riemann partition cannot resolve its area, because the function oscillates between zero and one inside every interval. Lebesgue's answer begins elsewhere: ask for the measure of the set where it is on. That set is countable, so its measure is zero, and the integral is zero. This does not say the switch is never on, and it does not say the rationals are absent from any small interval. It says that the appropriate notion of total amount distinguishes between having points everywhere and occupying positive length. That distinction is the heart of Lebesgue's insight. Once we have it, we can make serious statements about approximations, averages, and limits that were previously out of reach.

## Mathematical and historical companion (display only)

### Riemann versus Lebesgue, on a concrete test

For the indicator of the rationals in the unit interval,
\[
f(x)=\mathbf 1_{\mathbb Q\cap[0,1]}(x),
\]
every nondegenerate subinterval contains rationals and irrationals, hence every upper Darboux sum is 1 and every lower Darboux sum is 0. The function is not Riemann integrable. The rationals are countable and therefore have Lebesgue measure zero: \(\int_0^1 f\,dm=0\).

A countable covering argument enumerates \(\mathbb Q\cap[0,1]=\{q_1,q_2,\ldots\}\); for any \(\varepsilon>0\), cover \(q_k\) by an interval of length at most \(\varepsilon 2^{-k}\). The total covering length is at most \(\varepsilon\).

### Measurable simple functions and integration

For disjoint measurable sets \(E_j\) and a nonnegative simple function \(\phi=\sum_{j=1}^m a_j\mathbf 1_{E_j}\), define \(\int\phi\,d\mu=\sum_{j=1}^m a_j\mu(E_j)\). For measurable \(f\ge0\), let \(\int f\,d\mu=\sup\{\int\phi\,d\mu:0\le\phi\le f,\,\phi\text{ simple}\}\).

### Convergence has hypotheses

If \(0\le f_n\uparrow f\), then \(\int f_n\uparrow\int f\), possibly infinity. If \(f_n\to f\) almost everywhere and \(|f_n|\le g\) for some integrable \(g\), then \(\int f_n\to\int f\). These are the modern monotone and dominated convergence theorems; attribution to their fully modern formulations should not be flattened into the 1901 announcement.

The spike sequence \(f_n(x)=n\mathbf 1_{(0,1/n)}(x)\) on \([0,1]\) converges pointwise to 0 while \(\int f_n=1\). It is a counterexample to unrestricted interchange, not to dominated convergence.
