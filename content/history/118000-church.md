# 118000 — Alonzo Church: What can a procedure decide?

**Track:** History — People and ideas. **Focus:** Princeton's interwar logic community, the development of lambda calculus and recursive functions, the decision problem and Church–Turing thesis, students and collaborators, the *Journal of Symbolic Logic*, later type theory and UCLA teaching. **Background:** algorithms and formal rules are introduced in ordinary language before technical distinctions.

Suppose you have a machine that can check arithmetic proofs. Perhaps it cannot invent a clever proof, but it can follow a fixed procedure: read a formula, apply rules, and eventually answer yes or no. How far could a method like this go? Is there one general procedure that can settle any precise question stated in mathematical logic? In the nineteen-thirties, Alonzo Church helped establish that a seemingly modest version of that dream was impossible. He also developed a mathematical language for computation that looks startlingly like the basis of modern functional programming. His career shows how a question about the limits of reasoning became a foundational question about machines.

Church was born in Washington, D.C., in 1903. His father, Samuel Robbins Church, was a judge, and the family valued formal education. The decades of his childhood saw increasing use of electricity, telephones, and office machinery, but one should not project the later computer era back onto the young Church. When he studied mathematics, a computer was often a human calculator or a mechanical device with a specialized task. The abstract question of what any mechanical procedure could do remained to be defined rigorously.

He entered Princeton and completed his undergraduate degree in 1924. He stayed for graduate work, receiving a doctorate in 1927 under Oswald Veblen. His dissertation addressed alternatives to Zermelo's assumption in set theory. That topic placed him amid the foundational disputes surrounding infinity, axioms, and the consistency of mathematics. Set theory made precise claims about collections of mathematical objects, but its history also contained paradoxes and arguments about which principles should be accepted. Church's generation inherited both an expanding formal apparatus and skepticism about what a formal system could guarantee.

In 1926 he married Mary Julia Kuczinski, and they later had three children. This family history belongs in the portrait because Church spent much of his life in long institutional appointments rather than wandering without ties. Like many scholars of his era, professional movements involved households and teaching obligations as well as research opportunities. After the doctorate he held a National Research Fellowship, spending time at Harvard and then in Göttingen and Amsterdam. These visits mattered intellectually: Göttingen had become a central site of foundational research under David Hilbert and others, while Dutch mathematics had its own influential traditions in logic and intuitionism.

Hilbert's program called for making mathematical reasoning fully explicit and studying the proofs themselves. One ambitious question, the *Entscheidungsproblem*, asked whether there could be an effective general procedure to decide the validity of statements in first-order logic. Think of an instruction book that will always finish and correctly classify any properly formed input formula. If such a book existed, validity would become an algorithmic task. If not, the impossibility would have to be proved without relying on a vague claim that some questions are merely difficult.

The challenge was that the phrase effective procedure was not yet a formal mathematical definition. A familiar algorithm such as long division is a sequence of definite instructions that does not depend on creative insight at each step. But how can one make a theorem about every possible algorithm, including algorithms nobody has yet imagined? A proof that one particular method fails is not enough. A negative theorem must characterize an entire class of methods and show that no member of that class can succeed.

Church returned to Princeton in 1929 as a faculty member. His teaching and research helped form a distinctive community of mathematical logicians. Among his students were Stephen Kleene and J. Barkley Rosser, whose research became fundamental to the theory of computation. Kurt Gödel's work on incompleteness was changing discussions of formal systems, and John von Neumann and other mathematicians passed through Princeton's mathematical environment. This was a network with many independent approaches, sometimes agreeing on the mathematical result while disagreeing about the most illuminating conceptual formulation.

The mathematical culture surrounding Church was deeply attentive to the distinction between a symbol and what it means. The letters in a formula do not carry the meaning of a mathematical statement by themselves. A formal language specifies which strings are well-formed; a proof system specifies legal steps; a semantics specifies how the formulas are interpreted in mathematical structures. Confusing these levels creates bad arguments about what logic can and cannot do. Church's work made them subjects of precise analysis. The same separation later became central in programming-language theory, where source code is syntax and its behavior depends on a defined semantics.

Church developed what is now called the lambda calculus. At first it looks almost too simple to deserve the word calculus. Instead of adding numbers or differentiating functions, one writes down expressions that represent operations, then evaluates them by substitution. A function that adds one to its input can be described abstractly as: take an argument x and return x plus one. Applying that function to three yields four. One can also build functions that accept other functions as arguments. The system is about rules for applying and transforming expressions, not about a particular programming language or a physical computer.

Consider the operation of applying a function twice. If f is a function and x is an input, the result is f applied to f applied to x. When f adds one, applying it twice to three produces five. A lambda expression can represent that pattern without naming the particular function in advance. It can then be combined with other expressions to represent increasingly complicated calculations. In the untyped lambda calculus, a finite collection of syntactic rules can encode natural numbers, logical choices, recursion, and computations that would take many steps to carry out.

When a substitution rule is applied, even a small notational oversight can ruin the argument. For example, if a bound variable in a function body accidentally captures a variable from the substituted expression, the mathematical meaning changes. Modern presentations of lambda calculus insist on renaming bound variables when necessary so that this cannot happen. That technical rule is easy to overlook in popular accounts because it seems like bookkeeping. But it illustrates why formal definitions matter. A computational system that depends on substitution cannot rest on the vague instruction to replace one expression with another wherever it looks appropriate.

The beauty is that the same language describes both the operation and the machinery for building new operations. But it is also a trap for a naive theory of logic. Church's early attempts to develop a broader formal foundation ran into inconsistency, exposed through work by Kleene and Rosser. This is an essential episode in the history, because successful theories are often the result of identifying which tempting principles cannot coexist. Church did not discover a flawless system in a single attempt. He and his students learned where a powerful system of substitution needed restrictions or different logical interpretation.

In the mid-nineteen-thirties Church, Kleene, and others examined the relationship between lambda-definability and recursive functions. A recursive function is specified by controlled ways of combining basic functions and defining new ones from earlier values. The goal was not to mimic a desk calculator's gears. It was to capture the exact structure of rule-governed computation. Church's work suggested that the functions effectively calculable by a finite algorithm were precisely those definable within one of these equivalent formal frameworks. This proposal became known as Church's thesis.

A thesis is not the same kind of object as a theorem. The equivalence of two mathematically defined classes of functions can be proved. But the phrase any effectively calculable function begins as an informal human concept. To claim that a particular formal class captures every possible effective method is an identification of an informal notion with a precise one. It is supported by many lines of reasoning and by repeated equivalences among independent formal models. It cannot be established by simply applying the ordinary rules of proof to a completely undefined phrase.

In 1936 Church published the result that no general effective decision procedure could solve the first-order *Entscheidungsproblem*. The negative conclusion is narrower and more precise than saying computers cannot do mathematics. It means that no algorithm can correctly decide, for every formula of the relevant first-order language, whether that formula is logically valid, always returning an answer after a finite number of steps. Many individual validity questions are decidable; many restricted classes have algorithms. The theorem denies a universal terminating method for the unrestricted class.

Around the same time Alan Turing, working independently in Britain, analyzed what a human calculator could accomplish through operations performed step by step with bounded local instructions and written symbols. He formulated the abstract machines that now bear his name. Turing obtained a closely related negative result for the decision problem and supplied an interpretation of mechanical calculation that many readers found compelling. The relation between Church's lambda calculus and Turing machines was subsequently clarified. Their independent descriptions of computation turned out to capture the same effectively computable numerical functions.

This historical conjunction was not a race with one winner. Church had already published a technical route through recursive and lambda-definable functions; Turing provided an independent machine-centered analysis. Emil Post developed another related approach. Modern texts use the name Church–Turing thesis because the agreement between these formalizations is significant evidence for the general concept. When Turing visited Princeton in 1936 and stayed for doctoral research with Church, the two men were able to discuss a problem they had attacked from different directions. Turing completed his Princeton doctorate under Church in 1938.

To understand the negative result with an intuitive example, imagine a computer program that promises to answer a question about any other program: will it eventually stop? If such a universal stop-predictor existed, one could write a program that consults the predictor about itself and then deliberately behaves in the opposite way. When predicted to stop, it loops; when predicted to loop, it stops. The contradiction reveals that the predictor cannot exist for arbitrary programs. The familiar halting-problem argument is a later pedagogical formulation associated especially with Turing's framework. It is not a verbatim reconstruction of Church's 1936 paper, but it illustrates the type of self-reference at work in limits on decision methods.

There is also a useful asymmetry between proving validity and deciding validity. In classical first-order logic, a sound and complete proof system lets a systematic search eventually find a proof for a logically valid sentence. That does not mean the same search must terminate and report no for a sentence that is not valid. A universal decision procedure needs to stop in both cases. The gap between a method that eventually confirms a positive case and a method that always returns either answer is essential. It explains how logic can be complete as a proof system while its general validity problem is undecidable. Church's result did not make proof search meaningless; it clarified its limits.

The negative theorems left an enormous positive project. They showed researchers what a computation is well enough to classify whole families of algorithms, compare alternative languages, and reason rigorously about systems that may not terminate. The word computer soon came to mean electronic equipment, but these theoretical results were established before programmable electronic computers were commonplace. That order is historically important. A theoretical account of computation did not merely follow from watching modern machines run; it helped establish what such machines should be understood as capable of doing.

Princeton's proximity to the Institute for Advanced Study gave the logic community contact with visiting scholars from several countries. The intellectual atmosphere included disagreements over formalism, intuitionism, and the correct notion of function. Gödel's incompleteness theorems did not supply the exact definition of computation that Church needed, but they made clear that ordinary confidence in a formal system's completeness and self-certification was inadequate. Church's research group worked on one part of the foundational problem: which operations can be specified by precise finite rules? That made collaboration with students and comparison with Turing's independent construction especially valuable.

Church also helped build the infrastructure of logic as an academic subject. He was involved in the Association for Symbolic Logic and became the founding editor of the *Journal of Symbolic Logic* in 1936, retaining editorial responsibilities for decades. A journal can look like paperwork from outside a field. In a developing discipline, though, it creates a shared venue for proofs, corrections, reviews, and disputes over terminology. Church's meticulous work as editor helped establish a stable record for research scattered among mathematics, philosophy, and emerging theoretical computer science.

His bibliographical labor also mattered. Formal logic had been developing through many languages and traditions, and a field is difficult to study if researchers cannot locate earlier work or agree on which version of a theorem has already been established. Through reviews, indexing, and careful references, Church participated in making the subject easier to teach and harder to misattribute. That institutional work should be counted alongside his technical publications. Without such work a successful theorem may remain known only to a narrow circle, and new students may spend years rebuilding results already proved elsewhere.

Types can be heard as a discipline on the kinds of questions a program may ask. The operation of adding two integers has one kind of input; an operation that takes another operation as input has a different kind. A type system gives a language for describing these requirements. Church's simple theory of types used such distinctions to study logical expressions without reproducing every paradox possible in an unrestricted system. Modern functional programming inherits the idea that functions can be passed around and combined, but particular languages add features Church was not trying to design: input and output, data structures, memory management, and interaction with external devices.

In 1940 Church published a system of simple type theory based on lambda calculus. Types impose constraints on which expressions may be used as functions of which arguments. In everyday terms, an instruction that expects a number should not casually be handed a sentence unless a method exists to interpret the sentence numerically. Type systems can prevent certain nonsensical constructions and can make formal proofs and programs more manageable. The exact system Church developed is historically distinct from all modern programming-language type theories, but the conceptual connection to typed functional languages and proof assistants is substantial.

His textbook *Introduction to Mathematical Logic* appeared in versions that influenced generations of students. It showed how to reason about logic as a mathematical subject with formal languages, syntactic derivations, and semantic interpretations. A lecture on Church's work can easily disappear into symbols. Yet as a teacher he lived with the very practical problem of making formal definitions usable. A logical formula written correctly is only the beginning; someone must understand which conclusions follow from it and why.

Church spent most of his career at Princeton, becoming a full professor after the Second World War and later holding a chair that joined mathematics and philosophy. His intellectual network included Kleene, Rosser, Turing, and many later scholars whose own students carried the field into computer science. Not every development in computability theory emerged directly from his classroom. But his role as adviser and editor gave him sustained influence over what counted as a rigorous argument and how a younger discipline presented itself.

In 1967 he left Princeton for the University of California, Los Angeles, where he taught mathematics and philosophy for more than two decades. This move is often overlooked in a story concentrated entirely on 1936. The pioneer of algorithmic undecidability remained an active scholar as programming languages, computers, and computer science departments became major institutions. Church did not spend his entire later life revisiting one discovery. He taught, wrote, and participated in the growing contact between logical theory and practical computation.

It is worth noticing that Church's historical influence has at least two routes. In pure logic, his work helped delimit effective procedures and develop type theory. In computer science, lambda calculus became a model for understanding evaluation and programming languages. The second route is not an assertion that every modern compiler directly implements his original formalism. Rather, it shows how an abstract theory can be reused in ways its author did not originally need. The same caution applies to cryptography and artificial intelligence: theoretical computability is a background concept, but practical security and performance require additional ideas not supplied by Church alone.

He retired from UCLA in 1990 and died in 1995. Through those decades the meaning of his early questions became more visible in the working world. Compilers use formal rules to translate programs. Programming-language research studies which expressions can be reduced and when two programs are equivalent. Automated theorem provers confront the difference between verifying a supplied proof and discovering one. Researchers building proof assistants study type systems whose conceptual ancestry reaches through Church and other logicians. The direct historical routes are complex; not every piece of software descends from his papers alone.

A careful assessment should also distinguish Church's thesis from claims that all physical phenomena are digital computers or that minds are nothing but programs. His thesis concerns effective calculability, a mathematical and methodological idea. Stronger philosophical or physical claims require additional assumptions. In the same way, an undecidable decision problem does not imply that human thought is free of limits or that a clever mathematician can always solve what a machine cannot. The precise theorem is already remarkable without being stretched into a theory of everything.

Return to the imagined proof-checking machine. We can build systems that follow formal rules with astonishing speed, and we can formulate questions they are guaranteed to settle. Church helped show that there are also perfectly precise families of questions no universal terminating procedure can settle. His achievement required two movements of thought: describing computation accurately enough for a theorem, and proving a limit on that description. His life as a scholar—student of foundations, teacher of Turing and Kleene, editor, textbook author, and institution-builder—gave those ideas a durable intellectual home. Modern computation is not possible because he removed every uncertainty. It is more intelligible because he showed which uncertainties cannot be removed by a single algorithm.

## Historical companion — not spoken

### Evidentiary chronology

- **1903:** Born in Washington, D.C.; **1924/1927:** Princeton A.B. and Ph.D., supervised by Oswald Veblen.
- **1926:** Married Mary Julia Kuczinski; later raised three children.
- **1927–1929:** National Research Fellowship, with periods at Harvard, Göttingen, and Amsterdam.
- **1929 onward:** Princeton faculty; worked with Kleene, Rosser and other foundational researchers.
- **1936:** Publications on unsolvability of the decision problem; founding editorial role at *Journal of Symbolic Logic*.
- **1936–1938:** Alan Turing studied at Princeton and completed a doctorate under Church.
- **1940:** Simple type theory; later editions of *Introduction to Mathematical Logic*.
- **1967–1990:** UCLA positions in mathematics and philosophy; **1995:** died.

### Mathematical companion

- **Untyped lambda calculus:** Function abstraction, application and β-reduction are formal syntactic operations; substitution must avoid variable capture.
- **Numerals and iteration:** \(2\) can be encoded as \(\lambda f.\lambda x.f(fx)\); this does not mean Church numerals are the only representation of natural numbers.
- **Decidability:** First-order validity is not decidable by a total algorithm in the general case. This does **not** say every individual problem is undecidable or that first-order logic lacks a complete proof system (Gödel's completeness theorem shows otherwise).
- **Church–Turing thesis:** Informal effective calculability is identified with a formal model; equivalence of particular formal models is a theorem, while the identification with informal effectiveness is a thesis.
- **Attribution:** Church, Turing, Post, Gödel, Kleene and Rosser made distinct contributions to computability and logic. The halting example in the narration is illustrative, not a claim to reproduce Church's original proof.

**Historical cautions:** Do not conflate formal validity with mathematical truth in arbitrary models, or Gödel incompleteness with the negative answer to *Entscheidungsproblem*. Church's early general logic system was inconsistent, while subsequent restricted systems were viable; avoid saying lambda calculus itself is inconsistent. Do not present the Church–Turing thesis as a theorem of physics.
