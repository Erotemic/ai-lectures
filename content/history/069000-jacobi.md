# 069000 - Carl Gustav Jacob Jacobi: The energetic mathematics of Prussia

**Track:** History - People and ideas. **Focus:** Jewish family and conversion, Prussian education and professorial life, Königsberg's research community, correspondence with Legendre and competition with Abel, pupils, illness, financial losses and the revolutions of 1848. **Background:** historical curiosity; elementary calculus helps but is not required.

In the late eighteen twenties, a young mathematician in the Prussian city of Königsberg sent a letter to an elderly French scholar who had spent decades studying a particularly stubborn kind of integral. The older man was Adrien-Marie Legendre. The younger was Carl Gustav Jacob Jacobi. Jacobi claimed that he could transform the problem so dramatically that many formerly disconnected formulas became pieces of a new theory. Legendre understood the importance of the announcement, and, rather than defend his territory, encouraged it. At nearly the same moment, a young Norwegian named Niels Henrik Abel was making related discoveries independently. The episode is often remembered as a race in analysis. It is also a story about correspondence, journals, universities, state patronage and the ability of a mathematician to recognize talent in another person.

Jacobi was born in Potsdam in December eighteen oh four, when that town belonged to the kingdom of Prussia. His father Simon was a prosperous banker and a figure in the Jewish community. His mother was Rachel, from the Lehmann family. Among Carl's siblings was Moritz, who later became a physicist and engineer. Their household was not one of material deprivation, but the circumstances of Jewish life in Prussia placed boundaries around what educational and public careers could be pursued. Social position and intellectual brilliance did not erase those restrictions.

An uncle helped educate the child before he entered the Potsdam Gymnasium. There he studied the languages and literature of the classical curriculum and advanced so quickly that he reached the highest school class while still very young. The University of Berlin required entrants to be older than he was, so he remained at school while continuing to study beyond the prescribed lessons. He read Euler's great introduction to analysis, among other works. One of his interests was whether a general equation of the fifth degree could be solved by formulas involving radicals, the very question on which Abel and later Galois would illuminate the limits of algebraic methods. We should not imagine a child already holding all the answers. What his early studies show is the breadth of his preparation and the intensity with which he went beyond the classroom.

He entered the University of Berlin in eighteen twenty-one and did not immediately abandon literature and philosophy for mathematics. For a time he pursued all three. The nineteenth-century university did not yet divide intellectual activity into the familiar modern departments with their stable research pipelines. A talented student might learn classical philology in formal lectures and higher mathematics by reading books independently. Jacobi ultimately chose mathematics, prepared to teach Greek and Latin as well, and completed the doctoral work needed for an academic career.

He also met a professional reality that his mathematical gifts could not simply solve. Around eighteen twenty-five he converted from Judaism to Christianity, a change connected in the biographical record with eligibility for a Prussian university position. Conversion should not be retold as if we knew every private conviction behind it. It is evidence that universities operated within legal and social systems which could make a family's religion consequential for a young scholar's prospects. Jacobi taught briefly in Berlin, but better opportunities lay farther east.

He arrived at Königsberg in eighteen twenty-six. Now Kaliningrad in Russia, the city was then a Prussian center of commerce and education on the Baltic. There he joined a mathematical and physical research community that included the astronomer Friedrich Wilhelm Bessel and the physicist Franz Ernst Neumann. Bessel was a world-class observer, famous for careful measurement and calculation. Neumann was building a research tradition in mathematical physics. Jacobi's style of attacking formulas complemented their work. This was an environment where the boundary between mathematical proof, astronomical observation and physical theory was porous.

Jacobi wrote to Gauss about number theory, particularly cubic residues. Gauss's study of arithmetic had become a model for the next generation. To say that Jacobi corresponded with Gauss is not to claim that the two were intimate colleagues. It means that mathematical letters could bridge institutions and announce a researcher's arrival on the European scene. Gauss was sufficiently interested to inquire about the young man through Bessel. That kind of intellectual endorsement could matter when appointments and salaries depended on judgments by senior scholars.

But it was the theory of elliptic functions that made Jacobi famous. The name can mislead a listener. These are not merely functions associated with drawing an ellipse, and they are not the same as contemporary elliptic curves in every detail. Eighteenth-century mathematicians had encountered integrals arising from measuring curves and solving mechanical problems which would not reduce to familiar logarithms or trigonometric functions. Legendre devoted decades to classifying such integrals. Abel and Jacobi turned the viewpoint around: study functions inverse to those integrals, and investigate the repeated patterns, or periods, that those functions possess in the complex plane. The new viewpoint transformed what looked like a collection of difficult integrations into a coherent subject.

In August eighteen twenty-seven Jacobi wrote to Legendre. The letter was not simply a request for publicity. It announced particular new transformations of elliptic integrals and opened a correspondence that continued over years. Legendre was more than fifty years his senior. He saw that a field to which he had given much of his career was moving beyond his own methods. His generous response helped Jacobi's reputation, and Legendre's standing with the Paris Academy and European scholars was formidable. By the end of that year Jacobi had become an associate professor.

Abel, meanwhile, had been producing his own results, sometimes before Jacobi's publications and sometimes along a different line of attack. A popular version of this history makes the two into hostile competitors. The surviving mathematical exchange tells a more interesting story: overlapping discoveries, efforts to establish priority, mutual attention to each other's work, and an older mathematician who admired the speed of both. The independence of their breakthroughs is important, but priority is not one indivisible trophy. Different theorems, methods and publications carry different claims.

In eighteen twenty-nine Jacobi traveled to Göttingen and then to Paris. In Göttingen he met Gauss. In Paris he encountered Legendre, Fourier and Poisson. Travel was a way to compare results with people who had different techniques, patronage and publication venues. That year Jacobi published his *Fundamenta nova theoriae functionum ellipticarum*, a systematic account of the new theory. Its importance was not just a new formula. It organized the subject around theta functions and transformations, allowing subsequent mathematicians to ask broader questions about periodicity and complex analysis.

Only weeks after one of Legendre's letters celebrating the young researchers, Abel died in April eighteen twenty-nine. Jacobi continued, but the mathematical correspondence now belonged to a world altered by the loss of a major colleague. He also built a family life in Königsberg. In September eighteen thirty-one he married Marie Schwinck, from a merchant family, and they had a large household of children. A biography focused entirely on spectacular theorems would miss the substantial domestic and financial responsibilities attached to this career.

In eighteen thirty-two Jacobi became a full professor. His rise coincided with a change in how mathematical knowledge was taught. Instead of limiting higher instruction to a professor's polished sequence of lectures, he drew students into active research and discussion of problems not yet solved. Charles Borchardt, Eduard Heine, Ludwig Otto Hesse, Friedrich Richelot and other members of his circle later carried these methods into their own work. Königsberg became associated with a style of mathematical seminar in which students learned to do research by participating in it. Bessel, Neumann and Jacobi together helped make the city an important site for German mathematical training.

A research school is not only a set of theorems bearing the teacher's name. It is also a collection of conversations and skills distributed among people with careers of their own. Jacobi recognized Kummer's ability while Kummer was still teaching at a secondary school. In one correspondence he acknowledged that Kummer had advanced a differential-equation problem further than he himself had managed. This is a revealing historical detail: an ambitious, competitive mathematician could still recognize that another person's idea had overtaken his own calculation.

To appreciate how different this style of teaching was, imagine a mathematics student arriving at Königsberg with an excellent knowledge of published exercises. In an older model of university instruction the professor demonstrated results and the student mastered them. Under Jacobi's influence, research questions themselves entered the room. A student had to learn how one finds a problem, how to recognize a false start, and how a calculation becomes a general argument. Such instruction depended on the teacher's energy, but it also meant that students could eventually surpass him in directions he had opened. This school of research was one ingredient in the nineteenth-century transformation of German university mathematics, alongside changes brought by other institutions and teachers. It did not happen uniformly, and one should not claim Jacobi alone invented the modern seminar.

Moritz, Jacobi's elder brother, provided another connection to the physical sciences. He worked in engineering and electrical technologies and eventually made his career in the Russian empire. Their correspondence linked different forms of technical work: Carl's formulas and Moritz's practical investigations. Their lives also show how the borders of employment differed in the nineteenth century. A Prussian-born scholar might join a university in one kingdom; an engineer from the same household might work for an imperial institution elsewhere. Family, letters and professional networks crossed political frontiers long before mathematical organizations became international in a modern sense.

Jacobi worked in many areas. In elliptic functions he explored double periodicity. In number theory he helped investigate higher reciprocity questions. In mechanics he revisited the differential equations by which the movement of physical systems could be understood. His work on first-order partial differential equations developed ideas inherited from Hamilton and Lagrange. His writings on determinants studied what later generations called the Jacobian. The name is now on nearly every multivariable calculus syllabus, but it should not suggest sole invention: Cauchy had already studied functional determinants before Jacobi's major eighteen forty-one memoir.

There is a reason the Jacobian matters. Suppose a map takes one pair of coordinates to another. A tiny region in the first coordinate system usually changes size and orientation under that map. The determinant of its local derivative tells us how the area is scaled, at least where the map is sufficiently smooth and locally well behaved. If the determinant vanishes, the local map has lost a dimension of independent variation. This offers a bridge between algebra, geometry and calculation. Jacobi's historical achievement was to develop a body of theory around such determinants, not to originate every ingredient of the modern change-of-variables formula.

His mechanics is likewise more than a named equation. Lagrange had reformulated Newtonian motion using energy-like quantities and generalized coordinates; Hamilton went further in a new style of dynamics. Jacobi developed ways to relate the equations of motion to partial differential equations for a single function. In a simple mechanical system, information about a function called the action can be used to recover trajectories. In later mathematical physics this viewpoint became central. It would be misleading to say that Jacobi anticipated quantum mechanics in a modern sense. It is more accurate to say that later physicists found his transformation of classical mechanics extraordinarily useful.

There is a further complication in the way later generations use Jacobi's name. In number theory the Jacobi symbol extends Legendre's symbol, which was defined for an odd prime modulus, to any positive odd denominator. It is a convenient and important computational object. But unlike the original Legendre symbol, a Jacobi symbol equal to one does not automatically say the numerator is a square modulo the denominator when that denominator is composite. This little mathematical trap illustrates a larger historical point: a generalized notation can preserve the appearance of an old result while changing what can safely be inferred. Jacobi's achievements included such conceptual generalizations, not merely a vast output of lengthy calculations.

His attitude toward mechanics also changed over the years. In lectures at Berlin in the late eighteen forties he criticized aspects of Lagrange's ideal of deriving mechanics as if it were an axiomatic mathematical system insulated from empirical questions. Historical research on those lectures shows a more reflective Jacobi than the image of an indefatigable formula manipulator suggests. He had spent a lifetime drawing deep mathematical consequences from physical models, and he also asked where the authority of those models came from. This mattered at a moment when mathematics and physics were acquiring increasingly distinct identities.

Professional success did not guarantee physical or financial security. In the early eighteen forties Jacobi became ill, and diabetes was diagnosed. He was advised to recover in a warmer climate. At the same time, a business failure connected with a wider economic crisis destroyed much of his private fortune. The man who had once grown up in a prosperous banking household needed help to travel. Dirichlet approached Alexander von Humboldt, whose scientific influence and access to the Prussian court were valuable. With royal support Jacobi was able to go to Italy in eighteen forty-three with mathematical companions, including Dirichlet and Borchardt.

In Italy his health improved enough for renewed work. He also pursued interests in the history of mathematics, examining manuscripts relating to Diophantus in Rome. This is a useful corrective to the image of an analyst who only produced ever-faster computations. He read the work of ancient mathematicians and studied how ideas had traveled. Returning to the northern climate of Königsberg was judged undesirable, and arrangements were made for him to live in Berlin with a salary supplement. By eighteen forty-four he was there, still ill at times, but teaching and writing when he could.

Then Prussia entered the revolutionary crisis of eighteen forty-eight. Crop failures, unemployment, censorship and demands for constitutional government had shaken European monarchies. Fighting reached Berlin after the revolution in Paris. Jacobi spoke at a Constitutional Club meeting and managed to anger different factions. His attempt to secure a regular Berlin appointment ran into government opposition. After the revolutionary movement was defeated, the Prussian authorities withdrew the extra money that had helped support him in Berlin. He moved his family to Gotha and considered a professorship in Vienna. The state then made concessions to prevent him from leaving Prussia altogether, but the arrangement separated his work in Berlin from his household in Gotha.

We should not reduce this episode to a lone hero being punished for one brave speech, nor dismiss it as a minor inconvenience. It shows how dependent scholars could be on state institutions. A professorship came with political obligations and financial strings. Jacobi had to manage illness, children, teaching and dependence on royal favor at a time when even a renowned mathematician could be exposed by political conflict. The revolution mattered to the physical shape of his daily life.

In January eighteen fifty-one he contracted influenza, then smallpox before he had fully recovered. He died in Berlin in February, aged forty-six. The later history of mathematics would carry his name in elliptic functions, determinants, dynamical systems and number theory. But the life behind those names moved through a very specific succession of places: Potsdam's schools, the Berlin university, Königsberg's seminars, Parisian correspondence, medical travel in Italy, and the politically turbulent Berlin of the late eighteen forties.

His story also illuminates a change in mathematics itself. Mathematical discovery was becoming more international, more dependent on journals, and more closely tied to research training at universities. The discoveries of Abel and Jacobi did not make Legendre's earlier work irrelevant; they revealed an unexpected structure within it. The students of Königsberg did not merely memorize Jacobi's techniques; they helped build the research culture through which his ideas spread. And behind the technical vocabulary was a man whose career was shaped by religion, illness, family finances and Prussian politics. Those conditions do not prove a theorem, but they help explain how a theorem reached the world.

## Historical companion (not narrated)

### Chronology, people, and evidence
- **1804–1826:** Potsdam Jewish banking family; education in classics and mathematics; Berlin study, doctorate, conversion around 1825; departure for Königsberg.
- **1826–1832:** Königsberg collaboration with Bessel and Neumann, Legendre correspondence (from 1827), independent Abel–Jacobi elliptic-function work, the 1829 *Fundamenta nova*, marriage to Marie Schwinck in 1831 and full professorship in 1832.
- **1830s–1842:** Research seminars and students; number theory, differential equations and determinants; 1841 functional-determinant memoir; travel to Britain and France in 1842.
- **1843–1851:** Diabetes diagnosis, financial loss, Italy journey supported through Dirichlet and Humboldt, Berlin move, revolutionary politics, loss of supplement and Gotha household; smallpox death in Berlin.

**Attribution cautions:** The Abel–Jacobi story is about contemporaneous, partly overlapping independent work; do not assign a single overall priority. Cauchy studied functional determinants before Jacobi's 1841 memoir. Mathematical language such as Hamilton–Jacobi theory is a later synthesis. Conversion is documented in a setting of religious barriers, but private motives cannot be reconstructed confidently. Jacobi's political position in 1848 should not be replaced with an invented party identity.

### Mathematical display companion
- For a differentiable map \(F:\mathbb R^n\to\mathbb R^n\), the *Jacobian determinant* is \(\det DF(x)\). The change-of-variables factor in integration is \(|\det DF(x)|\) under appropriate injectivity and regularity hypotheses.
- A modern one-dimensional Hamilton–Jacobi equation: \(\partial_t S + H(q,\partial_q S,t)=0\). Do not attribute this exact notation to Jacobi.
- A nonconstant meromorphic elliptic function \(f\) may satisfy \(f(z+\omega_1)=f(z+\omega_2)=f(z)\) for two independent complex periods. Nonconstant *entire* doubly periodic functions do not exist; poles matter.
