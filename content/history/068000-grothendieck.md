# 068000 - Alexander Grothendieck: Exile, the new geometry, and a change of life

**Track:** History - People and ideas. **Focus:** antifascist family, refugee childhood, postwar France, Schwartz and Dieudonne, IHES, collaborative algebraic geometry, political engagement, family life, later writing and withdrawal. **Background:** elementary geometry and algebra.

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

In the nineteen sixties, mathematicians traveled to a research institute outside Paris to attend seminars in which problems hundreds of years old were recast in a new language. The lectures were demanding, often very long, and concerned objects that could seem far removed from the shapes ordinarily called geometry. Alexander Grothendieck was at the center of this work. Colleagues and students helped write, test, organize, and distribute the theory. For a period, the institute became a workshop for rebuilding algebraic geometry on a scale that few earlier research programs had attempted. Then, around nineteen seventy, Grothendieck turned away from that institutional world and made questions about war, pollution, and the responsibilities of scientists central to his life. Neither half of this story can be separated from what came before.

He was born in Berlin in March nineteen twenty-eight. His mother, Hanka Grothendieck, was a writer and political activist. His father, known as Sacha Schapiro, was a Russian-born anarchist of Jewish background who had endured imprisonment and political upheaval. The parents belonged to a radical European milieu opposed to authoritarian rule and committed to forms of revolutionary politics. The child did not begin life under the protective order of a stable national academic family. His parents' world was one of migration, precarious work, political surveillance, and the danger created by the growth of fascism.

When the Nazis came to power in Germany, his parents left for France. As a young child Alexander remained in Hamburg with the Heydorn family, who cared for him for several years. Political conditions eventually made remaining there too dangerous, and he traveled to France in nineteen thirty-nine. These events are sometimes reduced to a formula about the lonely childhood of a genius. That formula is inadequate. There were actual adults who sheltered and educated him, a European refugee crisis, and governments whose categories of nationality and political belonging could decide a child's safety. His later interest in mathematical universality did not remove him from those material facts.

The Second World War made the family's situation more precarious. French authorities interned refugees and people classified as undesirable foreigners. Alexander and his mother spent time in the Rieucros camp; his father was held at Le Vernet and deported to Auschwitz, where he was killed in nineteen forty-two. In the years that followed, Alexander received shelter and schooling in the area of Le Chambon-sur-Lignon, where local communities and networks of resistance provided refuge for people threatened by the occupiers. The young person who emerged from the war had experienced the destruction of political freedom as something intimate, including the loss of his father. It would be irresponsible to claim we can read every later choice backward from that loss, but it is equally irresponsible to tell his life as if the war were a small preliminary paragraph.

After liberation he studied in Montpellier. He had not enjoyed a smooth sequence of elite preparatory schools. The curriculum there gave him access to university mathematics, but he also pursued questions on his own. Accounts of his youth describe him trying to reconstruct central ideas of integration without realizing how far others had already developed them. This is not evidence that mathematical history began again with him. It suggests a habit: when given a problem, he preferred to discover the concepts that made its answer possible rather than merely memorize an existing method. That habit would become a strength, and sometimes a source of conflict, in his later career.

He traveled to Paris, where a new generation of French mathematicians was shaping the postwar field. The Bourbaki group, associated with an ambitious and systematic presentation of mathematics, had transformed standards of abstraction. Henri Cartan, Jean Dieudonne, Laurent Schwartz, and others were part of this intellectual world. Grothendieck did not instantly find his place in it. He had much to learn, and France's research hierarchy could be difficult for an outsider with uncertain citizenship and an irregular educational background to navigate. But he impressed senior mathematicians with the scale and energy of his reasoning.

He went to Nancy to work with Laurent Schwartz and Jean Dieudonne. The famous story says that he was given a list of difficult problems in functional analysis and returned having solved all of them. The anecdote appears in mathematical recollections and captures how his teachers remembered his ability; its precise numbers and timing are less important than its historical context. Schwartz was helping build the theory of generalized functions, or distributions, and the space of possible function spaces had become vast. Grothendieck attacked problems about topological vector spaces and tensor products, producing work that helped shape functional analysis. His doctoral research made him a recognized mathematician before he became primarily associated with algebraic geometry.

Nationality also mattered to his professional prospects. For years he lived without settled citizenship, and obtaining a permanent position within French academic institutions could be harder for a stateless researcher than for a colleague with a straightforward administrative status. His research appointments and visits to other countries were therefore not simply the glamorous travels of a rising mathematical star. They were also strategies for finding institutions that could employ him and allow him to work. Research talent does not float free of passports, salary systems, and immigration rules. In a life already marked by expulsion and war, those constraints had a particular weight.


The change of field is important because it prevents a mythology in which a person arrives fully formed with one grand plan. Grothendieck traveled, worked in research positions in Brazil and the United States, and interacted with different mathematical communities. He did not begin by announcing that all geometry must be reconstructed. He developed a taste for finding organizing principles: instead of proving a succession of unrelated results about particular objects, find a setting in which many results become instances of one general statement. This strategy could seem extravagant, and it demanded years of preparation before its full value became visible.

Algebraic geometry, in its classical form, concerns the shapes defined by polynomial equations. A circle is defined by an equation; so is a more complicated curve or surface. In the nineteenth century, such shapes had been studied using complex analysis, geometry, and algebraic transformations. In the twentieth century, the subject encountered new demands from number theory. How should one compare a polynomial equation over the real numbers, over the complex numbers, over rational numbers, or over a finite field with only a limited number of elements? A method that depends entirely on visual intuition about smooth complex surfaces may not survive when the same algebraic formula is considered over a finite field.

Grothendieck's answer was not simply to invent longer calculations. He helped build a language in which a geometric object could be studied through the algebra of functions defined on it, and in which the choice of underlying number system became part of the framework. The central word is *scheme*. A scheme generalizes the familiar algebraic variety and can keep track of arithmetic information that older geometric pictures suppressed. In a simplified example, an equation may define one kind of shape over the complex numbers, while its reduction modulo a prime reveals special behavior. The scheme perspective lets one study these cases as related pieces of a larger mathematical family. The technical definition is exact and abstract; the historical point is that it unified questions previously handled by separate methods.

In nineteen fifty-eight and nineteen fifty-nine, the newly founded Institut des Hautes Etudes Scientifiques, or IHES, became the institutional home of this program. Leon Motchane had established the institute, and Dieudonne was central to bringing Grothendieck into it. The arrangement gave unusually strong conditions for research: freedom from much ordinary teaching and the presence of capable colleagues and visitors. Grothendieck was one of the major forces behind *Elements of Algebraic Geometry*, usually abbreviated EGA, written with Dieudonne. The *Seminars in Algebraic Geometry*, known as SGA, developed through a large, changing community of participants. These works were not private notebooks produced by a solitary mind. They were edited mathematical architectures sustained by collective labor.

Grothendieck's generalization of the Riemann-Roch theorem also inspired developments outside algebraic geometry. Michael Atiyah later recalled encountering the new ideas at a mathematical meeting in Bonn and using them to develop a topological version of K-theory with Friedrich Hirzebruch. This is one example of how the IHES program changed neighboring fields. A theorem about algebraic geometry could suggest tools in topology; a topological result could motivate a new algebraic question. Grothendieck's work was enormously influential partly because he had built concepts that others could adapt in directions he had not specified in advance. The resulting research belonged to those others as well, with their own proofs and discoveries.


A long seminar may look from outside like one professor talking while others listen. In this case, the audience was a research community that supplied proofs, questions, corrections, examples, and eventual manuscripts. The people around Grothendieck included Michael Artin, Jean-Pierre Serre, Pierre Deligne, and many others. Serre in particular had been a close interlocutor, and their correspondence records a rich mathematical exchange. Deligne later made decisive contributions to the Weil conjectures. Scholarly credit matters here because the very mathematical style associated with Grothendieck depended on an environment in which an idea could be expanded, challenged, and made precise by people working together over long periods.

One achievement was a new form of the Riemann-Roch theorem in algebraic geometry. In its classical setting, Riemann-Roch relates geometric information about a curve to the dimensions of spaces of functions or sections. Grothendieck generalized this kind of relation to families of spaces and to a language adapted to modern algebraic geometry. Other advances concerned cohomology, which provides a way of recording global information that cannot always be detected by inspecting small neighborhoods separately. The technical theory of etale cohomology gave new tools for studying algebraic varieties over fields where ordinary topological techniques do not apply. These ideas became central to the effort to prove the Weil conjectures about counting solutions of polynomial equations over finite fields.

It would be a mistake to say that Grothendieck personally proved every major theorem made possible by his framework. Andre Weil posed the famous conjectures, and several mathematicians solved parts of them. Deligne proved the last and deepest remaining part in the nineteen seventies. Grothendieck contributed concepts, conjectures, proof methods, and a research program in which such a result could become thinkable. In mathematical history the architect of a framework and the solver of a particular celebrated theorem can be different people. Neither contribution is diminished by recognizing the other.

He was awarded the Fields Medal in nineteen sixty-six but did not attend the ceremony in Moscow, objecting to Soviet political repression and military policies. The decision was one of several signs that he saw scientific work and political action as connected. In nineteen sixty-seven he traveled to Vietnam and lectured to mathematicians during the war. The image of advanced mathematics being taught amid bombing and displacement is striking, but it should not be romanticized. It was a dangerous setting for the people living there, not an exotic backdrop for a celebrated visiting scholar. Grothendieck's appearance expressed his political commitments and his willingness to challenge the established separation between scientific institutions and global conflict.

The upheavals of nineteen sixty-eight sharpened that challenge. He increasingly asked whether the scientific profession could separate the pursuit of knowledge from its material supports and social consequences. Around nineteen seventy he learned of French defense-ministry support for the IHES. This became a major reason for his departure from the institute. Accounts differ on aspects of the departure and its formal timing, but the conflict about military funding is well documented. It was a consequential choice: he gave up the stable and productive home of an extraordinary mathematical research program, not because he had exhausted every mathematical idea, but because he challenged the conditions under which the institution operated.

He helped found the movement *Survivre*, later *Survivre et Vivre*, which addressed militarism, ecological destruction, and the risks of modern industrial society. It published a bulletin, organized discussions, and tried to make scientists answerable to wider communities.

The bulletin provides a documentary counterweight to the idea that he merely became disillusioned and vanished. It contained arguments about nuclear weapons, ecological harm, military research, politics, and the social authority of experts. Other scientists contributed, and the movement changed its emphases as it developed. Activism required a different kind of work from proving theorems: composing public arguments, distributing texts, negotiating disagreements, and participating in meetings. Grothendieck was not always effective at bringing others with him, and some colleagues objected to the tone or consequences of his positions. The disagreements are part of the history of a researcher changing professions in public, not evidence that he had suddenly ceased to think seriously.

These activities were not an unrelated phase tacked onto the biography of a geometric genius. They continued the questions he had lived with since childhood in a different register. How should a person respond when institutions present themselves as neutral but are implicated in political power? His answer did not necessarily persuade colleagues, but it was serious and sustained.

After leaving the IHES he taught in other French institutions and eventually at Montpellier. His mathematical output changed in character. He wrote long manuscripts on mathematical ideas, his own career, creativity, scientific responsibility, and spirituality. *Recoltes et Semailles*, often translated as *Harvests and Sowings*, is both a memoir and a difficult, expansive examination of relationships within the mathematical community. It names colleagues and disputes over memory and credit. Such a source is invaluable because it shows what Grothendieck chose to say about his life; it is not an impartial report whose every judgment should be adopted as the historian's verdict. A biography must allow the writer his voice while checking that voice against other records.

His family life was extensive and not always simple. He had children and long relationships, and friends and relatives did not all share his choices. The more sensational accounts of his later isolation can reduce these people to props in the story of a brilliant hermit. He gradually withdrew from academic and public contact, ultimately living in the French Pyrenees. Scholars have preserved an enormous archive of correspondence and unpublished writing. Portions of his materials have been made available through the University of Montpellier. Those documents suggest that even withdrawal from public mathematical life did not mean his intellectual activity stopped; it changed form and audience.

Grothendieck died in November two thousand fourteen. Since then, historians, mathematicians, former students, archivists, and family members have continued to negotiate how his work should be remembered and made accessible. The institutional archive is itself part of the story. Manuscripts kept by students and colleagues, later transferred for preservation, can illuminate research that was never published in the usual way. They may also complicate familiar narratives based only on award citations or selected recollections. A mathematical life's documentary afterlife depends on choices about custody, description, transcription, translation, and access.

One can now ask what his career means for the history of mathematics. He helped transform algebraic geometry by refusing to regard the classical objects as the only possible starting point. A more general language brought arithmetic, geometry, topology, and representation of spaces into new relationships. Yet this abstract accomplishment came from a very concrete biography: a refugee child sheltered during a genocidal war, a student arriving in postwar France without a conventional elite education, a researcher dependent on mentors and collaborators, and a political thinker willing to break with a powerful institution. His life was not a steady climb from obscurity to glory. It contained achievements, relationships, ruptures, commitments, and choices whose consequences remain open to interpretation.

The image of a 'cathedral builder' is often used for Grothendieck's mathematics, suggesting a vast structure in which different pieces find their place. It can be a useful metaphor if it does not hide the workers who built the cathedral, the patrons who financed it, and the community that inherited it. Grothendieck's theories taught mathematicians to change their questions, not merely to find faster answers. His biography asks a parallel question about intellectual work itself: what kind of world allows such work to happen, and what responsibilities come with accepting that world's support?

## Historical companion (not narrated)

### Chronology and evidence cautions
- **1928-1945:** Born in Berlin; parents' anarchist and antifascist activities; separated from parents and raised by the Heydorns in Hamburg; move to France, internment with his mother, father deported and murdered at Auschwitz; wartime schooling at Le Chambon-sur-Lignon.
- **1945-1958:** Mathematics at Montpellier, then Paris and Nancy; research with Schwartz and Dieudonne in topological vector spaces; visits to Brazil and the United States, turn toward algebraic geometry.
- **1958/59-1970:** Research at IHES; EGA with Dieudonne, SGA with participants, 1966 Fields Medal, 1967 lectures in Vietnam; studies of schemes, cohomology and Riemann-Roch in broader settings.
- **1970-2014:** Break with IHES amid controversy over military funding; *Survivre et Vivre* activism; later teaching, memoirs, writings and withdrawal; death in the Pyrenees.

Do not treat the 'fourteen problems' story as an audited count of completed dissertations. Important mathematics was collaborative: EGA credits Jean Dieudonne; SGA includes contributions by many students and colleagues, and Deligne completed the last Weil conjecture. IHES sources differ slightly about the formal departure date; 'around 1970' is safer. Retell his later life without retrospective medical labels, speculation about private experience, or the notion that wartime trauma explains every later choice.

### Mathematical display companion
- **Affine scheme:** \(\mathrm{Spec}(R)\), the prime spectrum of a commutative ring \(R\), equipped with the structure sheaf. Gluing such local objects constructs general schemes.
- **Arithmetic viewpoint:** the same polynomial equations can be studied over \(\mathbb C\), \(\mathbb Q\), or finite fields \(\mathbb F_p\). Reduction may change geometric behavior, so one uses a framework tracking base change.
- **Cohomology:** groups \(H^i(X,\mathcal F)\) encode obstructions to patching local information globally; etale cohomology is a specific theory suitable for many algebraic varieties over fields of positive characteristic.
- **Attribution:** Grothendieck's foundations were essential to the Weil-conjectures program, but the final proof of the Riemann-hypothesis analogue was by Pierre Deligne (1974).
