# 141000 — Dmitri Mendeleev: The empty places in nature's order

**Track:** History · People and ideas. **Background:** No formal chemistry background assumed; chemical elements, atomic weights, valence, periodicity and atomic number are explained in the narration.

In Saint Petersburg in the winter of 1869, a chemistry professor had a problem with a book. Dmitri Mendeleev was teaching a large university course and writing a textbook for students who needed chemistry in Russian. The first part had explained the familiar substances with which he could build the subject: water, oxygen, hydrogen and several other elements. But dozens remained. He could introduce them one by one, each with its own reactions, compounds and measurements, and hope his students would remember. Or he could find a reason for discussing them in a particular order.

Imagine what a student faced. Why does sodium behave in certain ways like potassium? Why do chlorine and bromine form related salts? Why is a metal's measured weight not enough to tell you which substances it will combine with? The information was distributed across papers in several languages, laboratory notebooks and chemical handbooks. Different chemists still disagreed about some atomic weights. Mendeleev had to turn this crowded landscape into something a beginner could follow. What emerged was not merely a teaching aid. It was a claim about elements that had never been seen.

His first table, dated February 1869 according to the Russian calendar then in use, was an attempt to put the elements into a system based on their atomic weights and chemical similarities. It was not laid out like the poster on a modern classroom wall. He rearranged and revised it. Some places were marked by question marks. Over the next two years he made a much bolder argument: if the pattern was real, those empty places belonged to elements that had yet to be discovered. He ventured estimates of what those elements should weigh, how dense they might be, and what sorts of compounds they should form.

This is often told as the instant an inspired man suddenly saw a table, sometimes in a dream. The better story takes longer. It begins on the far side of the Russian Empire, with a mother who ran a factory, a student who went abroad to learn chemistry, an international argument about atomic weights, and a teacher unwilling to hand his students a shapeless inventory of facts. It continues after the famous discovery, through public disagreements, industrial surveys, political protest and a government bureau devoted to measurements. Mendeleev wanted an order that could be used, and he was willing to quarrel with established opinion to defend one. Sometimes he was right to do so. Sometimes he was wrong.

Dmitri Ivanovich Mendeleev was born in Tobolsk, Siberia, in 1834. Tobolsk was an administrative and commercial center in an immense imperial territory, not a remote place outside history. His father, Ivan Pavlovich, worked in education. His mother, Maria Dmitrievna, came from a family involved in manufacturing and the print trade. Dmitri was the youngest in a large household. Sources disagree about exactly how many children had been born or survived, which is a reminder that even a celebrated scientist's early family record cannot always be reduced to one neat number.

His father lost his sight and could no longer support the household as before. Maria took responsibility for the family's glassworks. Think about the kind of knowledge a glass factory gathered. It consumed fuel and raw materials; it needed furnaces and skilled labor; it transformed sand and other ingredients through controlled heating; and it produced objects that could be sold or broken in transit. A child growing up near such work might encounter chemistry without yet calling it chemistry. It would be too easy to say that a factory made Dmitri destined to discover the periodic table. It did, however, put material transformation and industrial responsibility close to home.

Family circumstances worsened. His father died in the 1840s, and the glassworks was destroyed by fire. Maria decided that her son's education justified an arduous journey west. She took him toward the capitals where a formal scientific career might be possible. He ultimately entered the Main Pedagogical Institute in Saint Petersburg in 1850. Maria died soon after his admission. The boy who had crossed much of the empire under his mother's protection had to continue his education without her. The documentary record supports a formidable effort on her part; it does not give us license to invent a final conversation or claim to know exactly what either of them felt.

The institute prepared teachers. That detail matters. Mendeleev did not enter science only as a future laboratory discoverer. He was trained in a world where the ability to explain a subject was itself professional work. He studied the physical sciences with Aleksandr Voskresensky and other instructors. His first investigations included relations between the shapes of crystals and the chemical composition of substances. When he graduated in 1855, his next work was teaching, including a post in Odessa. He returned to Saint Petersburg, advanced his qualifications and lectured in chemistry. Teaching remained an important part of his scientific identity rather than a temporary obligation to be discarded once research began.

Russian chemistry in the middle of the nineteenth century was connected to European scientific centers, but connections had costs. New books could appear first in German or French. Translation took time. Travel depended on funding and political permission. A young chemist who wanted to participate in current arguments had to know the literature while helping build the institutions and vocabulary in which it could be discussed at home. Mendeleev received support to work abroad. From 1859 to 1861 he spent substantial time in Heidelberg, where Robert Bunsen's laboratory and other research groups drew foreign visitors. Mendeleev also pursued independent experimental work. The familiar story of a Russian student absorbing everything from one German master does not capture a network of laboratories, conversations and competing theories.

One great question concerned the meaning of atomic weight. It sounds simple today: atoms of different elements have different masses. But nineteenth-century chemists could not put a single atom on a balance. They inferred relative weights from measured proportions in compounds, volumes of gases and assumptions about how atoms assembled into molecules. If a compound had one atom of element A and two of element B, its mass ratios meant one thing; if the proposed molecular formula was different, the calculated atomic weights could change. Even when experimenters measured accurately, different interpretations of the same material could yield different tables of weights.

Consider water. The modern formula says that a water molecule contains two hydrogen atoms and one oxygen atom. If you know the formula and the masses in which hydrogen and oxygen combine, you can infer a relative atomic mass for oxygen. But if you assume the wrong molecular composition, the arithmetic may produce a weight that conflicts with other reactions. In the nineteenth century the distinction between an atom and a molecule was still being clarified, and chemists argued over formulas that now appear elementary. Atomic weights were not a neutral list waiting to be sorted. They were the result of chemical theories that had to be brought into agreement.

In 1860 Mendeleev attended the first international congress of chemists at Karlsruhe, in the German states. Julius Lothar Meyer was there too, along with many other working chemists. An important intellectual presence was the Italian Stanislao Cannizzaro, who championed a consistent way of using Amedeo Avogadro's ideas about gases to distinguish molecular weights from atomic weights. The arguments did not resolve every problem at once. But they helped create conditions in which chemists could compare substances using weights that meant approximately the same thing in different laboratories.

That international setting is important to Mendeleev's achievement. A periodic system based on weights could not be much better than the weights supplied to it. The table was not simply a private product of a Russian mind, nor was it a ready-made European scheme copied into a Russian textbook. It was an original synthesis made possible by an international body of measurement, argument and prior classification. Mendeleev himself would later reflect on this dependence in a lecture honoring Michael Faraday in London.

Returning to Saint Petersburg, he wrote and taught with energy. His Russian-language textbook on organic chemistry appeared in 1861. He took on appointments in technical and university chemistry and investigated solutions, including the measurable behavior of alcohol mixed with water. In 1865 he defended his doctoral dissertation on that subject. Mixing two liquids is not always the simple addition of their original volumes. Their molecules can pack and interact so that the resulting volume is slightly smaller than the two starting volumes added together. Mendeleev's measurements mattered to the physical chemistry of solutions and to practical standardization of liquid mixtures. They did not invent vodka, and his thesis did not decree the modern composition of Russian vodka. That later story tells us more about the appetite for national legends than about his experiment.

He also studied agriculture and industry. He examined fertilizer use, took an interest in experimental farming and asked how scientific knowledge could change economic production. These interests may appear disconnected from a famous arrangement of elements. To Mendeleev they shared a question: what reliable pattern will allow a person to do more than repeat what someone else did yesterday? Good chemistry might improve the analysis of a soil, the composition of a glass or the control of a factory as well as the design of a textbook.

In 1867 Mendeleev became professor of general chemistry at Saint Petersburg University. He needed an up-to-date Russian text substantial enough to support his course. His project became *Principles of Chemistry*. At first the writing went well. But his plan demanded a systematic treatment of the growing number of chemical elements. By 1869 chemists knew roughly sixty-three. A catalogue could describe them, but a course needed comparisons. One familiar group consisted of lithium, sodium and potassium. Another included chlorine, bromine and iodine. Why did these families exist? And could their arrangement reveal families that no one had yet completed?

Let us spend a moment with the chemistry rather than the printed rectangle. Lithium, sodium and potassium are all metals that can readily lose one electron in the modern account. That makes them chemically reactive and gives them a tendency to form compounds with a charge of plus one. Chlorine and bromine are nonmetals that readily gain an electron in many common reactions; they frequently form salts with the reactive metals. Sodium chloride is ordinary table salt. Potassium chloride is a different compound, but its formula and many of its broad chemical relationships follow the same pattern. The repeated forms are not coincidences of naming. They are clues to recurring arrangements of atoms.

Mendeleev did not know electrons in 1869. His students could nevertheless learn that certain substances combined in characteristic proportions. Chemists used terms such as valence, meaning an element's combining capacity in a given chemical context. A metal that typically forms one part of a chloride behaves differently from one that commonly forms a chloride with two chlorine atoms. The formulas of oxides, chlorides and hydrides supplied evidence of family resemblance. A chemical table could bring that resemblance into view, provided it did not mislead its readers about how consistent the data really were.

The organizing variable Mendeleev favored was atomic weight, roughly the relative mass assigned to the atom of an element. If you put the elements in increasing order of weight, their chemical properties did not simply increase smoothly with mass. Instead, related kinds of behavior returned. A very reactive metal would be followed by other kinds of elements and, after a run of substances, another metal with related behavior. That recurrence was periodicity. The word did not mean that every row contained a fixed number of positions from the beginning or that each repeated pattern was equally tidy. It meant that chemical similarity kept reappearing along the ordered sequence.

Others had seen parts of this before him. Johann Döbereiner had pointed to groups of three related elements. Alexandre-Émile Béguyer de Chancourtois arranged elements helically on a cylinder. John Newlands proposed an order resembling musical octaves, and his ideas had been criticized. William Odling considered related classifications. Lothar Meyer published a system of chemical families in 1864 and later showed periodic variation in atomic volumes: the space occupied by a given amount of an element changed in a recurring pattern with atomic weight. These are not merely runners-up in a race designed to crown Mendeleev. They were addressing related questions with partly different tools and explanatory ambitions.

Mendeleev's distinctive strength was to use the classification as an instrument for reasoning about missing information. He had developed a scheme that could accommodate the known elements across a single system. In a famous 1869 sheet, some positions carried question marks. Later schemes were revised to bring chemical families into better alignment. His most specific published predictions came in the 1871 development of the periodic law. He did not begin with the final polished textbook wall chart and then wait for nature to comply. The argument matured while he rearranged data, criticized weights, and tried to make the system more informative.

The first public presentation had an almost comic detail. At the Russian Chemical Society's March 1869 meeting, the paper was read by his colleague Nikolai Menshutkin, not by Mendeleev himself. Historians disagree over the reason for Mendeleev's absence. One tradition says he was ill; archival reconstructions connect it to a trip inspecting cooperative cheese factories. Whatever the precise explanation, the paper entered a professional conversation without its author in the room. And it did not immediately cause a stampede of chemists to rearrange their laboratories. Its consequences became clearer as the revised system generated testable predictions.

What is the intellectual difference between leaving a blank and making a prediction? Suppose you arrange library books by size and subject, and one shelf has an awkward empty space. You might be missing a book, but you could also have invented an arbitrary rule that makes holes inevitable. A blank alone is not evidence of a real object. Mendeleev went further. He argued that recurring chemical properties constrained what the missing element should be like, even before anyone isolated it. If a future substance appeared with the predicted weight and the predicted family of compounds, the scheme would have passed a test it had been able to specify in advance.

He named one of the missing elements eka-aluminium. The prefix, borrowed from Sanskrit counting, meant one beyond or one below a familiar member of the family. Its predicted atomic weight was about sixty-eight. Its density as a solid was expected to be around six grams per cubic centimeter. The oxide it formed with oxygen should have a chemical formula comparable to aluminium's oxide, two atoms of the metal for three atoms of oxygen. These were not arbitrary guesses scattered through a notebook; the estimates arose from comparisons above, below and beside the vacant location in the system.

Imagine the density calculation as a rough argument. The element should sit between known substances of related character. Its mass is constrained by its position, and its volume by the patterns seen among related elements. Density is mass divided by volume. If the family relationships are meaningful, these two lines of reasoning provide a testable approximate number. Other predicted properties, such as its behavior toward acids and bases, could be inferred by analogy to neighbors. The result was not the precision of modern atomic theory. But it was astonishingly concrete for a substance not yet isolated.

In 1875 a French chemist, Paul-Émile Lecoq de Boisbaudran, discovered gallium while investigating minerals by spectroscopy. Spectroscopy had become a powerful way to detect chemical substances: when an excited sample emits light at certain wavelengths, its bright lines provide clues to what is present. The detection belonged to Lecoq de Boisbaudran, not Mendeleev. The significance for the periodic law was that gallium appeared where eka-aluminium had been expected. Its atomic weight and chemistry matched important aspects of the prediction.

There was even a confrontation between theoretical expectation and experimental measurement. The first reported density of gallium was appreciably lower than Mendeleev's estimate. He argued that the reported value should be checked. Later measurements on a better sample yielded a density close to the predicted value. This is not evidence that a clever theorist can dismiss experiments whenever they disagree with his model. The exchange was productive because someone could repeat the measurement, improve the material and decide between the possibilities. The lesson is the mutual discipline of prediction and experiment, not the superiority of one over the other.

Then came two more conspicuous confirmations. In 1879 Lars Fredrik Nilson discovered scandium, corresponding to the missing place Mendeleev had called eka-boron. In 1886 Clemens Winkler discovered germanium, which matched the proposed eka-silicon. Here the predictions involved more than names and approximate masses. Germanium's typical valence, its oxide and several of its chemical behaviors fit the broad profile of the supposed element. Each discovery supplied an independent check on whether the pattern was connected to nature rather than simply imposed on a page.

But we should resist a tidy retrospective in which every prediction came out perfectly and every critic was foolish. The predicted properties had varying accuracy. Some empty positions were interpreted differently as the evidence changed. The rare-earth elements presented difficult classification problems. Atomic weights were periodically corrected. Mendeleev made bold forecasts that were not all confirmed. The periodic law's credibility rose because its most informative successes endured critical checking, not because a nineteenth-century investigator became incapable of error.

Nor did the field settle into universal agreement overnight. Meyer had constructed related systems independently and made important contributions to understanding periodicity. The two men disputed priority. Their controversy can be uncomfortable for anyone seeking a story in which scientific virtue automatically rewards generosity. People who create important things may still care fiercely about recognition, reputation and the precise dates of publication. What survives the dispute is that multiple scientists, using emerging agreement about atomic weights, were finding recurring relationships. Mendeleev's combination of a broad table, a flexible system and detailed forecasts made his version especially powerful. It did not erase the work on which chemistry depended.

Another difficulty lay in the order itself. Mendeleev broadly sorted elements by atomic weight, but sometimes chemical similarity suggested that an element belonged on the other side of a heavier neighbor. Tellurium and iodine are the classic pair. The atomic weight of tellurium is a little greater than that of iodine, yet tellurium belongs with oxygen, sulfur and selenium, while iodine belongs with chlorine and bromine. If weight alone determined every position, the two would be in the wrong order. Mendeleev placed stronger weight on chemical family resemblance than on a rigid sorting rule. He thought some measured weights might prove wrong. In this case the relative weights are not the real explanation.

We can now reveal a piece of physical knowledge he did not have. In the modern periodic table, elements are ordered by atomic number: the number of protons in the atomic nucleus. Tellurium has fifty-two protons; iodine has fifty-three. That makes their chemical order consistent even though the average mass of natural tellurium is slightly greater than the average mass of natural iodine. Mass is affected by the different isotopes of an element, which contain different numbers of neutrons. Atomic number, not average atomic weight, is the reliable organizing key. Henry Moseley's work in 1913, after Mendeleev's death, gave decisive evidence connecting characteristic X-ray spectra to atomic number. Mendeleev's anomaly was a sign that his table had reached beyond the explanatory physics available to him.

The electron-based explanation came later still. Electrons occupy quantum states around nuclei, and their arrangements determine much of an element's characteristic chemistry. A column in the modern table contains elements with similar patterns of outer electrons. Sodium and potassium are related because each has one easily removed outer electron, not because either scientist in 1869 knew the nucleus or electron shells. The periodic law identified an empirical pattern before quantum mechanics supplied much of the physical explanation for why that pattern exists. A successful classification can precede a satisfying theory of its cause.

Mendeleev's later life reminds us how much more he wanted than an explanatory triumph inside chemistry. Russia's industrial development, economic independence and scientific education mattered deeply to him. He investigated the petroleum industry, including fields around Baku in the Caucasus, and traveled to the United States in 1876 to study American production. He wrote on better use of petroleum, transport, refining and chemical manufacturing. The work involved engineering and policy as well as chemistry. Oil was valuable as a source of materials and an industrial resource, not merely as something to burn for heat.

It is tempting to read every industrial proposal as the forward-looking work of a modern environmentalist. That would conceal the world in which he actually operated. He advocated the development of Russian industry within an empire; extraction, tariffs, railways, ships and commercial competition were among his interests. His proposals should be judged in that context rather than enlisted as straightforward answers to questions he could not have known in their present form. Practical science can improve manufacturing while also serving state projects that deserve historical scrutiny.

He also wrote about agriculture and advocated experiments with fertilizers. One can see a continuity between the factory of his childhood and the working landscape of his adulthood. He wanted reliable quantities instead of loose guesses, and institutions that could transfer findings from individual experts into public practice. That did not make him gentle or universally effective. Contemporaries describe an emphatic and argumentative man; his dealings with organizations and authorities could be combative. His conviction that he had found a real natural law coexisted with a readiness to fight over matters much smaller than the ordering of the elements.

His private life could be difficult too. He married Feozva Leshcheva in 1862, and they had children. The relationship became strained. Later he formed a relationship with Anna Popova, a much younger art student. The separation, divorce and second marriage were controversial in Russian Orthodox society. There were children in both families. The basic record warrants saying that personal attachments and social rules complicated his life; it does not warrant treating the marriage as a romantic rescue story, the first wife as an obstacle, or the second as merely a footnote in a famous man's biography. Scientific distinction offered no special protection from the consequences of his decisions for other people.

There were public adventures less private and much easier for newspapers to describe. In 1887 he took a balloon flight to observe a solar eclipse. The planned scientific observations were constrained by the conditions of the ascent, and the story was soon told for its daring as much as for what was learned. Here was a professor famous for chemical order accepting the physical risk of an aerial observation. It would be misleading, however, to let one dramatic flight crowd out years of work on standards and engineering. Measuring the physical world was a continuing program, not merely a taste for spectacle.

Mendeleev also took public positions against spiritualism, whose advocates claimed that séances could produce objective evidence of communication with the dead. He participated in efforts to examine claims presented as scientific phenomena. The episode is part of a larger nineteenth-century argument about what counts as evidence, who may speak in the name of science, and how experiment should be organized when participants suspect deception or wish to believe. It also reveals a man who sometimes turned scientific authority into public controversy. His disagreements went beyond technical chemistry.

He clashed with institutions in ways more consequential for his career. In 1880 he failed to gain election as a full member of the Saint Petersburg Academy of Sciences, despite already being internationally recognized. The rejection provoked public argument. In 1890 a confrontation over students' treatment and a petition to the education authorities led him to leave his university professorship. Accounts differ in their emphasis on whether this was resignation, dismissal under pressure, or the final stage of an already troubled relationship, but the central fact is clear: the dispute ended a long university career. A man whose public achievement had made him a scientific institution still found that institutions could refuse him.

His later work moved heavily into government service. In 1893 he became the first director of a reorganized central board for weights and measures. That office sounds remote from the elements until we consider what a system of measurement does. A merchant purchases a given weight of material. A factory reports the volume of a liquid. A laboratory compares the density of one substance with another. If the balances, standards and procedures disagree, even conscientious measurements cannot be compared reliably. Science cannot travel through a country without shared conventions for saying what has been measured. Mendeleev spent substantial attention on that unglamorous foundation of reproducible knowledge.

His government service included work on industrial policy, shipping, and smokeless powder. He argued about the direction of Russian development and investigated problems of applied chemistry. Many of these projects were contested. An advocate for measurement standards could also support imperial industrial priorities and strong state intervention. In his old age he was not simply a retired discoverer receiving honors. He continued to take sides in contemporary economic and institutional arguments.

The periodic table itself also refused to remain still. In the 1890s the discovery of the noble gases presented a new family for which the early schemes had not made room. Argon, helium and other relatively unreactive gases did not fit the traditional pattern of elements forming compounds in familiar ratios. William Ramsay and his collaborators isolated and studied these substances. Ultimately a new group could be accommodated in the periodic system. But the experience mattered: the table had to make space not merely for one predicted substance but for a whole family no one had planned. Scientific order proved its strength partly through its capacity to be revised.

The discovery of radioactivity raised deeper problems. If an element could emit particles and transform into another element, what had become of the apparently permanent units of chemistry? The physics of radioactive decay, isotopes and nuclei developed in the years around and after Mendeleev's death. He was not an eager adopter of every radical new idea. At times he resisted theories that later became central to physics, and his own speculative attempts to accommodate new phenomena were not successful. The chemist who had urged others to question accepted data could himself resist changes to a framework he had built. That is not a contradiction we need to hide. A powerful discovery does not guarantee good judgment about every subsequent one.

In 1889 Mendeleev addressed the Chemical Society in London in a Faraday Lecture devoted to the periodic law. He could speak with the knowledge that gallium, scandium and germanium had been found. He also placed the achievement within the accumulated work of many experimenters. There is something satisfying about the setting: the lecturer from Saint Petersburg discussing a chemical generalization before an audience in the country where Faraday had made electricity and magnetism experimentally intelligible. They worked in different fields, but each had turned laboratory facts into a broader way of reasoning. In Mendeleev's case, the arrangement of elements was valuable precisely because it led to questions for future experimenters.

Mendeleev never received a Nobel Prize. The chemistry prize began in 1901, when his discovery was already more than thirty years old. He was nominated late in life, and historical accounts of the 1906 deliberations report that the Nobel committee favored him before the Academy awarded the prize to Henri Moissan. It would be easy to turn this into a story about a prize stolen from its obvious owner. Better to recognize that prizes have rules, committees, rival candidates and institutional politics. Moissan had substantial achievements of his own. The periodic law did not become more or less true because of a vote in Stockholm.

Dmitri Mendeleev died in Saint Petersburg in 1907. Students carried a periodic table at his funeral. That image is worth taking seriously as a public act, rather than as proof that everyone understood him in the same way. The students were honoring a teacher and a scientific achievement. They were also carrying a representation of a kind of knowledge: numbers, formulas, families, omissions, and relationships compressed onto a page. It was an object that other students could inherit, challenge and extend.

After his death, Moseley's atomic numbers helped repair the sorting rule. Quantum theory explained why the chemical families recur. Chemists discovered elements beyond uranium, and element one hundred and one was named mendelevium in his honor in 1955. None of those developments means that Mendeleev secretly knew modern atomic theory. He knew enough chemistry to see a pattern whose full physical cause was still hidden, and enough about uncertainty to leave some places open rather than force a premature answer.

Think back to the professor with the unfinished textbook. He could have offered his students sixty-three disconnected descriptions. Instead he built a system that joined the properties they knew to properties no one had yet measured. His great move was not merely to notice repetition. It was to take that repetition seriously enough to risk being wrong in public. If the missing elements had not appeared, his system would have been exposed. When they did appear, chemistry had gained a way to investigate nature before nature supplied the next specimen.

There is a second lesson in the rest of his life. The same man who trusted a general law also cared about the reliability of balances, the calibration of instruments, the education of teachers and the economic consequences of chemical knowledge. A table with a blank cell invites a question. A standard weight makes an answer comparable. Mendeleev's enduring contribution sits between those two commitments: daring to infer what is missing, and insisting that a finding must eventually be checked against the material world.

## Non-spoken companion: chronology, chemistry, and disputed stories

### The periodic inference

- **What was ordered?** Mendeleev's system used nineteenth-century relative atomic **weights**, chemical families, characteristic oxides and compounds, and valence. It was not built from proton numbers or electron configurations, both unknown in 1869.
- **Where the key claims appeared:** a first printed scheme dated 17 February 1869 **Old Style** (1 March Gregorian); a paper read to the Russian Chemical Society by **Nikolai Menshutkin**, not Mendeleev, in March; the stronger detailed eka-element predictions developed in 1871. Contemporaneous Russian accounts date the presentation 6 March Old Style. Accounts disagree on whether illness or a cheesemaking survey explains the absence; do not treat either explanation as settled. Do not collapse the document, society presentation and 1871 refined forecasts into one event.
- **Earlier and parallel investigators:** Döbereiner (triads), de Chancourtois (helical classification), Newlands (octaves), Odling (classifications), **Julius Lothar Meyer** (1864 families, 1870 atomic-volume evidence), **Stanislao Cannizzaro** (consistent atomic-weight reasoning), with many chemists measuring constituent substances. A table is not a one-person experimental discovery of all known elements.
- **The three particularly useful tests:** eka-aluminium → gallium, discovered **1875** by **Paul-Émile Lecoq de Boisbaudran**; eka-boron → scandium, discovered **1879** by **Lars Fredrik Nilson**; eka-silicon → germanium, discovered **1886** by **Clemens Winkler**. The prediction and the independent isolation should retain separate credits.
- **Gallium as a quantitative example:** Predicted atomic weight approximately 68, observed approximately 69.7; predicted solid density around 6 g/cm³, improved observed value near 5.9 g/cm³. Early density measurements disagreed and were repeated. This example is instructive because measurements can be corrected, not because chemical theory always outranks experiment.
- **Tellurium and iodine:** the tellurium mass is slightly larger than iodine's average mass, but tellurium has atomic number 52 and iodine 53. Atomic numbers order elements by proton count; mass differences include isotopic composition. **Henry Moseley's** decisive work dates to 1913, after Mendeleev's lifetime.
- **Mechanism after Mendeleev:** Modern electronic configuration accounts for chemical family similarities, with qualifications arising from electron subshells, oxidation states and periodic trends. Neither electron shells nor nuclei should be attributed to Mendeleev.

### Biographical interpretation

- **Childhood:** Birth in Tobolsk in 1834; father Ivan, mother Maria, family glassworks, death of father and loss of factory; Maria's effort to support his studies in Saint Petersburg. Sources disagree on exact sibling counts. This lecture does not invent family dialogue.
- **Education:** Main Pedagogical Institute, 1850–1855; early teaching (including Odessa), private research and advanced study in Heidelberg, 1859–1861; Karlsruhe congress, 1860; university work and Russian-language textbooks.
- **Vodka and dreams:** The 1865 thesis examined physical properties of alcohol–water mixtures; it did **not** invent vodka or establish the modern 40% rule. Popular accounts say the table appeared fully formed in a dream; archival/historical accounts provide a sustained research and teaching history instead.
- **Institutions:** the Academy's rejection of his full membership in 1880 and university departure in 1890 were controversies with specific political and professional contexts. We do not invent a single villain or a private motive that the sources cannot establish.
- **Personal life:** First marriage to Feozva Leshcheva, second to Anna Popova, and the family's experience are acknowledged without turning legal and religious controversies into a comic anecdote.
- **Later work:** Petroleum and applied industrial chemistry, agriculture, 1887 eclipse-balloon flight, inquiry into spiritualist claims, and directorship of the reorganized weights-and-measures bureau beginning 1893. His positions served Russian imperial state development and were sometimes disputed.
- **Recognition:** He did not win the Nobel Prize; the 1906 Chemistry prize went to Henri Moissan. His students' funeral tribute with a table is documented. **Mendelevium, element 101**, was named decades after his death.
