# 093000 - Richard Hamming: Errors, machines, and the art of asking better questions

**Track:** History - People and ideas. **Focus:** Chicago schooling and wartime mathematics, the Manhattan Project, Bell Laboratories, error-correcting codes, numerical analysis, colleagues, teaching, and the ethics of technical work. **Background:** binary digits and simple arithmetic; the code is explained from an example.

Imagine returning to work on Monday morning and finding that the calculations you assigned to a computer on Friday have failed. A single error has stopped the run, and the machine has been waiting for someone to notice. The machine is capable of detecting trouble, but not of repairing it. Why should a machine that knows it made a mistake be unable to locate the mistake and continue? Richard Hamming later used a version of this experience to explain his interest in error-correcting codes. It is a good story, and it captures a real engineering question, although the weekend incident reaches us through his retrospective accounts. His life was larger than the story. Hamming worked on differential equations, the Manhattan Project, telecommunications, numerical analysis, algorithms, and the education of engineers. His best-known contribution is a method for making unreliable information systems more reliable, but he also became a powerful advocate for asking whether a technically correct answer was answering the right question.

Richard Wesley Hamming was born in Chicago in February nineteen fifteen. His parents were Richard J. Hamming and Mabel Redfield. He attended local schools and developed mathematical ability, though his initial professional ambition was engineering. When he looked for a scholarship, the opportunity available to him was at the University of Chicago, which had no engineering school. He studied mathematics instead, earning a bachelor's degree in nineteen thirty-seven. That apparent detour would matter. In the decades that followed, computers turned many practical engineering problems into mathematical questions about algorithms, approximation, and information. Hamming came to these fields through the older academic language of pure and applied mathematics.

He continued at the University of Nebraska, receiving a master's degree in nineteen thirty-nine, and then the University of Illinois, where he completed a doctorate in nineteen forty-two. His dissertation, under Waldemar Trjitzinsky, concerned boundary-value problems for differential equations. The work lay in analysis rather than the codes for which he would become famous. Boundary conditions arise when a mathematical model must satisfy requirements at the edges of an interval or region. A vibrating string, for example, is not described completely by its equation of motion; the ends may be fixed or subject to other constraints. The mathematics asks which solutions exist and how their behavior can be understood. Such questions would later be transformed by computers, which approximate solutions with finite calculations.

During his graduate years Hamming read George Boole's work on logic, a connection he recalled long afterward. Boole had shown that propositions could be combined using algebraic operations. Digital electronics had a related need: make decisions and store information through discrete states. Hamming's early reading did not give him a complete theory of modern coding, and Boole was not the only precursor. It does reveal an interest in turning apparently verbal or physical tasks into structures that mathematics can operate on.

In September nineteen forty-two he married Wanda Little. He taught briefly at universities before joining the Manhattan Project at Los Alamos in nineteen forty-five. His assignment was to organize and operate computing equipment that carried out large numerical calculations for the bomb program. The project depended on physicists, chemists, engineers, technicians, human computers, and mechanical and electronic equipment; it was not a single room full of famous theorists. Wanda also worked at Los Alamos, initially with desk calculators and later with scientific groups. Her work belongs to the history of the project rather than merely to Hamming's domestic biography.

The Manhattan Project placed Hamming in a setting where an arithmetic error could have grave consequences. In a later personal account, he recalled being asked to check arithmetic associated with the possibility that an atomic test could ignite the atmosphere. He worried about the gap between checking a computation and verifying the physical model behind it. A retrospective lecture cannot establish every detail of that episode by itself. What it documents clearly is the concern Hamming later attached to the distinction between numerical reliability and scientific judgment. A computer may multiply accurately while its user has chosen the wrong equations, neglected a physical process, or misunderstood the uncertainty in the inputs.

After the war he remained at Los Alamos for a period, then joined Bell Telephone Laboratories in nineteen forty-six. Bell Labs was an industrial research institution with an extraordinary mix of mathematics, engineering, electronics, and communications. The telephone system created practical questions about noise, signal degradation, switching, transmission, and large networks. Research there was not driven by a single academic syllabus. It was driven by the need to make systems work, at scale, with dependable performance. Hamming worked in a mathematical research department that included Claude Shannon and John Tukey. Hendrik Bode was an influential leader. Colleagues shared offices and argued over problems, often discovering that work pursued for one purpose had consequences elsewhere.

Shannon was developing the mathematical theory of communication, describing information, channels, and limits on reliable transmission. Hamming was investigating how to build codes that actually locate and correct errors. These projects were related but distinct. Shannon's landmark nineteen forty-eight work analyzed what rates of reliable communication are possible under specified channel models. Hamming's coding constructions gave concrete methods for encoding information so that particular errors could be recognized or corrected. Theoretical limits and usable codes are partners in the same subject, not competing claims to one invention.

To see the problem, imagine sending four binary digits, each either zero or one. There are sixteen possible messages. If we transmit the four digits without protection, a flipped bit can turn one valid message into another. The receiver may have no way of knowing that anything went wrong. Now suppose the sender adds three check digits, making a block of seven. The extra bits are not arbitrary decoration. They are chosen to satisfy particular parity equations, equations whose answers say whether selected groups of bits contain an even or odd number of ones.

The receiver tests these equations. If all the tests pass, the block is consistent with the code. If a single bit has flipped, the pattern of failed tests can identify which of the seven positions is wrong. The receiver flips it back. This is the principle behind a binary Hamming code with seven positions carrying four bits of information and three check bits. In the standard arrangement, a valid codeword is at least three bit changes away from every other valid codeword. That distance is the key mathematical fact: a received block with one corrupted position remains closer to its original codeword than to any other. The construction can correct any single-bit error under the assumption that no additional error has occurred.

Why call this a distance? Consider the strings zero-one-one-zero and one-one-zero-zero. They differ in two positions, so their Hamming distance is two. This distance does not measure physical space or elapsed time. It counts how many coordinates would have to change to turn one string into the other. With that simple definition, the design of a code becomes a geometric problem: choose valid messages as points that are far enough apart in a space of binary strings. When noise moves a received point, the receiver tries to recover the intended valid point. A practical communications problem has become a question about discrete geometry and combinatorics.

The distance property also tells us what the code cannot guarantee. A code of minimum distance three can correct one arbitrary bit error, or detect up to two if detection rather than correction is the task. It cannot reliably correct every two-bit error. If the decoder blindly assumes every erroneous block contains only one wrong bit, two changes may be mistaken for a different one-bit corruption. In many computer systems an additional overall parity bit is used to help distinguish single-bit errors from certain multiple-bit errors. The precise guarantee depends on the code and the decoding procedure. These details matter because the phrase 'error correcting' is not a promise to undo all damage.

Hamming's foundational paper on error-detecting and error-correcting codes appeared in the Bell System Technical Journal in nineteen fifty. Coding theory grew quickly afterward, with contributions from many researchers in mathematics, computer science, and electrical engineering. Later codes could handle bursts of errors, much larger blocks, and different kinds of noise. Modern storage media, communications links, and computers depend on error-control ideas, but not all of their practical schemes are ordinary seven-bit Hamming codes. The name survives because he articulated a fundamental design and a useful distance, not because every reliable digital system uses his exact original construction.

His research interests remained broad. Bell Labs gave him opportunities to work on numerical methods, telecommunications, filtering, and computing systems. Numerical analysis asks how one can calculate trustworthy approximations to mathematical quantities with finite time and finite precision. Errors in these settings arise not only from a flipped bit but from rounding, truncation, and unstable algorithms. Suppose two nearly equal measured numbers are subtracted. Even if each number was stored with reasonable precision, the difference may have few reliable digits. Repeating a calculation with more steps does not necessarily cure the problem. The method and the conditioning of the question need examination. Hamming helped make such concerns part of applied mathematical education.

A recurring theme of his teaching was that machines make errors visible in new ways while also encouraging misplaced confidence. A calculation could finish without crashing and still be wrong. A program could implement an equation faithfully while the equation failed to represent the situation of interest. A researcher could optimize a method for a benchmark that did not describe real use. These are generalizations inspired by his work rather than verbatim doctrines from one paper. They explain why his later books ranged beyond coding theory into scientific computing, probability, filters, computers and society, and research practice.

Hamming spent roughly thirty years at Bell Labs, but he increasingly wanted to teach. He held visiting and adjunct academic positions while continuing industrial research. In nineteen seventy-six he moved to the Naval Postgraduate School in Monterey, California, to devote himself principally to teaching and writing. The move was not a retreat from computers into a comfortable past. It was a change in how he hoped to influence the people who designed and used them. He argued that professionals should develop the habit of understanding the important questions in their fields, not merely becoming efficient at problems assigned by someone else.

This argument appeared memorably in his talk 'You and Your Research,' delivered in the nineteen eighties and circulated afterward. Hamming urged listeners to ask what problems matter and to make room in their lives for sustained thought. He described conversations with outstanding colleagues and criticized research cultures that reward only quick, safe, incremental work. It is a personal address, not an experimentally established formula for scientific greatness. Its value lies in the challenge it poses to habits of attention. He had spent a lifetime observing the difference between work that keeps a machine or institution running and work that changes the questions it can ask.

There are hazards in such advice. Not everyone can choose freely what problems to pursue. Career stage, race, gender, family obligations, funding, or institutional position can constrain the choice. Hamming's own career benefited from access to remarkable colleagues and equipment, first through a wartime project and then through a large industrial laboratory. The advice becomes more useful when these material conditions are included rather than suppressed. An institution that asks its researchers to think deeply must provide them with time, security, and opportunities to take intellectual risks.

He continued teaching and writing late in life. His book The Art of Doing Science and Engineering: Learning to Learn was published in the nineteen nineties, bringing together lessons from decades of scientific and computing practice. Hamming died in Monterey in January nineteen ninety-eight. By then error-correcting codes had become a routine part of the technological world, often operating silently. Their purpose is easy to miss precisely because successful correction leaves the user unaware that an error ever occurred.

The weekend computer story leads us back to the idea that made his name. Hamming's mathematical insight was that redundancy could be structured so carefully that errors become locatable. A message could carry information not only about what it says but about how to repair it when a small part is damaged. The larger historical insight is that this work emerged from people sharing a laboratory, with communication theorists, machine operators, engineers, and mathematicians solving overlapping problems. Hamming's life does not tell us that all errors can be eliminated. It shows how a careful definition of error, an understanding of the available evidence, and the courage to ask a better question can transform a practical obstacle into a durable field of mathematics.

## Historical companion (not narrated)

### Historical chronology

- **1915–1942:** born in Chicago; mathematics at University of Chicago, Nebraska, and Illinois; doctorate in differential equations; married Wanda Little in 1942.
- **1945–1946:** Hamming and Wanda worked at Los Alamos during the Manhattan Project; he then joined Bell Laboratories.
- **1946–1976:** research at Bell Labs in codes, numerical methods, and communications; foundational error-control paper in 1950; extensive teaching and publications.
- **1976–1998:** taught at Naval Postgraduate School in Monterey; published books and talks on research and computation; died in 1998.

### Mathematical companion

For binary strings \(x,y\in\{0,1\}^n\), Hamming distance is

\[d_H(x,y)=\#\{i:x_i\ne y_i\}.\]

A code with minimum distance \(d\) can detect up to \(d-1\) bit errors, or correct up to \(\lfloor(d-1)/2\rfloor\) unknown errors under a nearest-codeword decoding rule. The binary \([7,4,3]\) Hamming code has seven transmitted bits, four information bits, and minimum distance three. It corrects one error, but not arbitrary double errors. An extended Hamming code can add an overall parity bit to provide single-error correction and double-error detection under its specified decoder.

The existence of coding schemes is related to, but not identical with, Shannon's noisy-channel coding theorem. Numerical roundoff and modeling errors are distinct from isolated transmission bit flips.

**Historical caution:** The failed weekend computation is a retrospective story, not a verified contemporary diary record. Claude Shannon's theory of communication and Hamming's code constructions are distinct but related. Do not claim that a distance-three code corrects arbitrary double-bit errors, or that every modern storage system literally uses the original seven-bit code. Wanda Hamming worked at Los Alamos and must not be reduced to a passing description of the household.
