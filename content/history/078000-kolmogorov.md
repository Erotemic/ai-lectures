# 078000 - Andrey Kolmogorov: Probability, Soviet institutions, and a life of mathematical breadth

**Track:** History - People and ideas. **Focus:** childhood during Russia's transformations, Moscow's mathematical school, Nikolai Luzin and Pavel Aleksandrov, political pressure in Soviet mathematics, axiomatic probability, turbulence, dynamical systems, information, and education. **Background:** elementary probability, calculus and functions; later concepts are introduced through examples.

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

Andrey Kolmogorov gave twentieth-century probability one of its most durable mathematical foundations, but his scientific life was far larger than probability. He worked on functions, topology, turbulence, planetary motion, information and education. His career traversed revolutionary Russia, Stalin's Soviet Union, the Second World War and decades of postwar scientific institution-building. He taught advanced researchers and schoolchildren, studied the organization of poetry as well as mathematical sequences, and maintained a long intellectual partnership with the topologist Pavel Aleksandrov. To understand Kolmogorov it helps to see both the power and the constraints of the institutions in which he worked.

Andrey Nikolaevich Kolmogorov was born in April nineteen oh three in Tambov, in the Russian Empire. His mother died in childbirth. His father did not raise him. He was brought up by his maternal aunt Vera, largely in the family home near Yaroslavl. The childhood was marked by loss but also by a household that valued education. His maternal grandfather had connections to the provincial nobility, a background whose political significance would change sharply in the years ahead. The boy did not grow up in an unbroken world of imperial security. The revolutions of nineteen seventeen, civil war and changing structures of employment transformed Russian society during his adolescence.

Kolmogorov's early interests were broad. Before entering university he spent time working on the railway and studying mechanics. In nineteen twenty he enrolled at Moscow State University. His interests included mathematics, metallurgy and Russian history. He worked seriously on the historical ownership of property in medieval Novgorod. A frequently repeated anecdote says that a history examiner demanded more independent proofs of a conclusion than mathematics would require. The story may capture a contrast between disciplines, but it comes through later recollection and should not be mistaken for a surviving examination transcript. What it does show reliably is that Kolmogorov's historical interests were not merely an ornament attached to a mathematical career.

Moscow offered a vigorous research community with its own generational history. Dmitri Egorov and Nikolai Luzin had developed work on real analysis and the structure of sets and functions. The circle around Luzin was nicknamed Luzitania, an informal scholarly name that reflected a dense network of students, collaborators and shared problems. Pavel Aleksandrov and Pavel Urysohn were associated with this world; so were Mikhail Suslin and others. The institutions of Moscow mathematics were not created in nineteen twenty. Kolmogorov entered a mature set of research conversations and was affected by their technical ambitions and human rivalries.

As a young student he read widely and began producing results. He studied under Luzin and others; the lectures of Vyacheslav Stepanov on trigonometric series especially impressed him. In nineteen twenty-two he constructed a summable function whose Fourier series diverged almost everywhere. It was an arresting example because many analysts had hoped that integrability alone would secure much better convergence. The achievement did not make Fourier analysis useless. It showed that the exact hypotheses of a convergence theorem matter and that a plausible generalization can fail spectacularly. His early work on sets, integration and logic similarly asked what a familiar mathematical process actually guarantees.

He graduated in nineteen twenty-five and published work in probability with Aleksandr Khinchin. Their research on sums of independent random variables contributed to a line of work that would become central to probability theory. Imagine repeatedly adding random quantities: the gain or loss on a sequence of bets, or a long accumulation of measurement errors. Under which assumptions does the sum stabilize, and when must we expect wild behavior? The question has an empirical flavor, but answering it rigorously requires clear definitions of independence, convergence and probability itself. Those are precisely the foundations the field was still organizing.

By nineteen twenty-nine Kolmogorov had completed a doctorate and produced a remarkable range of papers. That year he traveled with Pavel Aleksandrov, and their friendship developed into a long partnership in work and everyday life. Together they traveled through Russia and the Caucasus; Kolmogorov worked on probabilistic processes while Aleksandrov worked on topology. They later maintained a home at Komarovka outside Moscow, where visitors and students gathered for conversation, meals and walks. Such details matter because great research groups are sustained through informal hospitality as well as through published papers and lectures.

In nineteen thirty-one Kolmogorov became a professor at Moscow State University. International mathematical exchange had not yet been severed by the isolation of later Stalinist policy. He traveled in Europe, discussed probability with Paul Lévy and Maurice Fréchet, and learned from German and French mathematicians. The Soviet mathematical establishment was developing considerable institutional strength, but it remained intellectually connected to work abroad. The growing political pressure that would shape the next decade should not be projected backward as if such contacts were always impossible.

His nineteen thirty-three book, *Grundbegriffe der Wahrscheinlichkeitsrechnung*, or *Foundations of the Theory of Probability*, was published in German. It is often compared with Euclid's effort to organize geometry from axioms. Earlier mathematicians—Bernoulli, Laplace, Borel, Lebesgue, von Mises and others—had all contributed to the theory and philosophy of chance. Kolmogorov's importance lay in a particularly effective synthesis: define probability on a collection of events as a measure, with a small number of exact rules, and then derive theorems about random variables, independence and conditional probability.

In the simplest formulation, an event is a set of possible outcomes. The probability of the whole outcome space is one, probabilities are nonnegative, and the probabilities of countably many mutually exclusive events add to the probability of their union. That last rule extends ordinary finite addition to a carefully controlled infinite setting. It gives probability a place inside measure theory, a subject already developed by Borel and Lebesgue. It also makes room for continuous random variables, infinite sequences of trials and processes evolving through time. The formalism does not tell us how often a real event will happen merely from its name. Connecting a model to observations remains a scientific task, and Kolmogorov himself continued to explore philosophical and empirical interpretations of probability.

That mathematical success left a philosophical question unresolved. An abstract probability model can assign one half to an event because its axioms and parameters say so. A scientist still needs a reason to choose those parameters for an actual experiment. Is probability a long-run frequency, a measure of rational expectation, or an idealization connected to repeated trials by other principles? Kolmogorov's later writings returned to these questions rather than assuming the axioms were a complete philosophy. A logically consistent model is not automatically a well-calibrated account of the world, a limitation we still face when fitting probability models to climate, medicine, finance, or machine learning.

A familiar illustration is repeated coin tossing. One coin toss has two possible outcomes; indefinitely many tosses require an infinite sequence. A mathematical model should allow us to discuss events such as the long-run fraction of heads approaching one half. But one cannot simply add the probabilities of infinitely many arbitrarily overlapping events as though the problem were finite. Kolmogorov's axiomatic structure states which combinations are allowed and how the model behaves under countable operations. It makes possible a large body of rigorous probability while leaving open whether a particular physical coin is fairly modeled by an independent sequence.

Probability was not his only form of synthesis. Earlier papers concerned Markov processes, in which the conditional law of the future depends on an appropriate present state rather than the full past history. His work led to equations connecting the evolution of a probability distribution to time. A particle undergoing irregular motion can be modeled probabilistically even when the laws of its microscopic movement are described differently. Such models became important for diffusion and statistical physics. The division between pure mathematics and applications was less sharp in Kolmogorov's work than many disciplinary labels would suggest.

The political environment grew dangerous. In nineteen thirty Dmitri Egorov was attacked and arrested; he died in exile. In nineteen thirty-six Nikolai Luzin, Kolmogorov's former teacher and an important builder of the Moscow school, was the target of a public campaign in the Soviet press and an investigation by the Academy of Sciences. Scholars were pressed to denounce him for claims involving publication abroad, professional conduct and alleged political disloyalty. Kolmogorov's participation cannot be responsibly erased, but neither should it be reduced to a phrase claiming that he alone engineered the attack. Published historical studies describe his remarks at a university meeting in which he criticized Luzin within the coercive setting while also acknowledging his considerable mathematical services. Different participants took different positions, and the Soviet political leadership ultimately shaped the result.

The episode changed the scientific community. Publishing abroad became harder, and professional disputes acquired threatening political force. A career in Soviet mathematics could be brilliant and still have constraints that were not self-imposed. Kolmogorov held important positions within state-supported institutions, benefited from their resources and became an influential member of their leadership. The existence of coercion does not erase individual agency; the existence of individual agency does not erase coercion. The historical record is best served by distinguishing documented actions from speculation about private motives that cannot be recovered.

The documentary record of the Luzin affair is also a warning about how mathematical memoirs can be read. Some participants wrote recollections long after the events, when political conditions and their own reputations had changed. Contemporary press denunciations are primary evidence that an accusation was made, not independent proof that it was true. A later scholarly reconstruction must compare commission transcripts, institutional records and competing recollections. Kolmogorov was not outside these events as an innocent observer, but it is an error to give him a private motive that the records do not reveal. The larger event shows how a state campaign could turn academic grievances into a threat with consequences beyond any particular technical dispute.

During the late nineteen thirties Kolmogorov helped develop research at the Steklov Mathematical Institute while maintaining his university work. He directed probability and statistics activity and trained students including Boris Gnedenko. He and Aleksandrov hosted scholars at Komarovka, with research discussions continuing across fields. During the Second World War he contributed mathematical work connected with wartime science. His nineteen forty-one papers on turbulence became influential in describing the distribution of energy across scales in a flowing fluid. That theory has assumptions and limitations that modern fluid mechanics continues to examine, but it marked a profound use of probabilistic reasoning to understand a physical process.

Picture turbulent water moving past a rock. Large eddies can break into smaller structures, and those in turn into smaller ones. Kolmogorov analyzed statistical regularities in this transfer of energy, aiming to explain why some patterns recur across very different turbulent flows. The subject was not the exact path of every droplet. It was the relationship among scales under assumptions of sufficiently developed and approximately homogeneous, isotropic turbulence. Modern textbooks sometimes present a power law associated with his name as if it described every chaotic flow. It does not. Its importance lies in providing a tractable theory with conditions that experiments and simulations can test.

In the nineteen fifties he returned to questions about motion in another setting. Classical mechanics had long asked whether small changes in forces destroy the regular motion predicted for an ideal system. Kolmogorov presented a method for showing the persistence of many invariant structures under sufficiently small perturbations and appropriate nonresonance assumptions. Vladimir Arnold and Jürgen Moser later developed major parts of the theory. The resulting name, KAM, represents a line of work by several mathematicians, not a theorem completed by its first namesake in isolation. It connects precise analysis with an old question about the stability of planetary motion.

Kolmogorov also contributed to topology and to the representation of continuous functions. In the late nineteen fifties he showed how a function of many variables can, under the right formulation, be represented using continuous functions of fewer variables. That result overturned a conjectured obstruction associated with Hilbert's thirteenth problem, while later work by Arnold completed related formulations. As with his probability book, Kolmogorov's strength was often to rethink the language in which a question had been posed. A problem that seemed impossible under one picture could change after the correct abstraction was identified.

A further direction led toward what is now called algorithmic information theory. Ordinary probability often asks how surprising an outcome is under a specified model. Kolmogorov considered how much information is needed to describe an individual finite object, measuring descriptive complexity through the length of a program that produces it on a fixed universal computational model. A highly repetitive string may have a short description; a patternless one may require something close to literal reproduction. Related ideas were developed independently by Ray Solomonoff and Gregory Chaitin. The familiar term Kolmogorov complexity recognizes his contribution but should not erase theirs, or confuse algorithmic complexity with Shannon's earlier statistical concept of entropy.

His interests outside mathematical research remained active. He studied rhythm and structure in Russian poetry, including Pushkin, and was deeply committed to education. In nineteen sixty-three a specialized mathematics and physics boarding school was founded in connection with Moscow State University through the efforts of Kolmogorov, Isaak Kikoin, university leadership and other scientists. It aimed to reach talented students, including children from regions far from major research centers. Kolmogorov himself helped with teaching, course materials and activities beyond mathematics. Literature, music, excursions and broader cultural knowledge were part of his view of education. This was not simply an assembly line for producing technical specialists.

His standing brought honors from academies around the world and from the Soviet state. Yet late-life reverence can simplify a complicated history just as hostile political readings can. Kolmogorov died in Moscow in October nineteen eighty-seven. His work had been transmitted through publications, students, conferences and educational institutions, some built under severe political conditions. Many of his contemporaries left memoirs, but memoirs are perspectives rather than transparent recordings of the past.

The most illuminating thread in his biography is a repeated movement between structures and concrete problems. As a young analyst he exposed limits of Fourier convergence; as a probabilist he gave random processes a common foundation; as a researcher in mechanics he studied turbulence and stability; as an educator he tried to build an environment in which curiosity could cross disciplinary boundaries. His life belongs inseparably to Soviet institutional history, including its achievements and its pressures. To remember Kolmogorov only as the author of three axioms is to miss both the scale of his mathematics and the particular human world in which it took shape.

## Historical companion (not narrated)

### Chronology and evidence cautions
- **1903–1925:** Born Tambov; raised by aunt Vera after his mother's death; study of history, science and mathematics in revolutionary Russia; entered Moscow University in 1920; early Fourier and set-theoretic results.
- **1925–1933:** Probability research with Khinchin; relationship with Luzin, Stepanov and the Moscow school; partnership with Pavel Aleksandrov; European travel; Moscow professorship and 1933 *Grundbegriffe*.
- **1930–1945:** Egorov's persecution, the 1936 Luzin affair, political and institutional pressures; Steklov appointments; turbulence and wartime scientific work.
- **1950–1987:** KAM foundations, Hilbert's thirteenth problem and algorithmic complexity; education and the specialized 1963 boarding school; death in Moscow in 1987.

**Attribution cautions:** Kolmogorov's axiomatization synthesized preceding probability work and does not solve the philosophical problem of empirical interpretation on its own. The 1936 Luzin affair involved coercive institutions and complex individual testimony; avoid assertions about unknowable motives. Arnold and Moser were substantial co-developers of KAM theory; Solomonoff and Chaitin had independent algorithmic-information contributions. The 1963 school had several institutional founders and was not built by Kolmogorov alone. His personal relationship with Aleksandrov is documented as a long partnership; speculation about undocumented private detail is unnecessary.

### Mathematical display companion
- A probability space is $(\Omega,\mathcal F,\mathbb P)$ with $\mathbb P(\Omega)=1$, $\mathbb P(A)\geq0$ and countable additivity for pairwise disjoint events.
- If independent fair Bernoulli variables $X_i$ take values 0 and 1, then $n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_i\to1/2$ almost surely under the strong law; the independence and distribution hypotheses matter.
- The classical 1941 Kolmogorov inertial-range spectrum is often written $E(k)\propto\varepsilon^{2/3}k^{-5/3}$ under high-Reynolds-number isotropic turbulence assumptions. It is not a universal law for all flows.
- In algorithmic information theory, a version of complexity is $K_U(x)=\min\{|p|:U(p)=x\}$; complexities for different universal machines agree up to an additive constant depending on the machines.
