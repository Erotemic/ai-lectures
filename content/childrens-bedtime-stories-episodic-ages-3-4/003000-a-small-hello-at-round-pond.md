# A Small Hello at Round Pond

On the morning after they visited Pebble Bridge, Amy opened her yellow map at the breakfast table.

The blue square for home was there.

The green apple tree was there.

The little bench that looked like a beetle was there.

And now there was a bridge with a smiling face, stretched over a blue wavy brook.

But beyond the bridge was a large empty space.

Amy placed one paw on it.

“Round Pond goes here,” she said.

Grandma May packed some apple slices in a small box. Ben arrived wearing his red scarf, which had somehow been tied into one enormous knot.

“I can still breathe,” he announced.

“I should hope so,” said Grandma, helping loosen the knot.

The three of them walked through the Village Green and over Pebble Bridge.

Thump. Tup. Tip. Tip.

The reeds gave a soft hmmm as they passed.

On the far side, a narrow path led through grass to Round Pond. The pond was not very big. A willow leaned over one edge, and pale stones lay in a ring near the shore.

Grandma May chose a place well back from the water for their picnic cloth.

Amy and Ben sat down to look.

In the water floated a small brown leaf.

It turned once.

Then it turned again.

Under the willow, a young hedgehog was sitting very still.

A violet pouch rested beside her feet.

Amy recognized the pouch.

“That's the person we saw yesterday,” she whispered.

Ben raised a paw to wave.

The hedgehog didn't wave back.

She was watching the little brown leaf.

“Maybe she doesn't want us here,” Amy whispered.

“Or maybe she hasn't seen us,” Grandma said. “We can say hello gently and give her room to answer.”

Amy waited until the hedgehog looked up.

“Hello,” she said.

The hedgehog nodded.

Then she held up one finger, asking them to wait.

Amy and Ben stayed where they were.

They watched the brown leaf with her.

A tiny beetle moved on the pond's surface near the leaf. Round rings spread outward, one after another.

The leaf rocked, turned, and floated slowly toward the willow.

When the rings faded, the hedgehog came over to their picnic cloth.

“I'm Kim,” she said. “I didn't want to miss the rings.”

“I'm Amy. And this is Ben.”

Ben looked at the water.

“You didn't miss them,” he said. “They were very round.”

Kim opened her violet pouch. Inside were three smooth pebbles, each a different shape. One was long. One was flat. One was almost a perfect circle.

“I keep the round one for looking at,” she explained.

Ben leaned closer, but he kept his paws to himself.

“May I see it?”

Kim put the pebble on the cloth between them.

The pebble was cool and gray. A white stripe curled around it like a sleepy worm.

Amy looked from the pebble to the pond.

“Everything here is round,” she said. “The pond. The rings. Your pebble.”

“Not everything,” Ben said.

He pointed to his red scarf.

Kim considered the scarf.

“That is an extremely long shape,” she agreed.

Ben tried to fold it into a circle. It made a lumpy red heap instead.

All three laughed.

Amy took out her yellow map.

“Could you help me draw the pond?” she asked Kim.

Kim looked at the empty space beyond the bridge.

“It needs a willow tree,” she said.

Amy drew a blue circle and a green willow bending over it. She added one tiny brown leaf inside the circle.

Then she made three rings around the leaf.

“Now we'll remember,” Amy said.

They sat together for a while. Nobody needed to think of something clever to say.

Ben ate an apple slice. Amy ate another. Kim chose to have one after she had finished looking at the clouds reflected in the pond.

When the sun moved behind the willow, Grandma May said it was time to go home.

Amy told Kim about the Little-Light Walk at the end of the week.

“The neighbors will take lanterns along the paths and up Bell Hill. Would you like to come?”

Kim rubbed the side of her violet pouch.

“Maybe,” she said. “I like little walks. I don't like being in the middle of a big crowd.”

“We can walk by the edge,” Ben offered.

“And you can decide when we get there,” Amy said.

Kim smiled. “I'd like that.”

She packed the three pebbles back into her pouch, beginning with the round one.

On the walk home, Amy held her map open just enough to see the new blue pond. The bridge led to it, just as the real bridge did.

At home, she put the map beside her bed.

The drawn pond was only a small circle of blue.

But when she thought of it, she could remember the willow, the brown leaf, three slow rings, and a new friend who had needed a moment to finish looking.

Outside her window, the apple tree stood still.

And the valley had grown a little larger without growing any louder at all.

## For parents and caregivers — not spoken

**What carries forward:** The previously glimpsed figure is Kim, a young hedgehog with a violet pouch holding three pebbles, including a round one with a white stripe. Kim lives near Round Pond on the far side of Pebble Bridge and likes observing small details. She has not promised to join the Little-Light Walk; she may choose where or whether to participate. Amy's map now shows Round Pond, willow, leaf, and rings. Grandma May accompanies children near the water.

**Why this episode exists:** A first meeting is allowed to unfold at different speeds. Kim is not portrayed as deficient for preferring quiet or taking time before answering. Her interest in ripples brings the group together; it is also simply an enjoyable moment. The story does not teach that every silence has a friendly explanation—only that more than one explanation is possible.

**Optional prompt:** “What was Kim looking at before she said hello?” Avoid using her quietness as a problem for the child to diagnose.

**References and limits:** [AAP, *Social Development in Preschoolers* (2024)](https://www.healthychildren.org/English/ages-stages/preschool/Pages/Social-Development-In-Preschoolers.aspx), particularly friendship and cooperation, provides background on emerging social relationships and adult scaffolding. [AAP, *How to Share Books with Children 2 and 3 Years Old*](https://www.healthychildren.org/English/ages-stages/toddler/Pages/How-to-Share-Books-with-2-and-3-Year-Olds.aspx) provides context for optional conversation. Neither source asserts that this fictional encounter teaches empathy, improves sociability, or makes silence safe.

**Single-voice production:** Don't assign Kim a different voice. Her pacing and observation are clear from the words alone.
