# The Path Past Round Pond

A few days after the picnic beyond Bell Hill, Amy took her yellow map out of the drawer beside her bed.

The map was getting wonderfully busy.

There was the blue-door cottage, where Amy lived with Grandma May. There was the Village Green, with the Story House and its squeaky door. There was Pebble Bridge, Round Pond, and the orchard with the old mill.

And far to the west, beyond Bell Hill, was Wendy's home in Far Meadow.

But one edge of the paper was still mostly empty.

It was the edge beyond Round Pond.

Grandma May looked at it over her morning cup of tea.

"Kim knows that side of the valley," she said. "Perhaps she'd like to show us."

By the time Ben arrived, Grandma May had found a little blue notebook with six clean pages.

"You may use this for your walks, if you'd like," she told him.

Ben turned it over.

"What should I put in it?"

"Anything you want to remember."

Ben considered this.

"Could I put a noise in it?"

Grandma May smiled. "You can try."

So Ben tucked the notebook beside his red scarf, and the three of them set out.

They walked across Village Green. Ben waved to his tilted orange lantern, still hanging by the Story House window.

At Pebble Bridge, his feet made two different sounds on two different boards.

Tok. Tuk.

"That is a good noise," he decided.

"You can save it for another page," Grandma May said.

On the far side of the bridge, they followed the path to Round Pond. Kim was waiting by the willow with her violet pouch tucked against her side.

Inside were her three favorite pebbles, including the round gray one with a white stripe.

Amy showed her the blank part of the map.

"Do you know what goes here?"

Kim looked to the east, where a path curved behind the willow.

"There's a little meadow past the pond," she said. "The grass there is taller than my ears."

Ben touched his own ears.

"Even taller than mine?"

"Not all the grass," Kim said. "Some of it is quite ordinary."

Grandma May came along as they followed the dry, winding path. It kept well back from the pond's edge.

First they passed a fallen branch that looked like a sleeping crocodile.

Then a patch of clover that looked like a green cushion.

Then the path curved, and the children could see a meadow filled with slender, waving reeds.

"Reed Meadow," Kim said.

The reeds grew around a low, wet hollow. The walking path stayed on a little rise of dry grass above it.

When the breeze came through, the leaves brushed together.

Shhhhh.

Then the breeze stopped.

The reeds stood nearly still.

Then another little breeze came.

Shhhhh.

Ben took the blue notebook from his bag.

"I think I found a noise for the first page."

He tried drawing the noise.

First he drew a circle.

"That looks like a bun," Amy said.

Ben added three long lines beside it.

"Now it looks like a bun running away," said Kim.

Ben laughed. He turned to a fresh corner of the page and drew a handful of tall, thin reeds.

Grandma May wrote one small word underneath, just as Ben asked her to.

Shhh.

Amy unfolded her yellow map beside the notebook.

She drew the path east of Round Pond. She drew the bend beside the reeds. She drew a tiny green curve for the dry grassy rise.

"Now I know where we are," she said.

"We're here," said Ben, pointing at his shoes.

"Yes," said Amy. "And now the map knows too."

They sat on the grass for a while. Nobody needed to follow the breeze or hurry it along.

Kim watched one reed lean and straighten. Ben watched another one lean the other way. Grandma May watched the children.

On their walk home, Ben opened the blue notebook once more.

The page was still flat and quiet. No breeze came out of it.

But when he looked at the thin lines, he could remember the soft shushing sound.

"I have made a book that remembers a noise," he said.

That evening, Amy folded her map beside her bed. The eastern edge now held a path and a crescent of reeds.

At his own home, Ben put his blue notebook on a shelf by his pillow.

The first page said shhh.

The other five pages were waiting, but they were in no hurry at all.

Outside, a little breeze moved through the trees.

And Willow Bend grew quiet for the night.

## For parents and caregivers - not spoken

**What carries forward:** Reed Meadow and its dry grassy path are discovered east of Round Pond and drawn on Amy’s yellow map; Grandma May lends Ben a small blue six-page notebook; the first page depicts shushing reeds; book goes home with Ben.

**Why this episode exists:** Kim gets to know a place on her own side of the valley and guide her friends. The notebook captures a sound by picture rather than pretending paper contains recorded audio. This is a playful narrative device, not a tested developmental activity. This is original fiction; no developmental or sleep outcome from listening is claimed.

**Optional prompt:** "What would you draw to remember a soft sound?" Optional, especially at bedtime.

**References and evidence limits:** [U.S. National Park Service, Natural Sounds and Night Skies Division, *Education Resources — Natural Sounds*](https://www.nps.gov/subjects/sound/education-resources.htm); [American Academy of Pediatrics (HealthyChildren.org), *How to Share Books with Children 2 and 3 Years Old*](https://www.healthychildren.org/English/ages-stages/toddler/Pages/How-to-Share-Books-with-2-and-3-Year-Olds.aspx). Sources supply general guidance for play, reading or listening, and where applicable a physical or natural-history point; they do not evaluate this story or establish that it improves the listener's development or sleep.
