# Bo and the Humming Bridge

The next morning in Willow Bend, a bear cub with a red scarf waited outside a cottage with a blue door.

The bear cub was Bo.

He had come to see his friend Tess, who was carrying a folded yellow map.

“Does it have the bridge yet?” Bo asked.

“Not yet,” Tess said. “Today it will.”

Grandma Fern came out with a small basket of water cups and a cloth for sitting on the grass. She was going with them to the brook.

Together they passed the apple tree, walked through the Village Green, and stopped beside the little stone fountain.

Bo looked down at Tess's map.

There was the crooked apple tree. There was the bench that looked like a long beetle. There was the Story House, with a little curl drawn beside its squeaky door.

“There is the world we know,” Bo said.

“Some of it,” Tess answered.

Then they followed the path toward the creek.

Pebble Bridge was made of wooden boards, with a sturdy rail on either side. Smooth stones sat in the shallow water below, and tall reeds grew along the bank.

Bo stepped onto the first board.

Thump.

He stepped onto the second board.

Tup.

He stepped onto the third.

Thump, tup.

Bo stopped.

“Grandma Fern,” he said, “I think the bridge is talking.”

Grandma put a paw on the rail.

“Let's hear what it says.”

Bo took two careful steps.

Thump. Tup.

Tess took two smaller steps.

Tip. Tip.

Bo looked down at her feet.

“Your feet tell the bridge little things,” he said. “My feet tell it big things.”

Tess tried one extra-soft step.

The board hardly made a sound.

She tried one more.

Tip.

Soon they were taking turns, making footsteps as carefully as if they were playing a game with invisible drums.

Grandma Fern stayed close beside them.

Halfway across, a little breeze stirred the reeds below.

Hmmm.

Bo froze, his scarf pressed against his chest.

“That wasn't my foot.”

Tess stood still, too.

The breeze came again.

Hmmm.

The reeds swayed along the water's edge. Somewhere beneath the bridge, the brook slipped around the pebbles with a soft burbling sound.

“It sounds like someone thinking,” Bo whispered.

“Perhaps the reeds are thinking about supper,” said Grandma Fern.

“Reeds don't eat supper,” Tess said.

Grandma smiled. “Then perhaps it's the wind passing through them.”

They listened a little longer.

The breeze quieted. So did the humming.

Bo gave one last careful step.

Tup.

Then everyone crossed to the grassy bank on the other side.

Grandma spread the cloth where they could sit safely back from the water. She offered a cup to each of them.

Tess unfolded the map.

She drew two short brown lines for the bridge rails, then several little marks for its boards.

Under them she drew a blue wavy line for the brook.

Bo wanted the bridge to have a mouth.

“That's where the hmmm comes out,” he said.

Tess drew a small curved line at one end.

It looked like a smile.

Bo studied the map.

“Good,” he said. “It is a very polite bridge.”

On the far side of the brook, a path curved past tall grass toward Round Pond. Tess could just see the water through the reeds.

Near the path was a small figure carrying a violet pouch.

The figure bent down to look at something in the grass, then went on toward the pond.

“Who's that?” Tess asked.

“Someone who lives over here, I think,” said Grandma Fern. “We may meet another day.”

Bo looked past the bridge to the pond, and then back at the path toward the Village Green.

“There's more room on the map,” he said.

“There is,” said Tess.

They crossed back together, listening to the boards again.

Thump. Tup. Tip. Tip.

At the edge of the Green, Bo tried to make the same sounds with two fallen leaves.

He stepped on one leaf.

Crish.

He stepped on the other.

Crish.

“Leaves are not very good bridges,” he decided.

“But they are very good leaves,” Tess said.

That evening, Tess showed Grandma Fern the new drawing.

The brown bridge stretched over its blue wavy brook. At one end she had put a little smile, because Bo insisted that a bridge that said hmmm ought to look friendly.

She set the yellow map beside her bed.

The next blank space waited quietly beyond the brook.

And as Tess closed her eyes, she remembered the bridge's two voices.

The footsteps, saying thump and tip.

And the reeds, answering with a little hmmm.

## For parents and caregivers — not spoken

**What carries forward:** Pebble Bridge lies east of Village Green, crosses the creek, and leads toward Round Pond. Its wooden boards make different footstep sounds; breezy reeds near the bank make the soft “hmmm.” A figure with a violet pouch is glimpsed on the far side; episode three reveals this is Nori. Tess adds the bridge, brook, and a tiny smile to her map. Grandma Fern accompanies the children near water.

**Why this episode exists:** The repeated footsteps invite anticipation and auditory noticing without requiring a science lesson. The mystery resolves with a plausible cause; the bridge remains a playful metaphor rather than an animal with speech. The visit extends a world the child already knows.

**Optional prompt:** “If you walked over a bridge, what sound might your shoes make?” A sound imitation is enough; there is no test to pass.

**References and limits:** [CDC, *Milestones by 3 Years*](https://www.cdc.gov/act-early/milestones/3-years.html) mentions children discussing what is happening in pictures and asking who/what/where/why questions; these are broad developmental reference points, not requirements of story comprehension. [NAEYC, Janis Strasser, *Asking Questions to Stretch Your Child's Thinking* (2024)](https://www.naeyc.org/node/5321) discusses optional open-ended questions in shared experiences. Neither source documents this imagined bridge, its particular sounds, or any tested effects of the narration.

**Single-voice production:** Footstep words are brief rhythmic cues for one narrator, not distinct voice roles or an effects requirement.
