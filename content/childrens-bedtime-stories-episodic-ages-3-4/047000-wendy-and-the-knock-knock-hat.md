# Wendy and the Knock-Knock Hat

The wind was feeling lively in Far Meadow.

It shook the leaves on the sideways branch of Windbreak Tree and made the grass lean first one way, then the other.

Wendy came out of her cream-colored cottage wearing her straw hat with the blue ribbon.

Aunt Kay stood at the door.

"Hold on to your hat across the hill," she said.

Wendy held it with one hand.

The blue ribbon flapped against the brim.

Tap, tap.

"My hat is knocking," Wendy said.

"What would it like?" Aunt Kay asked.

"To come inside and have a biscuit."

"It can have one when we get home."

"Did you hear that, hat?"

They laughed and started toward Bell Hill. The wind grew calmer as they passed the halfway resting bench and walked down toward Blue Door Cottage.

Wendy and Aunt Kay were going to visit the Story House. Amy and Grandma May had invited them to look at the little garden, which now had leaves big enough to cast small shadows on the soil.

When they reached Village Green, Ben was sitting on the long bench with his red scarf folded neatly around his neck.

"The door is quiet now," Ben told Wendy. "I made a welcome committee, but it retired."

"Did it get tired?"

"Very. One member was a pebble."

Wendy considered this. "Pebbles are good at sitting."

They walked to the Story House together.

Amy opened the door with Grandma May beside her.

It swung without its old squeak.

Wendy was about to step inside when a small puff of wind reached across the Green.

Her hat ribbon swung forward.

Tap, tap.

It touched the wooden doorframe twice.

Ben froze.

"The door has learned to knock!"

"Not quite," Amy said.

Wendy tilted her head. The blue ribbon swung once more.

Tap.

"My hat has been practicing," she said.

Ben looked closely at the hat.

"Does it knock at your house too?"

"Yes. It keeps asking for biscuits."

Grandma May welcomed them inside. Wendy set her hat on the bench beside her, with the ribbon safely out of the doorway.

Ben peered at the quiet door.

"You may have to work harder," he whispered to it. "The hats are catching up."

They spent the afternoon looking at the garden. Some of the radish leaves were broad and green, and the sunflower plants were still growing. Amy pointed out a new leaf that hadn't been there when she visited the day before.

Wendy pretended her hat was the mayor of the garden. It had come to inspect the leaves.

"Very leafy," she announced, speaking in her own normal voice. "Excellent work."

Ben gave it a wibble-wave with his paw.

A tiny beetle crossed the soil, paying no attention to the mayor.

"A difficult audience," Wendy said.

As the afternoon softened, Aunt Kay said it was time to go back over Bell Hill. Wendy put on her hat and checked that its ribbon was still tied in place.

At the Story House door she waited for the wind.

Nothing happened.

"The hat is off duty," said Amy.

"A well-earned rest," Ben agreed.

Wendy waved goodbye and walked west with Aunt Kay, past Blue Door Cottage and over the familiar hill toward Far Meadow.

The blue ribbon moved gently against the brim, but it didn't knock again.

At home, Wendy hung her hat near the window. The old orange-blue-violet picnic banner was still inside the cottage, where Aunt Kay had kept it after their picnic.

The hat was home.

And if it wanted a biscuit tomorrow, it knew exactly where to ask.

## For parents and caregivers - not spoken

**What carries forward:** Wendy's ribbon briefly taps the doorframe in a puff of wind; the door has not developed a knocking sound. She keeps her blue-ribboned hat and returns with Aunt Kay to Far Meadow by the established route. Ben and Amy know the knock-knock-hat joke; garden has additional leaves, no harvest.

**Why this episode exists:** The wind creates a momentary funny false clue about the repaired door and gives Wendy a scene led by her established hat without turning the visit into a lesson or an event.

**Optional prompt:** "Was it the door or Wendy's hat that tapped?" Optional; do not interrupt a child settling to sleep.

**References and evidence limits:** [The Power of Play: A Pediatric Role in Enhancing Development in Young Children](https://doi.org/10.1542/peds.2018-2058); [Message in a Backpack: Asking Questions to Stretch Your Child's Thinking](https://www.naeyc.org/node/5321). These provide broader editorial or caregiver context, not evidence of benefits caused by these fictional episodes.

**Single-voice production:** Narration only between the spoken markers. No character voices, effects or music are required.
