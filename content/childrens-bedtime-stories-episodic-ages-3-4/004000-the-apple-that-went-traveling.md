# The Apple That Went Traveling

In Willow Bend, the morning after Tess met Nori at Round Pond, three friends gathered beneath an apple tree.

Tess was a rabbit with a folded yellow map.

Bo was a bear cub with a red scarf.

Nori was a hedgehog with a violet pouch and three favorite pebbles.

Grandma Fern had met Nori at Pebble Bridge and brought her across to spend the afternoon with the others.

Today, they were going to the orchard.

The orchard lay beyond the Village Green, down a gentle grassy slope. Its trees stood in neat, crooked rows. Some were tall. Some were short. One had two trunks that rose together like a pair of giant green trousers.

Bo stopped beneath that tree.

“I would like trousers with leaves on them,” he said.

“I think the tree would like trousers without leaves,” Tess replied.

Nori looked at the two trunks.

“I think the tree already has excellent trousers.”

Grandma Fern was carrying a basket of apples from the orchard keeper, who had left them by the gate.

One apple was particularly round.

It was red on one side, yellow on the other, and had a short stem that stuck straight up.

Bo admired it.

“That apple looks as if it has somewhere to be.”

Grandma set the basket on a flat stone so she could spread their sitting cloth.

Just then, a breeze lifted the edge of the cloth.

Grandma caught it with one paw.

Bo helped with the other corner.

And the roundest apple wobbled to the top of the basket.

Wobble.

Wobble.

Plop.

The apple rolled out and down the grassy path.

“Oh,” said Bo.

The apple rolled past the tree with the trousers.

It rolled around a tuft of daisies.

It rolled between two smooth stones, turning yellow side, red side, yellow side.

Bo began to run after it.

“Walking feet,” Grandma called gently. “We'll follow together.”

So the three friends walked down the path with Grandma Fern.

They could see the apple just ahead, where it had bumped against a little hill of leaves.

Bo bent to pick it up.

But the apple moved again.

A leaf slipped beneath it, and off it went, rolling around a bend.

“I was nearly there,” Bo said.

“You were,” Tess agreed.

They followed the bend.

The path led beside the old mill, a small stone building near the creek. Its waterwheel turned slowly behind a wooden fence.

The friends stayed on the path with Grandma Fern.

The apple had disappeared.

Bo looked under his red scarf.

It wasn't there.

Tess checked beside the path's big stepping stone.

Not there either.

Nori opened her violet pouch, then shut it again.

“Too big for my pouch,” she said. “Good thing I didn't catch it.”

They stood still and looked around.

At first there were only stones, grass, a mill wall, and a pile of soft brown leaves.

Then Tess noticed a small gap in the leaves.

It was exactly as wide as an apple.

“Something rolled through there.”

Nori bent beside the path and looked carefully.

She saw one red spot under the lowest leaf.

“There,” she said.

Grandma Fern lifted the leaf.

Underneath sat the apple, held in place by a root.

It was red side up, as if it had been waiting politely for everyone to arrive.

Bo put his paws on his hips.

“You have had a very long holiday,” he told the apple.

The apple did not answer.

“That is probably because it is tired,” Tess said.

Grandma Fern picked it up and placed it safely back in the basket.

When they returned to the orchard, the friends sat on the cloth and ate the apple slices Grandma had packed earlier. The traveling apple stayed in the basket; Grandma would wash it at home.

Tess opened her yellow map.

On it were the blue cottage, the Village Green, the humming bridge, and Round Pond with its willow.

She drew a cluster of green circles below the Green.

“The orchard,” she said.

Then she drew a little square beside a blue wavy line.

“The old mill.”

Bo insisted on adding a small red dot near the mill.

“That is where the apple had its holiday.”

Nori drew a tiny brown leaf over the dot.

“That is where the holiday ended,” she said.

Grandma Fern turned the map so they could see how the paths joined together.

“Your map is beginning to tell a story,” she said.

Tess studied it.

The blue cottage led to the Green. The Green led to the bridge and pond. A different path led down through the orchard toward the old mill.

The world had paths going this way and that way.

And one apple, for a short while, had taken a path all its own.

That evening, Grandma Fern washed the traveling apple. She cut it into slices for tomorrow's lantern preparations.

Tess placed the folded map beside her bed.

Bo went home with his red scarf.

Nori went back across Pebble Bridge with Grandma Fern, her three pebbles safe in her violet pouch.

Outside the blue door, the apple tree stood quiet.

None of its apples seemed to be planning a trip.

Not tonight, anyway.

## For parents and caregivers — not spoken

**What carries forward:** Nori can now visit Tess and Bo across Pebble Bridge while Grandma Fern accompanies them. The orchard is south of Village Green, with an old stone mill farther along the path near the creek; the waterwheel is behind a fence. A red dot and brown leaf on the map remember the traveling apple. Grandma washed the recovered apple for later use. The next story begins with lantern preparations; earlier promises should not be forgotten.

**Why this episode exists:** Mostly comedy and a small pursuit that ends safely. The three children contribute different observations to locate the apple. That cooperation is shown rather than praised as a moral. The new path makes the world branch outward instead of feeling like a single line of places.

**Optional prompt:** “If you were drawing the apple on Tess's map, where would you put it?” A pointing gesture or silence is fine.

**References and limits:** [NAEYC, Janis Strasser, *Asking Questions to Stretch Your Child's Thinking* (2024)](https://www.naeyc.org/node/5321), for optional open-ended questions during shared routines; [AAP, *How to Share Books with Children 2 and 3 Years Old*](https://www.healthychildren.org/English/ages-stages/toddler/Pages/How-to-Share-Books-with-2-and-3-Year-Olds.aspx), for following a young child's interests during a story. Neither evaluates this fictional apple chase or says a child must be able to map directions at age three.

**Single-voice production:** The red-side/yellow-side repetition carries the action without fast character voices; don't rush the quiet ending.
