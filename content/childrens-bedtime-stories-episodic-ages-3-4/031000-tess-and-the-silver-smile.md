# Amy and the Silver Smile

The day Amy first noticed the silver smile in the sky, she had been looking at very small things.

She and Grandma May had gone behind the Story House to check the little garden.

There were green radish leaves in the front row, and two sunflower shoots in the back. The smallest corner still looked like plain dirt.

Ben was there too, wearing his red scarf.

"I think the sunflowers are taller than yesterday," he said.

Amy looked closely.

"Maybe a little," she said. "I didn't bring a ruler."

Ben spread his paws as wide as a door.

"I think they are THIS much taller."

"Then they would be taller than you," said Grandma May.

Ben considered that.

"I would have noticed."

The friends laughed, and the three of them went back to Village Green. Ben headed home with his grown-up, while Amy and Grandma followed the westward path to Blue Door Cottage.

By the time supper was finished, the window over Amy's bed had turned the color of a soft peach.

Amy put her folded yellow map in its usual place beside her bed. It still had the curved orchard path, the meadows on opposite sides of the valley, and the little green marks for the garden.

Then she saw something outside.

A thin bright curve hung in the sky.

"Grandma," Amy called, "there's a smile in the window."

Grandma May came to look.

"Not in the window," Amy said, peering closer. "Past the window. Up there."

It was the Moon.

But it wasn't a round Moon, like the one Amy sometimes drew with a circle.

It looked like a silver peel from an orange.

Or the edge of a tiny white boat.

Or, if Ben had been there, the last piece of a very small pancake.

"Did the Moon lose the rest of itself?" Amy asked.

"No," said Grandma May. "The Moon is still round. We can only see a little of its sunlit side from here tonight."

Amy thought about that.

"So it isn't a broken Moon."

"No."

"And it isn't a baby Moon?"

"No."

"And nobody took a bite?"

"Not even Ben."

Amy giggled. Ben could eat an enormous oat biscuit, but not a Moon.

She looked again.

The narrow bright curve was still there.

She tried making the same shape with her two fingers. Her fingers made a much wider curve.

Then she tried with the corner of her blanket.

That made a triangle.

She tried with the handle of her little cup.

That was closer, although cups were not normally kept in the sky.

"I could draw it on my map," she said.

Grandma May looked at the folded yellow paper beside the bed.

"Would it be a place on your map?"

Amy shook her head.

Her map showed where she could walk: Pebble Bridge, Round Pond, Far Meadow, Reed Meadow, and the orchard.

The Moon was not beside the old mill or behind Bell Hill.

"It can stay in the sky," Amy decided.

Grandma May drew the curtain back a little farther so the silver curve would be easy to see from the pillow.

Amy lay down.

Far away, one last bird called from the apple tree by the Blue Door Cottage.

The garden, down the lane behind the Story House, had stopped receiving visitors for the day.

The fountain in Village Green made its quiet splash.

The Moon stayed round, even where Amy could not see its dark part.

Amy did not need to solve the whole sky before sleeping.

She could look again on another evening.

"Goodnight, little silver smile," she whispered.

And with the map beside her bed and the curtain open just a little, she went to sleep.

## For parents and caregivers - not spoken

**What carries forward:** On an evening with a thin waxing crescent, Amy and Grandma observe the Moon from the Blue Door Cottage window. Amy understands the Moon is not physically broken. The yellow map stays by her bed without a Moon symbol; nothing new is stored at Story House. Ben was earlier told he could look at the sky too.

**Why this episode exists:** The evening image is a real waxing crescent. The gentle questions about a missing piece and a map are child-centered speculation; no Moon knowledge test is imposed. The story rests in a familiar bedroom.

**Optional prompt:** "What did Amy say the skinny Moon looked like?" Optional, especially at bedtime.
