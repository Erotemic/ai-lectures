# Sue and the Castle Bakery Door

At the Round Window Cottage, Sue had a bakery.

It stood beside the window, made from the sturdy boxes that Grandpa Ed had unpacked when they moved in. Its roof was packing paper. Its windows were drawn in crayon. On top flew a carrot-shaped flag.

The bakery was also a castle.

That was important, because a plain bakery could not take orders from a hungry mountain.

One rainy afternoon, Sue sat on her green cushion, the one with a yellow star, and studied her castle bakery.

"It needs a door," she said.

Grandpa Ed looked up from his chair. "It has a window."

"The customers have to come in somewhere."

"Of course," he said. "I hadn't thought of the customers."

Sue fetched a piece of folded cardboard from the box of leftover packing things. Grandpa Ed helped make a small flap that could open and close.

Sue decorated it with a red crayon.

She opened the flap.

Fffup.

She closed it.

Flup.

"I like that noise," Grandpa Ed said.

"Listen! It makes a different noise when I shut it," Sue replied.

Amy came by with Grandma May when the rain had slowed to a soft patter. They followed the familiar orchard path and stopped at the cottage before the fenced old mill.

Sue showed Amy the new bakery door.

"It makes a little noise," Sue said. "Our Story House door doesn't anymore."

Amy opened the cardboard flap.

Fffup.

She closed it.

Flup.

"That's quite a good noise," she said.

They decided to open the castle bakery for business.

The first customer was a wooden spoon from Grandpa Ed's kitchen drawer.

The spoon stood outside the little door.

It was much too tall to fit through.

"May I have a moon biscuit?" Sue asked in her ordinary voice, speaking for the spoon.

"The moon biscuits are sold out," Amy replied. "We have square ones."

"I am a round spoon. I only eat round biscuits."

"We could bake you a round one," said Amy.

The next customer was a folded napkin pretending to be a mountain.

That customer was even taller.

"This is a very small door," Sue admitted.

"Perhaps the big customers should order at the window," Amy suggested.

They drew a little sign that said WINDOW ORDERS. Grandma May wrote the words underneath Sue's crayon drawing of a biscuit.

Now the spoon could order at the window. The mountain could order at the window. Even an imaginary whale, who lived nowhere near Willow Bend, could order a very enormous biscuit at the window.

Sue opened the new door for a tiny paper beetle.

Fffup.

The beetle went inside.

At last, a customer who fit.

When playtime ended, Sue tucked the paper beetle into the bakery. The cardboard door stayed attached. The important wooden spoon went back to the kitchen drawer, and the sign stayed by the drawn window.

Amy and Grandma May walked home before the sky grew dark.

Sue put her yellow-star cushion under the round window and sat on it while Grandpa Ed made supper.

Outside, the orchard leaves moved gently after the rain.

The real cottage door closed with an ordinary click.

The castle bakery door waited in its place beside the window.

It had a fine little noise of its own, and it was just the right size for a paper beetle.

## For parents and caregivers - not spoken

**What carries forward:** With Grandpa Ed's help, Sue adds a small flap to the bakery door; it makes a soft fffup/flup sound. Sue and Amy draw a WINDOW ORDERS sign by the crayon window for pretend oversized customers. A paper beetle remains inside the bakery. The spoon goes back to the kitchen drawer, cushion remains Sue's, bakery remains by the round window.

**Why this episode exists:** Sue has her own comic action rather than remaining a perpetual new neighbor. The castle bakery changes physically in an earned way, and the door works for a small paper customer.

**Optional prompt:** "Why did the bakery need a window for big customers?" Optional; do not interrupt a child settling to sleep.

These provide broader editorial or caregiver context, not evidence of benefits caused by these fictional episodes.

**Single-voice production:** Narration only between the spoken markers. No character voices, effects or music are required.
