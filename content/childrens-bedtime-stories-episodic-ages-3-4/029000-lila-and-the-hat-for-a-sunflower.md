# Wendy and the Hat for a Sunflower

Wendy came over Bell Hill on a bright afternoon, wearing her familiar straw hat with its blue ribbon.

Aunt Kay walked beside her, carrying a small parcel of oat biscuits for the friends to share at the Story House.

On Village Green, they found Ben waiting by the fountain.

"The garden has grown three green hats," he announced.

"Three hats?" said Wendy.

"According to Todd. He drew them all."

Wendy adjusted the ribbon on her own hat.

"Then I'd better visit. Perhaps they need a hat shop."

Grandma May met them at the Story House door, which squeaked a familiar greeting.

They walked around to the little patch behind the building.

The radish row was already greener than it had been a few days before. The first leaves had opened, making each seedling look like a very small pair of ears.

The sunflower row had changed too.

Near the back, a new green shoot pushed above the soil.

Two folded leaves sat at its top, still close together.

Wendy gasped.

"Look. A sunflower baby."

"It doesn't look like a sunflower yet," Ben said.

"I wonder if a little hat would fit it," Wendy replied.

Ben was immediately interested.

He had once made a very large sign for this very small garden. He was good at making things too big.

"We could make a very tiny hat," he said.

Wendy held up the blue ribbon that had been tied around Aunt Kay's biscuit parcel. It was not the ribbon on her straw hat.

"This could be the brim."

The grown-ups set a little table on the dry path, away from the planted rows. Amy arrived from Blue Door Cottage carrying colored pencils and paper.

Wendy drew a blue hat with a tall, pointy top.

Ben drew a hat with twelve windows, so a sunflower could look out in every direction.

Amy drew an ordinary green leaf.

"That's a leaf, not a hat," Ben said.

"It could be a hat in Button Town," Amy answered.

Ben thought about their imaginary town beside the Doorway Tree.

"Then it might be the mayor's hat."

A little breeze stirred the paper on the table. The drawings slid together as if the hats were all trying to trade places.

Aunt Kay laid a smooth stone on the corner of the paper so it would stay put.

"Can we put one on the real shoot?" Wendy asked.

Grandma May shook her head gently.

"The little plant needs room and light. Let's keep our paper hats on the table."

That was all right with Wendy. She liked the paper hats too.

They tried one on Ben's thumb. It fell over.

They tried one on the handle of the empty watering can. It looked very important.

They tried the long blue hat on the garden sign.

It was exactly the right size.

"The sign is the mayor," said Ben.

"Of the garden?" asked Amy.

"Only of the paper hats," said Ben. "The plants can be in charge of growing."

Wendy liked that arrangement.

When they finished laughing, she went back to look at the little sunflower shoot.

She kept her hands beside her, not over the leaves.

The shoot was green. The earth was dark. Sunlight lay across the back row.

"It doesn't need a hat, does it?" she said.

"No," said Grandma.

Wendy stood there a little longer.

"Then I'll keep the hat for when we play."

The spare ribbon and paper hats went indoors with the other art supplies. The little green sign stayed outdoors, without a paper hat that could get wet.

At the Story House table, Aunt Kay divided the oat biscuits. Ben's biscuit was shaped like a very flat moon.

Wendy's was shaped like a hat with no top.

Amy's looked almost exactly like a biscuit.

"A rare and remarkable biscuit," said Ben.

The friends laughed softly and ate their snacks.

When it was time to go, Wendy and Aunt Kay took the familiar way west, up Bell Hill and toward Far Meadow. The orange, blue, and violet picnic banner was still at Wendy's cottage window, waiting for them at home.

The garden stayed beside the Story House, with radish leaves in the front and one sunflower shoot beginning to stand in the back.

That night, Wendy hung her straw hat on its usual peg.

She dreamed of a town where every hat had its own small hat, and every tiny hat had somewhere comfortable to rest.

The real sunflower did not wear any of them.

It had its own two little leaves.

## For parents and caregivers - not spoken

**What carries forward:** A few more days have passed. Radish cotyledons have opened; one sunflower shoot is visible in back. Wendy visits with Aunt Kay; blue ribbon on her hat remains intact. Children draw pretend plant hats on paper, adults keep paper off the seedling; the separate ribbon from the biscuit parcel and paper props return indoors. Garden sign stays bare; oat biscuits shared indoors.

**Why this episode exists:** Wendy’s paper hat is make-believe; the real seedling is left unobstructed. Science sources explain light for healthy seedlings; broad AAP play research informs a playful-prop editorial choice without measuring this story.

**Optional prompt:** "Where did the friends put the paper hats instead of on the sunflower?" Optional, especially at bedtime.
