# Todd and the Bread with a Hole

Todd had two yellow boots. One boot had a blue patch. The other did not.

He put them on after breakfast and stood beside Aunt Pat at the door of their home near Reed Meadow.

"What are we doing today?" he asked.

"We're going to Market Street," Aunt Pat said. "A bakery there makes bread in all sorts of shapes."

Todd looked down at his boots.

"Do they make boot-shaped bread?"

"We can look."

They followed the familiar dry path past Round Pond, crossed Pebble Bridge, and walked through Willow Bend. Then they went up Bell Hill and down the far side, until they reached the green bus beyond Far Meadow.

It was a long outing, so they stopped on the bench partway up Bell Hill for water. Todd was glad his boots did not have to do the climbing without him.

Ben and his grown-up met them at the bus stop. Ben's red scarf was tucked inside his coat, ready to behave like a good passenger.

When the bus doors went psssh, Todd gave a small jump.

"Is it sneezing?"

"It's whispering," Ben said. "A very large whisper."

The driver waited while the grown-ups found seats for everyone, and off they went.

At Market Street, a warm smell came through the bakery door. Behind the glass were round rolls, long loaves, flat bread, and small twisted rings.

Todd pressed his paws together.

"There's bread shaped like a circle."

"There's bread shaped like a pillow," said Ben.

"There's bread shaped like bread," said Aunt Pat.

Ben nodded. "The most surprising kind."

The baker smiled from behind the counter. The grown-ups chose some bread for their snack. Todd looked at the twisted rings, each with a hole in the middle.

"Why is there nothing in the middle?" he asked.

"The baker leaves a hole when making the ring," Aunt Pat said.

"Wouldn't it be better with more bread?"

"You can choose a loaf with no hole if you like."

Todd thought about that, but he chose a ring.

They took their wrapped bread to a clean table by the window. After washing up, Todd held the ring in both paws and looked through it.

He could see the shop awning.

He moved the ring a little.

He could see the big clock.

He moved it again.

There was Ben, wearing his red scarf and trying to look very important.

"Aha," said Todd. "I have found a bear."

Ben peered through his own bread ring.

"I have found a frog," he said. "A frog in yellow boots."

The two friends looked through the holes at one another.

"Do you think bread can be windows?" Todd asked.

"Only if you don't eat the window first," said Ben.

Todd took a bite.

The hole was no longer quite round.

He looked through it again.

"Now my window is crooked."

Ben took a bite of his own.

"Mine too."

Aunt Pat laughed. "That's what happens when your window is also your lunch."

Soon Todd's ring was nearly gone. He held up the last curved piece. It looked like a small smile.

"This is a very thin window," he said.

"You might still see one pigeon through it," Ben offered.

Todd lifted the little curve. A pigeon walked past outside just then, but not through the space between Todd's fingers.

"The pigeon's too quick," he said.

"Try looking at the clock. That won't run away," said Ben.

They finished their snack and walked back toward the bus stop with their grown-ups. On the way, Todd spotted a real shoe shop. There were boots in its window, but none was made of bread.

"The baker could try it," he said.

"What would you do with a bread boot?" asked Aunt Pat.

Todd thought hard.

"Eat the toes first."

Ben laughed so much his scarf slipped loose.

The bus carried them back through the fields. Ben and his grown-up went one way after the stop, while Todd and Aunt Pat took their long familiar route east, with another rest on Bell Hill.

By the time they reached Reed Meadow, the light was soft and the first stars had begun to appear.

Aunt Pat helped Todd take off his yellow boots.

Both boots were still boots, not bread. One still had its blue patch.

Todd was glad. Bread windows were delicious, but he was not at all sure about bread shoes.

## For parents and caregivers - not spoken

**What carries forward:** Todd and Aunt Pat travel west by the usual bridge, Village Green and Bell Hill to the green bus stop, taking rests; Ben and his grown-up accompany them on the bus. Todd and Ben eat ring-shaped bread at a clean bakery table and go home with their grown-ups. No actual bread shoes or bakery goods are left as permanent props. Todd now knows the bakery and the shop clock across the street.

**Why this episode exists:** A simple shape becomes a ridiculous pretend window, then lunch, then an imaginary shoe. The episode closes without instructions to use food as a tool or a forced lesson.

**Optional prompt:** "What happened to Todd’s bread window when he took a bite?" Optional; do not interrupt a child settling to sleep.

**References and evidence limits:** [The Power of Play: A Pediatric Role in Enhancing Development in Young Children](https://doi.org/10.1542/peds.2018-2058); [Message in a Backpack: Asking Questions to Stretch Your Child's Thinking](https://www.naeyc.org/node/5321). These give general reading, play or conversational context; they do not demonstrate benefits from listening to these stories.

**Single-voice production:** Narration only between the spoken markers. No character voices, effects or music are required.
