# Todd and the Lake the Size of a Teacup

The morning rain had ended.

At the Blue Door Cottage, Amy could see drops shining on the apple tree outside her window.

She had arranged a visit to the orchard with Ben, Kim, Grandma May, Todd, and Aunt Pat.

Todd lived far to the east at Reed Meadow. He had never visited the southern orchard before.

So he and Aunt Pat followed the familiar path past Round Pond, crossed Pebble Bridge, and came to Village Green.

Todd wore his yellow boots. One had a blue patch.

Kim was waiting with her violet pouch.

"I have a picture for you," she said.

She showed him the little card she had drawn at Button Town's pretend post office. It had three ripples and two yellow boots.

"Those are excellent boots," Todd said. "They look almost as important as mine."

"You can keep it," Kim told him.

Todd held the card carefully, and Aunt Pat tucked it into her bag so it would stay dry.

Then everyone walked south with Grandma May to the orchard.

The leaves had been washed by rain. The grass smelled fresh. The familiar two-trunk tree stood beside the path, looking exactly like a tree and, to the children, rather like a doorway.

"This is the Doorway Tree," Amy explained. "And we pretend there's a place called Button Town."

Todd looked at the two trunks.

"Does it have any lakes?"

"Not yet," said Ben.

Just then Kim noticed a small puddle in a shallow dip of earth, beyond the dry edge of the path.

It was hardly bigger than Grandma May's teacup saucer.

The sky was reflected in its surface.

Todd peered from the grass.

"That," he said, "is the finest little lake I have ever seen."

"It's a puddle," said Amy.

"It's a puddle in the orchard," said Kim.

"And a lake in Button Town," said Ben.

They liked being able to call it both.

Ben pretended to launch a boat on the imaginary lake.

"My boat is made of a leaf," he said. "It has a pillow for the captain."

"Who is the captain?" asked Amy.

"A very sleepy beetle."

Todd pretended to be the person who watched the boats from shore.

"All boats must go slowly," he declared. "Our lake is very small."

"How slowly?" Kim asked.

Todd made one tiny step in his yellow boots.

"Like this."

Then one more.

"And this."

Ben took an equally small step.

Then Amy.

Then Kim.

Aunt Pat watched four friends walking very slowly along the dry grass, as if they were escorting a boat no one could see.

"That's an excellent speed for a small lake," she said.

The puddle didn't move much. The sky stayed reflected on it, with one little cloud passing across.

"Does Button Town have a lighthouse?" Todd wondered.

Ben considered the big tree.

"A lighthouse for such a tiny lake would need to be very tiny."

Amy pointed to a flat fallen leaf beside the path.

"We can imagine a lighthouse shaped like that."

They did.

In their game, the pretend lighthouse had a yellow roof and a blue door. Its light was only bright enough to show one very small boat the way home.

There was no actual lighthouse in the grass.

When it was time to leave, Amy took a little piece of paper from Grandma May's bag. She drew a tiny blue circle and wrote the name with Grandma May's help.

Lake Teacup.

"For the pretend map," she said.

"We have a map?" Todd asked.

"Not a finished one," Amy admitted. "But we're making one."

She folded the paper and kept it with the other Button Town pictures.

The friends returned to Village Green. Todd and Aunt Pat walked east again toward their home at Reed Meadow.

At the blue-door cottage, Amy put the Lake Teacup picture beside the brown envelope from the pretend post office.

Outside, the little puddle rested under the afternoon sky.

It might be gone when the ground dried.

But the lake in their game could be there whenever they wanted to imagine it.

And that night, Todd's yellow boots stood side by side by his bed, one with its familiar blue patch.

## For parents and caregivers - not spoken

**What carries forward:** Todd visits southern orchard with Aunt Pat and receives Kim’s picture, which Aunt Pat carries home. The friends name an ordinary shallow rain puddle Lake Teacup in their game; a separate blue-circle drawing stays at Blue Door Cottage for the future pretend map.

**Why this episode exists:** A small puddle has two labels: what it literally is and what it represents in shared play. The distinction is narrated, not claimed as a child-learning result. Children observe from a dry path with adults nearby; no water-play instructions. It is original fiction; no developmental or sleep outcome from listening is claimed.

**Optional prompt:** "Was Lake Teacup a big real lake, a little puddle, or both in different ways?" Optional, especially at bedtime.

**References and evidence limits:** [Michael Yogman, Andrew Garner, Jeffrey Hutchinson, Kathy Hirsh-Pasek, Roberta Michnick Golinkoff; American Academy of Pediatrics, *The Power of Play: A Pediatric Role in Enhancing Development in Young Children*](https://doi.org/10.1542/peds.2018-2058); [Center on the Developing Child at Harvard University, *Activities Guide: Enhancing and Practicing Executive Function Skills with Children from Infancy to Adolescence*](https://developingchild.harvard.edu/resources/handouts-tools/activities-guide-enhancing-and-practicing-executive-function-skills/). General developmental and reading/play references provide editorial context, not evidence that this specific story improves social skills, reasoning or sleep.

**Single-voice production:** Character names and actions identify speakers. No character impersonation, audio cues, music or effects are required. TTS must omit all text outside the spoken markers.
