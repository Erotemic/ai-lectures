# Wendy and the Flower Without a Hat

Wendy arrived at the Story House with a hat on her head and another hat in her basket.

The one on her head was her own straw hat, with its blue ribbon tied where it had always been. The one in the basket was made of paper, much smaller, and had a crooked red brim.

Aunt Kay carried the basket for her while they walked over Bell Hill, stopping at the familiar bench halfway up. By the time they reached Village Green, Wendy had told Aunt Kay three things the little paper hat might belong to.

A beetle.

A very polite walnut.

Or a king who was only as big as a walnut.

"I think the walnut is the most likely," said Aunt Kay.

"That's because you haven't met the king," Wendy replied.

They found Grandma May outside the Story House. The door opened smoothly and, when it closed, made its small click.

"Come and look at the garden," Grandma May said.

Wendy went around the corner and stopped.

The tallest sunflower had opened.

Its wide yellow petals spread around a dark middle. The other sunflower stood nearby with its own bud still closed, though the green folds had begun to look fuller.

"Oh," Wendy said, so softly that Aunt Kay nearly missed it.

She had seen the first small shoots weeks ago. She had pretended to make hats for them, but the paper hats had stayed safely indoors. Now one of the plants was a flower, wide and bright above her head.

"It's bigger than my hat," she said.

"Quite a bit bigger," Aunt Kay agreed.

Wendy looked at the little paper hat in her basket.

"And much bigger than this one."

She held the paper hat beside her own cheek. A breeze tried to lift it away. Wendy caught the brim and tucked it securely back into the basket.

"I don't think the sunflower needs it," she decided.

"Probably not," said Grandma May.

"It's already wearing yellow all the way around."

Ben came down the path with his red scarf, accompanied by his grown-up.

"Wendy!" he called. "Did you see? It's opened!"

"I saw."

Ben looked up at the flower.

"It looks like someone has taken a very large plate and put sunlight around the edge."

Wendy thought about that. "How would you eat soup off it?"

"Very carefully," said Ben.

"And with a ladder."

That was enough to make them both laugh.

They sat on the dry stones beside the garden and watched the flower moving gently in the breeze. A small bee visited the dark middle. It walked over one part of the flower head, lifted off, and flew away.

"A visitor," Wendy said.

"Without a hat," Ben added.

"Maybe bees are allowed."

"Maybe bees have tiny hats that we can't see."

Wendy imagined a bee wearing a hat no wider than a grain of rice. It would need a very small hat shop, and perhaps a very patient hat maker.

She did not try to catch the bee or follow it. There was plenty to watch from the stones.

Aunt Kay showed Wendy the green bud on the second stem.

"That flower hasn't opened yet," she said.

"Does it have to open today?"

"No. Some flowers open sooner than others."

Wendy sat quietly for a moment, then waved her little paper hat in the direction of the unopened bud.

"I'll come and see you again," she said.

The bud stayed closed, which was exactly what a closed bud ought to do.

Wendy took the paper hat home in her basket. The sunflowers were left in the garden, with their stems, leaves, and petals just where they belonged.

That evening, at the cream cottage in Far Meadow, she put the paper hat on a walnut-shaped wooden knob beside the window. It looked surprisingly grand there.

The orange, blue, and violet picnic banner still hung at the other window. Her blue-ribboned straw hat rested on its usual peg.

Before bed she looked once toward Bell Hill. She couldn't see the Story House garden from the cottage, but she could picture the broad yellow flower against the sky.

Some flowers took longer to open than others.

There would be another day to see the second one.

## For parents and caregivers — not spoken

**What carries forward:** The first sunflower is open with broad yellow petals and a dark center. The second sunflower remains budded. A single incidental bee visits and flies away; the children do not chase it. Wendy takes her little crooked-brimmed paper hat home to Far Meadow and puts it on a walnut-shaped wooden knob. Her straw hat retains its blue ribbon.

**Why this episode exists:** First bloom is a real garden payoff; Wendy’s joke about the flower already wearing yellow keeps the scene a story, not a gardening lecture.

**Optional prompt:** "What did Wendy think the sunflower was already wearing?" Optional; do not interrupt a child settling to sleep.

**References and evidence limits:** [Sunflowers (Helianthus): Home](https://libguides.nybg.org/Sunflowers); [The Power of Play: A Pediatric Role in Enhancing Development in Young Children](https://doi.org/10.1542/peds.2018-2058). These are horticultural facts or general play/conversation guidance; they do not demonstrate benefits from listening to these stories. The named characters, places and events are fiction.

**Single-voice production:** Only material between the spoken markers is narrated. No effects, music or acting required.
