# Kim and the Table by the Window

The next afternoon, the little blue book was waiting on the low table at the Market Street library.

A librarian had set it open to a picture of a bend of reeds. Beside it stood the handwritten sign inviting visitors to look at books made at home.

Kim came with her grown-up and the other five friends. They had all ridden the green bus west from the stop beyond Far Meadow, with their adults, and now they were inside where the windows made squares of afternoon light on the floor.

Kim chose the chair nearest the plant. She remembered it from her earlier visit. From there she could see the table, the door, and part of the street.

"Would you like to sit closer?" Sue asked.

"No, this one is good," Kim replied.

Sue nodded and sat by the table instead.

Ben lifted the book with two careful paws. His red scarf rested across his shoulders.

"It's a book of sounds," he told the small gathering.

A few visitors had stopped to look: a child holding an adult's hand, another grown-up carrying library books, and a little boy who had come for picture-book time. They sat where they liked.

"But it isn't going to make noises by itself," Ben explained. "You have to remember them."

"Or make them," said Todd.

Ben turned the page with the tall reeds.

"Shhh," Wendy whispered, thinking of the wind in Far Meadow and the reeds in Todd's meadow.

The little boy looked toward the librarian.

"Is that the sound we make when we have to be quiet?"

"Sometimes," said the librarian. "But this picture is about the wind."

The boy leaned toward the drawing.

"Wind can shhh too?"

"It's very good at it," Sue said. "It doesn't even have a mouth."

Ben showed the next page: feet on Pebble Bridge going tok and tuk, with his silly paper listening hat beside them.

"Why does the hat have such big ears?" the child asked.

"Because I drew them," said Ben.

"Did it help?"

"No. It crinkled."

That pleased the child.

Todd waited until Ben reached the frog page.

"Bup," Todd said, very softly.

The little boy laughed. Todd gave him a slightly longer "buuuuup," then grinned and let the grown-ups turn their attention back to the book.

On the fourth page, Wendy recognized the Windbreak Tree and the crescent of reeds. On the fifth was the watering can that had gone plink in the rain. Ben let the silence between imaginary drops last a moment.

Plink.

Pause.

Plink.

Kim watched from the chair by the plant. She didn't want to stand up in front of the room, but she knew what was coming next.

Ben turned to the last page. It showed two folded corners and a yellow rectangle.

Amy reached into her bag for the real yellow map, now with its western flap showing Market Street and the green bus line.

She unfolded it.

Fffft.

"That one is very quiet," the boy said.

Kim raised her paw a little.

Ben saw it. "Kim, did you want to show something?"

Kim stood without moving from beside her chair. She pointed toward the map's mark for Reed Meadow.

"That's where the shhh is," she said.

Then she sat down again.

"Thank you," said Ben, and he turned the book so the little boy could look closely at the first picture once more.

Nobody asked Kim to say anything else.

When the visitors had finished looking, the librarian told Ben she liked how the book showed places a person might never have been.

"You can visit them on the map," Amy said. "Or on the bus for some of them. But the bus can't go over Pebble Bridge."

"It would be a very wide bus for that bridge," Todd observed.

"A bus made of bread," Sue suggested.

"No," everyone said, except Ben, who needed a moment to think about it.

The little table was put back in order. The librarian gave Ben his book and thanked the children for bringing it. She had drawn a small picture of the library window on a paper bookmark, with the words THANK YOU, WILLOW BEND beneath it.

"For the book's journey," she said.

Ben slipped the bookmark loose inside the front cover, where it would not count as another page. The six drawings stayed just as they had been.

At the end of the afternoon, the friends rode the green bus east with their grown-ups. The book lay in its cloth bag on Ben's lap again.

Kim watched the hills through the window, feeling pleased that she had pointed to exactly the part of the map she wanted.

She did not need to tell the whole bus.

The bus said psssh at Far Meadow, and they all went home along their familiar paths.

## For parents and caregivers — not spoken

**What carries forward:** Sunday afternoon library sharing completes successfully. All six friends are present with adults. Kim points to Reed Meadow without being obliged to speak more. The librarian gives Ben a loose paper bookmark showing the library window and the words THANK YOU, WILLOW BEND. Ben tucks it inside the front cover: the book remains six pages. The book returns in its cloth bag with Ben on the bus, and all children are home that evening.

**Why this episode exists:** A small public encounter shows familiar pages traveling beyond their first audience. The episode resolves the invitation and preserves the friends' distinct ways of participating.

**Optional prompt:** "Which sound did Todd make for the library visitors?" Optional; do not interrupt a child settling to sleep.

**References and evidence limits:** [Education Resources — Natural Sounds](https://www.nps.gov/subjects/sound/education-resources.htm); [DAP: Creating a Caring, Equitable Community of Learners](https://www.naeyc.org/resources/position-statements/dap/creating-community/). These are editorial context for shared reading, active play, or observation; they do not demonstrate benefits from listening to these stories or verify the fictional events.

**Single-voice production:** Only material between the spoken markers is narrated. No effects, music or acting required.
