# Ben and the Pigeon Parade

When Ben heard that Amy had seen pigeons on Market Street, he looked at his red scarf.

"Do pigeons wear scarves?" he asked.

"I didn't see any," Amy said.

"How do they know which pigeon is which?"

Amy considered this.

"Maybe they know each other's voices."

Ben was not entirely convinced. But when Grandma May and Ben's grown-up arranged a trip on the green bus, he forgot about scarves for pigeons and started wondering whether he would need a ticket for his own scarf.

At the bus stop beyond Far Meadow, Ben stood very straight beneath the roof.

"One ticket for me," he told his grown-up, "and one for my scarf."

"Your scarf is coming with you," the grown-up said.

"What if it wants its own seat?"

"It can share yours."

Ben looked down at the ends of his scarf. They were hanging quietly against his coat.

"It agrees," he said.

The green bus arrived.

Psssh, went the doors.

Ben took one startled step backward.

"The bus is a very large whisperer."

The driver nodded to Grandma May, and everyone climbed aboard. Ben sat beside his grown-up. Amy sat in front with Grandma May.

Ben watched the road go by through the window. Each time the bus stopped, he waited for another psssh. He never quite stopped smiling when it came.

On Market Street, Ben spotted the pigeons before he spotted the clock.

There were four beside the square bench. One was gray. One had a pale patch on its neck. One was doing a little dance with both feet.

The fourth pigeon was looking at a stone.

"It's a parade," Ben whispered.

"Is it?" said Amy.

"Of course. That one is the leader."

The dancing pigeon took two steps to the left.

"That one is the drummer."

The gray pigeon turned its back on the others.

"That one has gone home," Ben decided.

The children stayed beside their grown-ups and watched the birds from the bench. The pigeons moved in small, important-looking steps. Sometimes two birds went the same way. Sometimes none of them did.

Ben put one paw against his scarf.

"A parade needs a conductor," he said.

"Are you going to conduct the pigeons?" Amy asked.

"I can try."

He gave his finest wibble-wave.

The pigeon looking at the stone continued looking at the stone.

Ben gave a slower wave.

A pigeon walked behind the bench.

He waved with both paws.

His scarf flopped forward and covered one eye.

"I can't see the parade," he said through the red wool.

Amy helped him move the scarf back over his shoulder.

The dancing pigeon had stopped dancing. It was resting on one foot.

"Maybe it is waiting for instructions," Ben said.

"Or maybe it's standing on one foot," said Amy.

Ben considered both ideas.

"It's not much of a parade, is it?"

He sat down on the bench. For a while, they watched the pigeons do whatever pigeons wanted to do.

A pigeon took six quick steps.

Another took one.

The gray pigeon opened its wings, flapped onto a low wall, and settled there.

Ben liked that part best.

"A marching parade that forgot to march," he said. "I think I can do that."

With his grown-up beside him, Ben made a little parade of his own along the empty stretch of pavement next to the bench.

One step. Two steps. A turn.

Then he stopped to look at a flower in a pot.

"Oh no," said Amy. "Our parade has forgotten to march."

"Perfect!" Ben said.

He did another turn. His scarf flew out behind him like a red flag.

The pigeons were still beside the bench, entirely uninterested in Ben's marching and perfectly happy about it.

When the grown-ups said it was time to catch the bus home, Ben made a final wibble-wave toward the square.

One pigeon bobbed its head.

"You see?" Ben said. "A farewell from the drummer."

"Which one was the drummer?" Amy asked.

Ben paused.

"I think I've forgotten."

On the ride home, his scarf shared his seat as promised. It was a very well-behaved passenger, even when the bus whispered psssh at the next stop.

That night, Ben folded the red scarf at the end of his bed.

"Tomorrow," he told it, "we can have a parade of two."

The scarf said nothing.

Ben was satisfied. It was already excellent at forgetting to march.

## For parents and caregivers - not spoken

**What carries forward:** Ben and his grown-up, with Amy and Grandma May, travel by the established green bus, watch pigeons from the square bench and invent a short silly pretend parade on the pavement, without chasing or feeding birds. Ben is familiar with the bus and square, and the story ends with him at home, still owning his red scarf. No object is left in Market Street.

**Why this episode exists:** The main joke is that the birds refuse the rules of Ben’s entirely imaginary parade; his improvised parade of one has a playful resolution.

**Optional prompt:** "Did the pigeons follow Ben’s wibble-wave?" Optional; do not interrupt a child settling to sleep.

**References and evidence limits:** [The Power of Play: A Pediatric Role in Enhancing Development in Young Children](https://doi.org/10.1542/peds.2018-2058); [Message in a Backpack: Asking Questions to Stretch Your Child's Thinking](https://www.naeyc.org/node/5321). These give general reading, play or conversational context; they do not demonstrate benefits from listening to these stories.

**Single-voice production:** Narration only between the spoken markers. No character voices, effects or music are required.
