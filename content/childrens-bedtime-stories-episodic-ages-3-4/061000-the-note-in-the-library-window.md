# The Note in the Library Window

Kim liked the bus's green seats. She liked the little window beside her, too.

A window was good for looking out of. You could sit still while a whole hillside went past.

That morning, Kim and her grown-up were riding the bus west from Far Meadow to Market Street. Sue and Grandpa Ed sat across the aisle. Sue held her small green cushion with the yellow star against her knees.

"I've brought a window," Sue announced.

"That's a cushion," said Kim.

"It's for looking through when I close my eyes."

Sue held it in front of her nose.

"What can you see?" Kim asked.

"An enormous green pillow. The view is terrible."

The bus gave its familiar psssh at the stop in town.

They stepped down with their grown-ups and walked past the clock over the shop awning. The clock had two hands and, as Sue knew very well, no pockets.

A few pigeons were sitting beside the square bench. One pigeon turned in a circle and then seemed to forget why.

"That's something Ben would like," Sue said.

They crossed to the public library. There were painted books on its front window. Behind the glass sat a broad, low table with three small chairs.

But that wasn't what Kim noticed first.

There was a piece of paper in the window.

It said:

**A LITTLE TABLE FOR BOOKS MADE AT HOME.**

Below the large words, someone had drawn a book shaped like a sandwich.

Sue considered this carefully.

"Does that mean the books should be made of bread?"

"No," Kim said. "I think somebody drew a silly sandwich."

"A very tidy sandwich."

Inside, a librarian was returning picture books to a shelf. The grown-ups greeted her, and Kim pointed to the paper.

"Is the little table for books that children make?"

"Yes," the librarian said. "This Sunday afternoon, anyone who wants to may show us a book they made. Or just come and look."

Sue lifted her cushion.

"Could I show a book about a sandwich clock?"

"If you've made one," said the librarian.

"I haven't," Sue admitted. "I have a clock with a sandwich in its pocket. That's different."

Kim thought of Willow Bend, of the Story House with its quiet door, and of a little blue book she had helped fill long ago.

"Our friend Ben made a book," she said. "It has sounds drawn in it."

"Sounds drawn in a book?" the librarian asked.

"Reeds that go shhh. A frog that goes bup. A watering can that goes plink."

Sue added, "The book does not actually go bup. Ben does, sometimes."

The librarian smiled. "I'd like to see that, if Ben wants to bring it."

"It belongs to Ben," Kim said. "We should ask him."

"That's right," said her grown-up. "And we'll arrange the trip with all the grown-ups."

The librarian showed them the little table. One chair was near the window, one beside a plant, and one tucked a little behind the others.

Kim tried the chair near the plant. From there she could see the whole table. She could also see a beetle crawling on the windowsill outside.

"A book about that beetle would be very short," Sue observed.

"Look at how fast it's walking," said Kim.

They borrowed nothing that morning. Kim only wanted to remember the note. Sue wanted to remember the sandwich picture.

On the ride home, the bus went east along the road to the stop beyond Far Meadow. Grandpa Ed carried the cushion when Sue grew sleepy.

The two friends returned with their grown-ups along the familiar paths, Sue toward the orchard and her round-window cottage, Kim over Pebble Bridge toward Round Pond.

That evening, Kim took three pebbles from her violet pouch and placed them in a careful line beside her bed. The round gray pebble with its white stripe was in the middle.

She thought of the little table and the book of sounds.

She had not promised anyone would bring it. There was plenty of time to ask.

She put her pebbles back, one, two, three.

Outside, the reeds rustled softly, though she was too far away to hear their smallest whisper.

And in the Story House, Ben's blue book was safe on the low shelf, still waiting to be asked.

## For parents and caregivers — not spoken

**What carries forward:** Kim and Sue learn that the Market Street library will host a small Sunday afternoon table for homemade books. The librarian invites Ben's book only if Ben agrees; no promise has yet been made. The book remains untouched on the Story House shelf. Sue retains her green star cushion, Kim her three pebbles, and both return home with grown-ups.

**Why this episode exists:** A familiar real place offers a new possibility without turning friendship into an obligation. The comedy comes from Sue mistaking a drawn sandwich for a rule.

**Optional prompt:** "What did Sue think the picture on the library note meant?" Optional; do not interrupt a child settling to sleep.

**Single-voice production:** Only material between the spoken markers is narrated. No effects, music or acting required.
