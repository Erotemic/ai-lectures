# Sue and the Clock with No Pockets

There was a clock above the shop awning on Market Street.

Sue had seen it from the green bus. It had a round white face and two black hands, one long and one short.

"It has hands," Sue told Grandpa Ed, "but no pockets."

"That's true," Grandpa Ed said.

"Where does it keep things?"

"What things?"

"Its minutes."

Grandpa Ed looked at the clock, then at Sue.

"I don't think it keeps them in pockets."

"Well, that's awkward," Sue said.

That afternoon, Sue and Grandpa Ed visited Market Street again. Wendy and Aunt Kay rode with them from the stop beyond Far Meadow. Wendy wore her straw hat with the blue ribbon. Sue had brought a little notebook, because a clock with no pockets seemed worth investigating.

They stood on the pavement near the shop, well away from the road.

The clock's long hand pointed toward the top of the circle. The short hand was a little to one side.

"They're waving in different directions," Wendy said.

"Perhaps they don't agree on where we are," said Sue.

"They tell the time," Grandpa Ed explained. "One hand goes around faster than the other."

Sue watched.

The hands appeared to sit still.

She waited.

They still appeared to sit still.

"Grandpa, are they asleep?"

"No, they're just moving very slowly. Shall we go look at the flower stall and come back?"

That sounded like a good idea.

The flower stall stood a little farther along Market Street. It had yellow flowers in one bucket, white ones in another, and some purple ones that looked as though they were wearing very large skirts.

Wendy tipped her hat at the purple flowers.

"You might want hats," she told them, "but I think you're dressed already."

Sue drew a circle in her notebook. She gave it a long arm and a short arm. Then she drew two large pockets on either side.

"Mine can hold its minutes," she said.

"What will you put in them?" asked Aunt Kay.

Sue looked around the flower stall.

"One petal. And a small sandwich."

"A sandwich for a clock?" Wendy asked.

"It's been working all afternoon."

They walked back to the shop with the clock.

The long hand was no longer pointing straight up. It had moved a little to the right.

"It wasn't asleep," said Sue.

"No," Grandpa Ed said. "It kept moving while we were away."

Sue checked the drawing in her notebook. The long arm of her pretend clock reached into its pocket. The short arm reached toward a sandwich.

"My clock is different," she admitted.

"Very different," said Wendy. "I like its pockets."

Grandpa Ed looked at the big clock above the awning. "The shop clock is useful for knowing when to catch our bus. I wonder what time your bakery closes."

Sue thought of her cardboard castle bakery at home, standing beside the round window. It had a flap door, a WINDOW ORDERS sign, and a paper beetle inside.

"Our bakery needs a clock," she said. "It can't be open all day. The paper beetle has to go to bed."

Back at Round Window Cottage, Sue put her green cushion with the yellow star by the window and brought over the cardboard bakery.

Grandpa Ed helped her cut out the drawn clock and attach it to the bakery's outside wall. Sue added one big pocket below it, just wide enough to hold a paper sandwich.

She made the sandwich from a scrap of folded paper.

Then she tapped the new clock.

"It is time for the beetle to close the bakery," she announced.

"What time is that?" Grandpa Ed asked.

"Pajama time."

The paper beetle was already lying flat inside its cardboard shop. It needed no help putting on pajamas.

Sue closed the flap door.

Flup.

The little pretend clock had no moving hands at all, and it had the biggest pocket of any clock Sue had ever seen.

She liked it much better that way.

## For parents and caregivers - not spoken

**What carries forward:** Sue visits Market Street’s working two-hand shop clock with Grandpa Ed, Wendy and Aunt Kay. Back at Round Window Cottage, Grandpa Ed helps attach a new paper clock face with drawn hands and a large paper pocket to the existing cardboard bakery; a folded paper sandwich stays in the pocket. The clock is pretend and does not run; the real market clock continues working. Wendy’s blue hat ribbon remains intact.

**Why this episode exists:** Sue independently turns an ordinary town clock into a silly bakery prop while retaining clear differences between the working clock and her pretend drawing.

**Optional prompt:** "What did Sue put in her bakery clock’s pocket?" Optional; do not interrupt a child settling to sleep.

**References and evidence limits:** [The Power of Play: A Pediatric Role in Enhancing Development in Young Children](https://doi.org/10.1542/peds.2018-2058); [Message in a Backpack: Asking Questions to Stretch Your Child's Thinking](https://www.naeyc.org/node/5321). These give general reading, play or conversational context; they do not demonstrate benefits from listening to these stories.

**Single-voice production:** Narration only between the spoken markers. No character voices, effects or music are required.
