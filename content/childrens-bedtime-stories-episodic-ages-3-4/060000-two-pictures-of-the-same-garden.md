# Two Pictures of the Same Garden

On a warm afternoon, six friends came to the Story House with their grown-ups.

Amy brought her folded yellow map. Ben wore his red scarf. Kim carried the violet pouch with her three pebbles. Wendy wore her straw hat with its blue ribbon. Todd wore his yellow boots, one with a blue patch. Sue brought a pencil and a sheet of paper from her round-window cottage.

"What's the paper for?" Ben asked.

"I thought we might draw something," Sue said.

"What sort of something?"

"A very large sandwich," Sue replied.

Ben looked delighted.

"Or," Sue added, "the garden."

They went around behind the Story House, where the LITTLE GROWING GARDEN sign stood in its new clear place beside the dry path.

The garden was nothing like it had been when they first put seeds into the soil.

The front row, once filled with radish leaves, was bare after the grown-ups' spring harvest. The small red radish picture label was still there to show what had grown. At the back stood two tall sunflowers. One wore a full ring of yellow petals around its middle. The other held a plump bud with just the beginning of yellow showing between its green folds.

"One open and one thinking about it," said Ben.

"I wonder if it'll open tomorrow," Kim told him.

Todd pointed upward. "I drew that bud before it had a yellow edge. Now it has one."

Wendy tipped her hat toward the open flower. "It doesn't need my paper hat anymore. It never did."

"What happened to the little hats?" Amy asked.

"They're in the house, not on the plants," said Wendy. "A walnut is wearing one now."

Nobody had any questions about the walnut. At least, nobody had a question with a sensible answer.

Grandma May opened the low Story House shelf with the other grown-ups nearby. Ben's blue Book of Little Sounds rested beside the brown Button Town envelope and the plain LITTLE GROWING GARDEN folder.

She took out only the garden folder and carried it to the outdoor table.

Inside was their first picture, made when two green sunflower shoots were barely taller than the row labels. Next to it lay Amy's smaller sketch of the tall stalks and a closed green bud, drawn weeks later.

Sue looked from one paper to the other.

"If I didn't know these were the same garden," she said, "I might think one of them belonged to a family of giants."

Ben raised his paw. "I would like to join that family."

"You would have to eat soup from a plate the size of a wagon," Wendy reminded him.

"I would manage."

They laughed, then Sue laid her new paper on the table.

"This one can show it today."

Amy drew the edge of the path and the sign. She put the sign where it really stood now, not behind the leaf where it had been before.

Kim drew the two flat stones where everyone could watch without stepping into the soil. She added a long shadow stretching into the empty front row.

Ben drew one open flower with enormous yellow petals. He gave it a serious face, then decided that a serious sunflower was too funny and left the face in place.

Todd drew the shorter sunflower and its almost-open bud. In a corner he drew his blue-patched boot, very small this time.

Wendy drew the dark center of the open flower and one visiting bee, from memory, as a tiny dot with wings. She did not put a hat on it.

Sue drew a narrow strip of empty earth in front, then a little red circle beside the radish label.

"The radishes have gone," Ben said.

"I know," Sue replied. "That's why I put the circle on the sign, not in the ground."

Grandma May helped them write the words THE GARDEN TODAY along the bottom. The children took turns looking from the page to the real plants, checking what they had remembered and what they had made up.

Ben's serious-faced sunflower was made up.

The two stalks were not.

When the picture was finished, they placed it in the existing garden folder, behind the first picture and Amy's in-between sketch. It was still just one folder, for one small patch that had looked different on different days.

Amy unfolded her real yellow map. The garden was a tiny square beside the Story House. It already held two green marks for the early shoots.

She added one small yellow dot inside the garden square, beside the two green marks, to remember the first flower.

"Don't make the dot too big," Sue said. "You don't want a flower as wide as the whole village."

Amy looked at Ben.

"He might," she said.

Ben nodded happily. "I absolutely might."

The sun moved lower, and the grown-ups began gathering bags and hats. Kim walked back toward Round Pond with her grown-up. Todd and Aunt Pat continued toward Reed Meadow. Wendy and Aunt Kay went west toward Bell Hill. Sue and Grandpa Ed took the southern orchard path to Round Window Cottage. Ben went home with his grown-up, still discussing the benefits of giant soup plates.

Amy returned with Grandma May to Blue Door Cottage.

That night she put the folded yellow map beside her pillow. Its little garden square held two green marks and one yellow dot. Its western flap still showed the green bus line to Market Street, folded safely over the rest.

Beyond her window, somewhere behind the quiet Story House door, one sunflower stood open and another was still getting ready.

The garden's new picture was on the same low shelf as the old one.

Amy closed her eyes. She could see both.

## For parents and caregivers — not spoken

**What carries forward:** Six friends and their trusted grown-ups make a new shared THE GARDEN TODAY picture and place it in the *existing* LITTLE GROWING GARDEN folder behind the original picture and interim sketch. Amy adds one small yellow dot to the existing garden square of her real yellow map while preserving its two green marks and the western bus flap. First sunflower stays open, second stays in bud, front row remains empty with red label. All go home; no new shelf folder or event.

**Why this episode exists:** Older and new drawings are both true for their days, closing the seasonal arc without stopping the plants from continuing to grow.

**Optional prompt:** "What did Amy add to the garden square on her map?" Optional; do not interrupt a child settling to sleep.

**References and evidence limits:** [Growing radishes in home gardens](https://extension.umn.edu/garden-and-home/yard-and-garden/gardening-in-minnesota/growing-radishes); [Message in a Backpack: Asking Questions to Stretch Your Child's Thinking](https://www.naeyc.org/node/5321). These are horticultural facts or general play/conversation guidance; they do not demonstrate benefits from listening to these stories. The named characters, places and events are fiction.

**Single-voice production:** Only material between the spoken markers is narrated. No effects, music or acting required.
