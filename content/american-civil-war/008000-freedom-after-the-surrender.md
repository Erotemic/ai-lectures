# Freedom After the Surrender: Reconstruction and the Battle over Citizenship, 1865–1877

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

Suppose you have spent your life enslaved on a plantation. The Confederate armies have surrendered. The law now says you are free. What do you do on Monday morning?

You may search for a child who was sold away years ago. You may want to move to a different town, choose an employer, refuse work without pay, establish a household, attend school, marry under civil law, or attend a church without your former owner's supervision. You may want land, because legal liberty without the means to support yourself can leave a person dependent on the very people who exercised power over you before the war. You may need protection from violence. And you may ask who will enforce the freedom that has been proclaimed.

This is the world of Reconstruction. It is often treated as an appendix to the Civil War, a troubled period after the dramatic battles. That misses its significance. The armies decided that the Confederacy would not become a separate country. Reconstruction would determine, in law and everyday life, what kind of country the United States had become.

## What freedom meant on the ground

In 1865 the federal government confronted the needs of millions of formerly enslaved people, displaced families, veterans, and destitute communities. The Bureau of Refugees, Freedmen, and Abandoned Lands, usually called the Freedmen's Bureau, was established during the war's final months. It helped provide food, medical assistance, labor supervision, and support for schools, though its resources and reach were limited. Its officers did not share one political outlook, and many local officials were unable or unwilling to overcome resistance from former slaveholders.

Freedpeople acted for themselves. They organized churches, sought teachers, formed political meetings, negotiated contracts, and tried to reunite families separated under slavery. Their letters, marriage records, petitions, and testimony show people defining freedom in practical terms. To leave an abusive employer, to claim control over one's children, or to learn to read were political acts in a society where those freedoms had been systematically denied.

Land was a central question. Some freedpeople hoped that confiscated or abandoned plantations would be redistributed. There were wartime promises and temporary arrangements, including land settlements associated with General Sherman's Special Field Orders Number 15. But broad redistribution did not occur. Much land was returned to former Confederates. Freedpeople frequently had to negotiate labor agreements with people who had previously enslaved them.

Sharecropping emerged as an important arrangement. A family might farm land and receive a portion of the crop, while relying on landowners or merchants for tools, food, and credit. In theory the arrangement could permit some independence. In practice, debt, unfair accounting, crop liens, racial coercion, and a lack of alternatives could trap families in dependency. Freedom from legal ownership was a decisive change, but economic power remained unequally distributed.

## What did formerly enslaved families try to build?

For a family emerging from slavery, the first concerns might be extraordinarily concrete. Where would the household live? Who had the right to keep the children together? Could a worker leave an employer? Would a marriage be legally recognized? Could people demand wages, make contracts, learn to read, worship independently, and find relatives sold years earlier? Freedom meant the ability to make these choices without another person claiming ownership of their bodies and labor.

After emancipation, many families searched for missing kin. Notices placed in newspapers, inquiries at military posts, church correspondence, and journeys across states reveal how enduring the violence of separation had been. Some families were reunited; many searches failed. Freed people formalized marriages, founded or expanded independent Black churches, formed schools, and organized mutual-aid institutions. Literacy had immediate practical importance for reading labor contracts, understanding official notices, participating in politics, and guarding against exploitation.

Land was a central issue because formal freedom without productive assets could leave a family dependent on landlords and former slaveholders. In some coastal areas federal military policies briefly enabled freed people to occupy or manage land abandoned during the war. Much of that land was later restored to former Confederates or returned to private owners through changing federal policy. Freedpeople's demands for land were not simply requests for charity. Many believed that years of unpaid labor entitled them to the material means of independence.

Where land ownership remained out of reach, labor arrangements developed under unequal bargaining power. Sharecropping could give families more control over daily work than plantation slavery had allowed, but tenants often faced debt, exploitative credit, crop liens, and landlords with political influence. These economic conditions varied by locality and over time; they did not simply recreate slavery unchanged. They did, however, reveal the enormous distance between legal emancipation and secure economic independence.

## The president and the people who rejected his plan

Abraham Lincoln's assassination left Andrew Johnson in office. Johnson moved quickly toward restoring southern state governments under conditions many congressional Republicans considered too lenient. Former Confederate leaders regained positions of influence, and new state governments enacted Black Codes attempting to restrict the mobility, work, and legal status of Black people.

The conflict between Johnson and Congress was not simply a disagreement about administrative speed. It concerned whether the United States had the authority and obligation to protect equal civil rights in the former Confederate states. If the states were restored largely on their old terms, much of the racial order of slavery could survive without the formal ownership of people. If the federal government enforced a new conception of national citizenship, relations among Washington, the states, and individuals would be remade.

Congress passed the Civil Rights Act of 1866 over Johnson's veto, asserting national protections for citizenship and civil rights. The Fourteenth Amendment, ratified in 1868, established birthright citizenship under its terms and required due process and equal protection of the laws. Its citizenship and protection clauses altered the constitutional foundation on which courts, lawmakers, and citizens could argue about rights. That did not mean every right was immediately made real, or that courts would consistently interpret the amendment as its supporters hoped.

Reconstruction Acts passed beginning in 1867 imposed conditions on former Confederate states and created military districts in much of the South while new state governments were formed. Black men participated in constitutional conventions, elections, and public office. The Fifteenth Amendment, ratified in 1870, barred denying or abridging the right to vote on account of race, color, or previous condition of servitude. It did not by its own terms prohibit every voting qualification, and its protection was later undermined by violence and exclusionary laws.

Women of all backgrounds who had contributed to abolition and wartime organizing faced another exclusion. The Fifteenth Amendment did not enfranchise women as a group. The resulting disputes divided the women's rights movement, including people who had previously worked together against slavery. The expansion of constitutional rights was real, but it did not resolve every demand for political equality.

## How citizenship became a national constitutional question

Andrew Johnson's approach to reunion permitted former Confederate elites to regain considerable political power while many Southern states enacted Black Codes restricting freedpeople's labor and civil standing. Congressional Republicans came to regard this as a repudiation of the war's result. The Civil Rights Act of 1866 sought to establish basic national civil rights, including the ability to make contracts and obtain legal protection. Johnson vetoed it, and Congress overrode his veto. That confrontation showed the federal legislature asserting a responsibility that earlier generations had often left to states.

The Fourteenth Amendment, ratified in 1868, defined national citizenship for people born or naturalized in the United States and required states to provide due process and equal protection. It was not a mere restatement of the Declaration's moral language. It changed the constitutional relationship among individuals, states, and the federal government. The Reconstruction Acts placed former Confederate states, with specified exceptions, under military arrangements and required changes to state constitutions and political participation as conditions for readmission.

The Fifteenth Amendment, ratified in 1870, prohibited denial of voting rights on the basis of race, color, or previous condition of servitude. Its language did not create universal voting rights: women remained largely excluded, and other qualifications and evasions would be used to narrow political access. But it was a decisive national statement that Black men's votes were not simply at the discretion of each state. Black voters and officeholders helped construct new state governments, public education systems, and legal institutions. They did so with allies and amid political disagreements, not as passive beneficiaries of laws written elsewhere.

These changes involved constitutional conflict over whose democratic legitimacy counted. Former Confederates argued for restoration of state self-government and challenged federal military supervision. Reconstruction supporters replied that allowing states to deny civil rights through local majorities was not democratic freedom for the people excluded from protection. The dispute cannot be understood by treating state authority as a value independent of what a state did with it. In communities where intimidation prevented voting, formal local control could conceal coercive rule by a minority.

## An experiment in democratic government

Under Reconstruction governments, Black voters and officeholders helped shape state constitutions, public education, infrastructure, and civil rights policy. Black men served in state legislatures and in Congress. Hiram Revels and Blanche K. Bruce represented Mississippi in the United States Senate at different times. These were not symbolic appointments made against the will of every Black community. They emerged from political organization, party coalitions, federal enforcement, local leadership, and formerly enslaved people's determination to participate in the republic.

Public education was a major undertaking. Freed communities sought schools and raised money to build them. Churches, teachers, federal programs, and northern aid associations contributed, though partnerships could be unequal and resources insufficient. Educational institutions became places where the meaning of citizenship was made tangible. Literacy could help a person read a contract, communicate with separated family, study scripture, or hold public office. But schooling also exposed teachers and students to threats from those who regarded Black education and political independence as unacceptable.

Reconstruction politics was not a conflict between universally virtuous reformers and universally corrupt opponents. Political coalitions contained competing priorities; public officials made mistakes; debt and economic development created controversy. But the familiar portrayal of Reconstruction as a period of Black incapacity or illegitimate rule was shaped by later racist histories that minimized white supremacist violence and Black political accomplishment. Corruption and political disagreement should be studied on evidence, not used to erase the central struggle over democratic participation.

## Terror and the retreat of federal protection

White supremacist organizations, including the Ku Klux Klan, used threats, beatings, murder, and intimidation to prevent Black people from voting, owning property independently, organizing labor, or serving in office. Violence also targeted white allies of Reconstruction. The objective was political as well as racial: to change election results, destroy community institutions, and restore a hierarchy in which Black people could be kept subordinate despite the formal end of slavery.

Federal authorities responded with Enforcement Acts in 1870 and 1871, which created tools to protect voting rights and prosecute conspiracies. In some places federal intervention temporarily weakened organized terror. But enforcement depended on presidential decisions, the courts, funding, public support, and the willingness of local authorities to cooperate. Each of those could change.

Several forces narrowed the national commitment to Reconstruction. Northern voters grew tired of military intervention and political conflict. Economic hardship after the Panic of 1873 shifted attention toward money, employment, and debt. Supreme Court decisions narrowed some federal protections. Southern white Democratic movements, sometimes calling themselves Redeemers, organized campaigns to retake state governments through elections accompanied in many places by intimidation, fraud, and violence. The achievements of Black political participation remained substantial, but the institutions protecting them became increasingly fragile.

## Violence, enforcement, and the narrowing of federal commitment

The Ku Klux Klan and other white supremacist organizations used assault, murder, threats, and economic intimidation against Black voters, officeholders, teachers, and white Republicans. This violence was not simply spontaneous neighborhood conflict. In many places it was organized political action intended to destroy multiracial government and restore the power of former Confederate elites. Elections could be formally held while their results were shaped by terror long before a ballot was cast.

Congress responded through Enforcement Acts in 1870 and 1871, including legislation allowing the federal government to prosecute interference with voting and organized conspiracies. President Ulysses S. Grant's administration used federal power against the Klan in parts of the South with some significant successes. That history complicates the idea that federal authorities did nothing after emancipation. They acted, but the scale of opposition, political resistance, judicial limitations, and changing Northern priorities made enforcement inconsistent and difficult to sustain.

The Civil Rights Act of 1875 attempted to secure equal access in certain public accommodations. Yet the federal judiciary narrowed constitutional protections in decisions during and after Reconstruction. The Supreme Court's Slaughter-House decisions in 1873 gave a limited interpretation to the privileges or immunities of national citizenship; later rulings further restricted federal tools against discrimination and violence. One should not collapse different decisions into a single theory, but the general result was that some of the amendment's potential as a national guarantee was curtailed.

Political support also weakened. The economic crisis beginning in 1873 redirected attention, many Northern white voters lost willingness to sustain federal intervention, and political parties sought other alliances. White supremacist Democrats regained control of Southern state governments through combinations of campaigning, coercion, and violence. The disputed presidential election of 1876 and the decisions associated with 1877 marked a critical shift, though federal retreat had been occurring for years.

To say Reconstruction failed is incomplete without specifying which aspirations failed, for whom, and when. Federal support for sustained Black political equality was sharply reduced. Terror and disenfranchisement undermined rights that constitutional amendments purported to protect. But Black communities continued building institutions and political traditions that were not erased. The record contains both an extraordinarily ambitious experiment in democracy and the deliberate destruction of much of its enforcement. These are not mutually exclusive verdicts; they describe different parts of the same history.

## What happened in 1877?

The presidential election of 1876 between Rutherford B. Hayes and Samuel J. Tilden produced disputed electoral returns from several states. A political process eventually awarded Hayes the presidency. The settlement is often described as the Compromise of 1877, associated with the withdrawal of remaining federal troops from the statehouse politics of South Carolina and Louisiana and the end of sustained federal support for Reconstruction governments there. Historians debate the nature and extent of any specific bargain. It is safer to describe a convergence of negotiations, political decisions, and a longer retreat from enforcement than to imagine one fully documented secret agreement that explains everything.

The year 1877 is a useful conventional boundary, but the consequences did not stop then. Black political activity continued in many places, sometimes against severe obstacles. Legal segregation, disfranchisement, and racial terror grew through later decades. The federal Constitution still contained the Reconstruction Amendments, and those provisions would eventually become central to twentieth-century civil rights litigation and political movements. But that later history did not make the losses of the late nineteenth century less immediate to the people who suffered them.

Nor did the national turn west cease during Reconstruction. The federal government was fighting and negotiating with Indigenous nations as it expanded into western territories. The restored Union increasingly claimed powers over a continent while refusing to recognize many Indigenous nations' assertions of sovereignty on their own terms. Emancipation and the expansion of United States power were simultaneous developments, not proof that national policies had become consistently liberating.

## The Civil War's unfinished consequence

There are several ways to tell the end of the Civil War. One is a military history ending at Appomattox with armies stacking rifles and veterans returning home. Another is a constitutional history ending with the Thirteenth, Fourteenth, and Fifteenth Amendments. A third follows freed families trying to make decisions over labor, education, land, migration, and political voice while people with money, weapons, and office attempted to control those choices. All three are needed.

The United States did not merely put the seceded states back where they had been in 1860. Slavery was abolished. National citizenship was redefined. Federal authority grew. Black Americans built schools, churches, newspapers, political organizations, and institutions that endured. These are enduring achievements, not trivial exceptions to a story of failure.

Yet the failure to protect equality and political participation over the long term was also real. Reconstruction's retreat made possible systems of subordination that would require prolonged struggles to dismantle. To say that the Civil War ended slavery is true and profoundly important. To say that the war made freedom secure is not.

The question that opened this lecture—what does a person do on Monday morning after becoming free?—is not a small domestic question beside the grand history of generals and presidents. It is where the meaning of victory becomes concrete. A government can announce equality. The harder work is making it possible for people to live under that promise without having their choices stolen by force. Reconstruction was the first large national attempt to answer that challenge after slavery, and the argument it began remained part of American life long after the last Reconstruction troops were withdrawn.
