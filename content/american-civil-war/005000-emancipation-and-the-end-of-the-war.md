# Emancipation and the End of the War: Gettysburg, Vicksburg, and Appomattox, 1863–1865

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

On January 1, 1863, Abraham Lincoln issued the Emancipation Proclamation. It declared free the people held in slavery in specified areas then in rebellion against the United States. It did not emancipate everyone in slavery that day. The border slave states loyal to the Union were excluded, and so were certain Confederate-held regions already under Union control. Much of the land named in the proclamation remained beyond effective federal reach. Yet the document changed what Union victory would mean. As federal armies entered Confederate territory, the area in which the proclamation could be enforced grew. People who had pursued freedom at enormous personal risk now had a federal military commitment that was increasingly aligned with their cause.

The war would last more than two further years. Nothing about that policy made victory automatic. But the conflict was now publicly and explicitly a war against the Confederate rebellion and against the slavery system on which the Confederacy depended.

## A proclamation that needed people and armies

Emancipation was neither a single piece of paper doing all the work nor a spontaneous side effect of armies marching. Enslaved people made choices that undermined the Confederacy, sometimes fleeing and sometimes organizing new lives under Union occupation. Federal commanders and lawmakers developed policies that made those choices more consequential. The proclamation bound the federal executive to a central antislavery wartime objective.

It also authorized the enlistment of Black men in United States armed forces. Black Americans had served in military roles earlier, and many had been demanding opportunities to fight for the Union. The new policy made large-scale federal recruitment possible. United States Colored Troops served in infantry, artillery, and other capacities; Black sailors served in the navy. By the war's end, roughly 180,000 Black soldiers had served in the Union army, together with many thousands of Black sailors. They faced discrimination in pay and treatment, and the threat of especially harsh punishment or murder if captured by Confederate troops.

The Fifty-fourth Massachusetts Infantry, one of the best-known Black regiments, assaulted Fort Wagner in South Carolina in July 1863. The attack failed to capture the fort and cost many lives. The regiment's service mattered because it challenged racist claims that Black men would not fight as soldiers and because the men themselves were asserting a claim to citizenship through military service. Their courage did not excuse the unequal treatment they often received from the institution they served.

The war required Black women's work as well: nursing, intelligence gathering, food preparation, organizing, teaching, and support for refugee communities. Harriet Tubman, already known for helping people escape slavery, worked with Union forces and participated in an 1863 operation along South Carolina's Combahee River that helped free hundreds of enslaved people. The history of emancipation cannot be told as a gift handed down to passive recipients.

## Two victories in the summer of 1863

During the first days of July 1863, two campaigns produced results that substantially shifted the war. In Pennsylvania, Lee's Army of Northern Virginia fought Union forces commanded by George G. Meade near Gettysburg. The battle lasted from July 1 to July 3. Both armies suffered severe casualties. Lee's forces retreated south afterward, and the offensive into the North had failed.

The battle is often remembered through Pickett's Charge, the Confederate assault on the Union center on July 3. It is a dramatic and devastating episode, but Gettysburg was not won or lost through one decision alone. The fighting involved the defense of high ground, repeated maneuvers across widely separated parts of the field, exhausted soldiers, imperfect information, and commanders unable to see more than fragments of an enormous battle. The Union army held, and Lee's invasion ended. Lee's army was not destroyed; the eastern war continued.

Far to the west, Grant's campaign against Vicksburg on the Mississippi River ended with the Confederate city's surrender on July 4. Control of the Mississippi mattered strategically: it could divide Confederate territory, improve Union movement and supply, and sever links across the river. The campaign involved maneuver, river operations, siege, and sustained pressure, not simply a pitched battle. Vicksburg's civilians also endured bombardment, scarcity, and displacement. The campaign's end was a profound Union success.

These two victories do not need to compete for the title of the war's single turning point. Gettysburg stopped a major Confederate offensive in the East. Vicksburg strengthened Union control of the Mississippi. Their significance becomes clearer if we see that they solved different strategic problems. But neither meant that all other obstacles had vanished.

In November 1863 Lincoln traveled to dedicate a soldiers' cemetery at Gettysburg. His short address did not simply congratulate the army. He connected the war to the republic's founding assertion of equality and spoke of a new birth of freedom. It was a political act of interpretation. The president was explaining why the tremendous deaths might serve a national purpose larger than restoring the exact prewar arrangement.

## How Gettysburg was fought over three days

Gettysburg matters partly because its details resist the neat story of one decisive charge. On July 1, 1863, Confederate and Union forces converged near the Pennsylvania town in a battle that rapidly exceeded an initial clash between advance units. Union troops fell back through the town toward high ground south of it, including Cemetery Hill, Cemetery Ridge, and Culp's Hill. The Union army's line took shape as more forces arrived. Geography offered defensive advantages, but those advantages depended on soldiers reaching positions, maintaining communication, and responding to attacks along a long front.

On July 2, Confederate assaults struck the Union flanks. Fighting around Little Round Top, Devil's Den, the Wheatfield, the Peach Orchard, and other places involved dramatic local struggles that later acquired their own legends. Commanders could not perceive the whole battlefield at once. Messengers carried orders through noise and confusion; reinforcements sometimes arrived because officers interpreted incomplete instructions or took local initiative. A victory on one patch of ground could be canceled by loss on another.

On July 3, after an artillery bombardment, Confederate troops crossed open ground toward the Union center in the attack remembered as Pickett's Charge. The assault failed at enormous cost. Its familiarity can conceal the scale of the larger three-day battle, and the armies continued to operate after Lee retreated. Gettysburg's significance was that an invasion was defeated, not that the Confederate army ceased to exist. The fighting around the same time at Vicksburg produced a strategic gain in another theater by restoring Union control of the Mississippi. Neither victory alone made the remaining war automatic.

We should also remember that Gettysburg took place in a community. Soldiers occupied farms and houses, fields were torn up, livestock lost, buildings turned into hospitals, and local civilians were forced into medical and burial work. In the weeks after the battle, the dead and wounded transformed the town's landscape. When Lincoln later dedicated the cemetery, he was speaking on ground where the physical consequences of the conflict had been impossible to ignore.

## The war becomes a struggle of endurance

The Confederacy still had armies capable of hard fighting. In 1864 Lincoln put Grant in command of all Union armies. Grant and Meade's forces pursued Lee through a sequence of savage engagements in Virginia, including the Wilderness and Spotsylvania. Grant was criticized for casualties, and the destruction was indeed immense. Yet his strategic choice was to maintain pressure rather than end a campaign after a bloody encounter. Union armies could replenish men and materials more readily than Confederate armies; the federal government aimed to deny the Confederacy time to recover or move forces freely between theaters.

The fighting eventually hardened into a long siege around Petersburg, a crucial rail center supplying Richmond. Both armies dug extensive earthworks. Trenches and fortifications made offensive movement deadly. Soldiers lived through mud, disease, exhaustion, and repeated attack. The war had become a contest of industrial supply and stubborn will as much as battlefield improvisation.

In the West, William Tecumseh Sherman led Union forces into Georgia. The capture of Atlanta in September 1864 provided a major political and military boost to the Union. Sherman then marched from Atlanta to Savannah, destroying railroads, military resources, and property that supported Confederate war-making. The campaign and later operations in the Carolinas brought the destruction of war into civilian landscapes. Historians disagree over the best terminology and moral interpretation of such campaigns, but it is clear that Union strategy increasingly targeted the Confederacy's capacity to sustain armies as well as the armies themselves. Noncombatants suffered, and the extent and character of destruction varied across places.

Confederate authorities also extracted food, labor, and military service from populations under their control. Enslaved laborers remained essential to building fortifications and maintaining the southern war economy. Hunger and inflation burdened households, and Confederate nationalism weakened in parts of the South as military defeat became more plausible. To describe these effects is not to confuse the cause of Union and Confederate states; it is to recognize that war restructured the daily lives of nearly everyone it touched.

## Why Petersburg was different from the war's early battles

The campaigns of 1864 made the Eastern war less like the imagined duel of two generals and more like a continuous struggle over a transportation system and lines of entrenchment. Grant coordinated United States operations across theaters while remaining with the principal army in Virginia, which was formally commanded by George G. Meade. After bloody fighting in the Wilderness and at Spotsylvania, the United States army did not retreat to reorganize for another year. It kept maneuvering, seeking to force Lee into positions where the Confederacy's smaller resources and shrinking supply network could no longer sustain resistance.

The assault at Cold Harbor inflicted heavy Union casualties and has become one of the most criticized decisions of Grant's command. It is important not to turn criticism into the claim that losses had no strategic significance, or defense into the claim that every assault was wise. The campaign's logic was to deny Lee the opportunity to recover and to keep the Confederacy's principal army occupied while federal operations proceeded elsewhere. The price was high, and specific judgments about tactics and timing remain open to historical analysis.

Around Petersburg, Virginia, both armies entrenched along lines that eventually extended many miles. Petersburg was not merely a suburb of Richmond; its railroad connections helped supply the Confederate capital and Lee's army. Cutting those routes would make the defense of Richmond unsustainable. Soldiers dug trenches and constructed shelters, endured rain and disease, and faced sharpshooters, artillery, patrols, and attempts to break fortified lines.

In July 1864 Union miners detonated explosives beneath part of the Confederate defenses, creating the enormous crater that gave the ensuing Battle of the Crater its name. The planned assault failed amid command confusion and devastating Confederate counterattacks. Black Union troops who fought in the operation were exposed to particularly brutal treatment in the aftermath. The episode shows how ingenuity in engineering could be wasted by failures in planning and execution, and how race could shape the fate of soldiers even within a conventional military operation.

By 1865 the siege and parallel campaigns had exhausted Confederate choices. But it is a mistake to imagine Petersburg was a static pause before the famous surrender. It was an active struggle of movement, destruction of rail links, skirmishes, assaults, and efforts to force an opponent out of increasingly untenable positions. The fall of Petersburg and Richmond in April 1865 reflected that cumulative process.

## The election that could have altered the war

In 1864 the outcome of the Union presidential election was uncertain. Lincoln faced criticism from people who wanted more aggressive emancipation and from others who wanted the war ended through negotiation. Democratic candidate George B. McClellan personally supported preserving the Union, while the Democratic platform included a call for an immediate effort to cease hostilities and pursue peace. Military developments, including the fall of Atlanta, strengthened Lincoln's position. He won reelection.

The significance is easy to overlook because elections now appear routine in the United States. The government conducted a national presidential election amid a massive civil war. Union soldiers voted under arrangements that varied by state. The result upheld the administration conducting the war at a point when the terms of peace and the fate of slavery remained contested.

Lincoln pursued a constitutional amendment to abolish slavery throughout the nation, not merely in areas covered by wartime proclamation. Congress passed the proposed Thirteenth Amendment in January 1865. It would not be ratified until December, after the military collapse of the Confederacy. Its text ended slavery and involuntary servitude, except as punishment for a crime after conviction. That exception would have a long and troubling subsequent history. The amendment was a constitutional transformation that required political struggle beyond the president's wartime order.

## Appomattox was an ending, not the only ending

By spring 1865 Confederate military prospects had deteriorated. Petersburg and Richmond fell in early April. Lee's retreating army could not secure adequate supplies or escape converging Union forces. On April 9 Lee surrendered the Army of Northern Virginia to Grant at Appomattox Court House. Grant offered terms designed to permit the soldiers to return home, subject to their pledges and the surrender arrangements. The event became the most famous symbol of the war's military end.

But Confederate armies remained elsewhere, and surrender proceeded in stages over following weeks. The war did not end through a single nationwide ceremony. Communities continued to face uncertainty, violence, refugee movements, and contested authority.

Five days after Appomattox, Lincoln was shot at Ford's Theatre on April 14. He died the next morning. Vice President Andrew Johnson became president just as the United States was preparing to decide how the defeated states would be reintegrated and what freedom would mean for millions of formerly enslaved people. The assassination removed a leader whose future Reconstruction policy remained a matter of debate and left the postwar transition under a new administration.

## The Constitution had to catch up with military freedom

The Emancipation Proclamation had been justified through wartime authority and applied in designated areas of rebellion. Enslaved people in loyal border states and certain exempted areas remained outside its direct operation. The progress of Union armies and the choices of enslaved people transformed circumstances on the ground, but a durable national legal settlement required more. The Thirteenth Amendment was the answer Congress ultimately proposed: slavery and involuntary servitude would be prohibited in the United States, except as punishment for crime after conviction.

The proposed amendment passed the Senate in 1864 but initially failed to secure the required two-thirds majority in the House. Lincoln and supporters pressed for a new vote, and the House approved it in January 1865. Ratification came in December, after the war and after Lincoln's death. This sequence matters: constitutional abolition was achieved through political organization and legislative voting, not simply through a military victory or the president's signature on an executive decree.

The amendment's punishment exception also deserves to be understood as written rather than edited out of the story. It did not preserve private chattel slavery as before, but the exception would become relevant to later systems of forced prison labor and racialized criminal justice. Abolition changed the fundamental legal status of millions, yet citizenship, voting, land, employment, and physical security remained to be decided through further conflict. The war produced a revolution in law whose practical boundaries were not settled when Confederate armies surrendered.

## Victory did not answer every question

The Confederacy had been defeated, the United States preserved, and the abolition of slavery was approaching constitutional completion. These were vast changes. But what would happen when formerly enslaved people tried to acquire land, make independent labor contracts, reunite families, vote, testify in courts, or establish schools? Would their former owners accept freedom as anything more than the loss of legal title to workers? Would the federal government enforce equal citizenship, and for how long?

Military victory provided the ability to answer these questions. It did not provide agreement. The United States had emerged from war with a stronger national government and a transformed legal understanding of slavery. Yet the social power of former Confederates, deep racial hierarchies, and the devastation of southern communities remained. Emancipation opened a struggle over what freedom required. The next chapter would be Reconstruction, and it would become a decisive part of the Civil War's meaning rather than a quiet administrative aftermath.
