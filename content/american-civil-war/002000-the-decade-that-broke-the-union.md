# The Decade That Broke the Union: Fugitive Slavery, Kansas, and Secession, 1850–1861

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

Imagine a country in which a presidential election is followed not by an opposition campaign but by the departure of states from the republic. That is what happened after Abraham Lincoln won in November 1860. Before he had taken office, South Carolina announced its secession. Six other slave states followed during the winter. By the time Lincoln was inaugurated in March 1861, a rival government styling itself the Confederate States of America had formed.

To explain this break, we have to examine the ten years leading to it. There was no single clock counting down to civil war. Politicians repeatedly promised that a fresh compromise would settle the matter. Courts issued decisions meant to clarify national law. Voters reorganized parties. Protesters published books, held meetings, and crossed into contested territories. Instead of dissolving the crisis, these actions often made more Americans doubt that the other side would respect any agreement.

## A federal law that reached into private lives

The Compromise of 1850 was a group of legislative measures, not a treaty of national friendship. One of them, the Fugitive Slave Act, strengthened the machinery for returning people accused of escaping slavery. Federal officials and commissioners were given roles in its enforcement. The law imposed duties and penalties affecting people outside slave states and denied accused fugitives protections they desperately needed, such as trial by jury in the summary federal proceedings.

In northern cities, Black communities organized vigilance, fundraising, legal defense, and sometimes direct rescue. White allies participated as well, though they did not all share a commitment to equal rights. For someone whose family member could be seized from the street, the argument was not a theoretical comparison of state and federal authority. It was about immediate safety and the reliability of legal identity. Free Black people as well as fugitives could fear seizure and wrongful return to bondage.

For proslavery politicians, northern noncooperation looked like a refusal to honor constitutional obligations. This produced one of the great ironies of the era: men who would later claim the right of states to withdraw from the federal union often demanded strong federal enforcement of slavery against unwilling northern communities. The slogan 'states' rights' tells us little unless we ask the next question: the right to do what, under which government, and at whose expense?

The public debate grew in print. In 1852 Harriet Beecher Stowe published *Uncle Tom's Cabin*, a novel whose antislavery message stirred immense readership and opposition. The book also embodied the racial assumptions and stereotypes of its period; its influence does not make it a flawless portrayal of the people whose freedom it defended. Black writers and speakers, including Frederick Douglass, resisted the claim that white authors or political leaders should have the final word on Black experience.

## The line that would not hold

The Missouri Compromise of 1820 had prohibited slavery in much of the Louisiana Purchase north of a designated latitude. In 1854 the Kansas-Nebraska Act, advanced by Stephen Douglas, effectively repealed that restriction. It proposed that residents in the territories could decide slavery's status through popular sovereignty. Supporters presented the policy as democratic local choice. Opponents saw it as reopening territories that had long been closed to slavery, under pressure from slaveholding interests.

The conflict did not remain a parliamentary abstraction. Proslavery and antislavery settlers, including organized groups from beyond Kansas, attempted to shape elections and institutions. Voting fraud, armed intimidation, and competing governments turned the territory into a battlefield. Violence escalated in episodes that became known as Bleeding Kansas, including killings committed by both proslavery and antislavery militants. John Brown, later famous for his raid in Virginia, was involved in the murder of proslavery settlers at Pottawatomie Creek in 1856. A cause can be directed against an unjust institution while a particular act committed in its name remains murder; historical explanation should not erase either fact.

In Washington the breakdown became physical. In May 1856, after Senator Charles Sumner denounced proslavery politics in a speech, Representative Preston Brooks attacked him with a cane in the Senate chamber. Sumner suffered serious injuries. Reactions to the assault exposed the widening gulf in political culture: an act that appalled many in the North was defended or celebrated by some in the South. Congress increasingly looked less like a place where differences could be bargained into a common future.

The Whig Party collapsed as the slavery issue fractured former alliances. The new Republican Party emerged in the mid-1850s as a coalition opposed to the extension of slavery into federal territories. This was not identical to a party promising immediate abolition in every slave state. Its members included abolitionists, free-labor advocates, former Whigs, and others. The party's growth changed electoral arithmetic by creating the possibility of a national administration based largely on support from free states.

## When a territorial election became armed conflict

The Kansas-Nebraska Act's principle of popular sovereignty sounded as if local residents could simply decide their territorial institutions by voting. In practice the decision over slavery helped determine who would settle there in the first place. Proslavery activists from Missouri and antislavery migrants entered Kansas, sometimes with national financial or organizational backing. Fraudulent voting, competing authorities, intimidation, and armed groups undermined the premise of a neutral local ballot.

In 1856 proslavery forces attacked Lawrence, an antislavery center. John Brown and his associates retaliated by killing proslavery men near Pottawatomie Creek. Brown's actions were not a peaceful expression of electoral opinion; they were deliberate violence within a political struggle already marked by coercion. Different groups claimed to defend the community from aggression. Some proposed constitutional settlements only to face challenges over the legality of their elections and the legitimacy of the resulting governments.

The Lecompton Constitution controversy demonstrated how difficult it was to count the will of the people when the terms and administration of the choice were themselves contested. A proslavery territorial constitution was defended by President James Buchanan but rejected by important antislavery politicians and also by Stephen Douglas, who objected to the manipulated process. Party loyalties fractured over an attempt to make an institution politically legitimate through electoral forms that many settlers regarded as fraudulent.

The violence in Kansas mattered nationally because it supplied evidence for rival predictions. Antislavery Americans could argue that slavery's defenders would use force and fraud to extend their system. Proslavery Americans could argue that hostile settlers and armed abolitionists made their property insecure. Newspapers spread the most alarming incidents well beyond the territory. A policy meant to take the slavery question out of Congress instead made western settlement a rehearsal for civil war.

## Dred Scott and the limits of law

In 1857 the Supreme Court decided *Dred Scott v. Sandford*. Dred Scott, an enslaved man who had lived in areas where slavery was prohibited, sought freedom through the courts. Chief Justice Roger Taney's majority opinion asserted that people of African descent within the categories he described could not be citizens of the United States for the purposes of the suit, and the decision also declared that Congress lacked authority to bar slavery from federal territories through the Missouri Compromise restriction. The ruling was expansive, controversial, and profoundly damaging to the possibility that territorial compromise could settle the issue.

The case was never simply an impersonal constitutional puzzle. It was a freedom suit brought by a man and his family, whose legal status was being decided by judges while the country argued over the meaning of citizenship. The Court's opinion aimed to make certain questions final. Instead, it widened fears that a federal government and judiciary dominated by slaveholding interests might make slavery secure everywhere.

Abraham Lincoln, a former Whig politician in Illinois, grew more prominent as a Republican opponent of slavery's extension. In his debates with Douglas in 1858, he argued that slavery's growth threatened the republic. His position was not the same as the demand for immediate national racial equality; his own statements about race changed across his career and included limits and prejudices that should not be omitted. But he understood that the question of extending slavery could not be reduced to a matter for settlers indifferent to the moral and political nature of the institution.

## John Brown and the fear of insurrection

In October 1859 John Brown led a small armed group in an attack on the federal arsenal at Harpers Ferry, Virginia, hoping to spark a broader uprising against slavery. The plan failed, and several people were killed. Brown was captured, tried, and executed. For some abolitionists he became a martyr to the struggle against an unjust system. For many southern slaveholders he appeared to confirm the fear that their society faced violent attack encouraged by northern opinion. People could condemn Brown's methods, honor his opposition to slavery, or do both.

Brown's raid mattered as much for the meanings Americans attached to it as for its military scale. It demonstrated the widening gap between a society organized to protect human property and a movement whose moral language treated that property claim as illegitimate. Violence in Kansas and Virginia had already begun before any formal secession ordinance was passed.

## The election that changed everything

In 1860 the Democratic Party split, with northern Democrats backing Stephen Douglas and southern Democrats backing John C. Breckinridge. Constitutional Union candidates attempted to preserve the national framework without centering a direct solution to slavery. Republicans nominated Lincoln, who opposed slavery's expansion but did not campaign on an immediate federal abolition of slavery within states where it already existed. The split among his opponents helped him secure an Electoral College majority without winning a majority of the national popular vote. He received no electoral votes from the slave states that would form the Confederacy.

Leading secessionists concluded that a political movement hostile to slavery's extension had achieved the presidency without their consent. They feared the long-term weakening of slavery, even though Lincoln could not simply abolish it by executive decree in the existing slave states. In December 1860 South Carolina seceded; Mississippi, Florida, Alabama, Georgia, Louisiana, and Texas followed before Lincoln took office. Their political leaders formed a Confederate government and selected Jefferson Davis as president.

We should read the secessionists' own explanations rather than replace them with later slogans. The declarations issued by several states discussed slavery directly, including grievances that northern states had obstructed the recovery of escaped enslaved people and the fear that slave property would not remain secure. The Confederacy was built to protect slavery as a legal and social institution. Its leaders also talked about sovereignty, but that did not make slavery peripheral. The threat they identified was the possible destruction or containment of the system on which their political order depended.

## Read the secessionists' own explanations

The argument over why the Civil War began is sometimes presented as if later observers must infer causes from vague differences in culture. Contemporary secessionists left documents explaining their case. Mississippi declared its position thoroughly identified with slavery. South Carolina protested the failure of Northern states to enforce obligations concerning people escaping bondage. Texas and Georgia produced justifications bound up with slavery, racial hierarchy, and the protection of the slaveholding order. Alexander Stephens, vice president of the Confederacy, spoke openly in 1861 about the centrality of slavery to the new government he was helping establish.

These statements do not mean every Confederate soldier enlisted for an identical personal reason. An individual might speak about home, fear of invasion, duty to neighbors, or regional pride. The political cause for which a state seceded and the immediate motives of every person who fought for that state are related but distinct questions. The declarations of secession and the Confederacy's founding constitutional arrangements offer powerful evidence for the first question.

The states' rights argument requires similar precision. Secessionists claimed a state could withdraw from the Union, but proslavery political leaders had repeatedly demanded federal intervention to enforce slaveholder claims in free jurisdictions. They were not consistently arguing that national power should always be weak. They opposed the exercise of national power that might restrict slavery and demanded forms of national power that would protect it. The conflict concerned the substantive status of slavery within the republic, not an abstract preference for local government detached from what local governments chose to do.

The sequence of events also matters. Seven Deep South states seceded after Lincoln's election and before his inauguration. The Confederate government formed and claimed federal installations within its territory. The attack on Fort Sumter in April 1861 and Lincoln's call for volunteers were followed by the secession of four additional slave states, including Virginia. In the Upper South, decisions were not foreordained; local politics, perceived military coercion, and the broader slavery crisis interacted. Four slaveholding border states remained in the Union, a reminder that slaveholding did not mechanically produce the same governmental decision everywhere. The war began through political choices about power and slavery made under specific pressures, not through a mysterious accumulated dislike between two regions.

## A president, two governments, and federal property

Secession did not happen by unanimous popular consent. Southern Unionists opposed it, and the voting procedures and conventions varied. Many enslaved people had no vote yet were the people most directly affected by the outcome. Nor was the North unanimously committed to war. Some hoped for compromise; others worried that armed coercion would destroy the union it purported to save.

Lincoln took the oath of office on March 4, 1861, with a crisis over forts, arsenals, and other federal property already unfolding. His inaugural argument was that the Union could not be dissolved at will by individual states. He promised not to interfere with slavery where it existed, while rejecting the legitimacy of secession. That combination distinguished the immediate cause of the war from the longer cause of the sectional crisis. Slaveholding secessionists had broken with the Union because of slavery. The federal government's initial public war aim was to preserve the Union.

At Charleston harbor, United States troops held Fort Sumter while Confederate authorities claimed authority over the installation. Resupply, sovereignty, and the possibility of forcing an incident became entangled. On April 12 Confederate batteries opened fire. The fort surrendered after bombardment. The fighting caused no battle deaths during the bombardment itself, though deaths followed in an accidental explosion during the evacuation ceremonies.

Lincoln called for volunteers to suppress the rebellion. The call prompted another round of secession: Virginia, Arkansas, North Carolina, and Tennessee joined the Confederacy. The border slave states did not all follow them. Maryland, Kentucky, Missouri, and Delaware remained in the Union, under different political pressures and circumstances. The geography of loyalties was not as simple as a single line through the country.

The country had passed from threatened disunion into open war. Yet what kind of war it would be was still undetermined. Would a brief military campaign force the seceded states back? Could the Union survive without attacking slavery? Could people held in bondage make federal armies into instruments of liberation? The next years would answer those questions through decisions that military and political leaders often did not anticipate when they began.
