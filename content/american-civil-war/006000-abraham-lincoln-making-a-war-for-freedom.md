# Abraham Lincoln: Making a War for Union into a War for Freedom, 1809–1865

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

In August 1864 Abraham Lincoln feared that the voters might remove him from office before the war was won. The Confederacy still resisted. Union casualty lists were long. Political opponents offered a route to peace negotiations. Lincoln wrote a memorandum predicting that the administration was likely to lose reelection and outlining his duty to cooperate with a president-elect to save the Union in the interval before inauguration. He sealed the memorandum and asked members of his cabinet to sign the envelope without reading the contents. This was a president trying to think beyond his own political survival.

A few months later Lincoln won reelection. In April 1865, shortly after Confederate General Robert E. Lee surrendered his main army in Virginia, Lincoln was assassinated. Between those two dates lay the ending of an enormous war and the beginning of a national transformation that Lincoln would not live to direct. How had a self-educated lawyer from Illinois become the politician whose choices were so entangled with the fate of slavery and the republic?

Lincoln's life is often told as a moral ascent from humble origins to martyrdom. That shape contains truths, but it can hide what makes the story worth studying. Lincoln did not begin his political career with a fully formed program for universal emancipation or racial equality. He inherited prejudices, belonged to parties, lost elections, misjudged circumstances, made compromises, and changed his policies in response to events and to people who refused to remain passive. He became a statesman capable of significant moral and political development. That development is more instructive than a story in which the right answers were always present from the start.

## From frontier work to the law

Lincoln was born in Kentucky in 1809 to Thomas and Nancy Hanks Lincoln. His family moved to Indiana, then Illinois. His mother died when he was a child; his father remarried, and his stepmother Sarah Bush Johnston Lincoln became an important presence in the household. Lincoln's formal education was brief and intermittent. He read avidly, studied arithmetic and grammar, and sought a life different from the farm labor required of him as a boy.

Later generations made his poverty into a national fable. The reality was more particular: a frontier household working within a changing economy, frequent moves, little formal schooling, and a young man who developed unusual skill with written and spoken language. Working on river journeys and in stores, serving as a local postmaster and surveyor, and studying law gave Lincoln direct experience with the economic connections of the growing United States. He was not naturally immune to politics; he entered it young and lost his first legislative election before winning later contests.

In Illinois he became a lawyer and Whig politician. The legal profession required him to listen, improvise, examine witnesses, work through contracts, and make complicated disputes comprehensible to juries. Many famous Lincoln anecdotes describe dramatic courtroom victories; some are embellished in later memory. The lasting point is less theatrical. Legal practice trained him to distinguish the argument he wished to make from the argument another person might be able to hear.

Lincoln admired Henry Clay's program of economic development: internal improvements, banking, trade, and a national market joined by transportation. He served in the Illinois legislature and one term in the United States House of Representatives, where he criticized President James K. Polk's conduct of the Mexican-American War. That opposition was politically costly. Lincoln was a working politician with positions on tariffs, economic growth, constitutional power, and political organization long before he became a wartime president.

In 1842 he married Mary Todd, who was educated, politically interested, and connected to influential families. They had four sons. Edward died as a young child in Springfield, and Willie died in the White House in 1862. Their surviving sons at the time of the assassination were Robert and Tad. The family experienced both intimacy and difficulty under the strain of public life. It is easy to speculate about Lincoln's private sorrow because his face appears so familiar in photographs. The firmer evidence is that Willie died during a terrible war, and his parents' grief occurred alongside decisions affecting hundreds of thousands of soldiers and families.

## A question that could not be postponed

Lincoln's national rise was driven by the expansion of slavery. For much of his early career he shared the mainstream Whig position that the federal government had limited power to interfere with slavery in states where it already existed. He opposed slavery morally and politically, especially its extension into federal territories, but he was not an abolitionist organizer in the style of Frederick Douglass or William Lloyd Garrison. He believed slavery endangered the future of a republic whose Declaration proclaimed human equality, yet he also calculated within a legal and electoral order built around slaveholding power.

The Kansas-Nebraska Act of 1854 opened territory previously understood to be closed to slavery under the Missouri Compromise framework. It helped destroy older party alignments and drew Lincoln back into sustained political conflict. He challenged the idea that one white settler's freedom to carry enslaved people into a territory was merely an example of local self-government. For the enslaved person, the result was the denial of self-government in its most basic sense. This was a powerful argument, and it did not require Lincoln to endorse every form of political or social equality.

In 1858 Lincoln contested a Senate seat against Stephen Douglas. The public debates made him nationally known, though he lost the election conducted through the state legislature. Lincoln warned of a house divided against itself, arguing that the nation could not indefinitely remain half slave and half free. That did not mean he predicted immediate civil war as an inevitable event in every circumstance. He believed the republic was moving toward one kind of national settlement or the other. The Dred Scott decision and the debate over western territories made it difficult to imagine that slavery could be held in a stable regional box.

The debates also show Lincoln's limitations. Under pressure from Douglas's racial appeals, Lincoln rejected some forms of social and political equality for Black Americans in terms that are disturbing to readers now. His opposition to slavery was genuine; so were the racial assumptions he sometimes expressed. Historical understanding does not improve if we remove those statements from the record. Nor does it improve if we ignore the widening consequences of Lincoln's later actions because he did not begin where modern readers might wish him to begin.

When the Republican Party nominated him for president in 1860, it presented a platform opposing slavery's expansion into the territories, not an immediate plan to abolish slavery within every state. Lincoln won the election through Northern electoral strength while gaining essentially no support across the Deep South. Southern secessionists interpreted his victory as a warning that slaveholding influence at the national level could be permanently contained. The secession documents make clear that protecting slavery lay at the heart of their case. Lincoln did not cause secession by promising general emancipation. Slaveholding political leaders chose secession because they feared the direction of the Union under a president who opposed slavery's expansion.

## The Union before the emancipation proclamation

Lincoln took office in March 1861 amid the collapse of federal authority across much of the South. He argued that a state could not lawfully dissolve the Union unilaterally and pledged to hold federal property. At Fort Sumter in April, Confederate artillery opened fire on a United States garrison; Lincoln called for volunteers, and additional states joined the rebellion. A constitutional crisis became mass warfare.

Lincoln's first war aim was preservation of the Union. That statement is sometimes used to suggest slavery was incidental to the conflict. It was not. Slavery drove secession, but the federal government's initial political coalition included people who wanted union without immediate abolition, including important voters in slaveholding border states. Lincoln sought to keep Kentucky, Missouri, Maryland, and Delaware aligned with the United States while organizing an army and avoiding the appearance that the government had abandoned its constitutional obligations.

Events repeatedly outran his plans. Enslaved people fled into federal camps and asked for protection. Union commanders had to decide whether to return them to claimants and whether their labor should be used to support the war effort. Congress passed confiscation measures and moved against slavery in areas under federal jurisdiction. Abolitionists pressed for a declaration that could make the war a campaign for freedom. Black Americans were already changing the situation through flight, work, military service, intelligence, and argument. Lincoln did not invent that pressure, and his policy cannot be explained without it.

He also faced difficult choices about civil liberties, military power, and political dissent. The administration suspended habeas corpus in certain contexts, authorized arrests and military measures that provoked constitutional controversy, and faced opposition to conscription and wartime restrictions. His defenders argued that rebellion threatened the government's existence and required emergency action; critics accused him of exceeding lawful executive power. The questions were real, not merely the noise of an enemy's propaganda. Lincoln had to decide what it meant to preserve constitutional government while exercising powers that tested its limits.

## The dangerous path to emancipation

By the summer of 1862 Lincoln had concluded that slavery could no longer be left untouched as a matter of war policy. The Confederacy depended on enslaved labor. People escaping slavery had already undermined that labor system and increased federal capacity. A public declaration could weaken the Confederate war effort, clarify the stakes of the conflict, and affect European perceptions of American claims. But emancipation by executive war measure raised constitutional and political problems, and Lincoln's cabinet debated its timing.

Secretary of State William Seward counseled announcing the measure after a military success, lest it appear a desperate act by a failing government. Following the Battle of Antietam, Lincoln issued a preliminary proclamation in September 1862. On January 1, 1863, he announced freedom for enslaved people in designated areas still in rebellion. The proclamation did not immediately free everyone enslaved in the United States. It did not apply in the same way to loyal border states or certain exempted areas already under federal control. It rested on the president's asserted war powers against rebellion.

Yet describing those legal limits is not a reason to belittle the change. The proclamation committed federal military authority to recognizing and maintaining freedom in territories brought under its operation. It authorized Black enlistment and made emancipation part of the Union's war objective. In effect, Union military advance and the destruction of slavery became bound together. The measure did not create Black agency; it recognized, strengthened, and made national policy out of a process in which enslaved people were already participating.

Lincoln's thinking remained complex. In August 1862 he had spoken to a delegation of Black men in favor of colonization, a proposal for the voluntary resettlement of Black Americans outside the country. The scheme reveals how far some of his ideas remained from full interracial citizenship even as he moved toward emancipation. Frederick Douglass and other Black leaders challenged assumptions that African Americans ought to leave the land of their birth rather than claim their rights within it. We should not project Lincoln's later commitments backward to smooth away this conflict.

After the proclamation, Black soldiers joined the United States Army and Navy in large numbers. Their service mattered militarily and politically. They confronted discriminatory pay and assignments, the risk of enslavement or death if captured, and racist treatment from some white comrades. By fighting, they helped establish a claim to citizenship grounded in the service they were giving to a republic that had long denied them equal protection. Lincoln eventually met with Douglass, and the two men discussed recruitment, discrimination, and the fate of Black soldiers. The relationship was not a fable of instant agreement; it was a political exchange between men of different power who could recognize shared stakes.

## Gettysburg, reelection, and the meaning of sacrifice

In November 1863 Lincoln spoke at the dedication of a military cemetery in Gettysburg. The address was brief, but its argument was consequential. The nation had been conceived in liberty and dedicated to equality; the war tested whether such a nation could endure; the dead had consecrated the work through sacrifice; the living bore responsibility for a new birth of freedom. The speech did not abolish slavery or settle citizenship. It reframed what the Union was defending, linking government by the people to a stronger claim about the republic's founding promise.

In 1864 Lincoln's political position remained precarious. General Ulysses S. Grant pressed a costly campaign against Robert E. Lee in Virginia; armies fought at the Wilderness, Spotsylvania, Cold Harbor, and Petersburg. Union soldiers endured staggering losses, and civilians questioned the war's end. Democratic opponents criticized Lincoln's government and disagreed among themselves over how to seek peace. Some wanted negotiations even at the risk of Confederate independence or a settlement that left slavery in place; others opposed the administration's methods without endorsing secession. Lincoln could not treat political support as guaranteed simply because he regarded his cause as just.

The sealed memorandum of August 1864 shows him preparing for the possibility of defeat. The capture of Atlanta by Union forces under William Tecumseh Sherman in September improved public confidence, and Lincoln won reelection in November. The election was not a referendum conducted in perfect equality: Confederate states were outside the electoral process, African American men were widely excluded from voting, and suffrage rules varied. But it demonstrated that, amid a devastating war, presidential power still remained answerable to an electoral process.

Lincoln's second inaugural address in March 1865 took a striking tone. Instead of announcing the conflict as a simple tale of northern innocence and southern wickedness, he recognized the centrality of slavery to the war and contemplated its suffering as a judgment upon the entire nation. That was not moral equivalence between the decision to defend slavery by secession and the fight to preserve the Union. It was a refusal to imagine that the consequences of generations of bondage could be laid entirely outside the republic's shared history. His appeal for reconciliation did not erase the obligation to determine what emancipation would require.

## What Lincoln did not get to finish

In the war's final months Lincoln supported the Thirteenth Amendment, which would abolish slavery across the United States, except as punishment for crime after conviction. Unlike the wartime proclamation, constitutional abolition did not depend on a continuing state of rebellion or geographical exemptions. Congress passed the proposed amendment in January 1865; it was ratified after Lincoln's death in December. The shift from military measure to constitutional rule marked a fundamental change in what the federal Union would formally permit.

After Lee surrendered at Appomattox on April 9, Lincoln faced the problem of how former Confederate states should return to national political life. That included the rights of people who had been enslaved, the future of the former slaveholders' political power, the protection of Unionists in the South, and the authority of Congress and the president. In an April 11 public address Lincoln endorsed limited Black voting rights, particularly for some educated Black men and Black veterans. This fell short of universal male suffrage, but it moved beyond his earlier public position. We cannot know with certainty what Reconstruction policy he would have achieved had he lived, or how he would have responded to later conflicts.

On April 14, 1865, the actor and Confederate sympathizer John Wilkes Booth shot Lincoln at Ford's Theatre in Washington. Lincoln died the following morning. His death occurred while slavery's national abolition had not yet been ratified and while the meaning of freedom remained bitterly contested. The assassination turned a living politician, whose future choices could be questioned, into a national martyr. That transformation has complicated his memory ever since.

There is no need to pretend Lincoln was always the most radical antislavery voice of his time. Others demanded emancipation earlier and paid immense costs while insisting that the nation live up to its declarations. Lincoln's historical distinction lies in how he responded as political and military circumstances changed. He helped hold together a coalition for the Union, then used federal power to make the destruction of slavery central to its victory, and supported constitutional abolition. Those achievements are neither diminished by acknowledging his limitations nor properly understood without acknowledging the people who pushed history beyond the limits of his first intentions.

When we remember him, the decisive story is not of an isolated genius bestowing freedom. It is of a republic in conflict, a president learning the consequences of the war he led, and millions of people making claims whose realization required politics, armies, legislation, and constitutional change. Lincoln helped carry the nation from one understanding of union to another. The unfinished work of turning emancipation into equality would fall to those who survived him.
