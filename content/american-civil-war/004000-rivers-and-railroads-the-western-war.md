# Rivers, Railroads, and the War for the West: From Fort Donelson to Atlanta

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

A map of the Civil War can create a misleading first impression. The capitals, Washington and Richmond, were only about a hundred miles apart. Large armies fought in Virginia, and Robert E. Lee's campaigns have dominated many accounts of the conflict. But to understand how the United States defeated the Confederacy, we must move westward, toward the Ohio and Mississippi rivers, the Tennessee and Cumberland rivers, the Gulf of Mexico, and the railroad junctions of the interior. Here the war was not principally a series of attempts to capture an enemy capital. It was an effort to unmake the geography that allowed the Confederacy to supply and sustain itself.

Imagine a bale of cotton, a barrel of flour, a load of iron, or a regiment that needs to move from one place to another. Roads could be slow and nearly impassable in bad weather. Railroads carried tremendous capacity but ran along fixed lines, required bridges and repair, and could be cut by cavalry raids. Rivers were natural highways, especially when the advancing army could use steam transports and gunboats. A city controlling a river junction or rail crossing might be valuable even when it was not the political center of a state. That is the logic of the western war.

## Two rivers break a defensive line

At the beginning of 1862 Confederate forces attempted to hold a line across Kentucky and Tennessee. Ulysses S. Grant, commanding United States soldiers in cooperation with naval forces, attacked Fort Henry on the Tennessee River and Fort Donelson on the Cumberland. The important fact is the connection between these waterways. Losing the forts opened navigable routes deep into Confederate-held territory, permitting troops, supplies, and armed vessels to bypass portions of the overland defenses.

At Fort Henry, Union gunboats helped force the surrender of a poorly situated fort in February. At Fort Donelson, Confederate defenders initially resisted and badly damaged attacking gunboats, but their command failed to exploit an opening to escape. Grant demanded an immediate surrender without negotiated conditions. The victory brought thousands of prisoners and made his name nationally recognizable. More important, it destabilized the Confederate position in Kentucky and Tennessee. Nashville fell to federal forces, and the United States gained a platform from which to move deeper south.

Military legends sometimes turn Grant into a commander who simply charged forward regardless of cost. Fort Donelson shows a more interesting capacity: to recognize that the enemy's position was part of a larger network and that a surrender could change the whole map. Grant could take risks, sometimes serious ones, but his campaigns repeatedly connected military action to control of transportation, supply, and initiative.

## Shiloh and the price of learning

In April 1862 Confederate forces under Albert Sidney Johnston and P. G. T. Beauregard attacked Grant's army near Pittsburg Landing in Tennessee. The ensuing Battle of Shiloh was among the war's most shocking early encounters. The first day brought ferocious fighting and the death of Johnston. Federal forces held near the river, received reinforcements, and drove back the Confederates on the second day. The battle revealed the capacity of volunteer armies to inflict casualties on a scale many civilians had not anticipated.

Shiloh is important for more than its toll. Both sides had entered the war with uncertain ideas about how quickly fighting might settle the political question. Here the industrial and administrative foundations of a prolonged war became more evident. An army could suffer, re-form, bring up reinforcements, and fight again. A commander might win a field and still leave the other army capable of renewed operations. The fighting consumed ammunition, medical supplies, food, horses, and human endurance. Every victory or defeat imposed new requirements on railroads, steamboats, hospitals, and governments.

The campaign also sharpened disputes among Union commanders. Grant was criticized. His superior Henry Halleck took a more direct role in operations. Ambitious officers competed over assignments, reputation, and credit. The western war was not conducted by a single harmonious team whose strategy emerged fully formed. It developed through organizational friction, changing command structures, and costly experiments.

## New Orleans and the river that still was not open

In April 1862 naval forces under David Farragut passed Confederate forts near the mouth of the Mississippi and captured New Orleans, the great port city of the lower river. The loss was severe for the Confederacy: it deprived the rebellion of a major commercial center and complicated communication. But controlling New Orleans did not mean the Union controlled the entire Mississippi. Confederate strongholds at Vicksburg and Port Hudson continued to obstruct federal passage.

This is a useful lesson in the geography of strategic claims. On a political map a river may appear as one line. In military operations it is a succession of bends, heights, harbors, batteries, crossings, and vulnerable transport points. A city perched high above a bend can command miles of river traffic. Vicksburg in Mississippi occupied such a position. Its defenses were connected to roads and railroads, and it protected communication between the Confederacy's regions on either side of the Mississippi. Capturing it was not as simple as sending an army to its gates.

Civilians living around the rivers experienced invasion, confiscation, supply requisitions, labor demands, and the rapid collapse of the slaveholders' control when enslaved people fled to United States positions. The advance of military power created opportunities and dangers that were not visible on headquarters maps. For a family on a plantation in Mississippi or Louisiana, the arrival of federal forces could mean the chance to escape bondage, the disappearance of a familiar but coercive economy, or new vulnerability to violence and disease.

## Grant's campaign against Vicksburg

Grant attempted several approaches before undertaking the campaign that captured Vicksburg in 1863. The difficulty was not simply that the fortifications were strong. Water levels, muddy terrain, waterways running through swampy ground, vulnerable supply lines, and Confederate cavalry raids complicated movement. Some operations failed. Others achieved small improvements but did not yet solve the problem. Rather than mistake repeated effort for certainty, we can examine how Grant changed the operational question.

In the spring he moved his army south along the western bank of the Mississippi, while naval vessels ran past Vicksburg's batteries at great risk. When an initial crossing plan at Grand Gulf proved impractical, forces crossed farther south at Bruinsburg in late April. Grant then chose not to make an immediate direct assault on Vicksburg from a predictable direction. He moved inland, defeating Confederate forces at Port Gibson, turning toward Jackson, the state capital and a rail connection, and preventing separate Confederate forces from combining against him.

The sequence of engagements mattered. The Union victory at Champion Hill on May 16 cut down the possibilities available to Lieutenant General John C. Pemberton, whose army retreated toward Vicksburg. Federal forces pursued through the Big Black River area, and the campaign became a siege. Grant had turned movement through an exposed region into a position from which he could isolate and compel the surrender of a fortified city.

The siege imposed severe hardship on soldiers and civilians inside Vicksburg. People sought shelter from bombardment, food became scarce, and disease compounded suffering. After failed assaults, the Union army invested the city more systematically. The surrender came on July 4, 1863, almost simultaneously with the Confederate retreat from Gettysburg in Pennsylvania. Port Hudson surrendered shortly afterward, giving the United States control of the full Mississippi River. The results were enormous: the Confederacy was further divided, and federal navigation and supply were opened along a principal artery of the continent.

Vicksburg makes Grant's military reputation clearer than the stereotype of relentless frontal attack. He sought operational opportunities, used navy-army cooperation, accepted the complexity of supply, forced separate enemy commands to respond on his terms, and exploited success in a sequence of related engagements. None of that made his leadership flawless. It does explain why his approach could alter the strategic possibilities of the war.

## Chattanooga: an army saved and a gateway opened

The fighting farther east in Tennessee shows how victory and defeat could trade places within months. In September 1863 the Confederate Army of Tennessee under Braxton Bragg defeated the Army of the Cumberland under William Rosecrans at Chickamauga in northern Georgia. The federal army retreated to Chattanooga. Confederate forces occupied threatening heights and restricted supply routes. An army might have control of a city and still become dangerously weakened if food and ammunition could not reach it.

Grant, now placed in command of the western armies, helped organize relief operations. The United States restored essential supply connections, reinforced the garrison, and in November attacked the Confederate positions. Battles around Lookout Mountain and Missionary Ridge ended with Bragg's army driven back. Soldiers of the Army of the Cumberland advanced up Missionary Ridge with an energy that surprised some commanders, demonstrating again that military events could diverge from headquarters plans.

Chattanooga was a transport center with strategic depth. Its capture secured a major logistical gateway into Georgia. After the fighting, the United States possessed a base from which an army could march toward Atlanta. Grant's successes contributed to his promotion to general-in-chief in 1864, when he would coordinate operations across multiple theaters. William Tecumseh Sherman would conduct the major campaign from Chattanooga to Atlanta.

## Atlanta and the politics of military time

Sherman's 1864 advance into Georgia was not a single spectacular battle. It was a campaign of movements, entrenched positions, cavalry activity, attacks on transport connections, and repeated attempts to force Confederate defenders from advantageous ground. Joseph E. Johnston initially directed the defense and often withdrew from threatened positions rather than allow his army to be trapped. Critics considered this too cautious. Confederate President Jefferson Davis replaced him with John Bell Hood, who launched aggressive attacks and suffered severe losses.

Sherman understood that Atlanta mattered as a railroad center, supply hub, industrial city, and political symbol. But advancing toward it depended on a long line of communication stretching back into Tennessee. Soldiers needed rail transport and guards for bridges and tracks; Confederate cavalry sought to disrupt those connections. Every few miles of advance required thinking about the means to feed the next day's army.

After months of fighting, Sherman maneuvered against Atlanta's remaining railroad connections and forced its evacuation in early September. For the people living there the military success meant evacuation, upheaval, and the damage of war to homes and industry. For the United States, the city's capture was an enormous political event. Northern voters had been reading casualty lists and questioning whether the war could end. The arrival of tangible success strengthened confidence in President Lincoln and helped him win reelection that autumn.

This relationship between battle and election matters. Military outcomes did not dictate what voters thought, and an army was not merely an electoral instrument. Yet a democratic government conducting a long war needed political consent, and public confidence could influence whether its strategy remained sustainable. The Confederacy hoped in part that continued resistance would exhaust that consent. Atlanta damaged the prospect that Union voters would choose a settlement leaving Confederate independence intact.

## Beyond Atlanta: destroying the means of war

After taking Atlanta, Sherman marched across Georgia toward Savannah in late 1864. His troops relied heavily on foraging and systematically damaged railroads, infrastructure, stores, and other resources useful to Confederate military efforts. The campaign brought hardship and destruction to civilians as well as military installations. Some actions reflected explicit orders; others were the products of discipline breaking down, coercion, opportunism, or the difficulty of distinguishing property sustaining an army from property sustaining a family. Describing the campaign as strategically aimed at resources does not make civilian suffering disappear.

The march also changed the possibilities available to people held in slavery. Enslaved people sometimes followed the army, aided its movements, fled owners, and asked whether the new freedom could include land on which to live and work. In January 1865 Sherman issued Special Field Orders Number Fifteen, setting aside specified coastal areas for settlement by freed Black families under military authority. The later reversal or erosion of these arrangements helped make land ownership one of Reconstruction's most painful unresolved questions. Even a victorious army could not alone determine the social order that would follow.

The western and southern campaigns thus explain why the Confederate war effort weakened. The Union did not need to win every battle in Virginia to destroy Confederate capacity. It captured rivers, ports, transportation hubs, and cities; it disrupted supply networks; it brought federal authority into regions that had supported secession; and it increasingly struck at the labor system on which the Confederacy depended. Each campaign changed what future armies could accomplish.

At the same time, military geography never tells the entire story. The same river that supplied an advancing army could carry people escaping enslavement. The same railroad that moved artillery could later bring aid to a wounded soldier or food to a desperate settlement. The strategic map was a map of human lives. The western war made those connections visible, and without it the story of Union victory is not merely incomplete. It is causally misleading.
