# Deliver ventilation and shared-system airflow

## Heat-Recovery Ventilation, Filters, and Pressure Boundaries

A well-insulated house can be uncomfortable for a reason that insulation cannot fix: the air inside it is not being exchanged, distributed, or cleaned appropriately. People breathe, cook, bathe, and bring materials into the house. Exhaust fans remove air. Outdoor air arrives whether invited through a planned system or pulled through cracks. Once the building's air boundary becomes tighter, deliberate ventilation matters more, not less. Heat-recovery ventilation is one way to make that exchange more controlled.

### Heat and air are different flows

Imagine two ducts running through a mechanical unit. One carries stale indoor air out. The other brings outdoor air in. In a heat-recovery ventilator, or HRV, a heat exchanger transfers some sensible heat between those separate airstreams. In winter, outgoing warm air can preheat incoming cold air without the two flows deliberately mixing. In summer, the same exchanger can reduce some cooling load when outdoor air is hotter than indoor air. An energy-recovery ventilator, or ERV, may additionally transfer some moisture as vapor, depending on the exchanger design. Neither device generates free heat; both use fans and have losses.

People sometimes imagine an HRV as a heater. That leads to poor expectations. If the indoor air is seventy degrees Fahrenheit and the outdoors is ten, a heat exchanger may bring the supply temperature partway toward seventy. The result can still feel cool near a diffuser. A well-designed system coordinates supply air location, balancing, defrost behavior, and any needed supplementary tempering. The purpose is ventilation with reduced heat penalty, not an independently heated bedroom.

A conceptual efficiency number can help. Suppose, for illustration, outdoor air enters at ten degrees and the indoor exhaust air starts at seventy. If the fresh supply leaving the exchanger is fifty degrees, it has recovered forty of the sixty degrees of temperature difference, an apparent temperature effectiveness of about two-thirds under those conditions. That is not automatically a certified energy efficiency value. Actual enthalpy transfer, frost, fan power, leakage, airflow imbalance, and equipment testing standards can change the appropriate performance metric.

### Balanced air requires actual measurement

A 'balanced' ventilator aims to bring in approximately as much outdoor air as it exhausts, under its design conditions. That does not mean placing both fan knobs at the same mark. Ducts may have different bends, lengths, filters, dampers, and exterior terminations. Their resistances differ. Fan curves show that airflow changes with system pressure. A supply filter that becomes loaded can shift flow even if the exhaust fan keeps spinning at the same speed.

Why care about balance? A persistent excess of exhaust can depressurize the house, drawing outdoor or soil air through unintended cracks. Supply-dominant operation can push humid indoor air toward cold enclosure cavities in winter in some climates. These are not universal predictions for every building; envelope tightness, weather, other fans, and mechanical systems all change the pressure response. The key is to measure and understand the resulting pressure field rather than assume balance from the wiring diagram.

Consider a bathroom fan, kitchen hood, clothes dryer, and HRV running at once. Each has its own airflow. The kitchen hood may move much more air than a small whole-house ventilator. The air must be replaced somehow. If replacement air does not have an intentional path, the house may pull it through leaks or competing appliance vents. Combustion appliances make pressure evaluation especially important. Ventilation cannot be designed in isolation from the building's other air-moving equipment.

### Filters have two jobs and one cost

A filter captures particles according to its media and rating. It also introduces pressure loss. Finer filtration is valuable only if the fan and duct system can sustain the airflow required through that filter. Selecting a higher-rated filter without checking capacity can reduce ventilation enough to compromise the purpose. A dirty filter may become progressively more restrictive, increasing fan energy or lowering flow depending on motor and controls.

The supply filter protects the exchanger and occupants from incoming particulates. The exhaust filter, where provided, can protect heat-exchanger passages from indoor dust. Their maintenance intervals cannot be set purely by the calendar: outdoor pollution, indoor activities, seasonal pollen, occupancy, and fan runtime all matter. Observe pressure drop or actual condition where the system permits. Filters should be changed using the specified size, orientation, sealing method, and approved grade.

Ventilation equipment can also accumulate condensate. In cold conditions, moisture from warm exhaust can freeze in the exchanger. Manufacturers may provide defrost cycles, bypass dampers, heaters, or fan modulation. A brief change in flow or temperature may therefore be part of normal operation. Persistent ice, blocked drains, or low airflow may indicate a fault. Never block a drain or disable defrost merely to quiet a symptom.

### Commission the entire path

A commissioning check traces the full system, not only the appliance. Identify outdoor intake and exhaust locations, duct insulation where needed, condensate routing, filter access, electrical controls, and the location of interior diffusers. Measure delivered supply and exhaust flows using an appropriate method. Check fan response with doors and other exhaust equipment in representative states. Document the settings so later maintenance can distinguish degradation from an unknown initial condition.

Then test actual use. Are bedrooms ventilated when doors close? Can occupants reach filters without disassembling a wall? Is there noise or short-circuiting between outdoor intake and exhaust? Are rain, snow, and wildlife likely to obstruct the terminations? A heat exchanger with excellent laboratory efficiency cannot compensate for an obstructed outdoor intake or a disconnected duct.

The essential idea is to treat ventilation as a system of mass flow, heat flow, moisture flow, and filtration. The heat exchanger is one component. Successful ventilation maintains adequate outdoor-air delivery while controlling undesirable pressure effects and keeping maintenance possible. That is what transforms a device into a useful building system.

## Commissioning Balanced Ventilation and Finding Fan-Flow Imbalance

A ventilation design has to be checked against delivered airflow, not just the indication that both fans are running.

A balanced ventilation system can be installed, powered on, and apparently operating while the building receives much less fresh air than the owner expects. One fan may be working against a blocked outdoor grille. Another may be moving air through a cleaner, shorter duct. The control panel still says the fans are on. To know whether the system performs its job, we must measure the air it actually moves.

An energy-recovery ventilator, or ERV, transfers some heat and often some moisture between outgoing exhaust air and incoming outdoor air through its core, without intentionally mixing the two air streams. A heat-recovery ventilator, or HRV, primarily transfers sensible heat. Both have a supply path and an exhaust path. Their nominal flow settings are starting points. Delivered airflow depends on fan performance, filter loading, duct resistance, installation details and operating mode.

### What does 'balanced' describe?

In its simplest sense, balanced mechanical ventilation delivers approximately as much outdoor air to the building as it exhausts through the device over a chosen operating condition. If the supply measures 80 cubic feet per minute and exhaust measures 80, the device is balanced at that test point. If supply measures 50 and exhaust 90, the unit has a 40-cubic-foot-per-minute exhaust excess at that condition.

That arithmetic does not mean the whole house has exactly a 40-cubic-foot-per-minute infiltration flow at every moment. Other fans, fireplaces, wind, stack effect and duct leakage affect pressure and exchange. Nor does equal flow automatically guarantee that each occupied room receives sufficient outside air. The required total flow and distribution must come from the building's ventilation design and applicable standard, not a convenient round number.

Airflow is often inferred incorrectly from noise. A loud supply fan may be laboring at high static pressure while delivering less air. The same fan can sound different when a filter loads. A nominal label such as '100 CFM' may refer to performance at specified test conditions rather than the delivered flow of an installed duct system.

### Separate air paths before reaching for controls

Trace the outdoor-air intake from its weather hood to its filter and heat-exchange core. Follow the supply through its fan and ducts into the building. Now follow stale indoor air through the extract grilles, exhaust fan, core, and outdoor discharge. At each step, note where a blockage, leak, damper, reversed connection or pressure loss could occur. A duct that leaks into an attic may move significant air at the fan but not deliver all of it to the intended rooms.

Outdoor intakes can be obstructed by debris, snow or ice. Exhaust hoods may be blocked or poorly located. Filters and heat exchangers can accumulate material, and defrost modes can temporarily change the normal flows. A clean-looking filter does not prove low resistance, and an unfamiliar fan speed does not prove a controls fault. The first step is to identify the actual operating mode and the physical state of the paths.

Duct layout matters. A long run with many tight bends offers more resistance than a shorter, properly sized run, although the exact loss depends on diameter, roughness and flow. Replacing a duct with a smaller one because it is easier to route can move the fan to a very different point on its pressure-flow curve. Simply increasing fan speed might compensate at a cost in noise and energy, or may fail to reach the needed flow at all.

### Commissioning is a measurement sequence

Before measuring, establish what design flow the system is supposed to provide in its normal modes, where the terminals are, and which filters and dampers should be in place. Confirm outdoor hoods are clear and that the system is operating in the specified mode. Then measure the flow with suitable, calibrated equipment at the designated test points or terminals using a method compatible with the installation. For some systems the manufacturer provides calibrated pressure taps or diagnostic procedures; for others a professional commissioning method uses flow hoods or traverse measurements. Measurement location and instrument uncertainty matter.

Imagine an installation where measured supply is 70 and exhaust is 105 cubic feet per minute. Closing an exhaust damper until the displays say 'equal' is not adequate if the fan is in an unexpected high-speed boost mode, the supply filter is blocked, or the controls require a different commissioning sequence. First find the cause of the mismatch. Then adjust approved balancing dampers or fan settings according to the equipment instructions, and remeasure both sides at the same operating condition.

Test more than one mode if the system has continuous background ventilation and boost. Verify control response, condensate drainage where provided, frost protection, filter access, and outdoor openings. Ask how the device behaves when a kitchen exhaust hood or dryer runs. A balanced ERV by itself cannot cancel every other exhaust appliance in the house.

### Indoor conditions reveal a second layer

After airflow commissioning, indoor humidity, odors, comfort and pressure observations help reveal what the numbers do not. High indoor humidity might mean insufficient ventilation, but it can also reflect a wet basement, excessive indoor moisture production, poor heating, or an unsealed envelope. Very dry winter air can result from high outdoor-air exchange, but the correct response is not automatically to throttle ventilation below design. The system must maintain its indoor-air-quality purpose.

A practical record includes the filter type and condition, device mode, supply/exhaust flow, pressure or fan setting as applicable, outdoor conditions, noise, control changes and maintenance dates. Repeating measurements after a filter change or seasonal service exposes drift. A device that was balanced when clean can become unbalanced as filters load at different rates.

Ventilation commissioning teaches an essential distinction: operating is not the same as performing. A pair of spinning fans is evidence of motion. Measured supply and exhaust flows under documented conditions are evidence of ventilation performance. The goal is not symmetry for its own sake; it is reliable delivery of the designed air exchange with the expected pressure, energy and comfort consequences.

## Measuring Duct Static Pressure and Room Air Delivery

A room is colder than the others. You can feel air coming out of its register, so the duct must be fine. Or perhaps not. A little airflow at the grille does not establish the quantity of air reaching the room. Conversely, a loud grille may be evidence of a restriction, not a sign of generous air delivery.

To diagnose a ducted heating or cooling system, we need two different kinds of measurement. Pressure tells us something about the resistance faced by the fan. Airflow tells us how much air the system actually moves. Neither one automatically gives the other without knowing the equipment and the measurement conditions.

### What static pressure means

Imagine a blower drawing air through a filter and return duct, then pushing it through a coil and supply ducts. On the suction side, the pressure relative to the room is often negative. On the discharge side it is positive. Static pressure describes pressure not associated with the air's directional kinetic energy. Technicians use a manometer and properly placed pressure probes to sample it.

For a specified equipment configuration, total external static pressure refers to the pressure difference the blower must overcome across the external components defined by the manufacturer's rating. That definition matters: a filter or coil may be included or excluded depending on how the equipment and test locations are defined. A number without a test-point diagram can be meaningless. The result is usually expressed in inches of water column or pascals.

A fan has a performance relationship between pressure and flow at a given speed. Increase the resistance and many fans deliver less air. An electronically controlled variable-speed blower may attempt to hold a target airflow by increasing speed and power, up to its limits. Thus a dirty filter may produce lower flow on one system and higher blower effort on another. Pressure data must be interpreted with the manufacturer's fan performance information and the active operating mode.

### Pressure is not flow

Picture two duct systems with the same measured supply pressure. One has a short wide duct, and the other a long restrictive duct with several bends. They need not have the same flow. A pressure measurement can indicate excessive resistance, but it does not directly measure the air volume moved unless paired with a suitable calibrated method or performance relation.

Airflow at room registers can be measured with an appropriate flow hood or other validated technique. An anemometer reading at one point on a grille is not automatically representative of the entire opening: velocity varies over the face, and the grille itself disturbs the profile. A proper test may need area correction or a specific capture method. Duct leak tests, fan airflow measurements and register delivery measurements are distinct procedures answering distinct questions.

Now consider an imaginary three-room system. The measured delivery is 110 cubic feet per minute to the first room, 85 to the second, and 55 to the third. Those figures add to 250 cubic feet per minute at the room registers. That total may be smaller than airflow through the fan because some air leaks to an attic or crawlspace, or because other registers were not included. It cannot by itself prove a fan rated at 300 is underperforming. You would need the design flows, complete room inventory and operating condition.

### A disciplined test sequence

Record the filter model and condition, equipment settings, mode of operation, fan speed command, open dampers, outdoor conditions and which doors are closed. Measure the return and supply static pressures at correct equipment-specific locations. Compare with the maker's published limits or fan tables. Then measure delivered room flows and compare them with design expectations. Document duct leakage and return pathways separately when indicated.

The sequence matters because the system can change while you test. A smart thermostat may change fan speed. A heat pump may enter defrost. A variable-speed system may ramp after several minutes. A closed bedroom door can alter the room's pressure relative to the hall and affect the return path. Numbers from different modes cannot be meaningfully combined as if they described one state.

One room's poor comfort might be caused by inadequate supply flow, excessive heat loss, solar exposure, a blocked return path, thermostat location, zoning decisions or a duct leak. Start by comparing how the symptom changes with doors, filter state, blower mode and outside conditions. Do not 'balance' a room by randomly closing other registers before you know the design intent. Increasing local noise while starving the equipment of total flow can make the system worse.

### A useful diagnostic distinction

Suppose total external static pressure is high and all registers deliver less air than expected. System-wide restriction deserves attention: a clogged filter, restricted return, duct geometry, blocked coil or closed damper. Suppose total pressure is within the equipment's expected range but one room is consistently low. Local duct or balancing defects rise on the list. Suppose airflow is adequate but the room still overheats in afternoon sun. The dominant problem may lie in solar gain rather than in the supply duct.

The measurements are useful because they divide possibilities. Good commissioning records identify exact test locations, operating conditions, instrument method and comparison criteria. A repair should be followed by the same measurements, under comparable conditions. Static pressure shows the cost of forcing air through a network. Airflow shows what the network actually delivers. Comfort depends on those quantities and on the building around them.

## Variable Refrigerant Flow: Shared Capacity, Zone Controls, and Heat Recovery

Shared refrigerant capacity adds another distribution problem in which one zone’s state interacts with the larger system.

Picture an office floor with a meeting room full of people beside a sunlit perimeter office. The meeting room needs cooling while another zone may need heating. In an ordinary single-zone system, one thermostat decides what the equipment does. A variable refrigerant flow system, often called VRF, coordinates many indoor units that share part of the same refrigerant infrastructure. Its appeal is local comfort control and the ability to modulate capacity. Its difficulty is that each room is independent only up to a point: the zones still share compressors, pipes, capacity limits and a control network.

To understand this technology, forget the product catalog for a moment. Think of it as a network of heat exchangers joined to a controllable heat-moving machine. An outdoor assembly contains one or more compressors and heat exchangers. Multiple indoor fan coils take or reject heat to the occupied spaces. Electronic expansion devices help meter refrigerant flow, and controllers request heating or cooling according to zone conditions. The equipment adjusts compressor operation and valve states to serve the combined load.

### Variable flow does not mean unlimited capacity

A conventional single-speed air conditioner cycles its compressor on and off in response to demand. A VRF system can vary refrigerant mass flow and compressor capacity over a useful range. When only a few rooms need conditioning, the system may operate at part load rather than producing full output and cycling repeatedly. But capacity modulation has minimum and maximum limits. At extremely low load, the equipment may still cycle; at peak load, it may not supply every zone's requested output.

Imagine four rooms with simultaneous cooling requests equivalent to one, two, three and four units of heat removal. The outdoor system must move ten units if all requests are to be met immediately. If its available capacity is eight, there must be some form of capacity management. The controller may redistribute output, change fan or valve behavior, tolerate temperature deviations or enforce priority rules. Nothing in the word 'variable' removes the shared bottleneck.

### Heat-pump operation versus heat recovery

A simple heat-pump-style VRF arrangement may permit many zones to call for different levels of heating, or many to call for different levels of cooling, but not necessarily both at the same time. A heat-recovery configuration uses additional refrigerant distribution and control to transfer rejected heat from cooling zones toward zones requesting heat, subject to system conditions. This can reduce the net energy demanded from the outdoors when the building has opposing loads.

One should not confuse simultaneous heating and cooling capability with free heat. Pumps, compressors, fans and controls still consume power. Refrigerant circuits have pressure drops; heat exchanger temperatures determine performance; the usable balance of heating and cooling varies. A building that needs much more heat than its internal cooling zones reject must obtain the remaining energy from outside or supplementary heat sources.

This leads to a design question: does the building's diversity of loads actually match the proposed system? An all-glass west-facing zone, a server room and a windowless conference room may have very different load patterns. A plan that assumes every zone peaks at the same hour can misstate shared capacity, while a plan that assumes diversity without measuring usage can be equally misleading.

### Controls as a network

Each indoor unit reports temperatures and operating status and receives commands from local and supervisory controls. Refrigerant circuit operation must coordinate those requests. A person may set a room thermostat to cool and see no immediate response because another operating mode has priority, a compressor has a protective delay, a connected controller limits the setpoint or the outdoor system is in defrost. That is not always a failed thermostat.

In diagnosis, distinguish a local fault from a shared one. If one room is uncomfortable while others perform well, examine its airflow, filter, temperature sensing, local valve operation and control configuration. If every zone served by a common outdoor unit deteriorates together, investigate shared capacity, refrigerant circuit, operating mode, outdoor conditions or communications. A control network failure can imitate a mechanical one. So can a dirty indoor coil or a badly placed temperature sensor.

Suppose a heating-mode complaint arises only after a cold outdoor morning. The system might be encountering defrost or a capacity constraint rather than a random loss of refrigerant. Defrost behavior and frost accumulation depend on outdoor temperature, humidity, heat exchanger conditions and manufacturer controls. The same symptom in a single zone on a mild day may point elsewhere. Record which units changed state together and the sequence of status messages.

### Commissioning before troubleshooting

A multi-zone system should be commissioned as a collection of connected subsystems. The installer needs correct refrigerant piping design, supported line lengths and height differences, addressing and network communication, condensate disposal, airflow, insulation, controls setup and prescribed refrigerant and electrical checks. Poorly documented branch connections can make later troubleshooting remarkably difficult. A room label should map to an indoor unit, controller address, branch connection and outdoor system.

Consider the handover value of a simple table: room name, indoor-unit model, controller address, connected branch, design load, airflow target and measured commissioning result. When an occupant reports a comfort problem, the service technician can compare present behavior to a known baseline. Without that mapping, even identifying the correct valve and sensor becomes guesswork.

VRF systems make a central engineering trade: local control with shared heat-moving infrastructure. They can be efficient and flexible, but the price of that flexibility is more coordination. The most effective diagnostic habit is to observe individual rooms and the common equipment at the same time. One thermostat tells you what a room wants; the shared controller and measured temperatures tell you what the system can actually deliver.

### Measuring Duct Static Pressure and Room Air Delivery

### 020000 — Measuring Duct Static Pressure and Room Air Delivery

#### Non-spoken technical companion

**Equations and units.** For a defined test boundary, external static pressure across the air handler is the signed difference between downstream and upstream pressure taps; with negative return and positive supply gauge readings, the magnitudes add. An inch water gauge is approximately 249 pascals. Manufacturer placement diagrams and component inclusion rules determine which pressures to use.

**Illustrative data record.** Mode / blower speed setting / filter state / return tap / supply tap / equipment limit / room-hood readings / door positions / outdoor and indoor dry-bulb temperatures. Never compare a measured pressure against a generic universal maximum without confirming the equipment's approved range.

**Common interpretation errors.** A face-velocity spot reading is not duct volume flow; grille capture requires a validated technique. Register sum is not necessarily total fan flow. Duct pressurization leakage testing is not the same as operating airflow. A single static-pressure reading cannot establish a precise flow without an applicable fan curve and test method.

**References.** DOE Building America Solution Center, *Heat Pump Quality Installation and Commissioning*; ENERGY STAR HVAC Contractor guidance and airflow measurements; ASHRAE, *Testing, Adjusting, and Balancing* (applicable edition). Invasive pressure-tap location and energized-equipment work require correct equipment procedures and qualified assessment.
