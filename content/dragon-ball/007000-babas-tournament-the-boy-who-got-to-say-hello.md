# 007000 — Baba's Tournament: The Boy Who Got to Say Hello

**Track:** Dragon Ball: From Penguin Village to the End of Z. **Audience:** Fans and curious general listeners. **Prerequisites:** None; the Red Ribbon Army and Grandpa Gohan are introduced in context. **Scope:** The Fortuneteller Baba section of the original *Dragon Ball* manga, approximately Tales 97–112. **Spoilers:** Baba's five fighters, Goku's reunion with his deceased grandfather, the final Dragon Ball, and Bora's resurrection. This is an interpretation of the story, not a claim about undocumented creator intention.

# Baba's Tournament: The Boy Who Got to Say Hello

Goku has just beaten the Red Ribbon Army. He has survived its factories, soldiers, machinery and hired assassin; crossed forests and mountains; climbed a tower to learn from a cat; and smashed through the headquarters of an organization whose officers could scarcely agree on what they were trying to accomplish. One Dragon Ball remains unaccounted for. One wish remains to be made. The boy who can knock an army apart cannot find a small glassy sphere.

He can ask a fortune teller.

The fortune teller is a tiny, elderly woman named Baba. She sits on a floating crystal ball, charges an absurd fee, and has arranged a system under which people who cannot afford her services may instead fight a sequence of monsters. She has a palace, a ghostly usher, a frightening room with a deadly pool, and a talent for turning supernatural power into a profitable business.

This could be a diversion. In another manga it might be a few chapters of comic relief before the important story resumed. But what happens at Baba's palace contains one of the most affecting moments of early *Dragon Ball*. At the end of the monster fights, a masked old man steps forward. Goku doesn't know who he is. The old man knows exactly who Goku is.

The fortune teller has brought Goku's dead grandfather back for a day.

It is such a peculiar way to arrange a reunion. A child cannot locate a magic ball, so his friends take him to a clairvoyant. To receive a reading, they agree to fight a vampire, an invisible man, a mummy, a devil, and finally a stranger. The last stranger is a parent, and the contest becomes the chance to say hello.

Toriyama's trick is not to stop being funny when something tender is about to happen. The tender moment grows out of the same world that has a bloodsucking martial artist and a ghost who seems to enjoy doing the paperwork. The silliness is what allows the tenderness to arrive without an announcement.

## A missing ball and a very personal wish

The preceding Red Ribbon adventure began with Goku searching for the four-star Dragon Ball, which had belonged to Grandpa Gohan. A keepsake mattered to him more than the extraordinary wish all seven balls could grant. But events changed the purpose of the search. At the base of Karin Tower, the assassin Taopaipai killed Bora, the father of a boy named Upa. Goku promised Upa that he would assemble the seven Dragon Balls and ask Shenron to bring Bora back to life.

The promise is a considerable change in Goku's understanding of the quest. At the beginning of the manga, Bulma had taught him that the Dragon Balls were collectible objects connected to a possible wish, and the first attempt to collect them ended in a spectacularly foolish joke at Emperor Pilaf's expense. Now a child is waiting for the return of his father. The wish is neither a prank nor a prize. It is an obligation Goku has voluntarily taken on.

Goku has overcome the Army, but the Dragon Radar cannot complete the work. Technology is useful in this world, not omnipotent. The last ball is out of reach of the machine's search, and the only remaining path is to visit someone who claims knowledge of what cannot be seen.

Master Roshi directs them to his sister, Uranai Baba, the fortune teller. One of the pleasures of the early *Dragon Ball* world is how casually new wonders are brought into it. Roshi has a sister who crosses between the living world and the dead. This fact does not require the series to become a theological treatise. It is news about a relative, and the relative happens to have a business.

Baba's fee is ten million zeni. If you cannot pay, you can defeat five fighters she employs. Think about the economic logic for a moment. The service is fantastically expensive, and the alternative is a spectacle that requires her to maintain a monster roster. She is not a benevolent oracle dispensing wisdom because heroes deserve it. She has devised an enterprise in which the poor risk physical harm while the rich can purchase certainty. And she conducts it with the confidence of someone who knows her terms are ridiculous but is in no danger of losing customers.

It would be easy to flatten this into a satire of commerce. The more interesting point is how Toriyama gives the supernatural ordinary social habits. Someone can walk from a spaceship, to a martial arts school, to a military base, to a fortune teller's establishment, and find a different version of the same human nonsense. Eccentric expertise, unfair rules and a boss who is entirely too pleased with herself are apparently universal.

Goku isn't bothered by the mismatch. He has been asked to fight and he knows how to fight. His friends are rather more aware that the terms may be dangerous. This is one reason a group matters, even when a single child is by far the strongest person in it. The other characters recognize the traps, negotiate the social world, and have the kinds of anxieties that Goku's confidence tends to bypass.

## Five kinds of monster

The lineup at Baba's palace would fit comfortably in a comedy about an exceptionally badly organized haunted house. The first fighter is a vampire, called Draculaman. The second is invisible. The third is a mummy. The fourth resembles a conventional devil. Then comes the masked man.

There is a progression, but it isn't merely from weak to strong. Every new opponent changes what the contest is about. The vampire is quick and transforms into a bat; the invisible man makes it impossible to hit what the audience cannot see; the mummy uses physical entanglement and the threat of an awful arena; the devil attacks a quality of the soul. The last opponent is almost entirely familiar: a martial artist who can fight properly.

The first match is a reminder that raw ability isn't everything and that Toriyama hasn't forgotten how to stage slapstick. Krillin is much stronger than an ordinary child, but the vampire can attack in a way his training does not prepare him for. He is bitten, drained, and knocked out. The solution comes not from a superior energy attack but from Upa and Puar, who cooperate to overcome the monster. Upa, remember, has come because he needs his father back. He is not simply a rescued child watching grown fighters settle the world's problems. He is allowed to do something on the road toward keeping that promise.

The invisible fighter presents a different joke. Yamcha is capable of fighting an opponent he can locate. Locating him is the problem. Invisibility sounds like a devastating advantage until the scene becomes a ridiculous effort to make the unseen visible. A spectacular nosebleed from Roshi provides the unlikely means. This is an old gag-comic author finding a way to solve a supernatural obstacle with embarrassment. It would be difficult to explain the appeal of this scene using a table of character abilities. The comedy depends on a sharp contrast between the elaborate problem and the undignified solution.

Then the group enters the Devil's Cesspool. Its name already seems to have been invented by somebody who worried that poison, looming statues and an arena over a lethal pool were not sufficiently theatrical. The mummy is strong enough to overwhelm Yamcha. He uses bandages not as a harmless costume but as a means of constriction. In another setting a mummy would belong in a tomb. Here he is a professional combatant in a venue operated by Roshi's sister.

The world has made room for supernatural creatures without surrendering its interest in fighting technique. A mummy can take a martial artist seriously. Yamcha's problem is not that he has wandered into a genre in which his abilities are meaningless. It is that the contest keeps finding ways to make those abilities inadequate.

Goku steps in. His strength resolves the mummy problem much more decisively than Yamcha could. But the story doesn't reward us with a neat final examination in which every opponent becomes harder and Goku eventually meets someone who is simply more powerful. The next fight takes a much stranger turn.

## The devil's examination

The devil fighter, called Akkuman in Japanese and often Spike the Devil Man in English releases, has an attack which converts evil within a person's heart into a lethal force. Against a victim with resentment, cruelty or corrupt intention, the magic can be devastating. The devil does not need to punch harder than Goku. He needs something dark inside Goku to exploit.

This should be a terrifying trap for a hero with a hidden vice. It is also a chance for Toriyama to make a perfectly serious supernatural threat collapse into a character joke.

The attack does nothing.

There is nothing of the required kind for it to work on. Goku is too innocent. The devil's ultimate move has encountered a child who cannot even cooperate by supplying an evil thought.

It is tempting to read this as a grand pronouncement that Goku has a perfect ethical philosophy. It isn't. Goku can be thoughtless, socially bewildered, ravenously selfish about food, and at times excited by violence in ways that will become important later. He also acts generously without consulting a doctrine. The devil's magic operates on the comic's idea of a pure heart, and Toriyama uses it for a reversal: a creature accustomed to exploiting moral weakness finds that the opponent has none it can reach.

This distinction is important. Innocence is not exactly wisdom. Goku is not the adult who has fully considered the moral implications of every choice and always decides correctly. He is a boy whose mind has not been organized by many of the motives that govern other people. That makes him comically troublesome in a shop, dangerous to an army, and surprisingly immune to a demon.

The first tournament had asked whether Goku could keep growing after losing a fight. The Red Ribbon Army had asked whether he could turn victory into responsibility for another family. The devil fighter briefly asks what kind of person he is when strength is irrelevant. The answer comes through a joke rather than a speech.

And notice how Toriyama avoids letting this become the end of the adventure. If an attack fails because the protagonist has a pure heart, the author could claim a complete moral victory and finish the sequence there. Instead there is a fifth opponent. This one doesn't look like an impossible monster. He looks like an old martial artist wearing a mask. He does not need an evil-ray to trouble Goku. He knows how Goku fights.

## The man who recognizes the boy

Grandpa Gohan has been present in *Dragon Ball* almost from the beginning, but usually as an absence. His four-star ball rests in Goku's house. He left the boy a staff and martial training. Goku talks about him with uncomplicated affection. The reader knows the grandfather died; the reader also knows that Goku sometimes becomes a destructive giant ape beneath the full moon. Those two facts acquire a dreadful relationship, even before Goku is capable of understanding it in full.

In the masked fighter, Gohan returns not as a ghost with a message but as a combatant. He knows his grandson's movements, traditions and vulnerabilities. His own knowledge of the Turtle School appears in his technique. He can use a Kamehameha. His strength is recognizably human in the way a well-trained martial artist's can be, even when the world around him has grown increasingly strange.

Most of the earlier Baba opponents declared themselves through a distinctive power. The vampire is a vampire; the invisible man is invisible; the mummy is wrapped; the devil has an attack tied to the soul. The fifth fighter's distinguishing power is familiarity.

He can recognize the child in front of him. For the audience, that makes his identity an answer waiting to be revealed. For Goku, it makes the fight puzzling. The masked man anticipates him, understands his stance and even knows about the vulnerability in his tail. There is the recognizable pleasure of a martial-arts contest: a fighter meeting someone whose experience has made him hard to surprise. But all those observations are also clues to a relationship. The opponent understands Goku because he helped make Goku who he is.

It is easy to talk about a mentor as someone who passes down techniques. Toriyama finds a more embodied way to show it. The grandfather's influence is contained in the rhythm of the exchange. How Goku stands, how he responds, how he fights: the old man recognizes the lessons in the younger body, and sees the ways the boy has moved beyond them. The scene makes inheritance visible as combat.

When Gohan removes his disguise, the contest is suddenly no longer about whether the visitors can afford Baba's information. Goku is allowed to know the identity of a person he has missed. He is allowed to hold him.

For all the extravagant reversals in *Dragon Ball*, the reunion remains small in its emotional vocabulary. Goku is joyful. Gohan is proud. They don't have to explain years of separation through a long monologue or perform a complicated argument about life and death. There is an embrace, the extraordinary happiness of recognition, and the knowledge that the visit cannot last.

It is hard to think of a better use for an otherwise whimsical story device. Baba's ability to bring someone from the other world for a single day is more poignant than a general ability to erase death. There is a boundary. The visit is temporary. A person can cross it briefly and return. The magical rule creates an hour of grace without pretending that loss was imaginary.

The embrace also changes the emotional scale of the entire Dragon Ball search. Goku set out wanting the four-star ball because it reminded him of Grandpa Gohan. He is now in the presence of the man himself, while still trying to assemble seven balls for another boy whose father has died. Two very different relationships to bereavement meet in one place. The keepsake, the reunion, and the rescue wish are variations on the same desire: to be with someone who is gone.

## Why Gohan stays dead

The obvious question in a world with wish-granting Dragon Balls is whether Goku should bring his own grandfather back. If the magic can restore Bora, why not restore Gohan too?

Gohan doesn't ask for that. He is content in the other world. The reunion is wonderful, but it doesn't become a demand that the ordinary arrangements of mortality be reversed for his sake. He is able to see that Goku is loved and growing strong, and that knowledge makes their short meeting enough.

We should be careful not to make this into a moral argument that nobody ought to seek resurrection. Goku has made a specific promise to Upa, who is grieving a newly murdered father. The story clearly treats the chance to restore Bora as a good thing. Gohan's decision is a personal one. His death belongs to a completed life in a way Bora's violent death does not. One man chooses to remain where he is; one little boy is waiting to be reunited with his parent. The same magic can respect both situations.

That contrast is a quiet achievement. Later *Dragon Ball* will make death, other worlds, temporary returns and resurrection part of a much more elaborate narrative system. Fans understandably debate whether easy revival eventually reduces the weight of sacrifice. But this early use of the system is strikingly attentive to individual circumstances. The story does not suggest that every loss is identical, or that every bereaved person ought to want the same impossible thing.

Gohan's presence also helps complete Roshi's role in the manga up to this point. The eccentric turtle hermit is an immensely flawed adult and an effective teacher. We have seen him teach Goku and Krillin through labor, rest, repetition and tournament competition. Now we meet a former student of Roshi who took those lessons and used them to raise a child. The Turtle School is no longer merely a curriculum. It is a chain of relationships, passing from one generation to the next, making an orphan's home possible.

Goku is strong enough to defeat his teacher's teacher in some ways, but strength isn't the only thing being transmitted. Gohan's generosity is probably the more consequential inheritance. When Goku promises to bring Bora back, he is doing something his grandfather would understand. He has come a long way from the solitary boy in the mountains, yet the story makes his decision to help Upa feel consistent with the kindness that greeted him there.

There is a further, darker current beneath the reunion. The full-moon transformation is linked to Gohan's death. The boy we love is capable, without choosing it, of terrible harm. Toriyama lets that implication exist beside the warm embrace. The story need not force Goku into a mature reckoning at precisely this moment. The reader can know more than the child. Innocence has limits, and a loving grandfather can forgive a catastrophe that the child cannot yet fully comprehend.

That tension is a reason the reunion resists becoming merely sentimental. There is happiness and a fact that happiness doesn't cancel. It is an encounter made possible by magic but complicated by ordinary questions: what a parent knows about a child, what a child does not know about a parent, and what remains unspoken when there is not much time.

## The world outside the arena

Baba supplies the location of the last Dragon Ball. It leads back to Pilaf, the first quest's comically grandiose villain, who is still trying to turn a magical wish into worldly domination. The return to Pilaf has the feeling of a familiar tune played after a more serious movement. The machinery and posturing are funny because we've met the pattern before. Goku, having survived the Red Ribbon Army and found an old friend in the land of the dead, can dispatch this obstacle without mistaking it for the main event.

The Dragon Balls are finally assembled. Shenron appears. The wish belongs to Upa, and Bora returns.

It is worth remembering how the original first wish went. A would-be ruler called up a magnificent dragon, and Oolong beat him to the moment with a comic request for underwear. In this later invocation, a child asks to have his murdered father back. Toriyama is using the same marvelous apparatus and a very different emotional register. Neither tone invalidates the other. The absurd introduction makes the later seriousness possible because a magical object initially valued as a prize has been given human weight.

After the wish, the Dragon Balls scatter. The narrative arrangement that began with Bulma's radar and a strange meeting in the mountains has changed the lives of people across the world. Goku no longer needs to pursue a wish for himself. He has used it for someone else. He can go back to learning, traveling, and eventually competing with people stronger than he is.

The structure of Baba's tournament helps make that departure feel complete. The Red Ribbon Army was enormous, distributed and relentless. Baba's palace is compact, theatrical and eccentric. One story tested Goku against an institution; the next tested him against a set of surprises, then returned him to the family from which his journey began. The first was an outward expansion. The second was an inward return.

There is a technique here that Toriyama will use again. He can present a scene as a joke or a challenge and let it become something else once the audience has committed to the circumstances. A disguised champion in the first tournament was secretly a teacher. A frightening android was secretly a gentle friend. A masked competitor at a fortune teller's palace is secretly a grandfather.

In each case, a fight is partly about misrecognition. We are asked to judge someone by costume, size, affiliation or power, and then discover an individual who exceeds that first impression. Baba's strongest fighter doesn't need to be a world-destroying monster because the reveal matters more than the contest's difficulty. The surprise changes how we remember the scene.

## One day is enough

There are many possible explanations for why *Dragon Ball* survived the transition from gag adventure to battle manga. Toriyama's fast action drawing is one. His inventive opponents and increasingly elaborate training ideas are others. But the Baba episode points to a quality easily lost when we recall only the biggest fights: the series has room for a genuinely small happiness.

A person Goku loves appears. They spar. They recognize one another. They part. The boy goes on to keep a promise.

Neither the meeting nor the parting makes Goku a different species of hero. He still likes food, competition and getting into trouble. He will still misunderstand people and run toward danger with an energy no sensible companion can match. What changes is the listener's sense of what all that motion is carrying. There is a relationship behind the four-star ball; a debt behind the wish; a grandfather behind the boy's martial arts; and, beyond the next challenge, a world of people who can be hurt or helped.

Baba is almost peripheral to her own greatest scene. She has charged an outrageous price for the privilege of passing through a haunted-house competition. Yet for one day, her strange business becomes the place where a child gets to hug his grandfather again.

That is a lovely thing for a story to know how to do.
