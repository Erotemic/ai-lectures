# 013000 — Before Z Was on Television: The Original Dragon Ball Anime

**Track:** Dragon Ball: From Penguin Village to the End of Z. **Audience:** Fans of the manga and television series, and listeners interested in animation history. **Prerequisites:** None; principal characters and the manga/anime distinction are introduced. **Spoilers:** The first Dragon Ball quest, its tournaments, King Piccolo, the Piccolo Junior finale, and the television-only wedding adventure. **Scope:** The Japanese 1986–1989 adaptation, with later export versions considered separately. **Perspective:** The production facts are documented below; observations about pacing, drawing, voices and music are critical readings, not attributed intentions.

# Before Z Was on Television: The Original Dragon Ball Anime

In February 1986, a young singer named Hiroki Takahashi was still trying to make a career in music. He had recorded the theme for a new television cartoon, but the assignment seemed improbable enough that he wondered whether somebody was playing a trick on him. On the evening of the first broadcast, he slipped away from his part-time job and went to a nearby shop that had a television. Then the program began, and his voice came out of the set.

Years later, in an interview published by the official Dragon Ball website, Takahashi recalled another moment. One summer, he heard children singing the opening theme outside his window. His song had left the recording studio and acquired a life among strangers.

That is a rather good image for what happened to Akira Toriyama's manga. The story had already been appearing in Weekly Shonen Jump since 1984. It belonged to paper, ink, page turns, a particular printing schedule, and the reader's private sense of how fast Goku moved or how long a joke should last. Now it was going to have an actual tempo. Somebody would decide how many seconds Bulma spent looking at an impossible boy. Somebody would decide what the world sounded like when a motorcycle started, a dinosaur appeared, or the Dragon Balls summoned Shenron.

Before the televised franchise became identified with long battles and enormous golden-haired fighters, there was another program: Dragon Ball, without the Z. It ran in Japan for 153 episodes, from February 1986 to April 1989. It begins with a boy who lives in the mountains and ends with a much older Goku winning a martial-arts championship and leaving to marry Chi-Chi. Between those points, it has to accommodate nearly every genre that the comic had become: road adventure, farce, quest, tournament, war against a criminal army, supernatural pilgrimage, and an unusually frightening story about a demon king.

It is tempting to call this the warm-up show. That phrase gets the history backward. To the people making and watching it at the time, this was Dragon Ball. The idea that the original adventure existed primarily as an introduction to its more famous continuation was not built into the 1986 premiere. The program had to be entertaining while Goku was still small, the world was still strange, and seven magical objects were an excuse to meet people rather than a mechanism for undoing the consequences of a planetary war.

The interesting question is not whether television made the manga better or worse. It is what changed when Toriyama's drawings started occupying other people's time.

## An opening that tells you how to watch

The original Japanese opening, Makafushigi Adventure, is performed by Takahashi. There is something wonderful about its invitation. The song presents Dragon Ball as a quest in which the fantastic is nearly within reach. An adventurer could run out the door and find a world full of small marvels. It does not announce a solemn prophecy that will determine the fate of galaxies. It asks you to come along.

A television opening is a contract. Before an episode has introduced its particular problem, images, rhythm, and music establish the sort of pleasure available. Toriyama's early comic has a rich catalog of pleasures: oversized animals, a flying cloud, capsules that produce machines, a hungry child who does not understand ordinary social conventions, a young inventor who sometimes behaves with less practical sense than the child. The opening can gather these pleasures in quick succession. Every week it says, in effect, that this bewildering variety belongs to the same story.

The ending theme, Romantic Ageru yo, associated with singer Ushio Hashimoto, makes a different sort of space. It belongs to the interval after the episode's urgency has passed. This is part of what television can give a serialized story that a comic cannot reproduce in quite the same fashion: familiar music frames the experience of returning to a place. A weekly encounter doesn't just end at the final panel. It receives a small ceremony of departure, and a promise that the place will still be there.

The songs' precise meanings to a particular viewer depend on the version they saw. People who first encountered Dragon Ball in translation may have heard other songs, different lyrics, or different arrangements. None of those memories is counterfeit. They are simply different historical encounters with the program. For now, stay with the original Japanese broadcast, because its soundtrack, cast, and editing created a foundation that later versions often altered.

Shunsuke Kikuchi composed the series' music. A successful score has to do more than announce that the strongest person in the room is dangerous. In early Dragon Ball it must move easily among curiosity, mischief, menace, triumph, and genuine loss. A comic encounter with an eccentric villager and the appearance of an assassin need different kinds of suspense. The program's musical identity helps bridge them. When a story's setting can turn from a ridiculous village to the death of a friend, the score has to make both places feel inhabitable within one universe.

I am not suggesting that every old television cue is subtle, or that a striking drum hit can repair a weak scene. Sometimes the music insists on an emotion the drawing has already supplied. But the collaboration between score, voice, silence, and image gives Dragon Ball's world a physical presence that no panel alone was meant to provide. We can enjoy that presence without making the insulting assumption that the comic was an incomplete cartoon waiting for sound.

## Goku speaks, and the drawing changes shape

Consider the casting of Goku. In the Japanese version, Masako Nozawa voices him. She gives the child a sense of directness that can change with startling speed from carefree bewilderment to furious determination. Much later she would also voice Gohan and Goten, but in 1986 the immediate problem was smaller: make this implausibly strong boy sound like a character whose ignorance is amusing without making his courage seem artificial.

That task is harder than it sounds. On the printed page, Toriyama can give Goku a round face, an open mouth, a simple line of dialogue, and a physical reaction. A reader supplies an internal voice, often one that changes silently between jokes and danger. The television performance must commit. Goku has to make a sound when surprised. His delight in food, his abrupt questions, his cheerful inability to read a room, and his anger at cruelty all have to share a recognizable personality.

Hiromi Tsuru plays Bulma in the original Japanese cast. Mayumi Tanaka plays Krillin. These performances make the emerging friendships audible. Goku and Bulma often misunderstand the same situation for opposite reasons. Bulma has extraordinary technical knowledge but lacks experience of his odd corner of the world; Goku knows the wilderness and little of the technological society that has just entered it. Their conversations work because the actors can make the exchange sound like a genuine argument, not merely two people delivering the setup and punch line of a gag.

Krillin adds another kind of intelligence. He knows how to compete socially, how to flatter a teacher, and how to measure his position against another student. Goku often does not. A comic can express that contrast through expression and timing; the voices make the little humiliations and gestures of generosity last exactly long enough to be felt. The rivalry begins as a set of irritations and slowly turns into one of the most durable friendships in the story.

It's worth resisting the urge to crown any single performance the definitive one for every audience. The franchise has been broadcast in multiple languages and re-edited for different markets. If your childhood Goku speaks Spanish, French, English, or another language, that version may be inseparable from your emotional memory. Here we can identify who performed the Japanese original and what artistic problem they solved, without announcing that your experience was invalid.

## Movement costs time

Toriyama's drawing can be astonishingly economical. A character moves in one panel, and the next panel gives us the consequence. The reader's eye fills in a body traveling through space. A comic fight can have the speed of a thought. A gag can land because the artist withholds the next image until a page turn. The amount of time between two panels belongs partly to the reader.

Animation cannot leave every interval blank. Even if it uses held drawings and swift cuts, the episode has a specified running time. Somebody must choose how long an opponent stands still before a blow, how an expression changes, where the camera seems to stand, and how the next movement begins. Sometimes the motion gives the sequence a grace the page could only imply. Sometimes it gives a joke room to breathe. Sometimes it makes an exchange that took two wonderful pages feel oddly leisurely.

The original Dragon Ball television crew worked within this constraint while the manga was still being serialized. The adaptation was not made after the complete story had been laid out as a finished object. The comic continued to invent new directions, and the weekly broadcast followed a moving target. Viewers sometimes call the result filler, a word that can mean almost anything from an entirely new side adventure to a few extra reactions before a punch.

It is useful to separate those cases. One extra beat can make a character's hesitation legible. A short extension of a fight can let an animator build an action idea. A separate adventure can offer room for a supporting character whom the manga has passed by. But five minutes of characters explaining what the viewer just saw does not automatically become character development simply because it is more footage. Added time has to earn its place.

What I like about the earliest episodes is that a little extra time can restore the possibility of wandering. Goku doesn't yet know the boundaries of the world. Meeting another stranger can be its own reward. A village, a castle, or a forest may offer a joke rather than a permanent change in martial-arts hierarchy. The anime can linger with a location until it feels like a place the travelers had to cross rather than a backdrop Toriyama placed behind them for one chapter.

The risk is that the charm becomes repetition. The same character misunderstanding an obvious social convention can be delightful once and laborious when the animation keeps returning to it. There is no guaranteed formula by which a slower Dragon Ball becomes a better Dragon Ball. What television changes is the cost of every pause. The adaptation must pay for its time with a laugh, a feeling, a movement, a discovery, or at least the pleasure of inhabiting the place.

## The people behind the line

The anime was produced by Toei Animation for Fuji Television. The production records name Minoru Okazaki and Daisuke Nishio as the series directors, Minoru Maeda as a key figure in character design and chief animation, and Shunsuke Kikuchi for music. These credits are worth saying aloud because adaptation is collaborative work. Toriyama created the printed forms and the serialized story. The television series required many other artists to interpret those forms, move them, color them, stage them, voice them, and return with another episode the following week.

Maeda's work presents a particularly interesting translation problem. A Toriyama drawing is precise in its simplicity. Slight alterations to a silhouette can change not only whether a character looks right, but whether the joke is still funny. Too much shading and the figure becomes heavy. Too little attention to structure and a combat pose loses the instant clarity that made it work on paper. Character design for animation is not a matter of faithfully tracing every illustration. It is the construction of a repeatable body that can be recognized and performed through thousands of drawings.

That repeatability is both the great strength and the vulnerability of a long-running animated series. Some scenes make a familiar gesture seem effortless. In others, the demands of weekly production become visible in held poses, simplified forms, or movement that lacks the crispness of an individual manga page. A fair assessment does not need to pretend every episode is equally well animated. Nor should it forget how much coordinated work is required to make even an imperfect weekly scene exist at all.

An important pleasure survives all this variation: Toriyama's shapes remain inviting. Cars, capsules, animals, buildings, uniforms, and peculiar monsters still appear to have been designed by somebody who enjoys objects. The early anime has reasons to linger with those things. Before nearly every conflict becomes a contest between people who can fly, a road can still demand a vehicle. A bicycle or motorbike can still be a genuine piece of the adventure.

## A martial-arts show grows inside an adventure

A strange thing happens as the episodes accumulate. The original first quest has a definite destination: gather the Dragon Balls, summon the dragon, discover what a wish costs or accomplishes. But Goku's life soon gives the story another organizing principle. He trains with Master Roshi, becomes friends with Krillin, meets rivals, and enters the World Martial Arts Tournament.

The tournament turns a gathering of colorful people into a question about bodies moving under shared rules. It gives artists an arena, recognizable geometry, and a set of outcomes that can be displayed without explanation. The audience can read a fighter's position relative to the edge of the ring. A strange technique becomes interesting because another person must solve it. A victory is not always a measure of who could destroy more property. It may depend on timing, awareness, deception, or stamina.

This is precisely the sort of material television can animate wonderfully. A tournament offers close-ups of opponents assessing one another, the sound of feet on a surface, the pause before an attack, and the sudden release of movement. Jackie Chun's battles also contain a gag that depends on recognizing an old teacher inside an absurd disguise. A voice performance and a pose can make the secret both obvious and fun without the plot admitting it.

Later arcs expand the demands. The Red Ribbon Army requires machines, bases and a sense of organized force. Mercenary Tao needs to seem more dangerous than a joke about an overconfident martial artist. King Piccolo requires an atmosphere in which death, fear, and the loss of the Dragon Balls are real pressures rather than mechanisms for the next gag. By the final tournament, Piccolo Junior can occupy a ring that once held comic competitors, while the story asks whether victory is enough to decide what should happen to a defeated enemy.

The theme song hasn't changed into a declaration of doom. Goku still inhabits the world whose opening promised adventure. The program learns how to tell a frightening story without completely losing the shape of a comedy about people with impossible abilities. Its success is not perfect tonal consistency. It is the audience's growing willingness to follow a familiar person through very different situations.

The original manga was also changing, of course. We should not credit television with inventing the turn toward martial-arts action. But television gives that change another register. You hear the threat grow louder. You hear a laugh stop. You see the animators decide that the same little body which bounced through the first adventure can now carry the weight of a desperate confrontation.

## The ending the manga did not have

The final episodes make a particularly revealing comparison. In the manga, Goku wins the 23rd tournament, spares Piccolo Junior, and departs with Chi-Chi. The closing movement is brisk. The old series' rules have matured, the hero finally has his championship, and the two characters leave together. The narrative can move forward with the confidence of a page turned.

The original anime doesn't stop there. Episodes 149 through 153 add a wedding-related adventure about fire threatening the Ox-King's castle and a journey to solve the problem. The story includes familiar figures, magical impediments, the kind of equipment search that could have occurred much earlier in Dragon Ball, and a renewed glimpse of Grandpa Gohan. These episodes are television-original additions, not a lost five-chapter epilogue by Toriyama.

I like this as a case study because it is not merely a stretch of longer punching. The writers have chosen to bring the show back toward an earlier kind of story. A problem is encountered, a journey is undertaken, an older relationship returns, and the practical details of a wedding become grounds for a fantasy adventure. After all the violence of King Piccolo and the intensity of the final tournament, the anime closes by asking the hero to do something besides become stronger.

This is also a good place to disagree about adaptation without turning disagreement into an accusation. A manga reader may prefer the superb economy of Toriyama's ending. Nothing else is needed after Goku makes his choice and leaves. A television viewer may enjoy a farewell in which the world itself receives a few more episodes, and Goku and Chi-Chi get to occupy the space between an announced marriage and its celebration. Both responses can be reasonable, because the two versions solve different artistic problems.

In one, the ending is an elegant release. In the other, it is an extended goodbye to the world we have been visiting every week. The second ending even lets the television show be a little more like its own beginning. Not every adventure needs a cosmic villain. Sometimes a wedding dress and an impossible mountain provide enough trouble for an episode.

The original Dragon Ball anime ended in April 1989. The story did not end with it. The following week, a new television series with a Z in the title would take up the continuing manga after a time jump, with a child of Goku's and an alien visitor who knows something the hero does not. The music and title would tell viewers that the tone had changed. But the continuity beneath the title had not been severed.

## What the letter Z can hide

There is a peculiarity in how audiences remember popular culture. The sequel can become so famous that the earlier work seems like a historical curiosity: the old show, the little one, the part before everyone learned to fly. That framing creates a misleading hierarchy of importance. It assumes that the later, larger event explains the value of everything that came before.

The original anime offers a way to resist that story. It is not merely valuable because it shows where Piccolo, Krillin, Bulma, and Goku came from. It is an accomplished television series whose sensibility is partly different from its successor's. The road adventures, the absurd villages, the tournaments before planetary stakes, and the feeling of growing up among people who become family are experiences with their own narrative shape.

Even its imperfections are revealing. The program sometimes lingers too long, sometimes invents a detour that doesn't justify its running time, and sometimes strains to animate action at the speed one remembers from the comic. But it also allows listeners and viewers to experience something the printed story necessarily leaves to imagination: a shared voice for Goku, the familiar arrival of a theme song, the quiet moment after a confrontation, and the sensation that this place will be waiting again in a week.

Think again of Hiroki Takahashi standing by a television in 1986, uncertain whether the opening he recorded would really be broadcast. He could not have known what that tune would eventually mean to people he had not met. Its success is a tiny history of the series in miniature. One group of artists made something new from another artist's work; audiences took possession of it through repetition and affection; and eventually it seemed impossible to imagine the world without those sounds.

The letter Z would arrive, with much greater threats and with an immense afterlife in movies, games, and international broadcasts. Before that, there was the weekly sight of a boy with a tail running toward a world he did not understand. A television series gave him a voice, a soundtrack, and a little more time on the road. That was not a rehearsal. It was an adventure worth having.
