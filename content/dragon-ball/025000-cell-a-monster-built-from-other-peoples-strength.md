# 025000 — Cell: A Monster Built from Other People's Strength

**Track:** Dragon Ball: From Penguin Village to the End of Z. **Audience:** Fans and interested newcomers. **Prerequisites:** The broad premise of the Android and Cell arc is introduced here; no previous audio lecture is required. **Spoilers:** The Android crisis, Cell's origin and transformations, the Cell Games, and the decisions that allow him to reach his final form. **Scope:** The original *Dragon Ball* manga, especially the Android/Cell material in volumes 29–34, and its *Dragon Ball Z* adaptation. **Perspective:** Horror, character design, inherited violence, technological ambition, and the movement from concealment to public spectacle.

# Cell: A Monster Built from Other People's Strength

A city disappears, and Dragon Ball briefly becomes a detective story.

There is no enormous explosion at first. No confident villain announces a challenge from the sky. The inhabitants of Ginger Town have simply vanished. The world has been emptied of people, leaving behind places where people were expected to be. The heroes who come to investigate are used to enemies who enjoy demonstrating their power. This enemy has been feeding.

For a series famous for fighters floating above shattered landscapes, that is a remarkable change of emphasis. Instead of asking whether Goku can withstand the next attack, the story asks what happened to the people who are not there to describe it. The threat is initially defined by absence, not by spectacle.

When Piccolo confronts the creature responsible, its body seems compiled from several incompatible designs. A hard, insect-like shell; a long tail; patches of dark pattern; a head and face that are recognizably calculating without being recognizably human. It knows techniques that belong to familiar fighters. It speaks like someone who understands exactly what it needs. And it has an explanation for its existence: Cell was manufactured from the biological material of the strongest warriors, assembled by the research program of Dr. Gero, a scientist who had once served the Red Ribbon Army.

Here is a villain born from Dragon Ball's own history. He is not simply the next emperor, stronger than Frieza. He is made from people we already know, people the series has asked us to love or fear, and he regards their gifts as components.

The result is a particularly vicious idea for a story about self-improvement. What if you could obtain another person's strength without learning anything that made that person worth admiring?

## The old army has not finished losing

Think back to the original Dragon Ball, when Goku was a boy searching for his grandfather's four-star ball. The Red Ribbon Army was a dangerous but often ridiculous institution: a sprawling military bureaucracy with uniforms, absurd commanders, mountain bases, machines, and a limitless ability to underestimate a child. Its episodes could be a satire of incompetent authority, a winter adventure in Muscle Tower, or a genuinely frightening encounter with Mercenary Tao.

Goku broke the organization. That seemed like the end of it. In an adventure comic, defeating the headquarters generally means moving on to the next adventure. But Dr. Gero never moved on. He kept studying, building, observing. The organization whose vast infrastructure a small boy had toppled left behind a scientist capable of preserving its resentment for years.

This is a useful way for Toriyama to change the past without pretending the original adventure had been secretly planned as an epic of artificial life all along. The earlier story still happened as the reader remembers it. A child defeated an army. Later, the manga asks whether an army's defeat can leave technology, ideology, and a thirst for revenge in circulation.

The scientist's tools also belong to the world Toriyama had loved drawing since his early comics. Vehicles, robots, absurdly clever machines, miniature mechanisms hidden inside larger ones: technology has never been foreign to Dragon Ball. Bulma's capsule inventions made the first quest possible. The difference is the use to which invention is put. Bulma builds devices that enable someone to travel, communicate, locate, survive. Gero builds weapons that continue a vendetta after its original reason has become almost meaningless.

The distinction is not that science is good in one person's hands and bad in another's. Both scientists are ingenious. The difference is in how they understand other living beings. Bulma's world is full of people to argue with and rescue. Gero's project treats people as things to measure, modify, and eventually replace.

## The threat that keeps changing names

The Android story is already unstable before Cell appears. Future Trunks has warned of two terrible artificial humans. The defenders spend years preparing, only to encounter a situation that doesn't resemble his recollections. Dr. Gero appears in a mechanical body. Android 19 fights. Other numbered Androids emerge. Trunks himself cannot reconcile what he sees with the future he knows.

In other words, this is not initially a tidy tournament in which an announced enemy waits politely for the protagonist. It is a story about incomplete intelligence. Who exactly is the threat? What does the number on the robot mean? Why are the present-day Androids behaving differently from the killers of Trunks' future? If the medicine saves Goku, why does everything else refuse to follow a recognizable script?

Android 17 and Android 18 worsen the confusion because they do not behave like pure instruments of destruction. They are extraordinarily dangerous. They humiliate powerful fighters, kill Gero when he tries to control them, and decide to pursue Goku. But they also spend time driving, arguing, choosing clothes, and enjoying the freedom to make detours. Android 16, a fully artificial being, shows an affectionate attention to birds and animals that his maker would probably have regarded as a malfunction.

The future Trunks knows is brutal. The present people he meets are capable of brutality, but they do not behave like inevitable embodiments of it. That difference is important. The saga tempts the reader to classify a group as monsters by name, number, and warning. Then it produces a monster who leaves little room for doubt about what he has done.

Cell's arrival sharpens the distinction. He has a task. Other people's preferences, personalities, even their physical survival are relevant only as obstacles or fuel. The Androids have escaped a creator who wanted to own them. Cell is a creator's fantasy of ownership made mobile, moving toward them with the intention of swallowing their bodies.

## The town without witnesses

Ginger Town is a strong piece of horror because it interrupts the ordinary visual grammar of Dragon Ball. We expect kinetic bodies: running, flying, lunging, dodging, falling. We expect a punch to send someone into a rock. Even death in a battle generally has a visible cause and often a visible opponent.

Cell's victims are absent. Empty clothing and the scale of the deserted town suggest an action that has already occurred. Piccolo and the others arrive after the decisive event. Investigating becomes a way of fearing the creature before the reader has fully seen what it can do. The buildings remain. The people have been reduced to the energy required to power a biological machine.

The image is especially cold because the city is ordinary. It is not Frieza's battlefield, where armies and warriors enter already expecting lethal conflict. It is a place where someone might have been shopping or walking to work. Those imagined routines are not drawn as individual backstories. They are implied by the wrongness of a town in which no one can continue them.

Cell doesn't merely kill. He takes the energy of living people into himself with a pointed tail. It is a grotesque revision of the way Goku grows stronger. Goku eats ravenously, trains, gets hurt, recovers, learns from teachers and rivals. Cell also accumulates strength, but the whole process is compressed into consuming someone else.

The tail makes the moral mechanism visible. There is no friendly abstraction of a power level rising on a screen. The increase comes at the expense of another body. His growth is a literal theft of life.

The official story record identifies Ginger Town as a place of roughly fifteen thousand inhabitants. The exact figure is less important to the scene than the fact that a population can vanish in the space between one investigative clue and another. An enemy this efficient does not need a battle cry to announce that the scale of danger has changed.

## A body that has not finished becoming itself

Consider how Cell first looks. Toriyama had already shown he could make a small, smooth-faced tyrant frightening with Frieza. First Form Cell uses a different vocabulary: plates, protrusions, a long cranial outline, patterned surfaces, and that extraordinary tail. The silhouette invites insect associations, but it is not simply an enlarged insect. It has enough human arrangement to stand and address Piccolo. Its face is expressive enough to look pleased with itself.

There is an instability built into the design. The creature announces that it has not yet reached its desired form. This body, already a terrible threat, considers itself incomplete. A great deal of the suspense comes from the idea that its most disturbing appearance might be merely temporary.

Cell's time travel makes the bodily horror stranger. To fit within the stolen time machine, he reverted to an egg and then grew after arrival. This is not a normal character traveling with a weapon in his luggage. It is a body that can become a previous stage of itself. The plot's clock is mirrored by a biological clock. Cell waits underground for years, then begins feeding in the present.

One could write an elaborate fictional biology to explain his development, but the manga is not trying to be a scientific treatise. The images do imaginative work that the technical explanation does not have to do. Egg, shell, insect-like form, tail: each piece makes him feel like an organism with a life cycle. That feeling matters more than whether he could exist in any laboratory.

When he eventually absorbs Android 17 and reaches his second form, the change is not merely cosmetic. His proportions and expression become more recognizably humanoid, but the result is hardly reassuring. He is stronger and more confident, and the grotesque tail remains the point of danger. The form looks like a stage on the way to a self-image he hasn't achieved.

His eventual Perfect Form is strikingly polished. The face is cleaner. The body is balanced and sleek. The tail no longer dominates the silhouette. It is as though the creature has exchanged an overtly predatory costume for the costume of an elite martial artist. That makes him more appropriate for a tournament and, in a way, more appalling. The acts that made him elegant have not been erased because the design is elegant.

## Stolen technique, absent understanding

Cell's body contains material collected from Goku, Piccolo, Vegeta, Frieza, and other fighters. He can employ recognizable techniques. This is fan-service in the productive sense of that term: the reader hears an enemy claim access to a shared history and then sees it demonstrated.

But the techniques are not biographies. A Kamehameha is not equivalent to having been Master Roshi's student. The ability to regenerate does not mean sharing Piccolo's long and difficult relationship with Kami. The strength of a Saiyan is not the same as Goku's delight in meeting a powerful opponent, and possessing Frieza's characteristics does not require being the exact ruler Frieza was.

Cell has the output without the history of learning. And because he has inherited abilities from several sources, he can imagine himself more complete than any of them.

That is where his name becomes especially sharp. A cell is a building block of living bodies. Cell the character is made from pieces, and the pieces are treated as enough to establish his greatness. But the people from whom they were taken are more than their physical properties. Piccolo, for example, became someone else through years of reluctant care for Gohan. No sampled material can include that choice as a transferable technique.

This is an interpretation, not a hidden moral that Toriyama is known to have set out to write. It is a pattern the story makes available. Dragon Ball had repeatedly argued, in different registers, that growth includes encounter and relationship. Cell embodies an alternative philosophy: obtain the strongest parts and make the self into a collection of them. No apprenticeship required, no friendship involved.

## The artificial being who loves birds

Android 16 is important partly because he prevents the Android arc from turning into a simple warning that machines are evil.

He is a machine through and through. Yet he is the one who pauses to notice animals and the natural world. He has a programmed purpose related to Goku, but his temperament resists the noisy combativeness surrounding him. When Cell comes for Android 17 and Android 18, 16 fights to protect his companions. He acts for beings he regards as people.

Cell is biological. Android 16 is mechanical. The biologically constructed being is the predator; the fully artificial being is the protector. Toriyama has set up a reversal that makes the relevant distinction ethical rather than anatomical.

There is a lovely perversity to the confrontation. Cell wants to become perfect by eating beings who are themselves mixtures of human lives and artificial alteration. He is not taking their consent seriously because consent has no place in his vision of perfection. Android 16 does, in effect, take their continued lives seriously enough to risk his own.

The battle does not reward 16 with a miraculous victory. He can hurt Cell, but Cell eventually gets the chance he needs to consume 17. What remains is a terrible practical problem. A creature with a specific appetite has found one of the two things it must devour.

It can still be stopped. But stopping it would require the stronger fighters to regard the lives of the Androids as more important than the satisfaction of seeing how powerful a completed Cell might be.

## Vegeta's gift to the monster

For much of Dragon Ball, the invitation to fight a stronger opponent is an invitation to wonder. A new tournament contestant appears. Goku smiles. The story gets more exciting. Even after the series becomes dangerous, that appetite remains one of its engines.

Cell learns how to exploit it.

After training in the Room of Spirit and Time, Vegeta becomes strong enough to dominate Cell's second form. The immediate problem appears solvable: defeat the monster before he can reach Android 18. But Cell promises that his final form will be a more worthy challenge. Vegeta listens.

The scene turns the heroic fighting tradition against itself. Vegeta wants the certainty of his own superiority, and the only way to enjoy that certainty completely is to test it against an opponent who might defeat him. If he destroys Cell now, he has saved the world but denied himself the contest he wants to win.

Trunks understands the practical danger and tries to prevent the transformation. The conflict is not between a cautious coward and a brave warrior. Trunks, who has seen a ruined future, is brave enough to insist that the enemy should be stopped while stopping him is possible. Vegeta, obsessed with his recovered pride, treats the lives of everyone outside his contest as collateral.

There is a fierce irony here. Cell's development has required material from stronger beings. But his decisive transformation is also enabled by a weakness that has nothing to do with biology: someone else's desire to prove himself. Cell is very good at using the personalities of his enemies as resources.

Tenshinhan briefly interrupts the process with his repeated Neo Tri-Beam, driving himself to exhaustion to hold Cell back. It is a small strategic success in a series whose headline attractions are increasingly Super Saiyans. An older fighter who knows he cannot win by power alone buys time with a technique and his willingness to bear its cost. The moment has no glamorous reward. It demonstrates that the stakes extend beyond the strongest person's self-image.

## Eighteen is not a missing component

Android 18 does not exist in this story only to complete Cell. She has a face, temperament, history, and relationships. She is frightened when the predator closes in, and she resists being turned into an object in someone else's design.

At the same time, Krillin has a remote control that Bulma built from Dr. Gero's schematics. It could shut 18 down, stopping Cell from absorbing her. For once the solution to an apocalyptic threat fits in someone's hand rather than in a transformed body.

Krillin cannot make himself press the button.

We can criticize that choice. It exposes others to immense danger. He has developed feelings for a person he hardly knows, and those feelings are entangled with the ethical repulsion of simply switching her off. The choice is not automatically wise because it is affectionate. Nor is it sensible to flatten it into a joke about romantic weakness. The machine in his hand embodies Gero's claim that the Androids are objects to be controlled. Krillin's refusal, however disastrous its timing, recognizes something Gero would not recognize.

Cell recognizes none of it. For him, 18 is the final ingredient. Once he takes her, the language of perfection becomes literal. This is the body he had been promised by the program that made him. He stands more elegantly. He speaks more grandly. His physical transformation seems to have removed the last visible trace of the desperate creature that consumed the people of Ginger Town.

But the story has not forgotten how he got there. Every page of stylish self-assurance rests on the earlier image of an empty city and a terrified person being swallowed.

## A monster that wants an audience

Perfect Cell changes the genre again. He has reached the goal that drove his hunting. He no longer needs to stalk a target or search for a missing body. What does he do with perfection?

He announces a tournament.

This is one of the best cruel jokes in the arc. The villain of a horror story has reinvented himself as the host of a martial-arts spectacle. He constructs a ring, issues a public challenge, and gives the world time to be frightened. Television coverage and the arrival of the self-appointed champion Mr. Satan make the event a grotesque parody of the tournaments that helped give Dragon Ball its shape.

The twenty-first tournament was a place where Jackie Chun could teach a young Goku that there will always be someone stronger. The twenty-second tournament was a place where Tenshinhan could learn that winning without honor is not worth much. At the twenty-third, Goku won and then spared Piccolo, choosing an uncertain future over the satisfaction of killing an enemy.

Cell has learned the external format. He has learned the ring, the time to prepare, the entertainment value of anticipation, and the boast that he will defeat all challengers. He has not learned the rules' social purpose. There is no shared agreement that participants are competing for a prize. The world has been coerced into watching a contest on which its survival depends.

This makes the tournament an extension of Ginger Town rather than a departure from it. There he took lives out of public view. Now he insists that the public view his power. Both actions reduce people to material for his self-construction. In the first case, the material is energy. In the second, it is applause, fear, and the prestige of beating famous fighters.

He wants to be witnessed. Perfection without witnesses is evidently not enough.

## A familiar smile on the wrong face

Cell's performance as a martial artist sometimes resembles Goku's pleasure in competition. He waits for an opponent worthy of him, admires skill, and can appear almost cordial when he believes he has control. We are tempted to call him Goku's dark reflection.

That is useful only up to a point. Goku may make irresponsible choices because he loves a difficult contest, but he has relationships that complicate what the contest means. His rival can become a friend. He can stop to feed or heal a fallen person. Cell's appreciation of strength is almost entirely self-referential. Other fighters are interesting insofar as they allow him to demonstrate his own completeness.

He is also not the same kind of villain as Frieza. Frieza is a tyrant with a history of command: soldiers, hierarchy, a political empire and an idea of inherited entitlement. Cell does not need to govern anything. He is an experimental being whose assigned goal has become a personal philosophy. Having achieved it, he invents a public arena in which to confirm it endlessly.

Frieza's transformations are a revelation of danger he already contains. Cell's are acquisitions that require taking other beings into himself. The distinction changes the shape of suspense. When Frieza changes, we fear what he was hiding. When Cell changes, we remember who had to be sacrificed to get him there.

## An unfinished victory

The cruelest thing about the Cell story may be that the heroes repeatedly have opportunities to prevent its worst outcome. Knowledge arrives, but not in time to make everything straightforward. Piccolo finds the feeding creature. Android 16 resists him. Tenshinhan delays him. Trunks sees the danger of letting him transform. Bulma builds a means of stopping Android 18; Krillin carries it. Vegeta has Cell beaten.

These are not decorative side trips to the eventual grand battle. They are the moral and dramatic machinery of the story. Each person has a partial answer. The catastrophe grows because fear, pride, desire, compassion and poor coordination interfere with the clean solution everyone wishes existed.

This is what makes Cell a more interesting antagonist than a list of his borrowed abilities might suggest. He exposes the vulnerability of a world in which powerful people habitually treat the next contest as more important than the present responsibility. And he does so while convincing himself that his own strength is entirely self-made, even though every part of it was taken or given.

The world finally has to meet the monster on the terms he has chosen. A ring has been built. A day has been announced. The fighters will arrive.

But underneath the polished face and the promise of a spectacular match is still the hungry thing that left a town empty. The Cell Games will ask whether the heroes have learned to see what he has been all along.
