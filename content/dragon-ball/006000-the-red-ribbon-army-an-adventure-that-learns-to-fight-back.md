# 006000 — The Red Ribbon Army: An Adventure That Learns to Fight Back

**Track:** Dragon Ball: From Penguin Village to the End of Z. **Audience:** Fans and curious general listeners. **Prerequisites:** None; the first Dragon Ball search and initial tournament are recapped only as needed. **Scope:** Goku's post-tournament search for the Four-Star Dragon Ball and the Red Ribbon Army conflict in the original manga, approximately Tales 54–97. **Spoilers:** Muscle Tower, Android 8, General Blue, Penguin Village, Mercenary Tao, Bora's death, Karin's training, Commander Red's wish and the Army's defeat. The separate Fortuneteller Baba reunion is not retold.

# The Red Ribbon Army: An Adventure That Learns to Fight Back

A boy flies across the countryside looking for a small orange ball. A military organization with bases, aircraft, communications systems, firearms, and enough personnel to make a map look crowded wants the same thing. It dispatches officers with impressive titles, then more officers, then a professional assassin. The boy keeps going.

The boy is Goku. The ball has four stars inside it. It belonged to Grandpa Gohan, the man who raised him. Goku's motives are so personal and simple that they make the Army's apparatus appear ridiculous. He is not attempting to overthrow a government. He is not hunting an international conspiracy. He wants an object that reminds him of someone he loves, and he will not stop looking for it merely because a powerful organization has decided that he should.

The Red Ribbon Army is one of the great transitions in early Dragon Ball. It gives Akira Toriyama something that Emperor Pilaf could not: an antagonist large enough to occupy a great deal of the world. Its power is dispersed among soldiers, stations, towers, submarines, inventors and hired specialists. Goku can win one encounter and still have more enemies to meet. A road-trip comic has acquired the structure of a campaign.

But this is not yet the cleanly serious world of later apocalyptic enemies. The Army is frightening and preposterous. Its senior officers can be cruel; some of them are fools. Its weapons are dangerous; its aims are childish. It has the resources of an international threat and the organizational judgment of an especially poorly managed costume party.

Toriyama draws real dramatic mileage from that contradiction. The journey still has room for talking animals and a visit to Penguin Village. It also contains the death of a father and a defeat that forces Goku to understand the difference between being stronger than most people and being strong enough to protect one person.

The Red Ribbon story is where those two possibilities begin to coexist: the comic world can be marvelous, and the harm done within it can be real.

## An army instead of a castle

The first Dragon Ball quest came to a climax in Emperor Pilaf's castle. Pilaf wanted world domination, but he was still a comic monarch in miniature, supported by a tiny crew and a tremendous amount of theatrical ambition. A handful of travelers could fall into his trap, escape by absurd means, and continue their lives. When Oolong interrupted the wish, the joke was almost an appropriate measure of the villain's grandeur.

The Red Ribbon Army arrives with an entirely different sense of scale. Its leader has subordinates who have subordinates. Those subordinates command units scattered across distant landscapes. Orders travel outward from headquarters and reports travel back. There are names, ranks, uniforms, vehicles, and consequences for officers who fail. The threat persists when the camera turns away from it.

That structure is especially useful for a serial cartoonist. A single great villain needs to remain interesting through repeated meetings. An organization can change its face each time the protagonist arrives at another base. A commander who relies on heavy equipment can be followed by a soldier with psychic abilities, then by somebody who defeats Goku in hand-to-hand combat. The organization links episodes that feel almost like separate genres.

The Dragon Balls provide the reason for this apparatus to collide with Goku. He is searching for the four-star ball, not assembling an army to fight an army. As the search progresses, his path intersects with a military hunt for the same scattered treasures. For the officers, the boy is an obstacle to an operation. For the boy, the soldiers are obstacles to a memory.

There is a quiet imbalance in the motives. The Army regards the balls as instruments of power. Goku values one because his grandfather held it. This does not make him a sophisticated moral philosopher. He is still capable of acting before thinking and treating fights as exciting interruptions. But the personal source of his quest supplies a human center to a story that could otherwise become a sequence of military bases to be destroyed.

The visual contrast works immediately. Toriyama loves drawing machinery. The Red Ribbon soldiers arrive with aircraft that have plausible engines and absurd proportions, guns that look functional, and equipment he can render with great pleasure. Their organization has material weight. Against all that engineering stands a child with a staff and the Flying Nimbus.

It is not a denunciation of machines. Bulma's technology is one of the series' great sources of generosity and possibility. The conflict is between different uses of technology: to search, to rescue, to explore, to dominate. Toriyama rarely states this as an ideology. He puts a cheerful little vehicle in one panel and a military aircraft in another, and lets the story reveal what the people operating them want.

## The tower of incompatible monsters

Muscle Tower is the adventure's first great proof that an institution can function as an engine of surprises. It rises above a snowy settlement, Jingle Village, where the Red Ribbon Army has made ordinary residents miserable. General White commands the fortress and holds the village chief captive. Goku goes inside and works his way upward through a series of increasingly strange encounters.

As an architectural idea, the tower is wonderfully literal. The hero moves from floor to floor. Each floor can be a new kind of comic. A mechanical soldier poses one problem, a ninja another, an android yet another. The vertical structure saves Toriyama from having to justify why these opponents are waiting in one convenient location. They have been installed there by a military bureaucracy that apparently has no objection to locating a Japanese garden, a monstrous laboratory creature and a robot soldier in neighboring departments.

Sergeant Murasaki's encounter captures the older gag-manga Toriyama inside the newer action writer. He is a ninja who wants to be feared, but his stealth techniques invite jokes about concealment and visibility. He can be dangerous and ridiculous in the same exchange. The point is not simply that Goku can defeat a ninja. It is that the ninja's own theatrical conception of himself keeps colliding with a boy whose reactions are too straightforward to respect the performance.

Then comes Android 8, whom Goku calls Hatchan. He looks like the kind of artificial monster the villains would deploy without hesitation. His body suggests strength and intimidation. But he does not want to fight. He refuses to serve the violent purpose for which the Army intends to use him.

This is one of the earliest and clearest statements of a Dragon Ball idea that the franchise will revisit: a powerful being is not morally defined by the person who made him or by the role assigned to his body. Android 8 is a kind person in a frightening shape. The surrounding officers, ordinary humans by comparison, are perfectly comfortable with cruelty. Design and behavior have been deliberately made to disagree.

Goku likes him. Not because he has performed a long test of the android's ethical principles, but because he recognizes the goodness in how Hatchan behaves. Their friendship is not negotiated through an account of whether a robot has a soul. It emerges through actions: a refusal to harm, the willingness to help, a shared resistance to a bully.

There is genuine warmth in the scene after General White uses violence against Goku. The large artificial man becomes the person who will not tolerate seeing his smaller friend hurt. Hatchan's strength has been available all along. The important question is what could make him use it, and for whom.

The lesson is sharper because the tower is a place where everybody has been assigned a function. White is the commander. Murasaki is a guard. The mechanical fighters are weapons. Android 8 is treated as another piece of equipment. His refusal disrupts the chain of command in a way Goku's punches alone do not. The organization discovers that making a powerful body does not guarantee authority over its choices.

## The story becomes a search again

A military campaign could easily make Goku's companions irrelevant. He has the strength to smash through ordinary opponents, and the Army keeps providing targets. Instead the search for the Dragon Balls repeatedly returns the story to forms of competence he does not possess.

Bulma's radar remains the essential tool. Her capacity to recognize the problem, repair equipment, and move between technology and adventure continues to make the search possible. Goku can break a vehicle. He cannot replace the technical knowledge of the person who designed the means of finding the ball. Krillin and the rest of the group contribute courage, experience and the kind of ordinary judgment that Goku sometimes lacks.

General Blue's pursuit through a submerged cavern is one of the best demonstrations of this mixed adventure structure. The geography does much of the work. There are passages, hiding places, threats and discoveries. An underwater search becomes a trap as the Red Ribbon officer brings men and weapons into the same confined environment. The story can shift from treasure-hunting excitement to danger without abandoning the idea that the next passage might contain something surprising.

Blue himself is drawn as an unsettling contrast between elegance and cruelty. He values immaculate appearance and military discipline. He can also paralyze people with psychic power, making the obvious superiority of Goku's physical strength much less useful. The encounter creates suspense because the boy who can overpower soldiers cannot immediately solve the problem of being unable to move.

Toriyama also subjects Blue to humiliations that the officer cannot accept. His concern with cleanliness and his comic terror of a mouse give an apparently efficient killer the temperament of somebody perpetually about to lose control of his composure. Some of the period's jokes about Blue rely on queer-coded stereotypes and deserve to be understood as such. They are not necessary to explain why the best confrontations with him work. His menace is clear in the brutality with which he treats the soldiers beneath him and the fear he inflicts on the children he pursues.

Blue's path ultimately leads to a return that makes the entire fictional world feel connected. Goku pursues him into Penguin Village, where Arale Norimaki lives. This is the star of Toriyama's earlier hit, Dr. Slump, wandering into the newer manga as though no border between their worlds required inspection.

Arale is magnificently indifferent to Blue's military importance. She is extraordinarily strong, enthusiastic, and untroubled by the rules that have recently made Goku's encounters more dangerous. The officer who can terrify a room becomes a target for cartoonish overwhelming force. The meeting is not merely a promotional cameo. It restores for a moment the logic of a story in which sheer power is a joke rather than an escalating requirement.

Goku is delighted. He has met somebody who can keep up with him. That reaction reveals how open he still is to the world outside the martial-arts rankings he has begun to enter. He does not demand to know whether Arale belongs in the same continuity chart as his future opponents. He likes her because she is fun, and because a strong new friend is always interesting.

## The assassin who changes the temperature

Then Toriyama introduces Taopaipai, commonly called Mercenary Tao in English. His entrance initially looks like another exaggeration in the Army's gallery of dangerous eccentrics. He is a professional killer who treats murder as a service rendered for money. He travels by throwing a stone pillar through the air and riding it to his destination. The image is preposterous and memorable: a man so strong that an architectural object can serve as his private aircraft.

The absurd method of travel does not soften what happens when he arrives.

Goku has reached Karin Sanctuary, where Bora and his son Upa protect the land around a towering pillar that seems to extend above the clouds. Bora gives Goku the four-star ball he has been searching for. For a moment, the journey's most personal purpose has been fulfilled. The small treasure from Grandpa Gohan is back in his hands, and the new people he has met are kind.

Taopaipai kills Bora.

It is worth staying with the change in feeling. The Red Ribbon Army has harmed people before; the cruelty of its officers has not been a secret. But Bora's death occurs after the story has introduced him as a father and as a protector of a place. Upa is present, and the loss does not vanish when the fight ends. Goku cannot solve what has happened by winning an argument or making the assassin look ridiculous. A child has seen his father die.

Taopaipai then defeats Goku. The boy survives the assassin's attack because the four-star ball happens to be positioned where it can absorb part of the force. The souvenir that motivated the journey becomes, by an extraordinary bit of physical luck, the object that preserves his life.

Toriyama's plotting is still fantastical, but the emotional arithmetic has changed. Goku is not merely behind in a competition. He has failed to protect somebody who trusted him. His body is not strong enough for the immediate problem, and an innocent person has paid for the gap.

The contrast with the first tournament is instructive. Jackie Chun beat Goku so that the boy would understand there was more to learn. It was a constructed lesson within an arena. Taopaipai beats him because he has been hired to kill, and nobody is guaranteed to survive the lesson. The idea of surpassing one's limit has crossed from a game into a moral emergency.

The assassin is still drawn with Toriyama's odd sense of theatricality. The story has not suddenly become a different artist's comic. But the behavior now has a weight that persists beyond the panel. Goku wants to get stronger, and this time the reason is not the pleasure of meeting an excellent opponent. He has a promise to keep.

## A cat at the top of the world

Goku climbs Karin Tower. The journey upward is physically demanding in a way that recalls the Turtle School. Here the exercise has a single enormous setting: a vertical distance that cannot be abbreviated by simply stating that the student wishes to become powerful. At the summit he encounters Karin, a catlike hermit whose appearance once again disrupts the expectation of what great martial authority should look like.

There is a sacred water said to grant extraordinary strength. It is an alluring proposition for someone who has just learned that his existing strength is insufficient. Perhaps the answer is simply to climb high enough and drink the substance that solves the problem.

Karin will not hand it over. Goku has to take it from him.

The pursuit becomes a training exercise disguised as a search for a prize. The little cat's movement and judgment force Goku to develop speed and responsiveness. Eventually the revelation is that the water itself is ordinary; the effort of trying to obtain it was what changed the student.

It is an elegant variation on Roshi's curriculum. Roshi disguised physical training as the work of daily life. Karin disguises physical training as the pursuit of a miraculous shortcut. The destination attracts the student, but the journey supplies the improvement. In both cases Toriyama makes the result visible through actions rather than a prolonged lecture about spiritual development.

The cat's lesson is also an excellent piece of fantasy irony. A story built around magical objects that grant wishes gives its hero a fake magical object that works because he cannot get it easily. The deception is a kindness only because the training actually succeeds. The distinction between the promised water and the later, genuinely dangerous supernatural water of the King Piccolo storyline is important; these are not the same event.

Goku returns to face Taopaipai, and this time he can meet the assassin's power. The fight changes because the boy has changed. The earlier defeat was not imaginary, and the second contest is satisfying precisely because we remember how comprehensively the first went wrong.

But the rematch is not the moral conclusion of the episode. The assassin's defeat is one part of a larger effort that Goku has undertaken for Upa. He can no longer be content to retrieve the four-star ball and go home. Bora is dead. The Dragon Balls have become the means by which the boy hopes to repair a loss that happened along his path.

## A ridiculous wish at the top of the chain of command

The Army's central ambition has been represented by Commander Red, the figure at the head of the worldwide search. His organization has the shape of a terrifying instrument of control. Its officers seize communities, terrorize civilians and die carrying out orders. The reader naturally expects a tremendous political or military purpose behind the effort to gather all seven Dragon Balls.

Then the story reveals Red's desire: he wants to be taller.

It is one of the purest jokes Toriyama ever placed inside a large action story. The scale of the machinery is grossly disproportionate to the goal. The Army has mobilized armies of men and enormous resources for its commander's private insecurity. The gap between means and ends is funny because it is so great; it is revolting because the means have included actual brutality.

The joke does not mean that the Red Ribbon Army was harmless. Quite the opposite. Its underlying purpose is petty, but people have been injured and killed in pursuit of it. Toriyama refuses to grant the supreme villain the grandeur he has implicitly claimed. The organization may be capable of enormous violence while remaining fundamentally ridiculous.

Commander Black, the subordinate who learns the truth, cannot accept it. His anger exposes a difference between thinking one is serving a grand project and discovering that one has merely served a man's vanity. He kills Red and assumes command, but the collapse in the leader's legitimacy does not instantly remove the Army's capacity to fight. Goku still has to confront its headquarters and the weapons Black deploys.

The boy's assault on the base is an immensely satisfying reversal of the threat established at the beginning. The Army was introduced as a thing bigger than any individual, spread across the map, with the capacity to replace each defeated officer. Now a single boy can enter its center and break the organization apart. The system is no longer too large to challenge.

If this were the only conclusion, the Red Ribbon arc would be a victory fantasy about superior strength overcoming institutions. Toriyama complicates the experience by leaving Goku with a more intimate obligation. An army can be defeated and a promise can remain unfulfilled.

## The wish that has to be earned

Goku still needs another Dragon Ball. He has promised Upa that he will bring Bora back. The search can no longer be described as simply a child's attachment to his grandfather's keepsake, although that attachment is what started the journey. The quest has acquired another person's grief.

That is a substantial change in what the title objects are for. On the first journey, the Dragon Balls were things people wanted for private wishes: a boyfriend, a cure for fear of women, a plan for conquest. The eventual wish was a spectacular gag about underwear. During the Red Ribbon story, Goku has learned that the power to summon Shenron could restore somebody who was killed in front of his child.

The manga is not naïve about the consequences of this new possibility. Once a story can reverse death, it must find ways to preserve danger and loss. But the first wish of this kind is introduced through a very particular relationship. Goku knows Upa. He saw Bora die. The promise is a personal act, not an administrative policy for fixing every death that has ever occurred. Its emotional meaning depends on the path that brought him to this place.

The next step is neither another massive fortress nor another superior soldier. To locate the remaining ball, the group will visit Fortuneteller Baba, a strange older woman who can tell them what the Dragon Radar cannot. Her price, when the visitors cannot afford it, is another set of fights. The serial circles back to an arena, but with a purpose different from choosing the strongest person under heaven.

That circular movement is part of the pleasure of early Dragon Ball. The plot learns to combine the treasure hunt with martial-arts competition, comedy with danger, technological inventiveness with supernatural intervention. No one form has eliminated the others. An absurd military organization can contain a gentle android. A professional killer can ride a stone pillar and then do something truly horrifying. A cat can teach a boy to become stronger by protecting an ordinary bottle of water.

Above all, the Red Ribbon adventure enlarges the meaning of Goku's strength. In the tournament, he wanted to see how good he could become. Here he has seen what happens when he is not good enough. It would be easy to draw the lesson as a simple demand for more power. The better reading is that strength has acquired a responsibility he did not seek and cannot now avoid.

The little four-star ball remains a reminder of Grandpa Gohan. It has also led Goku to another boy who loves his father. The world has become too full of other people's lives for the quest to be only his own.
