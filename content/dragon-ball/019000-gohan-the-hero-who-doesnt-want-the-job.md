# Gohan: The Hero Who Doesn't Want the Job

A boy who once saved the Earth from Cell goes to high school, tries to keep his identity secret, and starts dressing like a superhero.

The costume is ridiculous. There is a helmet, a cape, poses performed with complete sincerity, and a name that might have been invented by someone who had heard of heroic branding without ever having met an actual teenager. Gohan calls himself the Great Saiyaman. He uses his extraordinary strength to stop petty criminals and make earnest speeches about justice. People look at him. Sometimes they are impressed. Sometimes they have excellent reasons to be confused.

For a reader arriving directly from the Cell Games, this is a strange move. That battle ended with Gohan reaching a height of power beyond Goku's, destroying a monster that threatened the planet, and surviving an experience that killed his father. In a conventional heroic succession, the next story would present him as the new adult champion. Instead Toriyama gives him homework, a secret identity, and a tremendously awkward first encounter with ordinary school life.

It is tempting to describe those chapters as a detour before the real action returns. But they may tell us more about Gohan than his greatest fight does.

What does Gohan want when nobody is forcing him to save the world? Who is he when the emergency ends? And what if being able to win the hardest battle is not the same as wanting to spend a life looking for harder battles?

There is a sharp difference between Gohan's suitability for Dragon Ball's escalating conflicts and his own desires. That difference becomes the subject of this essay. It also helps explain why fans can be simultaneously moved by his victories and frustrated by the long intervals when he refuses to behave like the hero they have decided he should become.

## The introduction of a child, not an heir

When Gohan first appears, he is four years old. His grandfather's name has been passed on to him, and he is the son of the world's great martial artist. Those are obvious signs that a story might be preparing a successor. But he is also a polite child who wants to become a scholar. He is dressed and looked after like a boy whose family expects him to study. His first scenes are not a montage of his secret training. They establish a home, a parent, and the fact that the child is vulnerable.

Then Raditz arrives, kidnaps Gohan, and turns that ordinary vulnerability into a weapon against Goku. Gohan's first truly startling display of power is not the fruit of an athletic ambition. It is a desperate reaction to seeing his father hurt. He breaks out of confinement and strikes an enemy who has seemed impossible to defeat.

From that point on, adults repeatedly look at Gohan and see a resource. Piccolo sees someone who might help stop the next Saiyan invasion, so he takes the boy away to train. The preparation is harsh. Gohan spends time surviving alone, terrified in a wilderness that would be a serious ordeal for a grown person. He learns discipline and courage, but it would be dishonest to romanticize the method simply because it produces a good fighter. The danger was real and the child did not choose it.

Something more interesting happens during that year than the development of a new technique. Piccolo, who begins as the enemy of Gohan's father, starts changing in response to caring for the boy. Gohan learns to trust him. Piccolo eventually protects him at the cost of his own life. A relationship that began as coercion produces mutual attachment, and the story allows both facts to be true.

The connection gives Gohan an identity that is not a smaller copy of Goku. Goku approaches a strong opponent with curiosity and excitement. Gohan approaches many opponents with fear on behalf of somebody else. He fights not because fighting is an invitation but because the alternative is to let someone he loves be hurt.

That difference makes his extraordinary strength more dramatic. It also creates a recurring pattern that is not entirely comfortable: the people around Gohan need him to experience a terrible event before his potential becomes available. A power awakened by grief is useful in a crisis. It is not a healthy educational program.

## Namek and the choice to help somebody

Gohan's trip to Namek is often remembered as a sequence of desperate escapes from stronger enemies. He travels with Krillin and Bulma, looking for a way to restore the friends who died in the Saiyan attack. But Namek also lets Gohan act outside his father's shadow in a different way. He encounters the Namekians as people whose lives have been invaded and destroyed. He helps Dende, a child from the planet, even though such help increases the danger to the travelers.

Think of how much trouble that small choice causes. Dende becomes essential to communication and the Dragon Balls, and his presence changes the tactical possibilities of the entire journey. Yet Gohan does not have the foresight to rescue him because he will prove useful in a complicated wish strategy. He is responding to an endangered person.

This resembles Goku's generosity, but with a different emotional center. Goku may find an enemy worth saving because there is still a future fight, a possible friendship, or an untested possibility. Gohan does not need the possibility of a future competition to justify helping Dende. The other person's immediate suffering is enough.

Krillin matters here too. He is older, but not nearly powerful enough to guarantee safety on Namek. The two are repeatedly driven into situations where they must decide when to hide, when to flee, and when to help somebody despite the odds. Gohan's courage is not a little warrior's confidence that he can defeat anyone. It is the willingness to act while knowing he cannot. He is often brave because he is afraid and acts anyway.

Namek thus strengthens two things at once. His capacity for violence grows, and his sense of responsibility to people outside his family becomes clearer. Only one of those developments can be measured by a scouter.

## The strongest child on Earth

The Cell Games seem to offer a solution to Gohan's divided life. He trains with Goku, reaches a level that astonishes even his father, and eventually transforms into the fighter who can destroy Cell. For a moment the logic of the series seems to have chosen a new center. The next generation has surpassed the last.

Yet the actual sequence is much more uncomfortable than that summary. Goku wants his son to fight Cell because he knows the child can become stronger than either of them. He expects Gohan to respond to challenge in something like the way he would. Piccolo is horrified by the assumption. Gohan is not excited to test himself against the monster. He is reluctant to enter a battle whose cruelty he understands.

Cell does not give him the luxury of refusing. Android 16 is destroyed; Gohan's friends suffer; anger finally produces the transformation the story has been promising. What follows is not a demonstration that Goku's competitive psychology was secretly correct for his son. Gohan's new power arrives entwined with grief. He starts humiliating Cell and delays ending the battle, suddenly becoming capable of the same dangerous confidence that he once feared in opponents.

His victory requires another loss. Goku sacrifices himself to remove Cell from Earth, and Gohan must complete the fight with the encouragement of a father who is now dead. It is possible to love the father's and son's shared final attack and still notice that this is a harsh coming-of-age ceremony. The boy was right to feel that power should not require so much suffering. The story was also right, within its terrible fictional emergency, that someone needed to stop Cell.

We can see why many readers expected Gohan to inherit the whole series. The scene offers a perfectly legible transfer of the champion's place. But a championship is something an audience may confer on a character without asking him whether he wants it.

The most revealing question comes after the threat is gone. Does he treat his triumph as the beginning of a new search for stronger foes? No. He goes back toward the life in which getting stronger is not the only thing he knows how to do.

## A superhero costume as an argument for peace

Years later, Gohan enters a city high school and discovers that being inconspicuous is almost impossible for someone who can move as he does. He wants ordinary social relationships but does not know how to reconcile them with his extraordinary abilities. His answer is to become an overtly theatrical masked hero, a disguise so elaborate that it draws fresh attention to his behavior.

The Great Saiyaman chapters work first as comedy. Toriyama gets to enjoy the poses, the name, the disjunction between a fighter who can destroy monsters and a teenager panicking about classmates recognizing him. In the wake of the Cell Games, where a mistake could kill everybody, the fact that Gohan now worries about embarrassing himself is a luxury. A peaceful world ought to create problems small enough to be funny.

The costume is also revealing. Gohan is not trying to become the hidden master of every martial artist on Earth. He wants to intervene in the daily wrongs he encounters. Somebody is being threatened by a criminal; somebody needs help; there is a practical problem he can solve. This is a modest fantasy of public service, wrapped in the loudest possible performance of superheroism.

It is tempting to laugh at him for imitating less powerful heroes when he possesses strength beyond any masked television champion. But the scale of the task is part of the point. The boy who was made to confront Raditz, Nappa, Frieza, and Cell is finally in a position to select battles that do not require the world to be destroyed first. He can save one person and go back to class.

Videl complicates this new life in the best way. She is already brave, active in fighting crime, and unimpressed by Gohan's inadequate attempts at concealment. She knows that he has abilities other people do not. He cannot make the relationship work simply by being stronger. He has to explain himself, be embarrassed, and negotiate with another person who has her own ideas about courage.

He teaches Videl to use her ki to fly. This is a delightful reversal of Gohan's childhood. A teacher once dragged him into a brutal curriculum because the world needed a weapon. Now he is teaching an eager peer a technique that opens the sky. The lessons have their own comedy and impatience, but the basic relationship is cooperative. Videl wants to learn, and he wants to help. No invasion has to provide a deadline.

That may be one of the gentlest images of growth in the entire series: strength passed on without a catastrophe to authorize it.

## Chi-Chi, education, and the ordinary future

There is a familiar joke that Gohan becomes a scholar only because Chi-Chi forces him to study. The joke has a surface basis. She is insistent about education and tired of the dangers that have swallowed her family. Sometimes the manga plays her strictness for comedy. But reducing Gohan's life to his mother's instructions ignores the way the boy repeatedly reveals an orientation different from Goku's.

Gohan is curious about things besides combat. He enjoys the company of people who are not opponents. He does not experience the prospect of being strongest as the only meaningful reward available. Even when he trains hard or intervenes heroically, he lacks the restless desire to discover an ever stronger adversary. The desire for study is present at his introduction; it is not a late surprise inserted into the ending.

Chi-Chi also has reasons to fear that the martial-arts world will consume her children. She has already lost long stretches of life with Goku and watched Gohan return from dangerous conflicts. Her wish for a normal education is not evidence that she cannot appreciate courage. It may be an attempt to protect the possibility of a future in which courage is not constantly needed.

The story does not ask us to choose a single parent as the source of everything Gohan becomes. His life contains Goku's physical gifts, Chi-Chi's commitment to an ordinary adulthood, Piccolo's difficult mentorship, Krillin's friendship, and Gohan's own decisions. He does not have to reject one relationship to be shaped by another.

This is especially important because the word potential keeps appearing around him. People with exceptional gifts are often told that not maximizing those gifts is a failure. In Gohan's case, maximizing usually means returning to the cycle of harder training and more dangerous fights. What if he wants to devote his intelligence and care to something else? A person can have the potential to do a thing and still have good reasons not to organize an entire life around it.

## The Buu arc does not settle the argument

The Majin Buu story seems determined to test whether Gohan can really stay outside the heroic succession. At first he is in a school comedy. Soon the tournament becomes a disaster, Babidi's schemes unleash catastrophe, and Gohan is back in a fight he has not sought.

He receives extraordinary new power through the Elder Kai's long ritual rather than through another conventional transformation. In one sense, the ritual offers a different solution to his problem: his abilities can be drawn out without demanding a new outburst of personal rage. The powerful Gohan who returns to confront Buu appears capable of ending the crisis himself.

But his return is not a conclusion. Buu manipulates the situation and absorbs him. The story turns again toward Goku, Vegeta, fusion, and finally the energy gathered by the people of Earth. For readers who hoped the Cell Games had permanently transferred the lead role, this can feel like the last of several reversals. Gohan is once more presented as the answer and then removed so that somebody else can save the day.

There is a legitimate criticism here. A character can be granted a great entrance without being allowed to affect the ending in proportion to the hope the entrance creates. The Buu saga repeatedly makes possible endings and then destroys them, sometimes to thrilling effect and sometimes at the cost of momentum and satisfaction. Gohan is one of the characters most exposed to that pattern.

But it is also possible to confuse two disappointments: disappointment that the narrative does not fully use Gohan as a fighter, and disappointment that Gohan does not become a person for whom fighting is the only important life. These are different judgments. One can criticize the handling of his place in the climax without insisting that the character's eventual profession is itself a defeat.

The distinction is not a trick for excusing anything that happens. There is room for a better story about an adult scholar who still helps when necessary. There is room for the spectacular climax he was briefly promised. But there is also room for a character who does not spend every peaceful year preparing for the next apocalypse. He does not owe the audience constant escalation simply because he could provide it.

## What the final chapter allows him

At the end of the original manga, years have passed since Buu was defeated. The world is still capable of mischief and competition, but the immediate emergency is over. Gohan has a family with Videl. Their daughter Pan carries the next generation into the tournament ring. Gohan has the adulthood that was repeatedly interrupted when he was young.

The family is not a denial of his earlier courage. He saved people. He faced terror. He lost people he loved and helped restore a threatened world. Those acts remain true even if he does not keep proving his worth by repeating the same ordeal.

The contrast with Goku is fitting. The father seeks out Uub, a new student and potential rival, and leaves the tournament eager to begin another period of training. The son has built a life in which the tournament is only one part of the story. Neither choice needs to be condemned simply because it differs from the other. Goku's desire can be contagious and joyful. Gohan's steadier desire can be equally affirmative.

There is a small sadness in the way popular stories treat characters like Gohan. Their defining victories can raise expectations that make any subsequent ordinary life look like a retreat. The audience has seen him become nearly invincible, so an academic career or an awkward romantic relationship feels insufficient. Yet the purpose of saving a world ought to include allowing somebody to live peacefully in it.

Gohan's strength made it possible for people to have futures. His own future need not be a permanent continuation of the crisis that revealed his strength.

We might put the question this way. When Goku looks at someone stronger, he sees an invitation. When Gohan looks at someone suffering, he sees a responsibility. Both can act heroically. Neither temperament is a full moral system. But Gohan's version gives Dragon Ball a kind of moral imagination its protagonist alone cannot supply.

That is why the superhero costume is more than an embarrassing joke. A child who was drafted into a series of world-ending wars grows into a young man who wants to help neighbors, study, love somebody, and occasionally strike a pose so ridiculous that no one could mistake him for a hardened professional hero. The comedy does not erase the boy who defeated Cell. It reminds us why defeating Cell was worth doing.

He wanted the world to be the sort of place in which he could be ridiculous without anyone dying.

For all its battles, that is one of Dragon Ball's most generous visions of victory.
