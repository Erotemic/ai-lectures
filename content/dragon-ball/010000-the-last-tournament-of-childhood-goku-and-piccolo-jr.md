# 010000 — The Last Tournament of Childhood: Goku and Piccolo Jr.

**Track:** Dragon Ball: From Penguin Village to the End of Z. **Audience:** Fans and curious general listeners. **Prerequisites:** None; the relevant characters and earlier tournaments are introduced in the narration. **Scope:** Kami's training and the 23rd Tenkaichi Budokai, approximately original manga Tales 162–194. **Spoilers:** Piccolo's origin, Kami's predicament, Goku's marriage promise and first tournament championship, and Piccolo Jr.'s survival. **Perspective:** Close reading and criticism; character motivations are interpreted from the story, not presented as private authorial plans.

# The Last Tournament of Childhood: Goku and Piccolo Jr.

A familiar boy walks into the World Martial Arts Tournament, and his friends do not immediately recognize him.

The boy is Goku. He has grown taller. His face has changed. His clothes are familiar enough, but they no longer hang on the round little body that took its first trip with Bulma in search of the Dragon Balls. Three years have passed since he defeated the Great Demon King Piccolo. During that interval, Goku has been training high above the world, at the palace of its guardian, Kami. The other competitors have aged, too. Yet their astonishment at seeing him is a reminder that the reader has been living with a child for a very long time.

Toriyama does not stop the story to explain the psychology of adolescence. He lets the body do the work. The standing joke that the tiny boy could beat adults has been permanently altered. A fighter whose size once concealed his ability now arrives looking like someone who might plausibly win the championship. The change should be ordinary. In this world, however, ordinary physical growth is almost more surprising than a talking animal or a man who can fly.

It is a delicious reversal. The tournament once presented its competitors as mysterious figures to Goku. Now Goku is the mysterious figure to his friends.

The twenty-third Tenkaichi Budokai, the third great tournament in the original *Dragon Ball* television story, is often remembered for one enormous fight. Goku faces Piccolo Junior, the surviving successor of the monster who tried to subjugate the world. Their battle is a spectacular collision of techniques and terrible damage, but this is also a peculiar celebration: a wedding, the settling of old rivalries, a visit by God in someone else's body, and the long-delayed award of a trophy. It is at once the end of a childhood and the beginning of an adult life that the story barely has time to imagine.

The central question is not simply whether Goku is finally strong enough to beat his opponent. It is whether he can win without becoming the sort of person who believes strength entitles him to decide everything about another life.

## A child who learned from God

To understand why Kami's training changes Goku, it helps to remember why he went there. The Great Demon King Piccolo had murdered Krillin's generation of fighters, destroyed the dragon Shenron, and very nearly destroyed Goku. The victory came at a high cost. Goku needed his dead friends restored, and the creator of the Dragon Balls lived above even Karin's enormous tower. So he climbed farther than he had ever climbed.

At the palace, the face that greeted Goku looked horribly like the dead demon king. Kami and Piccolo had once been a single being. Seeking to become Earth's guardian, that being separated the evil within himself; the expelled part became King Piccolo, while the purified counterpart became Kami. The two remained bound by life. Killing one meant killing the other.

This is the kind of revelation that could make a less nimble adventure grind to a halt. A supposedly simple enemy becomes the rejected half of a god. The rules of the Dragon Balls become tied to the well-being of their maker. And the boy who solved the crisis with a punch discovers that a more powerful punch might kill the person who restored his friends.

Kami also warns that the demon king produced an offspring before dying. Piccolo Junior, who carries his predecessor's will and hatred, will return. Goku stays to train with Kami and the attendant Mr. Popo, both because he needs to prepare and because the story has opened a new height above the tower. The geography itself is a joke played on our sense of completion. The thing Goku thought was the top turns out not to be the top.

Three years pass. Toriyama skips most of the training. This is a substantial artistic decision. Earlier, we watched Goku and Krillin carry heavy shells and deliver milk; we understood Roshi's teaching through ordinary labor. At Kami's palace, the instruction is more mysterious. When Goku returns, we learn its results largely from how he moves and from how poorly his opponents can read him. The unseen interval has turned his growth into a surprise that the tournament will reveal, round by round.

He also arrives wearing weighted clothing. When he removes it to fight seriously, what looked like a familiar costume becomes a portable demonstration of years of preparation. Power is not announced by a numerical chart. It is concealed in what an opponent assumed was simply cloth.

## The bride in the ring

The first major opponent Goku encounters in the main competition is an anonymous woman who is furious with him. The situation becomes clearer only after Goku defeats her. She is Chi-Chi, the daughter of the Ox-King, whom he met years earlier while traveling with Bulma. As children, she asked him to marry her. Goku agreed without understanding what he had been asked. He thought he might be promised something to eat.

Now she has arrived to collect an answer. It is funny that someone who can face monstrous assassins with complete confidence becomes the object of an ordinary human demand he cannot decode. The joke also has a sharp edge. Chi-Chi has remembered a promise for years. Goku has almost entirely forgotten the person who received it. She has learned to fight, entered the tournament, and approached him on a stage where he cannot fail to acknowledge her.

A weaker version of the gag would make Chi-Chi a nameless punishment for a hero who wants to keep roaming. Toriyama gives her a choice to act, even if her identity is initially hidden and she is quickly beaten. Her insistence is not merely an inconvenience Goku needs to escape. She expects the same recognition that the fighters expect when they ask to face him fairly. She insists that words have consequences, even when the person who uttered them was too innocent to understand.

Goku's response is almost absurdly straightforward. Once he remembers and understands, he agrees to marry her. There is no elaborate courtship. The crowd celebrates. Chi-Chi cheers him on through the rest of the competition, at one point worrying that a newly married woman could immediately become a widow.

One may enjoy the scene and still notice how little space the story gives Chi-Chi's hopes beyond her wish to marry Goku. She is a capable fighter and an unusually determined person. She also gets folded rapidly into a romantic future whose practical meaning the manga has barely explored. That is a limitation of the writing, not a reason to pretend the scene has no charm. Its charm comes from the collision between an ordinary life event and a heroic tournament. Its weakness is that the ordinary life belongs much more fully to her than to him.

This collision tells us something about the coming transition. Goku has been allowed to stay a child while everyone around him remembers obligations. Marriage offers him a kind of adulthood no amount of combat training has prepared him for. Toriyama gives him the new responsibility, then promptly turns back to the fighting. We are left wondering what being a husband will mean for someone who understands promises and danger far better than domestic routine.

## The old assassin and the former student

Another preliminary fight provides a less affectionate reunion. The assassin Taopaipai, or Mercenary Tao, returns to confront Tien. Tao was once the nightmare of the Red Ribbon Army storyline: a cheerful professional killer who murdered Upa's father, Bora, and nearly killed Goku. Tien once admired the same ruthless tradition. His former Crane School masters treated violence and success as a single vocation.

Now Tao has been rebuilt as a cyborg after his earlier defeat. He brings concealed weapons and the determination to reassert the old school's authority over the pupil who rejected it. It is an almost comic transformation, another man in a world of mechanical prosthetics and impossible repairs, but it carries a serious question. What does it mean to outgrow the person who taught you how to fight?

Tien defeats him without taking the invitation to become cruel again. In the previous tournament, Tien's decisive change had been to refuse unfair help from his teacher. Here he can prove the change without a speech about his redemption. He can face a dangerous remnant of his past, recognize the danger, and decline to make that past his future.

The scene also illuminates Goku's own story. The most significant victories at this tournament are not always over stronger people. Tien has become more capable, but the harder achievement is keeping his judgment when the man he used to fear stands in front of him. An old teacher can continue to influence a pupil for years. So can the decision to stop obeying him.

Tien later faces Goku in a rematch of the previous tournament's final. Last time, a collision in midair sent Goku to the ground before Tien, handing the victory to Tien under the ring-out rule. The result was fair within the tournament's peculiar circumstances, and both fighters understood how narrow it had been. This time the contest measures how far each has traveled.

Tien reveals his technique for dividing into four bodies. It is visually wonderful: a single fighter becomes an apparently overwhelming group. But there is a cost. His power is divided among the copies. Goku recognizes the weakness and turns it into a tactical advantage. A trick that seems to multiply strength instead dilutes it. The younger student who once relied on surprising bursts of ability now reads the structure of his opponent's technique almost instantly.

This is a satisfying progression because the story does not simply tell us he has trained harder. It lets us hear and see a more experienced way of thinking. Goku has become frighteningly good at understanding a fight.

## The opponent who remembers being a monster

Piccolo Junior enters under the name Majunior, a name that conceals his connection to the Great Demon King. He is not an entirely new villain and not exactly the old one returned unchanged. He emerged as an egg from his dying predecessor, bearing a demand for revenge and a memory of hated Goku. He is taller, more controlled, and far better prepared than the tyrant whose rule ended when a small boy punched through him.

Krillin meets him before Goku does. The match deserves attention because Krillin is the character the last great disaster had seemed to remove from the story. He was murdered by the demon king's servant and later restored to life. Now he faces that enemy's successor in an arena before a crowd. It is as though the tournament has given one of its own a chance to answer the earlier violation of its peace.

Krillin cannot win, but he does not simply collapse. He fights with intelligence and resourcefulness. Piccolo is surprised by how formidable one of Goku's supposedly lesser companions can be. This is an important reminder that the human fighters are still participants in the art of combat, even as the upper limits begin to leave them behind. Krillin's achievement is not disguised as a win. It is the dignity of forcing a much more dangerous opponent to take him seriously.

A second competitor named Shen appears to be a rather odd older man. He fools his opponents with an air of incompetence and then displays disconcerting power. He is Kami, acting through a borrowed human body. His purpose is not to win the championship but to contain Piccolo Junior through the Mafu-Ba, the same dangerous sealing technique that once defeated the original demon king.

Here the cleverness turns against the divine authority. Piccolo has prepared a reversal. Kami's technique is sent back at him, and Kami is imprisoned in the very vessel meant for Piccolo.

The god is not the last line of defense after all. He has made an error. He has underestimated a growing opponent. He has tried to solve a human crisis with an old answer, and the enemy has learned the answer too. The final responsibility falls to Goku, who is neither divine nor particularly interested in receiving an order from heaven.

The danger also has a peculiar logic. Kami and Piccolo's shared life means that destroying Piccolo may destroy Kami. Destroy Kami, and the Dragon Balls lose their maker. The hero is not merely choosing between winning and losing. He must reckon with the consequences of an apparently decisive solution. The story has prepared this problem through the long relationship between the guardian and his rejected evil. It will become one of the most important facts about Piccolo long after the tournament ends.

## A tournament whose ring cannot survive

The championship match begins with a familiar promise: two opponents, an arena, rules, a referee, and victory determined by the shape of the fight. For two previous tournaments the rules have helped transform violence into something intelligible and, much of the time, enjoyable. A competitor can be stronger yet lose through a ring-out. Someone can learn a lesson without dying. The spectators can applaud a clever trick rather than a killing blow.

Piccolo does not share the tournament's affection for its own limits. His purpose is vengeance and domination. As the fight grows larger, he expands his body to gigantic proportions, unleashes waves of energy, regenerates damaged limbs, and eventually levels the arena itself. The setting that once controlled the action is blasted apart by the action it contains.

For Toriyama, destruction is also an invitation to invent movement. Goku fires a Kamehameha from his feet to propel himself toward his opponent, keeping his hands free. At another point his improved energy attacks deflect threats to the arena. Piccolo's expanding and regenerating body forces a change of perspective. The audience cannot keep imagining the fighters as two little figures trading punches on a floor. They are performing in an ever-changing landscape of craters, broken stone and air.

These inventions are funny in the technical sense: they take a known device and discover an unexpected use for it. A Kamehameha is not merely a bigger punch. It can be an engine. Flight is not merely a way to cover distance. It can preserve the possibility of an attack when every limb seems to be lost. Piccolo's immense size looks terrifying and spectacular, but that very magnificence creates new surfaces and openings Goku may exploit.

Toriyama keeps a trace of the old tournament alive even when the arena disappears. The difference between killing the enemy and defeating him by the competition's rules remains decisive. Goku's body is badly injured, and Piccolo seems to have rendered him unable to fight. The villain launches what he believes will be the final attack. The audience is invited to see an immobilized body and assume the story has reached the end of possible movement.

Then Goku rises. He has learned to fly, or more exactly, to move through the air under his own power, without relying on the Flying Nimbus. He dodges the attack and drives into Piccolo. The collision sends Piccolo out of bounds. After two tournaments in which a narrow technicality deprived Goku of first place, a narrow technical rule gives him the championship.

The payoff is almost mischievous. The final fight is monstrous enough to threaten the world, yet it can still be decided by the old-fashioned question of where a fighter lands. The rules are battered, but not completely discarded. Goku finally wins his prize not by executing his opponent but by demonstrating a better understanding of combat and of the conditions under which combat becomes a contest.

The championship is earned in a series that has often treated victory as a reason to keep learning. Jackie Chun defeated Goku partly to keep him from believing he had nothing left to accomplish. Tien beat him in a contest neither was fully satisfied with. Now Goku can win without treating the trophy as permission to stop growing.

## The Senzu bean nobody expected

A hero who has finally defeated a world-threatening enemy can be forgiven for deciding to end the threat permanently. Kami himself wants Piccolo destroyed, even though the shared-life problem makes the desire dangerous. Piccolo has attacked innocent people, threatened the world, and made no secret of his intentions. The simplest dramatic ending would be to kill him and declare a new era of peace.

Goku refuses.

He intervenes to stop Kami, then gives Piccolo a Senzu bean, a miraculous restorative grown by Karin. Piccolo regains his strength and flies away promising revenge. One can describe the gesture as merciful. One can also describe it as reckless. It is both a personal judgment about the defeated opponent and a decision that exposes other people to a future risk. The script does not need us to pretend this is morally tidy.

Goku has learned through combat that his opponent is not simply the same tyrant he killed. Piccolo Junior inherits the old demon king's hatred, but he is also a competitor who has trained, planned, adapted, and lost. There is the possibility of a future that does not consist entirely of fulfilling the father or predecessor's revenge. Goku leaves that possibility open. And he likes that the world may still contain someone strong enough to challenge him.

That final motive complicates the gesture. Goku is capable of kindness without demanding a reward. He is also hungry for competition, sometimes to a degree that would make a more cautious guardian nervous. His generosity toward an enemy cannot be separated perfectly from his confidence that he might defeat that enemy again. As the manga progresses into increasingly dangerous conflicts, this mixture of innocence, conviction and appetite will become one of the central arguments about his character.

For now, though, the act produces a possibility the Demon King storyline could scarcely imagine: Piccolo leaves alive, and the world does not immediately end. This is not redemption. It is the space in which redemption might eventually occur.

## A wedding without a domestic ending

When the excitement subsides, Goku and Chi-Chi leave together on the Flying Nimbus. The image is wonderfully economical. The boy whose first great journey began when Bulma persuaded him to leave his isolated home now departs with another person who expects a continuing place in his life. A device once associated with wandering freely becomes the conveyance of a newly married couple.

The ending does not show us how they will live. It has no interest in resolving the practical questions that Chi-Chi's appearance raised. We are meant to feel that the adventure has opened into an ordinary future, yet the world the story has invented makes ordinary futures hard to imagine. Goku can defeat demons and discover secret dimensions of the sky. He still does not completely understand the kinds of promises other people organize their days around.

This is why I like thinking of the twenty-third tournament as the last tournament of childhood, rather than the first tournament of the next, more famous phase. It completes several long-running jokes and ambitions. Goku finally wins. Tien's break with the Crane School is settled. Krillin and the others prove they have kept growing. The Dragon Balls, Kami, and the demon king's family become a more connected mythology. Chi-Chi's old question receives an answer, even if it is not the sort of answer an ordinary romantic drama would consider sufficient.

Above all, Piccolo Junior survives. The defeated enemy is both a remnant of the past and a person who will have to decide how long he wants to remain one. The door has not been closed on the frightening legacy Goku fought to escape.

In the original manga, the story does not change books at this point. There is no authorial wall declaring that the real series starts when an alien later arrives. The television adaptation does use a new title for that next era, but the tournament belongs to the same continuing adventure that began with a boy, a tail, a fish and an astonished girl with a car.

This ending is worth keeping intact. Before the fighters could learn the terrible secret of Goku's ancestry, before transformations and cosmic armies, *Dragon Ball* let its hero do something small and startling. He won the fight he had always wanted to win. Then he made sure the person he defeated would be able to get up again.
