# Before the Dragon Balls: Akira Toriyama Finds His Line

Before Goku ever learned to fly, his creator learned to draw socks.

Not metaphorical socks. Socks for advertising. Akira Toriyama worked in commercial illustration after finishing school, and among the things he was paid to draw were ordinary products that somebody had to make legible and attractive on a printed page. Household goods. Clothes. Objects with seams, curves, weight, and the awkward problem of being recognizable from the wrong angle. He would later recall that this work helped him understand the shapes of things.

That is an unexpectedly good place to begin the history of *Dragon Ball*. We are accustomed to starting with the heroic body. A muscular figure stands in a crater and makes an impossible promise. But Toriyama's drawing is just as memorable when the subject is a little scooter, a rounded spaceship, a box of equipment, a house folded into a capsule, or a car that appears to have been designed by someone who likes cars and is very willing to make fun of them.

He could make you believe in a machine without making you understand how it worked. He could give a vehicle a short wheelbase, an oversized windshield, a squat cheerful profile, and a cluster of mechanical details that suggested the machine could actually start. Its function might be ridiculous, but it had a body. And when the vehicle was next to a smiling animal in human clothes, both belonged to the same world.

The artist who created Son Goku was not born drawing superpowered martial artists. He became a professional cartoonist through commercial design, a sequence of rejected pages, a collision with an editor who argued with him constantly, and a comedy about a robot child who was far more interesting than the man who had built her.

His path to *Dragon Ball* leads first to Penguin Village.

## A commercial artist in search of a page

Toriyama was born in 1955 in Japan's Aichi Prefecture. He liked drawing as a child, and the influences he later remembered included cartoons, manga, film, television and the mechanical world around him. A profile published by the Japanese cultural site Nippon.com describes his early admiration for Disney animation and his encounter with Osamu Tezuka's comics. Later, he grew increasingly interested in movies: comedy, action, special effects, science fiction, and the sort of film that offers a new creature or gadget before it has finished explaining the last one.

These interests are easy to recognize if you already know his work. Toriyama's imaginary worlds rarely separate the modern from the mythical with much ceremony. A monster can ride in a car. A space traveler can arrive in a village that looks as if it is waiting for a school festival. Dinosaurs, dinosaurs beside cars, cars modified to look like dinosaurs: none of this requires a committee meeting. It simply requires a cartoonist who enjoys drawing all three.

He went to a school with a design program and then took a job producing commercial art. The advertising work was useful, but office life did not suit him. There were deadlines and demands, and by the time he left, after roughly two years, he did not yet have a successful manga career waiting for him. He had drawing skills and a desire to earn money by using them.

It is tempting to rewrite a story like this so that every early decision points directly toward a future masterpiece. That is not how the evidence reads. Toriyama tried to enter the manga business through contests and submissions, and he did not immediately win. Nor did his first published work become a great popular success. He was not simply waiting for the world to notice a fully formed genius. He was discovering which parts of his skill were useful for telling stories that readers wanted to follow.

There is a difference between being able to draw an impressive thing and being able to make a reader turn the page. An illustration can hold attention by being beautiful or intricate. A comic has another obligation: something must happen from panel to panel, the reader must know where to look, and the information on a page must arrive in a productive order. A joke can be technically excellent and still fail if you see the punch line too early. A character can be beautifully designed and still leave you indifferent if you do not want to see what the character does.

This distinction became central to Toriyama's professional education.

In 1978, *Weekly Shonen Jump* published his short comic *Wonder Island*. It was the beginning of his published career, but not the opening of an uninterrupted success. The follow-up work did not suddenly make him famous either. The stories contained the beginnings of an artistic personality: pop-culture parody, visual eccentricity, people who behave badly in absurd situations, a readiness to introduce something technically sophisticated simply because it is funny to draw. But a distinctive sense of humor and a reliably effective comic strip are different achievements.

Toriyama's editor was Kazuhiko Torishima. He was relatively new to the job too. In a lengthy interview conducted by Ollie Barder years later, Torishima described their relationship as argumentative, experimental, and full of rejection. Torishima remembered large numbers of pages being turned down. His recollection of their apprenticeship became part of the folklore around Toriyama, including a much-repeated figure of roughly five hundred rejected pages. We should treat that as his retrospective account, rather than as an audited inventory. The stronger point does not depend on the number: the two had to learn how to make good comics together.

An editor does not draw the page. A cartoonist does not control how a thousand strangers will read it. Between those two limitations lies a collaboration that can be productive precisely because the people involved disagree. Torishima thought hard about panel arrangements and how a reader responds to a character on sight. Toriyama could invent faces, bodies, objects, and jokes that the editor could not have drawn himself. The arguments forced the question that a creator working alone can avoid: this may be clever, but is it working?

## The road through Wonder Island and Tomato

Early Toriyama makes better sense as a sequence of experiments than as a list of obscure titles on the way to a famous one.

*Wonder Island* offers a kind of comic freedom: a strange place in which almost anything might appear, and the writer can throw in an elaborate visual joke without first constructing a plausible reason for it. The very name promises a world that will not behave. The trouble is that comic freedom can work against narrative momentum. If every panel could produce anything, why should the reader anticipate any particular development? Surprise is valuable, but surprise alone is difficult to sustain week after week.

Another early short work, *Tomato the Cutesy Gumshoe*, turns to a more immediately legible premise: a young woman working as a detective who is not especially good at doing the job the way a competent detective would. In this case, the collision between a role and a personality already produces a comic engine. You know what a detective is supposed to do. You can appreciate the disaster when this one does something else.

Torishima later said that he had encouraged Toriyama to try female protagonists, and Toriyama's own accounts support the importance of that push. We can see the direction without pretending that a single editorial remark caused every subsequent success. The young artist was experimenting with the shapes of comic characters and with the kinds of people who could carry an ongoing story. A protagonist does not have to be heroic or even effective. It can be enough to put a compelling personality in a situation where competence is never guaranteed.

There is also something important about the ordinary surface of these early jokes. A Toriyama character can be made endearing by the way a head sits on a small body, how a hat crowds a pair of eyes, or how a supposedly intimidating machine has been given a wonderfully stupid face. You do not need to read a complex backstory to understand that a character is pompous, anxious, greedy, excitable or innocent. The drawing speaks first.

Consider the alternative. Imagine *Dragon Ball* without that ability. Bulma would have to explain herself at length before we knew what sort of person had driven into Goku's life. Master Roshi would require a scene establishing the contrast between his mastery and his indignities. Oolong would need a paragraph of character description before he could become funny. But Toriyama knows how to make a design carry an expectation, and how to break that expectation with an action.

The early shorts are not yet *Dragon Ball*. They are where the reader can see Toriyama working out how a page becomes a performance.

## The wrong person in the title

Then came *Dr. Slump*, which began in *Weekly Shonen Jump* in 1980.

The nominal star was Senbei Norimaki, an inventor with enough technical skill to produce extraordinary machines and too little common sense to manage the results. His greatest invention, at the beginning, is a humanoid robot called Arale. She looks like a cheerful girl in glasses. She is also absurdly strong, startlingly naïve, and incapable of accepting the social rules that adults use to make everyday life appear orderly.

The joke has several layers. A scientist builds a machine intended to demonstrate his genius. The machine immediately becomes the more interesting personality. Its maker spends his time trying to preserve a respectable reputation that his creation does not understand and has no reason to respect. The invention is a triumph; the inventor has lost control of the story.

Toriyama initially imagined Senbei as the central character. Torishima thought Arale should take over. In his 2016 interview, Torishima recalled the argument vividly: he preferred the girl, while Toriyama doubted that a girl protagonist would work in a magazine marketed to boys. They wagered over reader response, according to Torishima's account, and the editor's preference prevailed. Toriyama kept the title anyway. The comic is called *Dr. Slump*, yet its most magnetic presence is the doctor's creation.

That is a good joke before a single panel is drawn. It also identifies a recurring Toriyama idea. A figure that society might underestimate turns out to possess a freedom that larger, louder, supposedly more responsible people cannot match. There is no elaborate argument to be delivered. Arale simply walks into the world and refuses to behave according to what everyone thinks she is.

The story did not require her to earn her power. She had it from the start. It was not the reward for moral improvement, technical mastery, or years of training. Her strength was a comic fact, and the humor came from asking what such a fact would do to an ordinary day.

That is one of the great differences between *Dr. Slump* and the later *Dragon Ball*. Many future battles will turn on the desire to become stronger. Arale is already stronger than she needs to be. What happens next is not an achievement arc. It is an exercise in discovering how much trouble a personality can cause when the usual consequences of physical force no longer apply to her.

This is also why the manga could take root as a weekly series. Every new ordinary situation was a fresh opportunity. The school, the village, the neighborhood, a visitor, a suspicious invention, an attempted theft, a romantic ambition: all could be distorted when Arale arrived. No single quest had to carry the entire work. The characters and the setting generated the next collision.

*Dr. Slump* became enormously popular and was adapted for television beginning in 1981. Shueisha ultimately collected the original manga in eighteen volumes. It was a serious achievement before *Dragon Ball* was an idea anyone outside the creators' circle could recognize. The later franchise can make its predecessor look like a practice run. That reverses the historical sequence. Toriyama was already a major manga creator because of a robot girl in glasses.

## A cartoonist's sense of machinery

Why does the drawing still look so alive?

Part of the answer is how Toriyama handles solidity without sacrificing speed. He was very good at small machines, especially machines with personality. Vehicles can be squeezed into rounded, compact masses. A large tire may dominate the design; a window may curve around the whole cabin; the bodywork suggests something that was carefully engineered by someone with an unreasonable sense of proportion. The object looks absurd because its shape is exaggerated, but convincing because Toriyama clearly enjoys the details that make things work.

This is not the same skill as photorealism. A photographic vehicle might have more exact surfaces but less readable character. Toriyama simplifies to the parts that tell you how an object would be used, and then delights in the extra parts that make it funny. The best designs can almost be imagined as toys without losing their identity as drawings.

There is an artistic lineage here, not a secret blueprint. The illustrator who once drew products for advertisements learned how to identify their visible structure. The cartoonist who would draw impossible vehicles learned to make the imaginary feel casually ordinary. Between those two skills he found a style in which the viewer does not have to choose between wanting to laugh at a machine and wanting to own one.

That sensibility affects people as much as objects. Toriyama can draw a figure with very few features, but the silhouette and the gesture are unusually exact. In a comedy, this means an expression can land before a sentence is read. Later, in a fight, it will mean the reader can tell whose body is moving through a panel even when the drawing is pared down to a few decisive lines.

The connection is stronger than saying that good comedy creates good action. Comedy and action use the same physical grammar. Both depend on anticipation and interruption. A figure leans. Another figure notices. Something seems certain to happen. Then the body moves in a way that changes everything. In one story, the result is a pratfall. In another, it is a devastating blow. Both require the artist to understand what readers thought was going to happen before the surprise arrived.

Toriyama also loved the discrepancy between a character's appearance and physical capacity. Arale looks fragile. She is not. Senbei looks like a grown-up with a full set of tools and the authority of an inventor. He is often the least qualified person in the room to predict the consequences of his own invention. The joke is visual, social and structural at the same time.

Years later, a tiny, soft-spoken villain could terrify muscular warriors. A cheerful child could defeat a trained fighter before understanding why anyone had been worried. The world would get more violent, but that pleasure in confounding appearances would remain.

## The pleasure and burden of making a hit

The story of an instant success is often told as though the creator simply repeats what worked. A weekly comedy makes that impossible in its own way. Every installment must find another premise, another surprise, another reason for the reader to look forward to the next one. You cannot simply bring back the same joke with the numbers made bigger, or at least you cannot do so forever.

Torishima remembered Toriyama growing weary of the strain relatively early. In the editor's retrospective, the challenge was precisely that a self-contained comedy could not always rely on an unfinished adventure to carry the reader into the following week. A failed gag had to be replaced. A new week needed a new comic situation. This is one account, delivered decades after the work began, and does not let us reconstruct Toriyama's private feelings on every deadline. But it explains a real formal pressure: the very freedom that makes an episodic gag manga delightful can make it demanding to invent.

There is a contradiction in the position of a successful comic artist. Readers love the world and want more. The creator may love inventing the world and still resent being unable to leave it. A series can become so good at delivering a familiar pleasure that it is difficult for its maker to experiment elsewhere.

In Toriyama's case, the experiment eventually came through another interest: kung-fu movies. He liked Jackie Chan's physical comedy and the way an action scene could display technical skill while remaining playful. A short work called *Dragon Boy*, published in 1983 while *Dr. Slump* was still running, put a powerful young martial artist into a fantasy adventure in a Chinese-inspired setting. This was not yet Son Goku. It was a move toward another story engine.

There is a charming continuity in the change. A gag can be triggered by somebody falling over a chair. A kung-fu sequence can be built around the body finding a surprising route around the chair. A cartoonist interested in slapstick has already been studying collision, acceleration, the peculiar way bodies change shape when they move, and the precise instant when expectation is broken. Jackie Chan's films put those questions into an extended physical performance. Toriyama could meet them on the page.

Another influence was the Chinese tale usually known in English as *Journey to the West*. The tale's Monkey King, companions, magical implements and journey supplied a framework for turning isolated comic incidents into a quest. But the later *Dragon Ball* would not abandon the visual imagination of *Dr. Slump*. The young martial artist would still encounter ridiculous people, convenient gadgets, and jokes that temporarily made the world forget it was an adventure.

One can understand the transition as an answer to a practical writing problem. *Dr. Slump* could introduce anything because Arale and Penguin Village could make it funny. *Dragon Ball* could introduce anything because seven scattered magical objects gave its characters a reason to keep moving. The invention did not vanish. It acquired a road.

## What the great battles cannot erase

When people look backward from *Dragon Ball Z*, they sometimes explain *Dr. Slump* as the cute stage that had to be outgrown before real storytelling could begin. That misses something about Toriyama and about the nature of the later success.

The first manga of a famous artist is not only interesting because it predicts the next one. *Dr. Slump* has its own pace, audience, jokes and characters. Arale's adventures were not practice fights waiting to become a Super Saiyan narrative. They were the work that made Toriyama a star, and they ask for a different kind of attention. It is entirely possible to admire the choreography of a later battle and enjoy Arale running through a quiet village without needing to rank the two experiences on a single scale.

The connection, instead, is a sensibility. Strong bodies can be funny. Tiny bodies can be dangerous. Powerful people can misunderstand the situation completely. Scientists may be childish; children may be invincible. Ancient folklore and new technology can share a page. A world can be absurd without being carelessly drawn.

If that sounds like an accurate description of early *Dragon Ball*, it is because the new story did not arrive from nowhere. Nor did its creator suddenly switch from drawing jokes to drawing fights. He discovered a way of joining the movement of an action story to the freedom of a cartoonist who had already made an entire village feel alive.

Imagine the artist's pencil approaching a blank panel. It could draw a machine, a funny face, a creature, a mountain, a man trying to seem more competent than he is, or a girl who is more powerful than any adult around her. The panel does not yet know whether it is going to contain a joke or a battle.

That is where the history of *Dragon Ball* begins: with an artist who knew how to make either possibility irresistible.
