# 012000 — Capsules, Cars, and Monsters: Toriyama Designs a World

**Track:** Dragon Ball: From Penguin Village to the End of Z. **Audience:** Fans, readers of manga and listeners interested in visual storytelling. **Prerequisites:** None; major objects and characters are introduced. **Scope:** Toriyama's early *Dragon Ball* world, with a few comparisons to *Dr. Slump* and later illustrations. **Spoilers:** The first quest, Red Ribbon Army, Karin Tower, Kami's palace and the 23rd tournament are discussed. **Perspective:** Specific visual and storytelling observations, not unsupported claims about Toriyama's private design motives. No manga images are included.

# Capsules, Cars, and Monsters: Toriyama Designs a World

Imagine an illustration of a motorcycle. Give it wheels you could almost buy, a headlamp, a saddle, a cluster of handlebars, and a complicated little engine somebody could plausibly repair. Then make it rounder, more compact, friendlier, and just a little too appealing to be entirely practical. Put an excited teenage girl on it, wearing a helmet and goggles. Behind her, let the countryside extend into mountains where a small boy with a tail is carrying an enormous fish.

The girl has a device that finds magical spheres. The motorcycle can be stored inside a capsule small enough to carry in a pocket. Somewhere nearby, a dinosaur might try to eat you.

This is *Dragon Ball* before any discussion of power levels, and it is already a complete aesthetic. Toriyama has made a world in which the mechanical, the prehistoric, the supernatural, and the everyday share the same road. A person can be amazed by a particular invention without wondering why inventions in general coexist with talking animals. A hero can cross a wilderness on a flying cloud and still need a technological solution when a normal journey becomes inconvenient.

The mixture is sometimes described as random. It isn't random in the way it feels to read. Toriyama makes the parts belong together by drawing them with compatible shapes and by giving them clear jobs. The vehicles look usable. The monsters look inhabitable. The characters look ready to move. One drawing can provide the gag, identify the place, and set up the next action before a narrator has named the city.

To understand what a *Dragon Ball* world looks like, we could count how many dinosaurs or hovering cars appear. I would rather ask how we learn to believe in them. And how the person who created that visual world turned its pleasures into the grammar of one of the world's best-known action comics.

## The little machine that makes the road possible

Bulma, the girl on the motorcycle, comes from a family associated with Capsule Corporation, a firm whose signature technology compresses useful objects into small capsules. A vehicle, a portable house, or equipment that would otherwise require a truck can be packed away and produced at the moment the story needs it. The scene is not treated as a lecture about matter compression. It is a visual action: take a capsule, activate it, and watch an impossible convenience become suddenly solid.

As a world-building invention, the capsule is almost too useful. If everything necessary can fit into a pocket, the traveler should never have to choose what to carry. But Toriyama uses the invention to eliminate the boring logistics while retaining the pleasures of travel. Characters can suddenly have shelter, a vehicle, or a particular gadget. Then the plot can become about where they are going and whom they meet, not a detailed accounting of camping supplies.

There is also a comedy of class in the capsules. Bulma regards marvels of convenience as everyday equipment. She has grown up around them. Goku has grown up where a staff, food and a flying cloud are entirely sufficient. The exchange reverses the usual hierarchy of fantasy and science fiction. Goku's apparently magical means of travel is familiar to him, while Bulma's manufactured equipment comes from a civilization he barely understands.

The contrast lets both characters be knowledgeable and ignorant at the same time. Bulma can explain the radar and the car but may badly misjudge a person. Goku can identify an animal, survive outdoors and do astonishing physical things while being unfamiliar with machines and money. Their practical abilities overlap poorly. That is precisely why they are good company for each other.

The small capsule also solves a drawing problem. A comic that wants to move quickly cannot spend every installment showing characters loading gear, buying tickets and transporting furniture. A single recognizable object can stand for a larger set of practical arrangements. It reduces narrative friction while making the world feel more inventive. The same device can be an elegant solution and a ridiculous cheat, and Toriyama seems perfectly comfortable with both descriptions.

## Machines drawn by someone who wants to sit in them

Toriyama's affection for vehicles is one of the clearest continuities between his early commercial-design work, *Dr. Slump*, *Dragon Ball*, and later short stories. The official *Dragon Ball* site has highlighted his 1988 one-shot *Sonchoh*, built around a village elder and an extended car chase, and *Dub & Peter 1*, a story organized around an absurdly ambitious automobile. The repetition of the subject is real. We do not need to invent a psychological explanation for why he loved cars in order to enjoy the evidence that he drew them repeatedly and inventively.

Look at a typical Toriyama vehicle drawing and consider the invitation it extends. The exterior is not a shapeless futuristic bubble. It has wheel housings, vents, seats, panels, a windshield, sometimes a conspicuously short nose and a cockpit that seems tailored for an awkwardly proportioned occupant. The contours are clean enough to see at a glance, but they contain just enough irregularity to suggest a machine that somebody has assembled rather than an anonymous geometric primitive.

Its proportions are frequently the joke. A tiny cabin sits on enormous wheels. A military craft looks compact and almost cuddly until its weapons are noticed. A machine with terrifying capability is drawn as though it would make a marvelous model kit. The reader can admire its design while understanding that its purpose may be terrible.

This gives the Red Ribbon Army a striking visual personality. Its soldiers, vehicles, emblems, surveillance apparatus and buildings suggest an organization that has been planned and manufactured. The army is not just a line of interchangeable strong men waiting for Goku. It has a logistics department, however ridiculous, and an industrial imagination. Military force appears as something built into the world, not simply emitted from a villain's hands.

That distinction gives the encounters a different feel. A boy with a pole and a flying cloud confronts an institution made of walls, guns, vehicles, uniforms and mechanical organization. He often dismantles it through strength, but the delight of the pages comes partly from seeing the physical consequences. An elaborate machine is introduced, its silhouette understood, and then a tiny person does something to it that the designers never expected. The mechanical setup makes the destruction legible and funny.

The series later puts characters in spacecraft and enormous training rooms. Those objects remain recognizably Toriyama's because they continue this habit of compressing visual complexity into a design that can be read instantly. A spaceship can look like a real place to sit, even if its acceleration and energy source are fantastical. The successful design gives the imagination somewhere to stand.

## The animals that nobody bothers to explain

The first *Dragon Ball* world is crowded with animal people, shapeshifters, dinosaurs and talking creatures. Oolong is a pig-like shapeshifter whose cowardice, vanity and opportunism provide some of the earliest comedy. Puar is a small, floating, catlike creature with a talent for transformation and a strong bond with Yamcha. Ordinary animals can enter the same scene as highly stylized humans without the narration stopping to explain the biological taxonomy.

That generosity of design is easy to lose from memory after spending years with the later humanoid martial artists. Early *Dragon Ball* is not merely a world containing people who happen to have animal features. It is a world whose visual population is deliberately broad enough to keep surprising us. The odd person behind a counter may be an animal. The person in charge may look ridiculous. The creature that threatens a traveler may be enormous but not fundamentally out of place.

The world feels populated rather than organized into a careful hierarchy of normal and abnormal. Toriyama has already used this freedom in Penguin Village, where a robot girl, an inventor, monsters and comic animals live close together. In *Dragon Ball*, that visual freedom meets a road story. An unfamiliar face isn't an exceptional event. It is what one finds at the next stop.

The choice has consequences for the characters. Goku, who has met relatively few people when the story begins, is able to treat a speaking animal as an interlocutor without first undergoing a crisis of belief. Bulma's culture has accustomed her to technology, yet it is Goku's social openness that helps make the fantastic world feel normal. Neither treats every nonhuman as a monster requiring a separate origin story.

We should not turn the visual diversity into a claim that the fictional society has been carefully worked out. The manga does not provide an ethnography for all its speaking animals, and its comedy sometimes uses stereotypes and visual shortcuts. The point is simpler: the art makes room for odd inhabitants without requiring them to justify their presence before they can be funny, frightening, generous or foolish.

The dinosaurs contribute a different sensation. They make the landscape seem ancient, physically dangerous and delightfully indifferent to modern conveniences. A traveler can pass between mountain wilderness, bustling city and prehistoric threat without the sense that a portal has opened to a different universe. The same cheerful line encloses them all.

## Cities, islands and the pleasures of changing scale

The first journey with Bulma moves through landscapes like a toy atlas. Roads cross a countryside that seems enormous when measured by the length of Goku's footsteps. Mountains give way to populated areas. A vehicle can be a miraculous shortcut, but the story repeatedly returns to the pleasure of walking into a place one has never seen.

Roshi's home, Kame House, is an especially good example of designing a place for its dramatic function. A small house sits on a tiny island with the ocean stretching around it. This is a training ground, a comedy location, and the dwelling of an eccentric old martial-arts teacher. Its isolation makes the arrival of visitors an event. Its small size means that people who need to train must go elsewhere for much of the work. The place has a recognizable shape before we learn much about its resident.

West City, by contrast, offers dense modern activity, industry and the practical ingenuity associated with Bulma and her family. The difference is not a hard division between a natural world and an artificial one. The world is already too strange for that. Instead the locations supply different story speeds. In a city, the problem may be technical or social. On a small island, somebody may be bored enough to invent a new exercise. On the road, trouble can appear from beyond the edge of the panel.

Then consider Karin Tower. It rises so far above the landscape that climbing it becomes a challenge rather than a means of transportation. Goku has to earn the right to stand at its summit through physical endurance. The architecture gives an abstract instruction a visible form: if you want to develop beyond your present limits, you must spend time going upward.

After the demon king's defeat, Goku learns there is yet another place higher than Karin's tower, the palace of Kami. The world literally grows a new vertical dimension at the moment Goku believes he has exhausted its current one. It is a playful use of geography. A horizon can be extended not by introducing a new country but by telling us the highest point we knew was merely a stop along the climb.

The places are not interchangeable backdrops. They modify what kind of story is possible. A road permits episodic encounters. A small arena creates recurring rules. A tower turns training into endurance. A palace above the clouds makes a new level of knowledge visible. The scenery helps teach the audience how each adventure is supposed to feel.

## Silhouettes before biographies

The same principle of immediate legibility works on people. Master Roshi's sunglasses, turtle shell and modest frame announce a comic eccentric before we learn what his martial-arts reputation actually means. Krillin's bald head and small body let us distinguish him instantly from his friend. Goku's hair, tail and pole give him an outline that survives almost any change of clothing or setting.

This is more than a merchandising advantage, although the designs later became recognizable on toys, posters and screens. A comic needs to communicate quickly. If the reader has to stop and solve the identity of the person in every action panel, the momentum of the scene is lost. A distinctive silhouette allows Toriyama to draw a moving figure in a fraction of the detail required for a formal portrait.

The character's social role can also be communicated through a gap between appearance and ability. Roshi looks like a frail, embarrassing old man. He is a world-class martial artist. Krillin's small size makes him appear permanently disadvantaged in a world of tall fighters, yet his quick thinking is often what makes his appearances exciting. Goku's own body in the earliest chapters is compact and childlike, so the scale of his strength becomes a visual surprise.

Villains use the same device. The Red Ribbon generals look organized and imposing until a child defeats their elaborate preparations. Taopaipai is an assassin whose precise manner and theatrical confidence make him frightening even before his actual ability is tested. The Great Demon King Piccolo brings a heavier silhouette and a more severe expression into a world that had often looked like a comedy.

By the time Goku grows taller for the twenty-third tournament, the design change itself becomes part of the drama. His friends have to take a moment to recognize him. We do, too, even though the hair and the expressions remain familiar. The reader has learned to recognize a character partly by body geometry; Toriyama can now announce the passage of years by changing that geometry instead of spending chapters explaining what maturity feels like.

## A page is a little machine for time

A comic is not only a collection of pictures. It is a device for controlling the order and speed with which a reader learns what happened. In a Toriyama fight, the reader often knows where the characters are before being asked to enjoy an impossible movement. The position of a fighter is established. An attack begins. The page gives an expectation of what will be struck. Then a new panel breaks the expectation with an evasion, a counterattack, or an expression of comic disbelief.

The reader makes movement from the gap between still pictures. That is not a defect of the medium. It is an enormous source of energy. An elbow has moved farther than any artist could plausibly draw in one instant; the absence of intermediate positions lets the body feel incredibly fast. The same omission can be used for comedy. We see a confident setup, then the humiliation that follows, with the awkward instant in between left for our imagination.

Toriyama's clean outlines and decisive poses help this work. A body can twist around an attack without becoming a tangle of inaccessible limbs. A strong diagonal can make the reader feel that the force continues past the border of the panel. A close view of a face may interrupt the movement just long enough to register that the attacker has realized something is wrong.

Consider the relationship between Jackie Chun and Goku in the first tournament. The older fighter has spent his life learning how to use minimal movements well. The young fighter brings astonishing instinct and resilience. When the fight changes direction, the pictures have to communicate not simply which person is faster, but which person has understood the next movement a fraction earlier. The comedy of a very old man outthinking a superhuman child depends on this readability.

At the twenty-third tournament, Piccolo Junior can enlarge his body and unleash devastating energy attacks. The choreography grows more extravagant, but the best moments retain the old principle. Goku uses a Kamehameha from his feet, converting an attack into propulsion. A familiar action acquires an unfamiliar function. The viewer can understand the trick because the manga already taught us what that energy attack normally does.

A transformation or technique is most satisfying when the artwork teaches us something new about movement, not merely a bigger number describing the person moving. Toriyama's comic intelligence survives inside the combat intelligence. He likes a reversal that makes the reader realize they had overlooked an affordance of the thing in front of them.

## When the drawings have to be heard

There is a genuine problem in discussing visual art in an audio lecture. If I say that the panels flow beautifully, I have not told you what the drawings do. If I recite the number of panels, I may give you an inventory rather than an experience. The challenge is to describe the sequence of expectation and surprise.

Think of the first time you see a Capsule Corporation device turn into a large object. The size of the capsule establishes one expectation. The expanding machine violates it. The object is then drawn with enough specificity to become ordinary again. A joke about impossible compression becomes a means of moving the story forward.

Think of Goku climbing Karin Tower. The vertical distance is not a statistic on a map. It is a repeated sightline: the tower disappearing upward, the body growing tired, the destination refusing to arrive. When the summit finally appears, the reader has felt a small fraction of the distance through repeated pictures. The drawing has made effort take time.

Think of the first encounter with a powerful villain whose strength the hero has not yet measured. The page can give the villain a large, balanced pose, while the younger fighter's uncertain movement occupies a smaller portion of the space. When the hero is knocked away, the relationship is suddenly explained in a new spatial vocabulary. One body occupies the frame as if it cannot be moved; the other becomes a line of travel.

These are not mathematical rules for composing a manga page. They are ways to appreciate a specific artist at work. Sometimes Toriyama breaks a pattern because a joke needs it. Sometimes he fills a panel with details simply because he clearly enjoys an object. Sometimes a battle becomes so enormous that it changes the scale of the landscape itself. The pleasure of reading him comes in part from how often the composition finds a fresh reason for its choices.

## The world that the later battles narrow

Fans who enter the franchise through *Dragon Ball Z* often meet a world already dominated by warriors who fly, fire energy from their hands and treat entire planets as possible battlefields. The early cities and inventions are still present, but the emphasis shifts. There are fewer reasons to cross a desert by motorbike when the strongest character can cross the sky. A contest of bodily power may seem more urgent than a new species of talking animal encountered beside the road.

This shift has costs and rewards. The later action can use silhouettes and movements developed in the early manga to produce confrontations of extraordinary scale. It gains intense rivalries and long emotional payoffs. But something becomes less central: the delight of arriving in a new place and not knowing what sort of person or machine will be there. The universe gets bigger in literal scale while some of its everyday variety receives less attention.

That is not necessarily a decline. It is a change of dramatic priority. The early world is organized around travel, improvisation and discovery. The later world often organizes itself around training and confronting a particular threat. The same artist remains capable of drawing wonderful machines and comic interruptions. The story simply has fewer occasions to linger with them.

Perhaps this is why Toriyama's side illustrations and vehicle drawings have a life apart from the chapters themselves. A little one-seat craft or eccentric motorcycle can evoke a *Dragon Ball* that has nothing to do with measuring the strength of two combatants. It recalls a place where the vehicle would have been the reason for an entire gag, a journey, or a wrong turn.

The image of Goku and Bulma traveling together contains the franchise's original offer. You could meet anybody. You could go anywhere. A capsule might produce the equipment you need, and an animal might speak, and a city might contain a machine nobody has ever thought to make. Strength can get you past the obstacle, but it cannot tell you in advance what the next place will be like.

## What the machines knew before the heroes did

There is a temptation to isolate Toriyama's gifts: first he was good at comedy, then at characters, then at fighting, and separately he had a talent for drawing machines. The separation misses how the skills reinforce each other.

A memorable machine has a personality before a character operates it. A readable character silhouette permits a complicated action to happen quickly. A comic interruption trains the audience to expect a reversal. A journey gives an artist a reason to invent another setting. Each strength makes work for the others, and the result is a world that seems capable of producing more adventures than any one story could contain.

Toriyama's first great success, *Dr. Slump*, demonstrated how much a comic world could enjoy its own inventions. The beginning of *Dragon Ball* put that pleasure on the road. The first tournaments converted it into bodily movement. The Red Ribbon Army turned mechanical abundance into both comedy and menace. Piccolo's arrival gave the same visual imagination a more frightening task. Nothing had to be discarded for the next form to develop.

This continuity helps explain the durability of the art. A vehicle drawn decades ago can still seem new because its silhouette is clever and its details invite inspection. A slapstick fall can still be funny because the pose is exact. An old fight can still feel fast because the order in which the pictures reveal movement remains thrilling. You do not need the characters to become stronger every year in order for these drawings to retain their energy.

Return to that opening road. A girl comes around the bend on a motorcycle. A boy with a tail is carrying a fish. A magical radar has brought a machine into a wilderness. The boy thinks the machine is dangerous. The girl thinks the boy is impossible. Both are right, and neither is ready for how large their world is about to become.

The brilliance of the image is that the vehicle, the road, the mountains and the people do not wait for a mythology to make them interesting. They already look like they belong in an adventure. Toriyama has drawn a world in which the next great thing might be a monster, a friend, a punch, or a very nice little motorcycle. All four possibilities are worth turning the page for.
