# 008000 — Tien and the Tournament That Changed His Mind

**Track:** Dragon Ball: From Penguin Village to the End of Z. **Audience:** Fans and curious general listeners. **Prerequisites:** None; the first tournament and Red Ribbon Army are explained as needed. **Scope:** The 22nd Tenkaichi Budokai, approximately Tales 113–134 of the original manga. **Spoilers:** Yamcha's injury, the Crane School's wrongdoing, Jackie Chun's withdrawal, Goku and Krillin's match, the final contest, and Tien's victory. **Perspective:** This is a reading of Tien's education and of tournament dramaturgy, not an invented account of Toriyama's private planning.

# Tien and the Tournament That Changed His Mind

A young martial artist steps into a tournament ring and behaves as though it is a stage built specifically for him to humiliate other people. He has extraordinary technique, a severe expression and a third eye in the middle of his forehead. His companion is a small, pale fighter named Chiaotzu, capable of floating through the air and interfering with a match from the sidelines. Their teacher, the Crane Hermit, wants his school to be recognized as superior to the Turtle School.

The young man is Tien Shinhan, or Tenshinhan. He is about to become one of Goku's most enduring allies. At the moment, he is not particularly interested in becoming anyone's ally.

There are two kinds of tournament story. In one, a fighter enters as a good person and wins because goodness deserves a prize. In the other, the tournament changes the fighter, and the result at the end may be less important than the person who walks out. The second Tenkaichi Budokai belongs to the second kind. Tien enters with the ambition of becoming a great assassin, under the guidance of a teacher whose highest principle is victory by any means. He leaves having begun to understand why the manner of winning is part of what makes a victory worth having.

It is a remarkable turn because the plot does not simply force him to lose until he behaves. In the end, he wins the championship. Goku, in his second consecutive final, loses again. The moral conclusion and the official result are not the same thing.

That separation is at the heart of the lecture. Toriyama gives us a man who can win and still discover something more valuable than winning. Goku and the audience are required to understand the difference too.

## Three years later

The first Tenkaichi Budokai had established a wonderful compact setting for Toriyama's action scenes. A ring, an announcer and a clear victory condition gave the cartoonist room to construct fights like little puzzles. The fighters had different bodies and special abilities. Their techniques produced an increasingly comic and exhilarating set of reversals. Master Roshi, disguised as Jackie Chun, won the final against Goku because he believed the young students needed to know that no matter how accomplished they became, there would always be someone better.

After three years of further training and travel, Goku returns. Krillin is no longer merely the boy who arrived at Kame House with the competitive awkwardness of a fellow pupil. He is Goku's friend. Yamcha has been practicing, too, and is eager to show that he has improved. Roshi comes with the old disguise. The people have changed, and the tournament setting invites the audience to measure how much.

That is one of the great gifts of a recurring competition. You don't need a narrator to announce that a relationship has deepened, that one person has outgrown a technique, or that a previous prodigy has met a new limit. Put the fighters on the same kind of stage again and let us watch their choices.

Goku's entrance is not a grand return from a cosmic mission. He is still a boy who loves a fight and likes his friends. But he has become a skilled reader of his opponents. He understands the pressure of a tournament now, and does not need Roshi to explain every basic lesson. The story can afford to present new teachers, and in the Crane Hermit it finds an image of everything that could go wrong with teaching.

The Crane Hermit, called Tsurusennin, was once a fellow student of Roshi under the martial-arts master Mutaito. There is a history of rivalry between them. The Crane Hermit's younger brother is the assassin Taopaipai, whose killing of Bora pushed the Red Ribbon story out of its comic military campaign and into something much more painful. Goku eventually defeated Taopaipai. So when the Crane Hermit learns who Goku is, the conflict acquires a personal edge.

None of these connections requires a secret grand conspiracy. The world has begun to remember itself. A comic villain from one adventure is linked to the lineage of a new fighter. Old training relationships influence the present. The man who taught Goku has a rival who has taught somebody very different.

## Two schools of strength

Roshi is not a model of maturity in every circumstance. Early *Dragon Ball* makes frequent jokes at his expense, sometimes with behavior a modern listener may find tiring or objectionable. But as a martial-arts teacher he takes seriously a question which the Crane Hermit does not: what kind of person does training make?

The Turtle School's exercises were famously practical and absurd. Goku and Krillin delivered milk, worked in fields, ran long distances, studied, ate and rested. Roshi didn't begin by teaching them a secret beam. He made strength emerge from having a disciplined life. He entered a tournament in disguise to ensure that success wouldn't end their desire to learn. These are not just training techniques. They are lessons about the relationship between skill and character.

The Crane Hermit's pupils are equipped with astonishing abilities. Tien can fly. He can fire a focused energy attack from his fingertip. He is clever, forceful and technically adaptable. Chiaotzu has psychic powers that make ordinary fighting rules difficult to enforce. Their school is not a joke in the sense of being incompetent. Its practitioners are genuinely formidable. The problem is the purpose for which the skills have been cultivated.

The teacher measures accomplishment through domination, humiliation and, when necessary, killing. If the opponent is beaten, further injury may still be useful as a demonstration. If the ring's rules interfere with an advantageous outcome, they can be circumvented. That outlook finds a willing student in Tien, who initially regards moral restraint as weakness.

It would be less interesting if the Crane School simply produced bad martial artists. Toriyama does something harder: he shows that a corrupt teacher can produce exceptional skill. Tien's abilities are worth respecting. His judgment is not. As a result, Tien's development can't be reduced to learning a superior move from a superior master. He has to decide what the moves are for.

Consider the visual design. The Crane students have a distinctive silhouette before the reader knows their methods: Tien's third eye and severe geometry, Chiaotzu's miniature body, and the Crane Hermit's unmistakable robes and long facial shapes. Toriyama can make a page legible by changing the proportions of the bodies in it. The Turtle School and Crane School appear to belong to the same universe, yet they carry different emotional temperatures. One group has the familiarity of people who eat together. The other initially enters like a sharpened instrument.

The design is a beginning, not a moral verdict. The tournament's entire purpose is to let a person turn out differently from the picture they first present.

## Yamcha and the price of cruelty

Yamcha's first major tournament opponent is Tien. This is where the new challenger demonstrates how far he is prepared to go. Yamcha has spent years improving. He is not introduced as an incompetent joke. We have seen him fight, fear embarrassment, find friends and keep training long after the original Dragon Ball search ended. The return to the ring matters to him.

Tien outclasses him. That outcome could be a straightforward demonstration of a new character's power, the kind of humiliating defeat a martial-arts serial often uses to announce that somebody stronger has arrived. But Toriyama gives it an additional sting: Tien breaks Yamcha's leg, inflicting needless harm after the essential contest has already been decided.

The cruelty changes our understanding of him. Tien has more than enough technique to win without this. The injury is a choice, and it reveals the sort of triumph he has been taught to value. Goku's anger is likewise a choice. He is excited by strong opponents, but this isn't the kind of fighting that delights him. Strength used to punish someone who can no longer resist is ugly even to a boy who has spent much of his life seeking combat.

The scene is an important correction to a simplified reading of Goku as someone who cares only whether a fight is difficult. He clearly enjoys the challenge of formidable rivals. He also cares how they treat people. These impulses can be in tension, and later stories will test that tension sharply. Here it is recognizable in an ordinary sports setting: somebody has injured a friend to prove a point, and another competitor considers that unforgivable.

The panel that captures the outcome is stark. The new fighter's superiority becomes visible through the physical consequence of a deliberate act. It is not merely a bigger explosion than the one in the last chapter. A body is hurt. We can remember Yamcha's broken leg long after forgetting the precise sequence of attacks.

There is also a quiet service Yamcha performs for the story. He makes the cost of the Crane School's philosophy personal. An anonymous early-round contestant suffering the same injury would still establish Tien as dangerous. When it happens to someone who has been traveling with Goku and Bulma since the beginning, the reader is invited to be angry for a specific reason. Our knowledge of Yamcha is a piece of dramatic equipment Toriyama has spent many chapters building.

## Chiaotzu and the comedy of unfair advantage

Chiaotzu could easily have remained a gimmick. His body is small and almost doll-like. He flies with little visible effort, and his face rarely conveys the boisterous excitement that makes Goku's expression so inviting. The contrast between his appearance and his techniques is another of Toriyama's reliable devices. In a tournament full of athletes, he looks as though he has wandered from a different category of cartoon.

He is also Tien's close companion. That relationship will eventually matter more than any single power in his repertoire. Here it comes into view within a hierarchy: the Crane Hermit instructs, Tien aspires to the sort of mastery his teacher values, and Chiaotzu assists with powers that make the matches less fair.

Against Krillin, Chiaotzu demonstrates his energy attacks and psychic abilities. Krillin has developed an agility and tactical quickness that the early chapters could scarcely have promised when he first turned up to study with Roshi. Their contest is a duel between two very different kinds of comic design. The small floating figure may stop or manipulate what the sturdier human fighter is trying to do. Krillin must think rather than simply throw a harder punch.

There is a delightful bit of childish comedy surrounding Chiaotzu's capacity for arithmetic. The opponent who can manipulate bodies at a distance can still be distracted by a simple demand to calculate. The trick works because it identifies the gap between magical ability and practical versatility. One character possesses powers an ordinary human could never acquire; the other sees a way to turn a child's uncertainty into a tactical opening.

This is not an argument that Chiaotzu is foolish and Krillin is wise. It is an argument about Toriyama's sense of proportion. No ability is so solemn that it cannot be made vulnerable to the timing of a joke. In the same tournament, a focused energy beam and the problem of counting can belong to the same contest.

Chiaotzu's bond with Tien has a different function. Tien's story is partly an emancipation from his teacher's moral program, but not necessarily an abandonment of everyone he has known. The small fighter is someone Tien cares about, and their affection creates a path out of the Crane School that is not simply swapping one set of orders for another. A person who has been taught domination might still know loyalty. The question is whether that loyalty can survive a change in values.

## Roshi knows when to leave the ring

Roshi returns as Jackie Chun, the champion from the earlier tournament. This is initially funny. The audience knows who he is, and the younger fighters' attempts to take his disguise seriously extend the old joke. But his function changes considerably.

In the first tournament, Jackie Chun had engineered a defeat to keep Goku and Krillin humble. He was the hidden adult capable of teaching through competition. By the second tournament, the younger fighters have become impressive enough that the old teacher can recognize their independence. Roshi does not remain in the ring for the sake of proving that the old generation always deserves the last word.

During his match with Tien, he recognizes what Tien may become. He addresses the young man's moral course and ultimately withdraws. The withdrawal is often less memorable than an explosive knockout, which is precisely why it deserves attention. An experienced martial artist chooses not to treat the match as the only way to reach an opponent. He sees that Tien must make a decision of his own.

The contrast with the Crane Hermit could hardly be stronger. That teacher would rather use his pupils to defeat his rival and erase an embarrassing reminder of Taopaipai's loss. Roshi is capable of stepping aside to allow the student of the opposing school to choose a better direction. One teacher tries to preserve his authority by commanding; the other accepts the moment when authority is no longer necessary.

It is also a graceful development for Roshi himself. The womanizing old man, the teller of jokes and the martial-arts legend are the same person, but Toriyama gives his teaching life an arc. He has progressed from training his pupils, to beating them in disguise, to recognizing that his job is no longer to stand between them and the world.

The old master makes his most important intervention not by landing a strike but by deciding not to. The story's martial-arts vocabulary grows wider at the very moment its moves become more spectacular.

## Two friends can fight seriously

Goku and Krillin eventually face one another in a tournament match. It is easy to lose sight of what a delightful development this is. The boys once trained together under absurd conditions, resented one another a little, then developed a friendship that survived their first experience of public competition. Now the stage asks them to separate their affections from their fighting instincts.

Their match doesn't become a tragedy of friendship betrayed. Neither needs to prove himself through cruelty. Krillin has enough pride to want the victory. Goku enjoys the encounter because Krillin is skilled and inventive. Their shared history makes the tactics more interesting: each has seen the other's movements up close and knows how stubborn the other can be.

The contrast with Tien's earlier treatment of Yamcha is instructive. A tournament is meant to make opponents of people who might otherwise have no quarrel. It does not require them to hate one another. The competition can be fierce because the rules give it a boundary. An injury inflicted to humiliate somebody violates the spirit of that boundary even when the crowd regards the outcome as a decisive victory.

The fight also gives Krillin the dignity of improvement. In later *Dragon Ball*, power differences expand so far that the human characters can seem to belong to a different scale of existence. Here the two Turtle School pupils can still meet as intelligible rivals, and Krillin's strategies make Goku work. We can admire the protagonist without making every other character a helpless measuring stick for his growth.

Toriyama draws much of the humor from how seriously the boys take something that is also a game. A move can be clever and ridiculous. A fighter can be surprised by an opponent he knows. There is no need for hostility to produce excitement. By the time Goku advances to meet Tien, the manga has placed two moral examples side by side: ruthless superiority over Yamcha and vigorous, friendly competition with Krillin.

Tien must choose which kind of fighter he wants to be.

## The temptation to cheat

The final match begins as a contest between prodigies. Goku's body is an astonishing instrument of speed and adaptation. Tien has a range of techniques that seems to increase whenever the match demands something new: levitation, blinding light, energy beams, multiple arms and an especially dangerous attack called the Kikoho, often rendered Tri-Beam in English.

The early tournament format gives the reader a way to follow these extraordinary powers. The boundaries of the ring still matter. A contestant can lose by falling out, even if he is strong enough to demolish stone. Flying changes the geometry of that risk; a focused blast can change the battlefield itself. Every new power is also a potential solution to a rule already established.

Then the Crane Hermit tries to make the outcome no longer depend on the fighters. He instructs Chiaotzu to use telekinesis against Goku, interfering from the outside. He wants his pupils to kill the young man who defeated Taopaipai. At this point the distinction between competition and assassination collapses for the teacher. Winning the tournament is no longer enough. He wants revenge.

Tien confronts a decision that his earlier self would have found easy. He has been trained to accept precisely this kind of advantage. But now he recognizes the meaning of the fight as something shared by two fighters, and the outside interference makes the result worthless. He refuses the dishonorable help.

This is the center of his transformation. Nobody has persuaded him that fighting itself is immoral. He loves the test as much as ever. What changes is his understanding of what the contest is for. If Goku cannot respond freely with all the skill he has earned, then beating him will not prove that Tien has surpassed him. A rigged victory would defeat the point of competition.

The moral principle emerges from Tien's own ambition, not from an imported lecture about proper behavior. To respect a rival is to want a genuine answer to the question both have agreed to ask. Can you beat me, and can I beat you, without somebody else deciding the result?

The Crane Hermit cannot make that transition. In his eyes, an advantage unused is foolishness; an opponent who may be killed should be killed; a student's loyalty is measured by obedience. His authority disintegrates because the pupil no longer accepts the premise that a teacher's commands automatically define the highest form of strength.

Tien's independence is not yet saintliness. He remains proud and intensely competitive, and the combat that follows is still aggressive. That is why the choice is convincing. Toriyama does not replace a distinctive young man with a generic agreeable friend. He changes one governing assumption and allows the rest of the character to remain.

## The ring disappears

The climax of Goku and Tien's fight is a wonderful demonstration of action drawing developing its own logic. Tien's Kikoho is powerful enough to wipe out the tournament ring. The physical place in which the battle was supposed to be decided vanishes. The contest must continue above the ground.

This is a literal escalation and a formal one. In the first tournament, the ring was the stable center that turned bizarre abilities into intelligible sportsmanship. The audience always knew where a fighter had to remain. Now one of the fighters destroys the stage itself, and the concluding problem becomes a contest in the air. Toriyama can shift the action from close exchanges of fists to wide spaces and falling bodies without losing the rules that give it shape.

Goku adapts with a Kamehameha, using the energy of an attack as a means of moving through the air. This is one of the most satisfying things about early *Dragon Ball* technique. A move isn't simply a number on a scale. It has a direction, a reaction, a timing, a purpose. Using a blast for propulsion has a kind of physical intelligibility even if the physics is cartoon physics. The reader can imagine why it works and why it is surprising.

The fight comes down to who touches the ground first. Tien has the power of levitation, but the rules and the circumstances force both men into a final struggle in which a tiny difference decides the winner. Goku reaches the ground first, after a collision on his descent costs him precious time. Tien becomes champion.

The victory isn't a simple declaration that Tien is morally or physically superior. The story has let us see his integrity develop during the match, and the official result carries a measure of contingency. Goku loses without disgrace. Tien wins without the shame of having accepted the Crane Hermit's interference. Their rivalry can continue because each has taken the other seriously.

There is a comic fairness in how narrow the difference is. Goku lost the first tournament to Jackie Chun in a contest decided by the body that could rise and stand at the end. The second ends with the race to avoid touching ground outside the vanished ring. Toriyama has twice arranged a tremendous conclusion around a wonderfully mundane physical question. Which body can still stand? Which body lands first?

It is action storytelling with rules, bodies and consequences. A later series could replace such precision with the mere announcement that a fighter had found another level of hidden power. Here, the specific limitations of the setting make the outcome interesting.

## Winning the right thing

The cheerful atmosphere after the tournament is one of the story's great emotional traps. An antagonist has been transformed into a potential friend. The old master has yielded the stage. Krillin and Goku have competed without losing their affection for one another. The champion seems to understand more about what being a martial artist should mean. Everyone can eat and recover and look forward to the next meeting.

Then Krillin is murdered.

The shock belongs to the King Piccolo story, but the placement makes it impossible to read the second tournament in isolation. The event that has just celebrated human improvement and fair competition becomes the site from which an enemy starts hunting martial artists. Suddenly the skills honed in a controlled ring will be needed in circumstances where nobody cares about the rules. The young fighters will discover how much of their ethical education survives when the contest isn't voluntary.

Tien's development is particularly important in that light. Had the Crane Hermit's philosophy remained intact, the future champion might have faced a monster equipped with immense strength and no respect for human life, and found very little moral distance between the monster and himself. Instead he has just rejected the principle that strength entitles you to destroy a weaker person. That recognition gives his later attempts to defend others meaning beyond another increase in his power.

There is no requirement that Tien become soft or uncompetitive. His strongest quality may be his continuing seriousness. He trains, he takes responsibility for what he can do, and he grows impatient with shortcuts. In that respect he is a compelling foil for Goku, whose joy in fighting can sometimes make his discipline look effortless. Tien seems to work at being the person he wants to be. His moral life resembles his training: a daily decision not to settle for the easier result.

The second tournament ends with a championship awarded to someone who began the story wanting to become an assassin. Goku's losing streak continues. The Turtle School has acquired an ally from the school that despised it. The old teacher has accepted that his students can carry the tradition without his disguise.

That is a richer set of outcomes than a trophy.

A martial-arts story can ask who is strongest. This tournament asks what strength is for, and allows the answer to come from the very person we first expected to get it wrong.
