# 011000 — Before the Transformations: What Made Young Goku Compelling?

**Track:** Dragon Ball: From Penguin Village to the End of Z. **Audience:** Fans and curious general listeners. **Prerequisites:** None; the childhood encounters are placed in context. **Scope:** Goku's personality from the first manga chapter through the 23rd Tenkaichi Budokai, before the Saiyan revelation. **Spoilers:** Goku's first encounters, major deaths, later childhood tournaments and his decision to spare Piccolo Jr. **Perspective:** Interpretive character essay, not a retrospective claim that Toriyama planned the later Saiyan mythology.

# Before the Transformations: What Made Young Goku Compelling?

The young Son Goku is not especially impressed by the first automobile he sees. He attacks it.

A girl named Bulma is driving through the mountains looking for seven magical spheres. The car is a perfectly reasonable way for a person like Bulma to travel. Goku, who has grown up largely alone in the wilderness, sees a noisy mechanical creature bearing down on him. He tries to defend himself, discovers that the creature contains a person, and then has to revise the world he thought he knew.

The scene contains a surprising amount of the character in miniature. Goku is strong, fast, confident, largely ignorant of the modern world, and not the least bit embarrassed to learn that he misunderstood something. His ignorance is funny, but it is not the empty-headedness of a person incapable of understanding. He has been given poor information. Once he gets better information, he is ready to act on it.

Later versions of Goku are often judged by the terrible stakes attached to his decisions. Does he enjoy combat more than he should? Does he neglect his responsibilities? Is he too trusting of enemies? How much of his generosity is really a desire to fight stronger people? These are fair questions to ask of a hero who can threaten or save entire planets.

But they can obscure why this character made such an effective center for an adventure long before the planets started exploding. The original Goku is not an ordinary child on his way to becoming a moral paragon. Nor is he an innocent whose goodness will be spoiled by power. He is a particular mixture of curiosity, physical certainty, literal-mindedness, hunger, loyalty, occasional anger and a preference for meeting the world directly. Toriyama has enough confidence in those qualities to let them collide, instead of harmonizing them into a lesson.

Imagine what would happen if Bulma had found a boy who understood every social convention, admired her technology, wanted to save the world, and could already explain his motivations in polished speeches. He might have been easier to manage. He would have been a far less interesting traveling companion.

## He is raised by somebody we barely meet

Goku begins his story with an absent guardian. Grandpa Gohan, the old man who found and raised him, is dead. Goku remembers him affectionately, guards the four-star Dragon Ball as a keepsake, and appears to have absorbed at least some practical habits from him. He knows martial arts, can use his pole as a weapon, and has a sense that the world contains people to whom he ought to be kind.

The fact that Gohan is dead changes the texture of Goku's solitude. A child who has never known another person might be written as a blank slate or an animal. Goku has known one beloved person and lost him. He has a home, routines, and a connection to the past, even if he has not learned what cities are for. The four-star ball is valuable before he understands its magical function. It represents the grandfather whose warmth he remembers.

Bulma is interested in that ball because of what it can do for her. Goku is interested in it because of whom it brings back to mind. That difference drives their early negotiations, and it gives the treasure hunt a quiet emotional center. We are not merely following someone gathering pieces of a machine. We are following a boy who carries the memory of a family into an unknown world.

When Grandpa Gohan later returns briefly as one of Fortuneteller Baba's masked fighters, the reunion has an effect no enormous explosion could reproduce. Goku recognizes him and embraces him. The story can pause its relentless joking because it has earned the pause. The fighter who has not been particularly worried by monsters is overcome by being allowed to see someone he loves.

Notice that Goku is not embarrassed to show this affection in public. He does not think tenderness makes him less strong. He is capable of experiencing a reunion as a reunion, even while everyone else is trying to understand the supernatural rules that have made it possible.

The boy's fearlessness is not a refusal to feel. It is a lack of certain social habits about how to display feeling.

## Innocence is not the same as perfect judgment

The early comedy frequently depends on Goku's unfamiliarity with bodies, gender, flirtation, wealth and polite conversation. Some of those jokes have aged poorly. Their force came partly from his complete failure to read the conventions other characters assume everyone knows. He tries to answer a question literally when the question is actually a social maneuver. He may offend someone without knowing he has done so. Bulma, who often believes she can manipulate him, discovers that his ignorance can be as stubborn as her sophistication.

It would be too easy to praise every such moment as evidence of a higher purity. Some gags are simply broad, crude comedy. Sometimes Goku is rude because he lacks experience. Sometimes he is dangerous because his first idea is to hit something. His strengths are not a reliable guide to behaving well in every situation.

And yet he rarely suffers from a more corrosive problem: the constant need to look impressive. He doesn't mind asking a question. He doesn't pretend he understands a car. He can be astonished without feeling humiliated. An old man who offers genuine instruction gets his attention; a person who demands respect through costume or title does not automatically receive it.

Think about how often villains in *Dragon Ball* perform authority. Pilaf dresses and talks like a world ruler before he has ruled much of anything. Generals in the Red Ribbon Army surround themselves with uniforms, equipment, ranks and grand plans. Mercenary Tao cultivates the style of a professional killer. The Great Demon King Piccolo has an entire theater of terror.

Goku is not immune to intimidation because he knows those people are weak. He sometimes discovers that they are much stronger than he imagined. Rather, their claims to authority mean little to him until their actions make those claims real. He can be terrified of a deadly opponent's power without sharing the opponent's reverence for his own status.

This is a particularly effective comic arrangement. The villain prepares a grand entrance. Goku wants to know whether there is food, whether somebody is being hurt, and whether the stranger would like to fight. The inflated performance meets an entirely different set of priorities. Sometimes the result is a joke; sometimes it makes the villain furious enough to expose what is underneath the performance.

## He wants a good fight, but not only that

Goku's love of fighting appears long before anything called a Saiyan is mentioned. He is genuinely excited to encounter someone who might be stronger. He wants to test his abilities. He trains because he enjoys getting better, and he takes defeat as evidence that better is possible.

There is an almost cheerful refusal to reach an endpoint here. Most treasure stories organize themselves around a final acquisition. Get the treasure and you have won. A tournament seems to offer the same structure: become champion, and you have reached the summit. But Goku's interest in a fight does not depend entirely on a trophy. The event itself is pleasurable. A worthy opponent is somebody who can reveal a part of his own ability he hasn't yet discovered.

Master Roshi understands the danger of this pleasure. He enters the first World Martial Arts Tournament disguised as Jackie Chun in part because he wants his students to learn there will always be someone stronger. If Goku wins too early, Roshi fears he may become complacent. The master defeats him and leaves him with the oddest possible gift: a reason to keep training.

Goku receives it as a gift, too. He is disappointed, of course. He wanted to win. But he does not spend the following story trying to take revenge on Jackie Chun for humiliating him. The loss enlarges rather than shrinks his world. There is another summit above the one he thought he had climbed.

When Tien wins the second tournament after a hair's-breadth accident of timing, Goku can recognize the quality of the contest without treating its outcome as evidence of a cosmic injustice. This does not make him indifferent to winning. The third tournament will prove how much he still wants the championship. But wanting victory and being destroyed by defeat are different things.

A useful distinction emerges between Goku and many of his rivals. Some opponents need to win so they can know who they are. Goku enjoys knowing there is someone he cannot yet defeat. For him, the unmastered world is an invitation. The opponent who is stronger today may be a friend, a teacher, or a wonderful challenge tomorrow.

That attitude becomes more ethically troubling when a future opponent is capable of mass murder. In the childhood stories, the relationship between a fight and its consequences is still being established. We should neither excuse the later risks in advance nor import every future catastrophe into the early boy's enthusiasm. The original adventures give us the character's appetite before they fully test its limits.

## A friendship can begin with a quarrel

Krillin arrives at Kame House as Goku's fellow pupil and sometime rival. He is more worldly, more competitive, and more willing to calculate small advantages. Goku is stronger in raw ability, and he often doesn't notice the things Krillin is manipulating. Their early exchanges could have fixed the pair into a stock arrangement: the gifted innocent and the crafty, resentful sidekick.

Instead the training makes them friends. They deliver milk and do exhausting work together. They train under the same ridiculous master. They practice, argue, and discover that competition need not divide the people participating. At the second tournament, their match is serious because the friendship is real, not because it has been broken.

Goku does not require Krillin to be his admirer. Krillin is allowed to want things for himself. He can be jealous and brave, clever and frightened. In return, Goku's affection is expressed with almost embarrassing directness. When Krillin is murdered after the second tournament, Goku's response is not a speech about a great abstract principle. It is the anguish of losing his friend.

The contrast with his enjoyment of combat becomes necessary here. Goku can take pleasure in a fair fight; he cannot treat murder as a superior form of competitive skill. Tambourine is not an exciting challenger who deserves a sporting chance. He has killed someone Goku loves. The scene reveals that Goku's moral instincts are not reducible to wanting a challenge.

Of course, those instincts are also not always calmly measured. His anger sends him chasing a dangerous killer when he is exhausted. The result is another defeat. Loyalty makes him brave, but it can also make him impulsive. This is a boy whose affections are powerful enough to overwhelm his tactical sense.

## What he sees in people other heroes might dismiss

Android Eight provides an early test of Goku's ability to recognize goodness where conventional roles say it should not exist. The Red Ribbon Army has created a powerful artificial fighter. A military organization might reasonably expect its machine to obey. Instead Eight refuses to kill. His gentleness is the very quality the army cannot use.

Goku responds to the person rather than the label. The artificial fighter's reluctance to murder is not incompetence in his eyes; it is a reason to like him. He can see immediately what the supposed builders of a superior weapon have failed to value.

The relationship with Upa is another revealing episode. Goku's larger mission remains his search for the four-star ball, but when Mercenary Tao kills Upa's father Bora, Goku does not dismiss the boy's grief as an unfortunate side effect of a world adventure. He decides to use a wish to bring Bora back. The Dragon Balls, once Bulma's route to a frivolous personal fantasy, become a means of repairing another family's loss.

There is something wonderfully unfussy about the change of purpose. Goku does not deliver a lecture explaining his new ethical framework. He has met a grieving child. He can help. He decides to help.

Goku is also imperfect as a judge of danger. He forgives or spares people who might do harm again, and his eagerness to make friends or pursue a contest can obscure their motives. But these childhood encounters establish that he is capable of adjusting his expectations of someone else. He does not insist an artificial man must be a weapon. He does not insist a bandit must remain a bandit. Yamcha can become a companion. Tien can move away from the Crane School. A person who was an enemy yesterday can be addressed differently tomorrow.

That capacity to let people change is one of the manga's great sources of renewable story. A defeated villain need not disappear from the world; a rival may become a friend without surrendering his pride; a comic nuisance may turn out to be important at a moment of real danger. The social universe grows because Goku does not spend his life enforcing a fixed arrangement of heroes and enemies.

## The first time he meets a killer

It would be misleading to pretend Goku can reconcile with everybody. Mercenary Tao is willing to kill for money. He murders Bora, defeats Goku, and threatens the people Goku wants to protect. The Great Demon King Piccolo is not simply someone with a different competitive philosophy. He kills martial artists, terrorizes the world, and tries to remove the possibility that anyone can challenge him.

In these encounters, the boy whose early story began with slapstick discovers threats that cannot be laughed away or negotiated into a friendly tournament. Strength takes on the burden of preventing harm. In defeating King Piccolo, Goku delivers a killing blow. There is no suggestion that he has solved the dilemma by persuading the demon king to adopt good sportsmanship.

This is why the later mercy toward Piccolo Junior is so interesting. It cannot be explained by claiming Goku has always refused to kill. He has killed the first demon king. He knows the younger Piccolo has inherited violent ambitions. Yet after defeating him at the twenty-third tournament, Goku not only prevents his death but restores his strength with a Senzu bean.

The gesture deserves both admiration and suspicion. Goku recognizes a possibility in the young enemy that he did not see in the father. He also welcomes the prospect of another tremendous battle. His generosity carries a risk that other people may pay for. The story does not present a committee to evaluate the decision. It presents a man who believes his own judgment and accepts its consequences, although his confidence is easier to admire while the consequences remain hypothetical.

It is tempting to turn this into a verdict: Goku is truly selfless, or Goku is secretly selfish. Neither verdict explains the character very well. He can be moved by another person's pain and thrilled by an opportunity to fight, sometimes within the same decision. Those motivations do not have to cancel each other. They can make each other dangerous.

## The advantage of not knowing who is supposed to be important

There is a curious democracy to Goku's early attention. Powerful people fascinate him, but not because society has placed them at the top. A master wearing funny clothes may earn his respect by teaching him something. A child with no fighting ability can be worth the same desperate loyalty as a formidable companion. A talking pig may be a nuisance, then become useful. A king can be an idiot. A hermit can behave disgracefully and still be a good instructor in another part of his life.

Goku doesn't have a coherent political philosophy, and it would be a mistake to attribute one to him. He simply does not begin by treating status as evidence of worth. A person must become meaningful through what happens between them. The manga's wildly mixed population gives this trait room to operate. Talking animals, shapeshifters, scientists, peasants, soldiers, hermits and demons can all walk onto the same page. Goku's first question is rarely whether they belong in the correct category.

That quality makes him a useful guide for the reader. We enter the world alongside someone who has not already been taught its boundaries. He can ask the naive question that reveals a ridiculous custom. He can be amazed by a simple machine after already proving capable of a miraculous feat. He can feel at home with a stranger whose appearance would make a more conventional protagonist suspicious.

But he is not a neutral observer. He often answers unfamiliar situations with action before reflection. He may misunderstand a promise, forget an obligation, or fail to imagine why another person would value something he does not. His lack of prejudice is not identical to empathy. Empathy requires learning what is important to somebody else, and Goku sometimes learns slowly.

Chi-Chi's marriage proposal is an excellent example. He remembers a childhood promise imperfectly, and he originally misunderstood what the promise meant. When she reminds him, he accepts it. That is both admirably straightforward and remarkably inadequate as an account of what marriage entails. The story makes a joke of the gap. Later, the gap becomes a source of arguments about his life as an adult.

A character may be good at seeing through empty ceremony while remaining bad at recognizing obligations that arrive disguised as ordinary ceremony. Goku embodies that contradiction.

## The body that taught the story to change

For years the manga could make a child physically superior to almost every adult he met. The visual joke was never far away. A short figure with a tail carried an enormous fish, smashed a military machine, or jumped high enough to make an opponent's confidence look silly. His roundness and small size made his strength seem astonishing even after we knew to expect it.

At the twenty-third tournament, Toriyama returns him as a taller young man. The old physical contrast is reduced. The story compensates by making his command of movement more astonishing. He can wear weights unnoticed, read an opponent's feint, use energy for propulsion, and eventually fly under his own power. He is still Goku, but the reader must find his identity in timing and behavior rather than in the comic sight of a small child defeating a giant.

Some of the best continuities are minor. He remains direct. He still likes a challenge. He can still lose track of how other people understand a situation. The newly grown body does not produce a carefully polished adult personality. It simply allows the old personality to confront larger obligations.

That distinction is easy to forget when looking backward from the later series. We know another origin story will soon arrive: a brother from space, a Saiyan lineage, and a violent inheritance Goku does not remember choosing. It is possible to reinterpret early feats through that later mythology. A tail becomes evidence. Incredible strength becomes a biological clue. The mysterious alien element now seems to have been present all along.

But the first adventures were not experienced by their earliest readers as a puzzle whose correct solution had already been supplied. They were a tale of a strange boy whom Bulma found in the mountains, whose family history was partial and whose gifts were marvelous. The later revelation changes our understanding without abolishing the earlier experience. Goku's appeal was established before anyone needed to know the word Saiyan.

## Not a perfect child and not a simple adult

The young Goku can be endearing because he is innocent about things other people take for granted. He can be compelling because he is not innocent about the experience of exertion. He knows how to try. He knows the pleasure of improving a skill. He knows what it is to lose a friend, to be defeated, to have his pride challenged, and to meet an opponent who will never offer him a fair contest.

These are not separate character modes that Toriyama turns on according to the genre. They belong to the same person. The comedy helps us tolerate his tremendous power; the capacity for grief prevents the comedy from making him empty; the appetite for competition prevents his virtue from becoming generic; and the occasional failure of judgment keeps his innocence from becoming a complete moral defense.

I think the early manga gives us the most useful answer to the question of what made Goku compelling: he approaches a new world as if he is genuinely delighted that it contains more than he knows. He is not embarrassed by discovering limits. He is not satisfied by a title other people give him. He can love someone without needing to control them, and he can admire a rival without needing to destroy them.

The same disposition that makes him a marvelous adventure hero will cause trouble when the people near him need predictability more than marvels. It can produce courage or carelessness, depending on what is at stake. That tension is not a flaw to be explained away. It is a source of story.

At the beginning, a boy sees a car, attacks it, and discovers there is a girl inside. The encounter becomes a friendship and a journey. Many chapters later, a grown fighter sees the successor of a terrible enemy, defeats him, and discovers that the story might continue if he lets the enemy live. Both decisions begin with a version of the same possibility: there may be more to this person, this creature, this world, than Goku thought five minutes ago.

That is a remarkably durable way to build a hero. Before the transformations, before the galaxies and the enormous numbers, the excitement of *Dragon Ball* came from watching someone strong enough to get almost anywhere discover that he still had a great deal left to learn.
