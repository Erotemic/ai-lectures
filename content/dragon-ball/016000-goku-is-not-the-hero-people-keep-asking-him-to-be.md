# 016000 — Goku Is Not the Hero People Keep Asking Him to Be

**Track:** Dragon Ball: From Penguin Village to the End of Z. **Audience:** Longtime fans and interested newcomers. **Prerequisites:** The essential relationships and rules are introduced in speech. **Spoilers:** The entire original manga, including Cell, Majin Buu, and the final tournament. **Scope:** Adult Goku's generosity, love of competition, risk-taking, parental responsibilities, willingness to sacrifice himself, and tendency to leave room for others to choose. **Perspective:** A critical reading of the published story, not a psychological diagnosis or a claim about Toriyama's private intentions.

# Goku Is Not the Hero People Keep Asking Him to Be

There is a moment in the Cell Games that seems perfectly designed to make viewers shout at the television. Goku has fought Cell. He knows what the monster can do. He has spent an enormous amount of effort preparing for this encounter. Now he announces that he is finished fighting and sends his young son Gohan into the ring. And before Gohan can begin, Goku gives Cell a Senzu bean.

Cell, who has killed strangers and intends to destroy Earth, gets to recover his strength. Gohan gets to fight him at his best.

It is a magnificent competitive gesture and, on its face, a terrible piece of crisis management. If you have ever tried to explain Goku to someone who isn't a fan, the bean makes an efficient exhibit. One side of the argument calls Goku absurdly irresponsible. Another sees a fighter who knows his son's power and refuses to secure a victory by handicapping the opponent. Both readings find something real in the same action. But neither quite explains why the scene is also painfully consistent with the boy who first met Bulma in the mountains.

Goku is frequently placed in the position of a conventional hero. He must protect a world, save a child, answer for a family, perhaps even become the strongest person in the universe. Yet the way he understands a good life begins somewhere else. He wants to meet people, learn what they can do, discover what he can do, and leave tomorrow open to a challenge he cannot yet imagine. His generosity and his appetite for competition have the same root: a world in which another person can surprise him is better than a world already settled.

This makes him an exhilarating protagonist. It also makes him someone whose decisions should be criticized when another person's life is at stake. A better reading of Goku does not excuse his worst choices or pretend they cancel his acts of courage. It allows the two to belong to one person.

## A boy who doesn't need to be the champion

Long before anybody knows about Saiyans, Goku loves a good fight. At the first World Martial Arts Tournament he is defeated by Jackie Chun, who is secretly his teacher Master Roshi. Roshi wants Goku and Krillin to learn that the world contains stronger people and that reaching a summit need not end their education. Goku is disappointed by losing. He is also invigorated by the knowledge that he can become better.

That response is an important part of the character. Goku does not need every rival to acknowledge his superiority before he can enjoy their company. Tien becomes a friend after having been a dangerous opponent. Piccolo Jr. is spared at the end of the twenty-third tournament despite the real threat he poses. Goku has won the championship, but he does not seem to think winning means the opponent's existence should end. The competitive world can survive the contest.

Notice the difference between this and the usual fantasy of a hero eliminating evil. A hero who defeats a villain may reasonably conclude that the villain must never hurt anyone again. Goku can draw that conclusion in some situations, but he is drawn toward another possibility: the opponent might become someone different, or might become an even better opponent. He sees futures in enemies that other people, with good reason, cannot afford to trust.

At the end of the Saiyan invasion, Vegeta is not harmless. He has helped bring devastation to Earth, and his return could be catastrophic. Krillin has a chance to end the threat. Goku asks him not to. It is not a decision made after Vegeta has repented or offered compensation. Goku wants the chance to face him again. Here his sense of an open future seems to extend beyond the right he has to make such a gamble for everybody else.

And yet the series vindicates the possibility in a way that does not erase the original risk. Vegeta eventually fights alongside the people he tried to destroy. He forms a family. He remains vain, often cruel, and capable of doing grievous harm. The old enemy's future becomes one of the manga's richest character stories. The fact that Goku's hope helped make that future possible does not prove the choice was responsible at the time. Stories can reward a gamble without giving every gambler a moral blank check.

## Mercy is sometimes generosity, and sometimes desire

Goku's relationship with Frieza brings this ambiguity into sharper focus. Frieza is not a misunderstood tournament rival. He commands an empire of conquest, treats populations as disposable, and has slaughtered the Namekians. When the decisive fight on Namek turns against him, Goku attempts to leave. He does not have to keep punishing a beaten enemy to feel that his strength has been recognized. At one point he even provides energy to the damaged tyrant so that Frieza may survive.

Frieza responds by attacking again.

The moment is often told as evidence of Goku's bottomless mercy. It is that, but it also forces a confrontation with what mercy cannot control. You can offer another person the opportunity not to harm you. You cannot make them accept it. Goku's desire to leave Frieza alive is not a guarantee that Frieza will stop being Frieza.

A useful distinction runs through these episodes. Goku is generous with his own opportunities. He can surrender a victory, let a defeated opponent depart, or risk a better contest. These choices feel noble because he rarely wants to humiliate somebody simply for being weaker. But when an opponent can annihilate towns, planets, or children, generosity with an opportunity becomes a decision about the safety of people who were never asked.

This is why the Senzu bean given to Cell provokes such strong reactions. Its symbolism is beautiful inside a tournament. It tells the opponent that the victory must be earned against a whole person rather than a damaged remainder. But Cell has transformed a tournament into a hostage situation with the entire Earth as his hostage. The rules of honorable competition are no longer adequate by themselves.

Goku behaves as though he can carry a rule learned in a martial-arts ring into a situation where nobody agreed to the match. The story lets that rule be emotionally recognizable and frighteningly misplaced at once.

## Gohan knows something Goku does not

The deeper error with Cell is not only the Senzu bean. It is Goku's understanding of his son.

Goku has trained beside Gohan in the Room of Spirit and Time. He has seen the power Gohan can summon. He knows that anger can release what ordinary practice does not. In a fight conceived as a problem of maximizing available power, his plan makes a kind of sense. Gohan is the person who might defeat Cell.

But there is a difference between possessing strength and wanting the encounter in which that strength will emerge. Goku's pleasure in fighting is not universal. It is his own temperament, not a family inheritance that arrives automatically with Saiyan blood. Gohan is capable of terrible anger and extraordinary courage. He also hates the suffering that gives those capacities a reason to appear.

Piccolo recognizes this more clearly. When he rebukes Goku for assuming his son shares his appetite, the rebuke is not a denial of Gohan's power. It is a reminder that a child is not merely an extension of a parent's talent. Gohan is frightened, under immense pressure, and unable to turn a murderous situation into the thrilling contest Goku imagined.

Goku listens. That matters more than proving he was right all along. He is capable of recognizing when a trusted friend understands the situation better than he does. The scene also reveals that his optimism has a blind spot: he can respect an opponent's freedom while misunderstanding his own child's wishes.

The resolution is not a clean endorsement of Goku's plan. Gohan's anger is awakened in a scene of cruelty, and he becomes so intoxicated with his advantage that he delays finishing Cell. Goku then has to intervene when Cell threatens to destroy the planet by self-destructing. He teleports Cell away, dying with King Kai in the explosion. Even after that sacrifice, Gohan must face a regenerated enemy. The victory is collective, painful, and far less orderly than any plan that begins with a healed Cell in an arena.

The story preserves a productive contradiction. Goku saw Gohan's potential more clearly than many adults did. Piccolo saw Gohan's emotional life more clearly than Goku did. Both insights were necessary; neither gives the speaker ownership of the child.

## A father, not a universal model of fatherhood

There are two easy mistakes when discussing Goku as a parent. One is to wave away his absences because he is a beloved hero. The other is to treat an exaggerated adventure manga as a complete record of domestic life and calculate a final grade for him as though he were a real household member.

Goku's life is not ordinary, but it contains ordinary obligations. Chi-Chi raises Gohan, manages a household, cares about education, and spends stretches of time without her husband. Goku loves his family, yet he repeatedly leaves for training or is lost to conflict and death. An enormous sacrifice may make an absence unavoidable, but not every absence is a sacrifice. The comic can make light of domestic tensions without making the burden disappear.

Goku also teaches through what he actually knows. He brings Gohan into an experience of disciplined practice and treats him as somebody who can surpass his teacher. That can be generous. He does not always notice that the boy's life contains goals outside fighting. The failure at the Cell Games is more than a single embarrassing error: it dramatizes the limits of trying to care for another person through the thing you yourself love most.

The Buu era returns to those questions in a changed household. Goku has been dead for years; Goten has grown up without knowing him. Gohan has reached school age and is looking toward a different kind of life. Goku returns for a day, and the story almost immediately becomes a crisis. Once again he can offer knowledge and power that nobody else has. Once again the question is whether solving everything himself is the best thing he can offer.

During the fight with Majin Buu, Goku reveals an extravagant third Super Saiyan form. Later he says he wanted the living to have a chance to defend Earth themselves. The statement can be read as an attempt to create successors, and it is fair to ask whether the lesson comes at too great a price when the danger is so extreme. He knows that the world cannot depend forever on one fighter appearing at the last possible moment. He also seems very ready to believe an ordeal will make somebody stronger.

That is the tension in his teaching: he offers people a future in which they will not need him, but sometimes tests their readiness by allowing events to become far more dangerous than they needed to be.

## The people who keep him answerable

Goku is surrounded by people whose relationship to a fight is different from his. That is not an incidental feature of the cast. It is part of how the story can put such a powerful character at its center without agreeing with every conclusion he reaches.

Bulma often approaches danger as a problem of information and engineering. She builds a radar that makes the first adventure possible, repairs equipment, and later creates the time machine that gives Future Trunks a chance to avert disaster. Her response to catastrophe is not to hope that a fair contest will reveal the stronger fighter. It is to invent a way for the situation to change. When Goku is dead, absent or simply focused on training, her work can create a future his punches could not reach.

Krillin is another necessary counterweight. He knows what it feels like to be in a fight without any guarantee of winning, and he is often willing to help anyway. His mercy toward Android 18 and his complicated response to Vegeta cannot be reduced to Goku's general love of worthy opponents. Krillin is thinking about actual people, fear, loyalty and attachment. He can be frightened for good reasons. It is not less heroic to notice that the person threatening you has a weapon you cannot possibly stop.

And there is Vegeta, who gradually becomes almost a negative image of Goku. Goku enjoys a stronger rival without needing to prove he was born superior. Vegeta begins with a claim to superiority and spends much of his life wounded by the existence of a rival he cannot fully master. As he acquires bonds to other people, he learns obligations Goku has always recognized in some form but does not always know how to honor in practice. Each man can teach the other something that his own temperament conceals.

This ensemble helps us resist the idea that a protagonist's viewpoint is the moral law of the universe. Goku may be the character who completes the largest attacks, but he is not the only character allowed to understand what is happening. Piccolo can correct him about Gohan. Bulma can imagine a technological answer to the impossible. Mr. Satan can speak to the public in a voice that reaches people who would not recognize Goku's name. A world in which its strongest resident were also always its wisest would be smaller than this one.

If we forgive every one of Goku's mistakes by pointing to the happy ending, we lose that argument among his friends. The story becomes far more interesting when they are permitted to be right about things he has missed.

## He sacrifices himself without becoming a martyr

It would be false to say that Goku values fighting above every life. He holds Raditz in place so Piccolo can kill their common enemy, knowing the attack will kill him too. He pushes himself to dangerous limits against Vegeta and Frieza. He removes Cell's self-destructing body from Earth at the cost of his life. Whatever one thinks of his preparation and decision-making, he does not avoid paying the price personally.

Nor does he wear these sacrifices as credentials. He seldom turns the story of his suffering into a demand that others owe him admiration. After the Cell Games he even chooses to remain dead, partly because he has come to believe that his presence attracts enemies. This is not an entirely persuasive theory of history. The Red Ribbon Army was dangerous before Goku became its enemy; Babidi's schemes have causes beyond Goku's existence. But his explanation is recognizably an attempt to account for the cost his life imposes on others.

What makes Goku compelling is that this concern does not make him suddenly cautious. He does not become a man who conducts risk assessments before training. He remains fascinated by the next formidable opponent. The result is not character growth in the tidy sense of an old flaw being removed. It is the persistence of a desire within changing responsibilities.

Consider the final battle with Buu. Victory eventually depends on the energy of living beings across Earth, gathered into an attack Goku himself cannot supply alone. Vegeta has to persuade people. Mr. Satan's reputation has to open a channel of trust. Dende and others have to manage resurrection and recovery. Goku is the person who throws the attack, but he is not its sole author. His most powerful weapon is made from the help of people he could never defeat or recruit through a duel.

There is a joke buried in that image. A boy who always wanted to find the strongest person ends up needing the strength of millions whose names he does not know. His defining competition gives way, for a moment, to a collaborative effort with no individual champion.

## Why the ending is not a retirement

The original manga ends years after Buu's defeat at another World Martial Arts Tournament. Goku has learned that the evil Buu was reborn as a boy named Uub. He wants to meet him. He wants to fight him. He also wants to train him.

Imagine a different final chapter. Goku might stand on a mountain as the strongest warrior in the world, finally satisfied that no one can challenge him. He might give a speech about the wisdom he has acquired and the enemies he has overcome. He might become a ruler, a general, or a moral authority. Any of these endings would satisfy a familiar image of heroic completion.

Instead he finds another student. The world contains another possibility. Goku departs the tournament with Uub to begin teaching him, leaving his friends behind again and making the ending both wonderfully characteristic and slightly exasperating.

You can feel two things at once. There is pleasure in his refusal to settle into self-congratulation. There is also the old question about everyone he leaves waiting. The story does not make him perfect in order to let him have a beautiful ending.

The final act is more revealing because his new companion is not simply a rival. Uub is a child with a future, a life, and reasons for fighting. Goku's curiosity can become mentorship if he learns to see that whole person, not just the next astonishing level of strength. We cannot know every lesson the future holds for them from this ending alone. We can see the possibility of a different relationship to his favorite activity: finding a worthy fighter is now inseparable from helping somebody become one.

There is a tendency to argue about Goku as though the only available verdicts are saint, fool, or villain. The comic has given us a more durable figure. He is kind without being consistently prudent. Brave without being uniformly responsible. Generous without always noticing whose safety he is gambling with. A great teacher of technique who sometimes needs another teacher to explain the people beside him.

The first time we meet him he does not understand Bulma's car. He hits it, discovers his mistake, and goes on learning about the world. The universe gets much bigger; the power required to cross it becomes absurd. Goku never entirely loses that willingness to meet something he does not understand by finding out what it can do.

He is not a model citizen of a coherent moral system. He is the protagonist of a world that stays alive because he expects it to contain someone he has not yet met.

That is a wonderful disposition for an adventurer. For a guardian, a father, or a leader, it is an unfinished education. And Dragon Ball is better for allowing the education to remain unfinished.
