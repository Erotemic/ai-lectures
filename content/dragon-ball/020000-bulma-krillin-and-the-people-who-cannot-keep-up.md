# 020000 — Bulma, Krillin, and the People Who Cannot Keep Up

**Track:** Dragon Ball: From Penguin Village to the End of Z. **Audience:** Fans and interested newcomers. **Prerequisites:** The essay supplies its own context but assumes willingness to hear major Dragon Ball and Z developments. **Spoilers:** Original quest, King Piccolo, Saiyans, Namek, Future Trunks, Androids, and the resolution of the Cell Games. **Scope:** Toriyama's continuous *Dragon Ball* manga and the corresponding *Dragon Ball* and *Dragon Ball Z* television stories. **Perspective:** Invention, friendship, physical vulnerability, the ethics of aid, and what an increasingly superhuman adventure owes to its supporting cast.

# Bulma, Krillin, and the People Who Cannot Keep Up

Bulma builds a machine to find seven magical balls. Krillin learns how to punch.

In the early days of Dragon Ball, these are both tremendous accomplishments. Bulma's invention supplies the route by which a strange boy can enter a wider world. Krillin's arrival at Master Roshi's island gives that boy someone his own age to train with, compete against, and finally love as a friend. One works with tools, the other with his body. Neither is the strongest person in the world, and neither needs to be.

Much later, Goku will trade blows with a creature containing the abilities of the mightiest warriors in the series. People will transform into legendary beings whose speed makes ordinary combat almost impossible to follow. A martial artist who once seemed formidable will be knocked aside before the headline confrontation even begins. A person who does not fight may hardly appear in the battle at all.

This expansion creates a peculiar question for a long-running story. What happens to its original companions when the hero rises beyond them?

There is a familiar, disappointing answer available: the old friends become a chorus, standing at the edge of the battlefield to be shocked by the next level of strength. Sometimes Dragon Ball does exactly that. But its history is more interesting than a chart of who has fallen behind. Inventions, friendships, imperfect choices and practical acts of courage keep shifting the events that a punch might later resolve.

Bulma and Krillin make a good pair of subjects precisely because they do not do the same thing. Bulma is not a martial artist at all. Krillin is a highly trained martial artist who lives in a world where even exceptional training cannot make him a Saiyan. Between them they reveal two forms of importance that Dragon Ball occasionally forgets and then remembers with wonderful force.

## The first character who wants something

The opening of Dragon Ball belongs, in one sense, to Bulma. Goku is living in the mountains. He is astonishingly strong, socially innocent, and content with a life organized around food, his grandfather's keepsake, and the immediate landscape. Bulma arrives with a vehicle, a map of her ambition, and a device capable of finding the seven objects she wants.

She is not asking Goku to fulfill a prophecy. She wants to use the Dragon Balls to get a wish. The original joke is wonderfully adolescent: a brilliant girl can build a detector for magical treasures but cannot solve her romantic dissatisfaction by ordinary means.

Her brilliance and her foolishness coexist from the beginning. She is impatient, proud, quick to improvise, and frequently wrong about the people she meets. She also knows things Goku could not possibly know about the modern world. The contrast produces jokes, but it also creates the journey. Goku has strength, innocence and physical fearlessness. Bulma has purpose, logistics, machines, and a willingness to leave home in pursuit of a ridiculous idea.

Their partnership establishes an important truth about Dragon Ball. The adventure was never only about the strongest boy. It needed someone to turn his strength into an itinerary. A magical ball is a curiosity until a person devises a way to locate the others. A road across the world is a sequence of encounters because someone insists on following it.

The Dragon Radar is almost the perfect supporting-character invention. It gives the plot a direction without dictating how the plot will unfold. It can identify a destination, but it cannot make a mountain safe, negotiate with a bandit, or explain the motives of a person who has the next ball. Bulma's device creates possibilities for comedy, danger and discovery. It does not replace them.

And Bulma does not become a solemn figure of technological progress. She remains selfish, irritable, ingenious and often hilarious. Keeping that personality matters. If we praise her only for being useful to more powerful characters, we make her smaller than Toriyama drew her.

## The rival who became a brother

Krillin enters a different part of the story. He comes to Roshi's island hoping to become a great martial artist. At first he is something of a schemer, willing to flatter the teacher and exploit any chance of gaining an advantage. Goku's straightforward strength and sincerity make him both infuriating and fascinating as a training partner.

The early training has an ordinary physical comedy that is easy to lose in memories dominated by later energy attacks. Delivering milk, plowing fields, racing through difficult terrain, learning endurance: improvement comes from repeated effort, patience and the ridiculous demands of a teacher who seems determined not to become too solemn about the whole enterprise.

Krillin is not there to reveal a hidden alien bloodline. He is a boy who works. His skill grows because he practices beside another boy who is, in certain respects, almost impossible to compete with. The relationship begins with rivalry and becomes affection. That change is one of the emotional foundations of the manga.

The friendship also gives Goku a connection to something beyond food, travel, and the next exciting fighter. Krillin can tease him, become angry with him, depend on him, and be afraid for him. He is a person who knows Goku at his least legendary, from the days when becoming a stronger martial artist meant getting up early and completing the training exercise.

The first World Martial Arts Tournament gives Krillin a public test of that work. He is capable, fast and inventive, but the field includes opponents who are more experienced and more gifted. In later tournaments he continues growing without necessarily becoming the one who wins the final match. That is not a narrative failure. It creates the human scale by which Goku's impossible talent can be appreciated.

## When an old friend dies

The first time Krillin dies, the entire tone of Dragon Ball changes. Following the twenty-second tournament, he is murdered in an attack connected to King Piccolo's resurgence. The news drives Goku into grief and fury. The deaths in this period do not feel like routine combat statistics. They are the loss of people who shared the comic and competitive life of the earlier story.

Krillin's death hits hard because the audience knows him from before everything became a crisis. We have seen the effort, the rivalry, the jokes, and the ordinary satisfaction of friendship. His absence does not simply increase the stakes. It tears a hole in the world that the story had taken time to build.

This is one of the great uses of a supporting cast. A character does not have to be strong enough to defeat the principal antagonist to be central to the principal antagonist's effect on the audience. If Krillin had always been a generic soldier standing behind the hero, his death would be a generic warning. It is devastating because it happens to a friend whose life existed before the villain's arrival.

Dragon Ball has magical resurrection, and it eventually restores Krillin. That does not turn his first death into an empty trick. The scene changes Goku's relationship to danger and changes the kind of harm the audience expects the story to admit. Later resurrections can complicate that emotional structure, but they cannot erase the memory that first taught us the friendship could be lost.

## The people who can still plan

By the Saiyan era, Krillin has become a remarkably powerful human martial artist. Calling him ordinary would be absurd. He can move at superhuman speed, fly, sense energy, and employ techniques that would seem miraculous in the first chapters. Yet he is no match for Nappa or Vegeta in an uncomplicated contest of strength.

The invasion therefore makes him useful in a different way. He must survive, choose moments, cooperate with other defenders, and accept that his attacks have limits. The final struggle against Vegeta is not a duel won cleanly by Goku. It involves Gohan, Krillin, Yajirobe, and the enemy's own mistakes. When Krillin has the chance to kill the defeated Vegeta, Goku asks him to spare the Saiyan. Krillin's willingness to listen is not an automatic triumph of wisdom. It is a choice made under terrible conditions, one that later acquires consequences nobody then knows.

On Namek, the difference between the greatest warriors and the surviving companions becomes even sharper. Gohan and Krillin move through a battlefield dominated by Frieza's soldiers, Vegeta's ambition, the vulnerable Namekian population, and the search for the Dragon Balls. Their survival often depends on hiding, negotiating, escaping or rescuing someone before a stronger enemy arrives.

That makes their expedition more suspenseful than a straightforward contest of strength would be. They cannot simply land in the middle of the villains and announce that the adventure is over. Krillin can make a dangerous decision because he recognizes a child in need. Gohan can act before measuring the consequences. Their moral reflexes repeatedly create new problems and new alliances.

Krillin's techniques still matter. The Destructo Disc, for example, is an elegant answer to raw power. It is not just a bigger fist. It threatens to cut a target despite a difference in conventional strength, though the ability is not a magical guarantee against every later enemy or adaptation. The lesson is that martial imagination survives the growth of power levels. One need not be the strongest to be dangerous, useful, or dramatically interesting.

But the story is honest about the gap. Krillin can be hurt, terrified, and outmatched. When Frieza kills him on Namek, the loss becomes the emotional trigger for Goku's first Super Saiyan transformation. Krillin is again at the center of a story he does not personally win, because the friendship has become one of its deepest sources of meaning.

## The engineer returns through the side door

What is Bulma doing while all this happens? Not always what we might wish. Some phases of the later manga reduce her to a worried observer, a source of complaints, an adult arranging logistics while other people take the decisive blows. The story can be impatient with characters whose strengths do not look like fighting. That limitation is part of its history and need not be excused.

But Bulma's inventions retain an ability to alter the entire shape of events. Her family and Capsule Corporation make transport and survival technology available in situations that would once have been impossible. The technology supporting Goku's journey and training exists within the same broad world of invention that Bulma introduced with her first radar and capsule vehicles.

Then the Android story restores her to almost shocking prominence.

Future Trunks arrives from a timeline in which the familiar heroic rescue has failed. Goku is dead. Earth is devastated. The surviving fighters cannot simply train their way to victory. Future Bulma responds by building a time machine. She also gives Trunks the medicine that may save Goku in another timeline.

The machine does not solve every problem. Indeed, it makes the plot much more complicated. The future is not cleanly overwritten when Trunks changes the past; different histories persist. But that is exactly why the invention is narratively impressive. It does not provide a convenient command for undoing tragedy. It creates another world in which different choices can be made.

The person who first took Goku from his mountain home with the help of a radar has now made it possible for the story to travel between futures. Her work has changed the narrative's dimensions twice, in strikingly different ways. The strength of the central hero cannot do either job by itself.

## Two scientists, two imagined futures

Bulma's role becomes even more interesting when placed opposite Dr. Gero. He is the former Red Ribbon scientist whose work produces the Androids and Cell. His machines are sophisticated; his designs are not the work of a fool. In fact, their capacity to threaten the strongest fighters makes his ingenuity one of the great powers of the Android saga.

Yet Gero's inventions are organized around revenge, compulsion and the idea that a person can be reduced to a programmable system. Android 17 and Android 18 were originally human, and his attempt to control them treats their continued lives as extensions of his will. Cell goes further, making their bodies necessary ingredients in his promised perfection.

Bulma also builds mechanisms that can control or interrupt another machine. When the heroes discover Gero's plans, she produces a remote designed to shut down 17 and 18. It is a reasonable defensive response to a terrifying threat, but the ethics are not perfectly simple. The people the remote targets have been modified without their consent and are now trying to live outside the authority of their maker.

The difference between the inventors is not that Bulma never creates a morally difficult device. It is that her inventions can enter a living world of disagreement and be judged there. She does not insist that building a switch grants her permanent ownership of the person connected to it.

This is where her technical work and Krillin's human hesitation meet.

## Krillin holding a button

During the Cell crisis, Krillin receives Bulma's shutdown controller. If he activates it when Android 18 is near, Cell will be unable to absorb her and achieve his final form. The practical argument for using it is formidable. A creature who has already drained a town of life is pursuing her. Even the strongest fighters may fail to stop him once he transforms.

Krillin has come to see 18 differently from the nightmare figure Trunks described. The present Androids have beaten him and his friends, but they did not kill them when they had the chance. Android 18 gave him a teasing kiss before leaving. He is attracted to her. He has also seen enough to question whether these people should be treated as instruments that can simply be shut off.

And so he destroys the controller instead of using it.

This decision is one of the most contestable in Dragon Ball. A person may regard it as an irresponsible sacrifice of everyone else's safety to a crush. A person may also see an imperfect act of respect toward someone who has repeatedly been denied control of her own body. Neither reading captures everything by itself. Krillin is not an impartial moral philosopher weighing a hypothetical case. He is frightened, hopeful, and emotionally involved. His decision comes from a confused mixture of desire and recognition.

The outcome is terrible. Cell absorbs 18 and reaches his Perfect Form. Krillin's refusal does not become wise merely because the story later gives him a relationship with her. He has made an ethically intelligible choice whose timing and consequences can still be criticized.

That complication is precisely why the scene is worth an essay rather than a joke about a man falling in love. Krillin's limitations are not only physical. The story can give him an impossible choice, let him make it badly, and let the world continue with the consequences.

## When a person is not a number

Android 18's movement from threatening antagonist to a person in the wider cast is not instantaneous. She has hurt people. Krillin hardly knows her. He cannot turn their encounter into a complete account of her character, and the manga occasionally uses his infatuation for comedy.

Still, her presence changes the moral situation. She is not the kind of enemy the heroes thought they had been warned about, and she is certainly not a component manufactured for Cell's use. Krillin's attraction may be comic, but the recognition that she has a life outside the villain's design is serious.

After the Cell Games, Krillin looks after the unconscious 18 when she has been expelled from Cell. He later wishes for the explosive devices implanted in 17 and 18 to be removed. This is another technological detail with ethical force. He cannot turn back the past in which Gero changed them, but he can ask that a particular threat of control be taken away.

Their later life together, including their daughter Marron, is not the end of every moral difficulty. It is evidence that Dragon Ball can create consequences other than a victory or defeat in a tournament. One of the most memorable relationships in the later series grows from the fact that a fighter could not bring himself to treat a powerful opponent as disposable machinery.

Krillin's willingness to accept a future not built around being the strongest is also significant. He was once a competitive child who wanted to be a superior martial artist. He does not cease being brave or capable, but his life acquires forms of meaning his childhood training never required him to imagine.

## Strength that the battlefield cannot measure

It is tempting to rescue supporting characters from irrelevance by inventing a new ranking in which they secretly surpass the people who overshadow them. Bulma is the smartest, Krillin is the bravest, therefore everyone remains a winner. That is too easy, and it would make them less interesting.

Bulma is not always the cleverest person in every room. She can be impulsive and selfish. Krillin is not fearless; fear is one of the most believable things about him. Both can make mistakes. Neither can defeat Cell by simply producing a larger beam of energy. Dragon Ball has allowed the physical distance between them and its strongest fighters to become enormous.

But a narrative is not a combat simulator. The question of who can defeat whom is only one of its ways of generating interest. Who can invent an escape route? Who will protect a frightened child? Who understands an enemy as a person? Who will risk making the wrong decision for a reason that is not trivial? Who reminds the strongest fighter that an injured friend is more than an observer?

The answers often belong to characters outside the main duel.

This is one reason the earliest Dragon Ball adventures retain their charm. The journey had room for a girl driving a car, a boy hiding a magical ball in his pocket, an old teacher giving impossible exercises, and a rival who became a friend. They were not gathered only to certify that Goku had reached the next stage. They could want things the hero did not want and solve problems he could not solve.

As the series expands, it does sometimes leave these possibilities underused. Krillin may be reduced to watching the spectacle. Bulma may be reduced to waiting for news. The criticism is fair. The answer is not to pretend their agency has been constant, but to notice where it returns and what those moments reveal about the kind of story Dragon Ball is capable of being.

## The invisible machinery of the climax

Look back at the defeat of Cell. The final image is Gohan and an overwhelming Kamehameha. It would be absurd to deny the importance of his power. He had to be strong enough to meet that threat.

But the climax exists inside a chain of events built by people who could not fight the monster on equal terms. Bulma's time machine made Future Trunks' warning possible. Trunks brought knowledge and medicine. Bulma helped build the shutdown controller. Krillin made a choice about the life of the person the controller would affect. Android 16, himself incapable of winning the final battle, gave Gohan a reason to see protective action as compatible with his gentleness. Vegeta's final interference helped Gohan complete the attack. The victory is visibly spectacular and structurally collaborative.

Some of these contributions are good decisions. Some are poor ones. Their consequences include suffering as well as survival. That is the advantage of looking beyond the strongest fighter: a world begins to appear, filled with people who are doing things rather than merely ranking themselves.

The story that begins with Bulma asking a strange boy to come along on a quest ends up needing an engineer who can change the possible future, and a human fighter who cannot always separate care from desire. That is not a tidy lesson in which the power of friendship wins every encounter. It is a more durable achievement: the people who cannot keep up can still change what happens next.

They always could. The first adventure would never have begun without them.
