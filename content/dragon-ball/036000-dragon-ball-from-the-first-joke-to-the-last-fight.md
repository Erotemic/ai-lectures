**Track:** Dragon Ball: From Penguin Village to the End of Z. **Audience:** Fans and newcomers comfortable with full-story spoilers. **Prerequisites:** None; the work's basic story, artistic history and relationships are introduced here. **Spoilers:** The entire original 1984–1995 manga and the broad development of its 1986–1996 television adaptations, with selected references to later media. **Scope:** Akira Toriyama's early comic practice and *Dr. Slump*; the changing shape of *Dragon Ball*; its characters, visual language, deaths and endings; television and global reception; Toriyama's artistic legacy. **Perspective:** A concluding historical and critical synthesis, not a sequential recap of every arc.

# Dragon Ball: From the First Joke to the Last Fight

Near the end of the final chapter of *Dragon Ball*, a boy enters a martial-arts tournament and meets Son Goku. The boy is named Uub. He comes from a poor village, he is nervous, and he possesses strength he does not yet know how to use. Goku has come looking for him because the child is the reincarnation of the terrible enemy once known as Majin Buu. What the audience remembers as a battle for the existence of Earth has returned in the body of a new student.

Goku provokes him into fighting, sees his promise, and decides to teach him. Then they leave together. The final pages do not depict a completed reign or a monument to the universe's strongest fighter. They depict the beginning of another relationship, another journey, another set of things Goku does not know.

The closing chapter of 1995 also looks backward. In the original *Weekly Shonen Jump* presentation, Toriyama recalls the meeting of Goku and Bulma from the opening of the series, eleven years earlier. A boy and a girl beside a fantastic machine, about to find out what the world contains. By the time of the final tournament, the boy has become a grandfather. The girl is a parent and a technological genius. Their friendship has survived battles, deaths, marriages, children, and an astonishing expansion of the fictional universe.

It is fitting that the series should end by remembering a meeting rather than a punch. The manga eventually contained punches powerful enough to shake planets and threats large enough to erase life. But almost every great change in *Dragon Ball* begins when two people meet and misunderstand each other. A girl meets a boy and mistakes him for a useful bodyguard. Two pupils compete for a teacher's approval. An enemy is offered the opportunity to survive. A child sees a friend in his father's old rival. A vain celebrity discovers a lonely monster can be kind.

The series is famous for becoming bigger and bigger. Its more interesting achievement is that it keeps finding ways for bigness to lead back toward intimacy. Its strongest characters change the world, but the world becomes worth saving because it contains people whose lives are smaller than the next fight.

To see how Toriyama arrived at that peculiar balance, we should begin with a different child: a robot girl in a village where almost anything can happen.

## Before the fighting: a cartoonist who liked things

Akira Toriyama did not begin by designing the architecture of a vast fictional cosmos. His early career included commercial art, rejected manga, and a gradual collaboration with the editor Kazuhiko Torishima. Torishima later described their apprenticeship in terms of trial, rejection and learning how a page guides the reader. Toriyama's own interviews emphasize his love of films, machines, jokes, and the pleasure of drawing a character with an instantly readable shape. The surviving accounts do not all tell the same story in the same way; recollections of a creative partnership are still recollections.

By 1980, Toriyama had found a successful form for his talents in *Dr. Slump*. Its setting, Penguin Village, has the logic of a place sketched by someone who is delighted that a village can contain anything. Cars have charmingly impossible bodies. Houses stand among animal people and talking creatures. The inventor Senbei Norimaki is nominally a genius and frequently an idiot. Arale, the robot girl he creates, combines overwhelming physical force with the curiosity and social judgment of a child.

Consider how Arale's strength functions. In a more earnest adventure, a character who could destroy almost anything would be the endpoint of the power system. She would leave no obstacle for other protagonists. In *Dr. Slump*, invincibility is a joke-generating instrument. Arale does not have to conquer the world. She can smash something because she has misread a situation, or run joyfully into a problem that everyone else is trying to manage with solemn adult plans. The stronger she is, the greater the difference between the size of her action and the modesty of her intention.

That comic disproportion survives in *Dragon Ball*. It is visible in young Goku's astonishing appetite, the confidence with which he mistakes a car for a monster, and the way an unimaginably strong opponent can still be embarrassed, hungry, vain, impatient or afraid. Toriyama could draw intimidating bodies. He could also puncture intimidation by drawing somebody's baffled face at exactly the right moment.

The crossover is literal as well as stylistic. During the Red Ribbon Army adventure, Goku arrives in Penguin Village, and Arale becomes part of the pursuit. Two works with very different trajectories suddenly coexist without either having to surrender its identity. For the audience, the meeting is a reminder that the enormous fighting universe is still close to a world where a robot child can innocently break the rules of strength.

This is not an argument that Toriyama never changed. He changed radically. It is an argument that his later action drawing carried into new territory an older devotion to the physical comedy of bodies and the pleasurable specificity of things: a capsule that unfolds into a vehicle, a creature shaped like a joke, an ordinary little house in a world of bizarre machines. Those details create a place where characters can do more than fight.

## A journey that discovered its real subject

In 1984 Toriyama launched *Dragon Ball* in *Weekly Shonen Jump*. The earliest promotional pages emphasized comedy. The premise was a road adventure loosely informed by *Journey to the West*: gather seven magical balls and a dragon will grant a wish. Son Goku's name and monkey tail connect him with the older tale's Monkey King. Bulma's vehicle and Dragon Radar bring the whole idea into a playful modernity.

Toriyama later described his interest in kung-fu films, particularly Jackie Chan, and the 1983 *Dragon Boy* story he made before the serial. He also described a plan that could have ended when the seven balls were found. Those comments matter because they resist the comfortable illusion that the complete later saga, from Saiyan genealogy to cosmic gods, was waiting invisibly in the first chapter. The story's eventual shape is an artistic history, not merely the unveiling of an architectural blueprint.

The first quest is already a little demonstration of how an adventure can outgrow its formal goal. Bulma wants the Dragon Balls for a wish. Goku wants to keep the four-star ball that reminds him of Grandpa Gohan. Oolong, Puar and Yamcha enter for their own comic and selfish reasons. Pilaf believes the wish will give him dominion over the world. When the dragon finally appears, the coveted wish is squandered on a gag. The apparently world-changing treasure produces almost no lasting benefit.

Yet something irreversible has happened. Bulma has met Goku. Goku has gone beyond his mountain. People who began as obstacles, rivals or tricksters have become friends. The quest has proved far more productive as a way of changing relationships than as a way of granting wishes.

This is the first version of a pattern that will endure through the manga's most serious battles. The declared goal may be straightforward—obtain an object, win a tournament, defeat an enemy—but its lasting result is a new connection or an altered idea of who somebody can be.

Toriyama and Torishima then confronted a practical storytelling problem. Once the balls had been gathered, repeating the same treasure hunt forever would exhaust the premise. Toriyama's retrospective account says he decided to emphasize Goku's wish to become stronger. He turned to a tournament, an idea he had also used for comic competition in *Dr. Slump*. The World Martial Arts Tournament changed the series without requiring that it cease to be funny.

A tournament is a near-perfect machine for drama. It gives the fighters a common objective, a clear geography, and rules that the audience can remember. It gives an opponent a personality before that person has to become an enemy. It organizes a succession of duels while allowing the writer to rearrange expectations about who will win.

And crucially, it permits defeat to be useful. Goku loses his first final to Jackie Chun, secretly Master Roshi. Roshi has entered to demonstrate that there will always be someone stronger. Goku's loss does not humiliate him out of the story. It gives him another direction in which to grow.

The lesson is more radical than a simple command to train harder. If winning the tournament ended the pursuit of strength, the narrative would have to keep raising the stakes by discovering more trophies. Instead the hero learns to enjoy the existence of a stronger person. The end of one contest becomes the beginning of a new ambition.

## The enemy who gets a future

This openness to defeated opponents becomes the source of Dragon Ball's greatest relationships and some of its deepest moral difficulties.

The early antagonists are often comical. Pilaf is absurdly grandiose. Several Red Ribbon soldiers are ridiculous in their confidence. But the Red Ribbon adventure also introduces more dangerous violence, particularly through Mercenary Tao. The arrival of King Piccolo makes death a direct assault on the show's social world. Krillin dies. Master Roshi sacrifices himself in a failed attempt to stop the monster. The familiar security of the Dragon Balls is destroyed when Shenron is killed.

These developments are frequently described as the moment when *Dragon Ball* grows up. There is truth in that description, but it can make the earlier comedy sound like a disposable childhood. In fact the new violence is powerful because of what the earlier chapters built. Krillin is not a casualty invented to increase the stakes. He is a friend we have seen train, compete, be jealous and laugh. A comedy has made his death worth grieving.

The same story eventually presents Piccolo Jr., the child and successor of the Demon King, at the twenty-third tournament. Goku defeats him and spares him. Their rivalry continues into the beginning of the television era called *Dragon Ball Z*. When Raditz arrives from space, Goku and Piccolo must cooperate to stop him. Piccolo then takes Goku's son Gohan as a pupil; the old enemy becomes the guardian who will eventually die to protect the boy.

Think about the distance between those two uses of Piccolo. In the first, he is the return of a mortal danger. In the second, he is somebody a frightened child learns to trust. The turn is extraordinary, but it did not come from a sudden declaration that evil was good. Time, necessity and attachment changed the relationship. The manga let a defeated opponent survive long enough to become a person with a future.

Vegeta takes the same structure onto a much larger stage. When he arrives on Earth, he is arrogant and murderous. By Namek, he is also an oppressed subordinate trying to escape Frieza's control, even as he inflicts violence on others. By the end of the original manga, he has a wife, a son, and a history of choices that cannot be erased by one noble act. His eventual participation in saving Earth does not make his earlier crimes imaginary. It makes his story one of protracted, contradictory change.

This pattern is emotionally satisfying because it allows a character to be more than the role first assigned. It is also ethically unstable. Goku's insistence that Vegeta live is a gamble with other people's lives, not a universally defensible rule of mercy. The manga often rewards the gamble by turning an enemy into an ally. It does not always show sufficient concern for the innocents placed at risk while the old villain learns.

A serious celebration of *Dragon Ball* should preserve this discomfort. It is possible to admire the imaginative generosity of a world where an enemy may become a friend without concluding that every decision made in the name of that possibility was wise.

## Earth becomes small, and the page stays readable

The arrival of the Saiyans changes the official television identity of the series in 1989. Toei's original *Dragon Ball* adaptation had begun in 1986. The follow-up was called *Dragon Ball Z*. In print, however, Toriyama's manga continued as one *Dragon Ball* serial. The distinction is useful to viewers, but it should not be mistaken for a new manga beginning with the revelation of Goku's alien origin.

The story suddenly expands its background. Goku has a brother. His people come from another planet. The moon transformation that was a disturbing comic event in the first quest acquires a new explanation. More powerful opponents make the Earth look smaller. After the devastation of the Saiyan invasion, the search for Dragon Balls takes the surviving party to Namek and into Frieza's imperial territory.

It is tempting to remember these changes as a simple arithmetic progression: a stronger fighter appears; the hero trains to overcome him; the next opponent must have a still larger number. But Namek in particular succeeds by doing something almost opposite. It restricts the heroes' freedom and gives information more importance than raw power. Gohan, Krillin and Bulma try to survive among rival groups who all want the same objects. Vegeta hides, steals, bargains and lies. Frieza's troops hunt for spheres and for the people who can use them. The apparent galactic epic becomes a game of vulnerable bodies moving through a landscape of unequal knowledge.

The combat grows spectacular, but Toriyama keeps the immediate physical situation legible. A punch may be too quick to see within the fiction, yet the reader can tell where one person was standing before the blow and where the body ends up afterward. A thick black shape, a clean silhouette or a blank stretch of ground can communicate speed more effectively than a crowded page. The violence may become astronomical, but the eye still needs to understand the distance between two figures.

That connection between enormous stakes and local clarity is one reason the first Super Saiyan transformation has survived as a defining image. Frieza kills Krillin. Goku's anger has a human source the audience remembers from decades of fictional friendship. The new form is graphically economical: altered hair, changed eyes, a new silhouette and manner. The manga does not have to explain the entire future taxonomy of transformations at once. A change of body tells us a change of reality has occurred.

The anime adds voice, duration, color and sound to that moment. It creates a different experience, neither a pure replacement nor a simple reproduction of the page. Every reader can cross from the manga's compressed time into television's held stare and extended scream, but something changes in the journey. The distinction would later become a major source of disagreement among fans who had met the scene through different editions, broadcasts and languages.

## A family story hiding inside the escalation

The Saiyan revelation gives Goku a history, but the great surprise of the later manga is that it also gives him a family.

Gohan's introduction brings a four-year-old into a story built around adult combat. He wants to study. He has inherited enormous power without inheriting his father's joy in testing it. Piccolo trains him, often brutally, and becomes attached to him. On Namek, Gohan acts out of concern for strangers even when doing so puts him in danger. At the Cell Games, he reveals strength that surpasses Goku's, but his victory grows from grief and reluctance rather than a wish to win a championship.

The Cell story is often remembered as a succession crisis. Goku cannot win, so the son must take his place. For one climactic moment that is exactly what happens. Yet the subsequent Great Saiyaman chapters make a more complex proposal. A person can have saved the world and still wish to study, go to school, fall in love and make an embarrassing spectacle of himself in public. Winning the greatest battle does not oblige him to organize his whole adulthood around the next one.

There is an appealingly human problem embedded here. People who are gifted at a difficult activity are often treated as though the gift itself supplies an obligation to devote their lives to it. Gohan is stronger than almost everyone, so readers expect him to keep becoming the strongest. But his aspiration to become a scholar was part of his identity before Cell existed. The power was real; so was the child who did not ask to discover it through violence.

This does not mean Toriyama found the most satisfying way to use him later. The Buu saga offers Gohan a dramatic return, briefly makes him look like the answer to the new crisis, and then removes him from the decisive battle. That is an artistic choice readers can criticize without treating Gohan's interest in an ordinary life as a character flaw.

Other relationships acquire an unexpectedly domestic force. Vegeta, the proud prince who once regarded weakness with contempt, becomes vulnerable to the existence of Bulma and Trunks. Krillin's connection to Android 18 turns an enemy into a partner and later a spouse. Mr. Satan becomes important not through a previously hidden combat technique but through his ability to befriend Buu, the frightened and dangerous being that other fighters only know how to attack.

The late series often strains under the need to invent still greater power. At its best, however, it makes the people who cannot keep up in combat necessary for a different reason. Bulma's inventions create opportunities no punch can supply. Krillin's decisions alter the fate of people he loves. Mr. Satan has a reputation among ordinary people that the strongest warriors do not possess. The world is saved by more than the largest explosion.

## The cost of making death reversible

The Dragon Balls themselves change their dramatic function as the story grows. In the beginning, they are an adventurous treasure and a source of comic possibility. Later they become instruments of rescue, reunion, strategic planning and the restoration of entire populations.

It is easy to claim that resurrection makes death meaningless. The criticism has force. When the reader knows a character may return through a wish, a sacrifice risks becoming a temporary inconvenience. As the wish system grows more flexible, the story must work harder to make loss frightening. A planet can be destroyed and restored. The rules can be expanded or circumvented. It becomes harder to know which finality to believe.

But a death has more than one dramatic effect. The loss of Piccolo in the Saiyan battle destroys Earth's access to its own Dragon Balls, changing the goals of the surviving characters. Krillin's death on Namek forces Goku into a new emotional and physical state. Goku's death against Cell leaves an absence in his sons' lives even though the fiction possesses techniques for contacting the afterlife. An event can be reversible in one sense and still reshape relationships permanently.

The danger is not simply that a dead character might return. It is that the series may learn to use death as a cheap way to insist a scene is serious. Toriyama often escapes that trap by turning attention to the living. Who has to make the journey to Namek? Who must find the people who can grant the wish? Who has learned something while the absent friend was gone? The restoration of bodies cannot restore the exact same social world.

And yet the criticism does not disappear. A late story can undo more than an earlier one could. If every loss becomes only a step in a rescue procedure, we may still care about the characters while losing some trust in the scale of the claimed disaster. The original manga never completely resolves that problem. It keeps changing its own conditions for grief and recovery.

The most revealing answer may lie in its eventual ending. The final act is not a wish that makes Goku invincible or installs him forever as Earth's guardian. It is the appearance of Uub, whose body carries a transformed history. The ultimate enemy has become a new person. The hero makes a choice about what sort of future to give him.

## The finale that admits it is funny

The Majin Buu saga is often criticized for refusing to settle on an ending. It begins with high-school comedy, turns into a tournament, introduces a magical catastrophe, produces transformations and fusions, and repeatedly raises the possibility that some new fighter will end everything. Each promised solution is undone. The plot feels both exuberant and exhausted, as though the story wants to keep inventing possibilities after it has run out of patience for its own hierarchy of strength.

That is a weakness worth acknowledging. A series that has taught readers to invest in Gohan's growth can undercut the investment by shifting the final responsibility elsewhere. A new transformation loses force when another appears soon after. An extended fight can repeat emotional information instead of developing it.

But Buu also opens a door back to the comic's origins. The villain changes shape as though his body were made from a joke, from a nightmare, or from both. A great champion named Mr. Satan, who is nowhere near the strongest person in the cast, becomes one of the few people capable of reaching the friendly side of Buu. The ridiculous public figure forms a relationship that transforms the situation more profoundly than another demonstration of power could.

When the final confrontation arrives, the solution involves a gigantic accumulation of energy from the living world. Goku needs the help of people who cannot hope to fight alongside him. Mr. Satan persuades them to give it. Vegeta, who once treated others as disposable, participates in a scheme that depends on millions of strangers. The last great attack is delivered by a hero whose greatest skill has always been fighting, but it cannot be made from his strength alone.

The joke is that this is a perfectly sensible culmination for the manga. An adventure that began when Bulma recruited a powerful boy for a quest ends with an absurdly powerful man needing the cooperation of a world he has repeatedly saved. The difference is that the people doing the cooperating do not even know who he is. They trust a boastful entertainer instead. The world turns out to need a liar's public credibility and a fighter's private courage together.

I do not think the ending absolves the Buu saga of its meandering. It does something better: it reminds us that an adventure need not be organized around one flawless hero or one consistent tone. It can give its most important work to the least majestic person in the room.

## From one manga into many televisions

For readers in Japan, the manga's transformation unfolded weekly across the 1980s and 1990s. It began in 1984, the original television adaptation started in 1986, and the TV series rebranded as *Dragon Ball Z* in 1989. The manga ended in 1995; the original *Z* broadcast concluded in 1996. Toriyama's continuous serial has often been split in international editions and discussions because the two television series created such powerful cultural identities.

Television changed the meaning of duration. A manga panel can suggest that an action occurs instantaneously, and a reader can move past it at a chosen pace. A weekly television episode has a fixed time slot to fill, a production schedule and an audience expecting the next installment. The *Z* anime sometimes expands a brief exchange into a prolonged confrontation and adds scenes that are absent from the original manga. Some additions become cherished character moments; others interrupt a story's rising pressure. The later *Dragon Ball Z Kai* re-edit offers a different balance, but it cannot serve as a neutral replacement for every viewer's earlier experience.

Then the work travels again through language. Japanese audiences know performances, songs and instrumental music tied to the original broadcasts. In North America, viewers encountered a succession of dubbed and edited versions, with different casts and replacement scores. In Latin America, Brazil, Europe and elsewhere, local performances and broadcast histories developed their own attachments. Not everyone has the same childhood Goku in their ear.

Those differences do not make the original manga meaningless, or imply that every cut and adaptation is equally faithful. They explain why a single set of drawings can generate such divergent emotional memories. The appearance of a transformation may be constant while its soundtrack is different. A translated joke may shift tone. A television edit may make an act of mercy sound more conventionally righteous than the original scene's ambiguous mix of compassion and desire for a future opponent.

The growing franchise gave audiences still other ways to inhabit the world. Films could stage fights in condensed spectacle. Television specials could tell a tragedy about Bardock, a figure the manga had barely explored. Games could let a player inhabit a character, alter a familiar battle or invent a new one. Those forms are not merely imperfect copies of one finished story. Each changes what the audience is allowed to do with it.

This is where *Dragon Ball* acquires a life quite separate from the chapters Toriyama drew. A child may know the series first from a game. A friend may recognize a character's voice before reading a page. Someone who grew up with one regional dub may later discover an earlier score and find that the tone of a familiar scene has changed. The work is continuous, but our encounters with it are not.

## A finished work, an unfinished influence

Toriyama's manga stopped in 1995 after 42 collected volumes. He went on to create and contribute to other work, including *Sand Land* and character designs for video games such as *Dragon Quest*. The original *Dragon Ball* did not require every future franchise continuation to be considered part of its ending. A later television series, film or comic may reimagine a character, add a god, revise a backstory or create a new transformation. Those are further works with their own creative histories. They do not retroactively make Toriyama's earliest chapters a coded forecast of everything to come.

Toriyama died in March 2024. The announcement from his studio and publisher drew attention to a body of work much broader than one fighting saga. Fellow artists described the formative force of his drawings. There are many ways to describe that influence: a recognizable visual vocabulary, the commercial expansion of manga and animation beyond Japan, an approach to action that succeeding artists could study, or the memory of characters who seemed capable of walking out of the page.

Influence is not the same as perfection. Some early jokes are coarse and cruel. Some women in the story are given less freedom than the men to determine what happens next. The repeated demand for stronger opponents can make the less powerful characters feel temporarily irrelevant. The later arcs occasionally sacrifice the coherence of a character's growth to the pleasure of another reversal. Criticizing these choices does not require dismissing the generosity of the whole.

Indeed, the best reason to study the manga closely is that its strengths can be described rather than merely announced. A dramatic page has geometry. A comic reversal has timing. A rival becomes a friend through a series of choices, not because a merchandise label says the character has changed teams. A boy may be stronger than everybody and still misunderstand his son. A father may become capable of defending the world while remaining deeply unsuited to conducting ordinary household life. These are observations a reader can test against the work, rather than articles of nostalgia.

One striking feature of the series' legacy is that its influence operates on several scales at once. There are the famous public images: spiky hair, the gathered energy of a Kamehameha, a training room, a competition that begins under a banner and ends with the universe in peril. But there are also small private imitations. A reader begins to draw machinery with more affection. An artist tries to make an action scene readable with fewer panels. A child invents a game in which the character they like most loses, trains and returns. A friend tells another friend that the best part of the story is not the final attack but the moment someone chooses not to abandon an enemy.

The manga supplied all those ways of playing with the world without insisting that they lead to the same conclusion.

## The last page leaves room for a road

We can now return to the tournament where Goku meets Uub.

There is a satisfying formal symmetry in the meeting. Early Goku was a strange child with an enormous gift who needed somebody to show him the world. Uub is another child with enormous strength and far less experience than the people around him. Goku, who once required Bulma's map and Roshi's teaching, has become somebody who can offer a map and teaching of his own.

The symmetry is not exact. Young Goku was cheerful and nearly fearless. Uub is anxious, conscious of responsibility to his village, and vulnerable to embarrassment. The new student is not a replacement for the old hero. He is a person different enough to make the relationship worth beginning.

That small difference is essential. If *Dragon Ball* were only an endless climb toward stronger fighters, it might have ended with Goku finally undefeated. But undefeated strength is a dead end for a protagonist whose delight comes from discovering people who can surprise him. The final chapter must preserve the possibility that somewhere in the world there is a fighter he has not met, a life he does not understand, a child who could become a friend.

The closing image does not settle every argument about Goku's responsibilities. He is leaving his family and companions again. The future beyond that panel is not guaranteed to be wise, or safe, or domestic. He goes because there is somebody to teach and somebody to learn from. The world has become large enough for both roles to belong to one person.

Now picture the very first encounter. Bulma drives into the mountains. Goku mistakes her vehicle for a monster and attacks it. Two people with wildly different habits cannot yet imagine how long they will know each other. One brings technology and a plan. The other brings strength and curiosity. Neither knows that the most valuable thing produced by the search for the Dragon Balls will be this acquaintance.

An artist who loved ridiculous machines and ridiculous people put them on a road. Then he drew for more than a decade as the road changed shape beneath them: treasure hunt, tournament, army, demon, alien invasion, galactic expedition, war between futures, magical apocalypse. The road did not always turn gracefully. There were wrong directions, repeated landmarks and stretches that went on too long. Yet the central pleasure survived the transformations.

Somebody new might be waiting around the corner. The person who arrives may become a rival, a teacher, an enemy, a friend, or all four at different times. The story is not finished merely because a wish has been granted, a championship won, a planet saved or an invincible form discovered.

At the end Goku flies away with Uub. A beginning has appeared inside the ending.

And behind that ending, still sitting on a mountain road in the first chapter, are a boy with a tail and a girl with a radar, about to find out that neither one can make the journey alone.
