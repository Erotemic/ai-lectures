# 003000 — The Boy with a Tail and the Girl with a Radar: Where Dragon Ball Begins

**Track:** Dragon Ball: From Penguin Village to the End of Z. **Audience:** Fans and curious general listeners. **Prerequisites:** None; the two earlier Toriyama and *Dr. Slump* essays provide useful context. **Scope:** The opening *Dragon Ball* manga adventure, Goku's meeting with Bulma through the first summoning of Shenron; the beginning of the 1986 TV adaptation and the work's creation. **Spoilers:** The entire first Dragon Ball search, Pilaf's failed wish, Goku's transformation, and a brief reference to the later meaning of his tail. No detailed *Z* plot spoilers.

# The Boy with a Tail and the Girl with a Radar: Where Dragon Ball Begins

There is a version of Goku so famous that it can almost erase the boy he used to be. He is standing in a wasteland. He has torn his clothes in battle. Energy rises around him. An enemy threatens the planet, or several planets, or the continued existence of a universe. And somewhere, inevitably, the question is whether he has found a stronger form.

The first Goku is carrying firewood.

He lives by himself in the mountains. He has a tail, enormous strength, an appetite that seems impossible to fill, and a treasured orange ball left to him by his grandfather. He knows very little about other people and nearly nothing about modern life. Then a girl arrives in a car, and he mistakes the machine for a monster. This is how one of the great action stories in popular culture begins: not with a solemn declaration of destiny, but with a collision between two people who cannot quite believe the other exists.

The girl is Bulma. She is clever, impatient, resourceful, materially comfortable, and profoundly unsuited to the wilderness. She has built a radar capable of finding a magical object. Goku can hit things so hard that most of the world's ordinary dangers are no danger to him at all. She knows where they are going. He knows how to stay alive on the way there. Neither has the least idea what the next day is going to look like.

There is a lovely difference between these two forms of competence. Bulma knows how machines work, how to make a plan, and how to pursue something she wants. Goku knows how to move through a world without needing to master it first. He is not stupid in the sense of being incapable of learning. He is ignorant of the conventions that Bulma assumes everyone must know. His misunderstandings can be preposterous, occasionally embarrassing, and sometimes unexpectedly revealing. What looks to her like a perfectly ordinary car looks to him like a creature that needs hitting. What looks to him like a sacred family keepsake looks to her like the item that will finish her collection.

For a while, the entire series runs on the friction between those perspectives. A boy from the mountains and a girl with a radar. Everything that follows, including the great transformations and the legendary battles, begins with the fact that they agree to travel together.

## An adventure before it was a battle epic

Akira Toriyama's *Dragon Ball* began appearing in *Weekly Shonen Jump* in Japan in 1984. The first television series began in 1986. For people introduced through *Dragon Ball Z*, the earlier story can feel like a distant prologue, a collection of jokes and lesser opponents waiting for the serious business of combat to begin. But this was not a separate prequel manufactured to explain the world of *Z*. It was the beginning of one continuing manga. The Japanese manga did not suddenly restart as a new publication with an enormous letter Z on the cover when Goku became an adult. That distinction arose in the television presentation of different portions of a continuous story.

Toriyama had made his reputation with *Dr. Slump*, an exuberant comic featuring a robot girl, improbable machines, visual puns, and an entire neighborhood apparently operating under different laws of physics. When he looked for his next major series, he and his editor, Kazuhiko Torishima, turned toward martial arts and adventure. Toriyama spoke about his enthusiasm for Jackie Chan's kung-fu films and about an earlier short manga called *Dragon Boy*. He also identified *Journey to the West*, the Chinese adventure traditionally associated with the Monkey King, as a formative model for the new series.

That source helps explain the staff, the cloud, the tail, the journey and the company Goku collects. But *Dragon Ball* is not a straight retelling of an old religious and literary epic. It moves with the improvisational speed of a comic whose author enjoys imagining the next object, face, vehicle or joke. In the pages of this new adventure, a Chinese-inflected fantasy landscape can contain an extraordinarily modern motorbike. A turtle may have an important errand. A man who lives on an island may know a technique that can erase a mountain. A little capsule can hold a large house. Nobody spends much time explaining how these technologies fit into a single consistent history.

That looseness is an artistic resource. *Dragon Ball* does not need every corner of its world mapped before the characters can go somewhere new. The world is elastic enough to produce a fresh surprise whenever the road turns. And the drawings make that surprise inviting. Toriyama loves machinery enough to make even a throwaway vehicle look as though its bolts, seats, tires and instruments might actually fit together. His cars and planes have weight and contour. They can be wonderfully compact or absurdly elaborate. Then he puts them beside a round-faced creature that looks as though it belongs in a completely different genre. Somehow, they share the same convincing space.

Look, in the mind's eye, at the contrast on that first trip. Bulma has devices. Goku has a staff and a body that does not respect ordinary human limits. Bulma can find the Dragon Balls, but she does not have the strength to seize them from everything that wants to keep them. Goku has the strength, but without Bulma the search would not exist. If the story were only about power, the boy would be enough. If it were only about solving a puzzle, the inventor would be enough. The first *Dragon Ball* requires both.

There is also a creative history hiding in the way the debut was advertised. In a later official retrospective of *Weekly Shonen Jump*'s 1984 launch issue, promotional material emphasized comedy as much as action. Toriyama recalled the impression that some of the early promotion expected another comedy along the lines of *Dr. Slump*. Reading the opening today, that expectation makes sense. There are punches and dangerous encounters, but much of the suspense is comically disproportionate. A person may threaten to rule the world; the next page may be concerned with a pig behaving disgracefully.

The action will grow. The comedy will never entirely disappear. In these early chapters, neither element needs to be the other's disguise.

## The ball that cannot be replaced

Bulma's machine identifies the Dragon Balls by the faint signal they emit. Gather all seven, and a dragon named Shenron will appear to grant a wish. It is an almost perfect adventure premise. Each object can be hidden in a different place; every owner can have a different motive; a rival searcher can arrive before or after the heroes; a completed quest promises something breathtaking without explaining exactly how anybody will use it.

Bulma has a personal desire. Depending on the language and version through which the story is encountered, she hopes for a wonderful boyfriend. This is a very human and very funny use for an object of cosmic power, especially when the person making the wish has enough confidence to assume the world should cooperate with her romantic plans. Her wish is not noble. It does not need to be. Bulma's energy moves the plot before the story has discovered a large evil to oppose.

Goku's four-star ball is different. It is a possession he associates with Grandpa Gohan, the man who raised him. It has emotional meaning before it has magical meaning. Bulma sees its place in a set of seven. Goku sees a connection to someone he misses. This is the first small demonstration of how a magical object can mean entirely different things to people standing in the same room.

Bulma cannot simply order Goku to hand it over. The trip they negotiate is built around this difference. They may collect the other balls, but Goku's connection to his grandfather is not just another item to be inventoried. If the object were ordinary treasure, his attachment would be a childish obstacle to a sensible plan. Because the object is something that carries memory, it becomes the sensible part of Goku's otherwise bewildering behavior. He is the boy who can break an automobile, but who also refuses to treat a small round souvenir as disposable.

The initial journey is often described as a treasure hunt. It is also the accumulation of people who seem to have wandered in from unrelated cartoons. A turtle needs help getting home. Goku helps it, and that act introduces Master Roshi, the legendary martial artist living at Kame House. Roshi can reward Goku with the extraordinary Flying Nimbus, a cloud that only someone pure of heart can ride. The cloud is an elegant gag and an efficient way to establish character. Where another fantasy might require a long explanation of a chosen hero's moral nature, this one lets a little boy climb onto a cloud, while the people around him discover whether it will accept them.

Roshi himself is a more troublesome proposition. He has great knowledge, considerable generosity and appalling habits. Early *Dragon Ball* repeatedly mixes sexual comedy with situations in which people, particularly Bulma, are subjected to unwanted attention or coercive bargains. These scenes are not merely a handful of unfortunate English translations; they are part of the original comic's sensibility. They can be grating now, and they were always jokes at somebody's expense. But reducing the series either to those jokes or to a sanitized memory of a wise teacher would miss the friction of its actual tone. Roshi can be a brilliant master, a dependable ally, and an old man whose behavior deserves criticism, sometimes within a single scene.

His Kamehameha provides a vivid example of that early looseness. We remember the move as a universal emblem of combat. Here, when Roshi demonstrates it at Fire Mountain, the blast is an astonishing escalation in a local problem. An old man who seemed to belong to a comedy about beaches and turtles suddenly unleashes enough force to change the landscape. Goku, watching with a child's delighted attention, is already trying to do the same thing. A legendary martial technique appears in the middle of what has been an odd road trip. Later the series will treat such attacks as standard equipment for elite fighters. Initially, the event can still astonish a small group of people who have never seen anything like it.

This willingness to let the universe expand without stopping to say that it is expanding is one of Toriyama's strengths. A staff is funny until it saves someone. A cloud is charming until it becomes a lifeline. An old man's trick seems impossible until the boy copies it. None of these things arrives with a history lecture. They become part of the world because somebody uses them.

## The thieves, the pig, and the desert bandit

Oolong is a shapeshifter. He first presents himself as a menace, terrorizing a village by assuming frightening forms. The secret is that he can turn himself into something frightening without becoming courageous. When Goku challenges him, the supposedly terrible monster's limitations become the joke. As a traveling companion he remains selfish, impatient and frequently cowardly, yet he is also capable of a decisive act when it arrives.

Yamcha enters as a desert bandit, accompanied by Puar, who can also transform. Yamcha is competent enough to seem dangerous, at least in the world as the story currently measures danger. But he has a crippling awkwardness around women, and that weakness transforms his ambitions. He wants the Dragon Balls for a problem that a stranger watching from the outside might think requires a conversation rather than a supernatural intervention. Bulma wants an ideal boyfriend; Yamcha is terrified of talking to women. The two possible wishes are practically begging to meet.

These contradictions are typical early *Dragon Ball*. Oolong can change shape but not character. Yamcha can fight but cannot flirt. Goku is immensely strong but cannot recognize common objects. Bulma can build remarkable technology but cannot plan away embarrassment or disappointment. Each power comes attached to an incapacity. Sometimes that incapacity is crude, sometimes lovely, and sometimes it is what makes the character continue being interesting after the immediate joke has finished.

It also means that the people Goku meets do not remain fixed as monsters or obstacles. Yamcha can begin as a thief and become a friend. Oolong can begin as a bully and end up in the company of the people he tried to trick. These transformations in allegiance are almost more important than the literal transformations that the shapeshifters perform. There is an ethic underneath the nonsense: defeating somebody does not require erasing them from the story. The character who loses today may have a reason to be standing beside you tomorrow.

One can already see a line from this flexibility to later *Dragon Ball*, when enemies become rivals and rivals become companions. It would be wrong to pretend that every future alliance was secretly planned in the opening chapters. It is more persuasive to say that the comic discovered a useful habit early. A good adversary is also a potential new source of stories. Keep the person alive, and the world becomes more populated, more complicated and more fun.

Bulma is essential to that process too, even when the story treats her badly. She is not the martial artist who resolves every dangerous encounter. She is the one whose knowledge and desire make these encounters possible. She can make sense of the radar. She understands capsules. She is capable of irritation on a heroic scale. A series that eventually becomes obsessed with ever greater fighting power begins by placing much of its narrative authority in the hands of a young woman who cannot win a battle by punching. As the franchise progresses, her genius remains consequential. The scale of the threats changes; the need for someone who can build a machine does not.

There are moments when she is reduced to the target of a joke, and there are moments when she has the best practical idea in a scene. The tension between those uses of Bulma should not be erased in either direction. She is not a model of unqualified enlightened characterization, and she is not merely a helpless companion carried through the plot. She is co-founder of the adventure.

## The emperor who almost wins

Eventually the quest reaches Pilaf. He has the ambition of a world conqueror and the instincts of a theatrical nuisance. He and his associates want to assemble the seven balls and make the wish before anyone else can. The searchers become prisoners. The magical objects fall into the wrong hands. For a moment, an adventure that has been getting away with almost anything appears to be headed toward a recognizably terrible conclusion.

Then Shenron appears.

This is the moment when the story cashes the promise made at the beginning. A vast dragon rises to answer a wish. The scale is enormous: the little balls have summoned something that appears genuinely outside human capability. In a traditional heroic fantasy, the wish might decide the fate of nations. The villains might be rewarded for their patience, or the hero might make a noble request that proves what sort of person he is.

Instead, Oolong gets the wish.

He blurts out a request for women's underwear, preempting Pilaf's bid to rule the world. It is an exceptionally silly solution to a genuine problem. The dignity of the supernatural dragon is pressed into service for a gag. There is a valid criticism available about where the comic's sexual humor aims and who becomes an object in it. But as a plot device, the interruption is also brilliantly suited to the adventure we have actually been reading. A world full of magical treasures, talking animals and gadget-obsessed teenagers does not suddenly turn solemn merely because a villain has arranged for it to do so.

Notice who saves the day, and how. Goku does not win a duel with Pilaf to earn the wish. Bulma does not produce an improved machine that commandeers the dragon. Oolong, whose chief qualifications have been cowardice and dubious judgment, has the presence of mind to speak before the conqueror can. His smallness is part of the answer. An enormous wish has been denied by a character who has previously shown very little grandeur.

This is also the first occasion when the title of the series becomes temporarily unusable as a mission statement. The balls scatter and lose their power for a period. There is no immediate second collection to repair a botched request. Bulma has not obtained the ideal boyfriend by supernatural means. Pilaf has not conquered the world. Goku has been carried far beyond the little life he knew. The objects accomplished precisely one wish, and it was ridiculous. Yet the story has somehow acquired a permanent collection of relationships.

That is the difference between a premise and a series. A premise can be exhausted when seven objects are gathered. A series survives if the gathering has changed the people who were looking for them.

## The moon is not in on the joke

There is one more surprise before the first journey ends, and it is the strangest.

Goku sees the full moon and becomes a gigantic ape. His transformation is far more destructive than the harmless tricks of Oolong or Puar. The boy who has seemed innocent, indestructible and reasonably safe to travel with becomes a force that does not recognize his companions. His immense body tears through the confinement around them and wrecks Pilaf's plans. The group has to survive something that comes from Goku rather than something that threatens him.

Here the tail stops being only an amusing feature borrowed from the Monkey King. It becomes a visible sign of danger the child himself does not understand. Earlier, the grandfather he adored was simply gone, and stories about a monstrous creature carried a terrible unexplained weight. Long afterward, as the fiction develops, the relation between Goku, the moon and his grandfather comes into painful focus. The first time through, though, the ape is a disturbing intrusion into a funny adventure. Something about the strongest member of the party is not entirely known, even to himself.

That scene demonstrates a quality the early manga can have that retrospective viewing sometimes obscures. Goku is not born onto the page as a solved piece of franchise mythology. His tail, his strength and his odd relation to the moon invite questions without obliging Toriyama to have written all future answers already. It is easy to view these chapters with a completed encyclopedia and treat each detail as a planted clue. But there is an enormous pleasure in allowing the early story to retain its original uncertainty. The boy turns into an ape. The castle comes down. His friends are alive. The people who know the most about the world still know very little about Goku.

The ape also rearranges the group's moral position. They have spent chapters benefiting from the boy's inhuman strength. The same capacity that protected them now threatens them. Strength is not the same as judgment. Invulnerability is not the same as safety. For a series that will eventually build enormous battles around the question of controlled power, it is a striking early image: power without a mind capable of directing it.

I do not think the scene requires us to read a complete future philosophy of combat into the opening story. It has enough force as a scene. An absurdly powerful child looks up at the sky and becomes a monster, while his companions scramble to undo the consequences. But it leaves behind a productive thought. The question is not only how strong Goku can become. It is what sort of person must live with that strength.

## Why the journey can end and the story can continue

After Pilaf, Goku and Bulma do not simply reset to their first meeting. There is a natural parting. Bulma has a city to return to. Yamcha's life begins moving toward something beyond banditry, with Puar beside him. Oolong has been folded into a company that knows exactly how ridiculous he can be. Goku, meanwhile, has encountered the possibility that someone could teach him what to do with his extraordinary gifts. Master Roshi is waiting, and the world's martial artists are waiting beyond him.

That change of direction might sound inevitable from the vantage point of a franchise famous for training and combat. It was not inevitable at the beginning. Toriyama later described the movement toward the Tenkaichi Budokai as a response to the story's early reception and to his sense that Goku's desire to become stronger could supply a more compelling direction. A tournament was a way of revealing a character motive that had been present without yet being the main engine.

It is worth separating two things here. The first quest discovers the world: villages, mountains, machines, hermits, bandits, creatures, and a dragon. The martial-arts stories discover what Goku wants to do once that world exists. He has enjoyed traveling, eating, rescuing people and discovering what his body can do. He has not yet encountered a long line of people whose skill surpasses his own. It is one thing to be effortlessly stronger than a bandit or a bully. It is quite another to discover a teacher whose technique is more refined than anything you know.

The shift also lets friendship take a different form. Bulma is Goku's first partner because she needs his help, and he needs her knowledge. In the future, his fellow student Krillin will become a companion who shares his ambition, trains beside him, competes against him and learns to appreciate him beyond the fight. The first relationships teach Goku about other kinds of people. The second kind will teach him that someone can want what he wants and still be different from him.

All of this grows from an opening adventure in which almost nothing stays in its original category. Machines are mistaken for beasts. Bandits become friends. A master behaves like a clown. The clown-like pig saves the world. The innocent boy becomes a monster. A mythical dragon grants the least majestic wish imaginable. The story's identity is not yet settled, and that is exactly why there is so much room to move.

There is an affectionate way to remember early *Dragon Ball* as the smaller, gentler part of the franchise. It is smaller in the scale of its battles, certainly. It is not always gentle, and its jokes can be cruel. But it is extraordinarily generous with possibilities. It allows an artist's curiosity to run ahead of the map. Every new character can become a companion. Every background vehicle can look as though it deserves an adventure of its own. Every road may lead somewhere the story did not anticipate when it began.

The Dragon Balls were supposed to deliver one miraculous wish. They did. The wish was almost worthless.

But Bulma found Goku, and Goku found the world. A girl with a radar had changed the life of a boy with a tail. The rest of the series would spend years discovering just how much could follow from that.

The essay describes the manga first; the opening 1986 anime adapts and adds to it. Chapter titles and English transliterations can vary by edition. The main story is interpreted critically rather than reproduced or paraphrased chapter by chapter.

**Creation context:** Toriyama's retrospective interview in *Daizenshuu 2* (English fan translation by Ian Kelley) describes *Dr. Slump*, *Dragon Boy*, Jackie Chan films, *Journey to the West*, Bulma, Oolong and Yamcha's counterparts, and the tournament pivot. Kazuhiko Torishima offers his own overlapping but not identical retrospective in a 2016 interview by Ollie Barder. Neither recollection is treated as evidence of a prewritten long-range plan. The 2021 official site presentation reproduces launch-era promotional framing and attributes comments to Toriyama about the first illustration.

**The material's comedic history:** The first wish and early treatment of Bulma are discussed as part of the original story's period-specific tone. The lecture does not excuse the jokes, claim everyone received them the same way, or erase them from the work. Descriptions of panel composition are the essay's critical interpretation; no images or scans are supplied.
