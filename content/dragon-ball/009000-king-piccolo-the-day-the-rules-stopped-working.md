# 009000 — King Piccolo: The Day the Rules Stopped Working

**Track:** Dragon Ball: From Penguin Village to the End of Z. **Audience:** Fans and curious general listeners. **Prerequisites:** None; Krillin, the tournaments, the Dragon Balls and the relevant relationships are established in the narration. **Scope:** The Great Demon King Piccolo conflict in the original *Dragon Ball* manga, approximately Tales 135–161, with a short look at the aftermath. **Spoilers:** Deaths of Krillin, Roshi and Chiaotzu; the release and rejuvenation of King Piccolo; Shenron's destruction; Goku's victory and Piccolo Jr.'s birth. **Perspective:** Dramatic interpretation, not an assertion that Toriyama planned the later franchise from this point.

# King Piccolo: The Day the Rules Stopped Working

The tournament is over. The new champion, Tien, has begun to find a conscience. Goku has lost a final by an absurdly narrow margin and has no reason to be ashamed. The friends have assembled again in the relaxed atmosphere of a world martial-arts competition: tired, hungry, triumphant or disappointed, but alive. For a moment the story seems ready to become cheerful.

Krillin goes off on a small errand. Then somebody finds him dead.

It is a brutal change of direction. There is no dramatic duel in which he displays everything Roshi taught him and falls only after a heroic final exchange. The audience hasn't been prepared by a week of solemn farewells. A boy who has spent a great deal of the manga learning, quarreling, competing and laughing with Goku is simply killed, and a name connected to a terrible figure from the past begins to circulate.

The culprit is Tambourine, a creature produced by the Great Demon King Piccolo. The murderer has not come to test himself against the tournament's finest competitors. He is hunting them.

This is the first major shock of the King Piccolo story: the people whose abilities we have measured and celebrated in the ring have become names on a list. The tournament made the world's strongest martial artists visible. Piccolo's servant uses that visibility to find them and kill them before one of them can learn how to oppose his master.

Toriyama has taken a familiar institution and altered its meaning. A record of competition becomes a target register. The martial-arts community, once a place of excitement and pride, is suddenly vulnerable because its members know one another and can be found.

The scale of the threat changes, but its first proof is small. One familiar person is absent. His friends can no longer ask him what happened. The heroic rules have stopped working, and Goku does not yet know what to do.

## Krillin is not a disposable victim

The death is powerful because Krillin has mattered for a long time in ways not defined by strength alone. He arrived at Kame House as a somewhat calculating rival, gained Goku's friendship through shared training, impressed the spectators in tournaments, and kept developing ideas that made his physical limits less important than his quick thinking. He is competitive and occasionally petty, with enough ordinariness to make the wild events around him feel inhabited.

Early Krillin could have remained the funny, shorter pupil who made the protagonist look gifted. Instead he became somebody Goku likes, somebody who can challenge him, and somebody whose own victories are worth celebrating. Their semifinal in the second tournament is a good example. Both are determined to win; neither needs to damage the friendship to take the contest seriously.

So when Krillin is found dead, we are not primarily asked to admire the killer's power. We are asked to feel the removal of a relationship. Goku reacts with a desperate anger that is very different from his normal appetite for a good fight. He has enjoyed battling strangers and even dangerous opponents, but he cannot make this murder into a contest he chose to enter. The opponent has already violated the terms under which strength had been exciting.

It would be inaccurate to say that *Dragon Ball* had never presented death before. Bora was murdered by Taopaipai. Goku's beloved grandfather had long been dead. The story had already discovered that a wish can restore life. But Krillin's murder is arranged differently: immediately after a jubilant tournament, against a close friend, and as the opening move in an attack on the whole community. The shock is a change in emphasis as much as in subject matter.

A villain can be evil in a cartoonish way, announcing plans to dominate the world, and still serve primarily as an entertaining obstacle. This new enemy introduces something more intimate. He can kill one of the people the story has asked us to know.

There is a further cruelty in the timing. Goku had recently used Shenron's wish to restore Bora, fulfilling his promise to Upa. That history means the reader is aware of a possible response to Krillin's death. It also means that resurrection isn't the same as being safe. The friends will need to act, to find the Dragon Balls, and to survive long enough to make the wish. A power that could save a life becomes a possible objective for the villain as well.

The Dragon Balls are no longer merely what the heroes pursue. They are something an enemy may exploit and destroy.

## An old terror in a small container

King Piccolo is an ancient evil who was once sealed away by Mutaito, the master of both Roshi and the Crane Hermit. He has been confined in a vessel at the bottom of the sea through a dangerous technique called the Mafu-Ba, sometimes translated as the Evil Containment Wave. Emperor Pilaf, still eager for an easy route to domination, helps release him.

There is something appropriate about Pilaf finding a threat well beyond his ability to manage. He has spent the series wanting to be a ruler. When an actual tyrant appears, Pilaf becomes a comic reminder of how little he understood the thing he was asking for. His costumes and vehicles had the shape of villainy without the weight. King Piccolo possesses the weight.

Notice the lineage. Mutaito, Roshi, the Crane Hermit, Tien, Chiaotzu, Goku and Krillin all occupy a history of martial arts; the threat from the past reaches directly into that history. The second tournament had asked which school teaches strength responsibly. Now the teachers and students confront the enemy that once required their master's sacrifice. A rivalry that looked intensely important to the two hermits becomes secondary to a shared inheritance of danger.

This is also a way of making the world older. Before the demon king arrives, we have met ancient-seeming places, fantastical technologies, gods of one kind or another and people who can cross into the afterlife. But much of the action has the cheerful scale of a child stumbling upon a new adventure. Piccolo brings history with him. His mere existence suggests that the present is only a brief period of peace between catastrophes somebody else had to confront.

The method of his confinement is an effective piece of supernatural engineering. The Mafu-Ba is not a superior martial-arts technique that happens to cause more damage. It is a procedure for containing what cannot safely be left free. Its cost is high. It depends on the person performing it, on timing, and on the existence of something that can hold the prisoner. It belongs to a different family of solutions from punching an enemy harder.

That distinction will matter repeatedly. A story can become tedious if every crisis is settled by increasing the hero's physical power. The containment technique offers a different path, with its own terrible risks. And when those risks defeat the older generation, the possibility of merely repeating their sacrifice becomes less and less reassuring.

## The music of a frightened world

Piccolo's offspring have names like Tambourine, Cymbal and Drum. The names belong to musical instruments, which is very much the sort of naming joke Toriyama enjoys. The creatures themselves can be terrifying. That contrast is one of the reasons the arc works without requiring the author to renounce his earlier comic imagination.

Tambourine carries out the hunt for martial artists, killing Krillin and others. When Goku pursues him in anger, Goku is exhausted and hungry, not in the condition we have come to associate with his best fighting. The killer defeats him and wrecks his Flying Nimbus. The familiar yellow cloud, a visual emblem of the boy's freedom to travel, can be taken away from him. The defeat is physical, but the broken means of travel gives it a second meaning. The world is no longer arranged to carry him safely from one interesting destination to another.

Then a swordsman named Yajirobe appears. He is irritable, hungry, reluctant to help and astonishingly capable with his blade. He does not treat the crisis with the solemnity Goku's friends might wish. When a creature named Cymbal seeks a Dragon Ball, Yajirobe handles him with startling efficiency and, in a grotesquely comic turn, considers him food. The story has rediscovered its appetite for the ridiculous at exactly the moment the reader might expect all jokes to stop.

Yajirobe is an important presence for that reason. He is not a grand moral exemplar. He is also not a coward in any uncomplicated sense. He does not want responsibilities imposed on him, but he can act decisively and eventually helps Goku when help is needed. His stubborn practical instincts are a relief from the frantic momentum of a global catastrophe. He sometimes seems to be participating in a different adventure, one in which the most urgent question is whether there is dinner.

There is a useful artistic contrast in his introduction. Toriyama could have rendered the demon king's arrival as a complete stylistic conversion, cutting away the jokes and presenting every surviving character as traumatized. Instead the page remains populated by people with their own desires and absurdities. An enemy's tyranny is frightening precisely because it takes place in a world worth inhabiting. There are still meals to argue over, friends to complain about, and unexpected companions on the road.

When Goku, properly fed, can defeat Tambourine, the victory brings some satisfaction. It does not end the crisis. Tambourine was an agent of the demon king. The greater threat has remained beyond the scene. The difference in scale is important: a personal act of vengeance may remove the killer, but the system that commissioned the killing remains.

## The strongest fighter is not enough

Goku eventually confronts King Piccolo and is soundly beaten. The fight is one of the manga's clearest statements that his existing talents cannot simply be trusted to carry him to victory. A child who has defeated an army and come within a hairsbreadth of winning the world championship meets a force that makes those accomplishments look limited.

Toriyama had used defeat constructively before. Jackie Chun's victory helped Goku understand that learning was never finished. Tien's championship taught that a loss may coexist with a fine performance and an ethical success. Piccolo's victory is not educational in that comfortable sense. A teacher has not secretly entered to offer a lesson; an honest rival has not met Goku in a ring. A cruel tyrant has chosen to eliminate an obstacle, and Goku survives only because other people can help him.

This matters to the mythology of Goku as an invulnerable savior. He is never most interesting merely because he is strong. He becomes interesting when strength doesn't suffice, when he is angry or confused, or when the ordinary relationships of friendship and assistance make his next decision possible. Yajirobe carries him toward Karin Tower. Karin offers a dangerous path forward. Other fighters attempt desperate interventions while he recovers. Goku is a central actor, but he is not doing the work of saving the world alone.

Roshi decides to confront King Piccolo through the technique inherited from Mutaito. The effort has an awful dignity. An old man who can be ridiculous in daily life knows what the danger requires and accepts a cost that the young fighters may scarcely understand. The attempt fails, and Roshi dies. Chiaotzu is also killed when he tries to interrupt Piccolo's use of the Dragon Balls.

These are different deaths from Krillin's, but together they accomplish something sharp. The villain has cut across generations: pupil, teacher, new ally. He destroys relationships that were recently antagonistic as easily as relationships that were intimate. The tournament's victories and defeats have been made irrelevant, and the school rivalry no longer protects anyone from sharing the same danger.

Tien survives to attempt what the older man attempted, practicing the Mafu-Ba with immense seriousness. The plan is defeated in part by the frustrating material details of containing the demon king: a damaged sealing vessel means that mastery of the technique alone will not solve the problem. This is the kind of setback that gives an invented martial art dramatic substance. You cannot invoke its name and expect the story to obey. The method has conditions. The people attempting it have bodies, time constraints and breakable equipment.

The crisis is therefore not simply a ladder on which Goku must climb one more rung. Several kinds of courage are being tested, and more than one of them ends in disaster.

## The wish that changes everything

King Piccolo seeks the Dragon Balls to restore his youth. He is not asking to become benevolent, to acquire a kingdom peacefully, or even to remove the opposition by some clever loophole. He wants the power that age has taken from him. Once the wish is granted, his menace grows.

Then he kills Shenron.

The act is disturbing because it attacks the premise that made so many earlier adventures possible. The wish-granting dragon had been the resource that made restoration imaginable, a force whose appearance could transform comedy into miracle. Piccolo uses it, then destroys it to prevent others from making wishes against him. The magical safety net is gone at the moment when the story has accumulated losses it desperately wants to reverse.

A villain who merely acquires a stronger body can be overcome by a stronger protagonist. A villain who removes a future solution changes how the reader thinks about everything that follows. Krillin, Roshi and Chiaotzu cannot simply be wished back by a group of friends who outlast the battle. They have lost the apparent means to repair the damage.

This is why the destruction of the dragon is more consequential than one more demonstration of Piccolo's destructive power. It affects the story's future tense. Not only is the present terrible, but the obvious escape route has been closed.

Piccolo proceeds to seize the World King's position and rule through terror. His power is no longer just a matter for martial artists. The public is threatened with arbitrary destruction, including a grotesque system of selecting places to annihilate. Here Toriyama shifts the villain from specialist to tyrant. He does not merely wish to defeat the strongest fighters. He wants ordinary people who cannot fight him to live with the knowledge that he can destroy them whenever he chooses.

The Red Ribbon Army was large, well equipped and often comically incompetent. Piccolo has fewer administrative layers and a far more direct relationship to fear. The Army could be opposed base by base. Piccolo's rule is an attack on a whole population's sense that tomorrow is reasonably secure. His announcements are performances of domination. Even people who have never heard of Goku may now be in danger.

## The cost of borrowed strength

Goku's return to Karin Tower leads to another supposed magical water. Previously, the sacred water involved an exercise in pursuit: the challenge of getting it from Karin mattered more than anything in the water itself. Here the Super Water of the Gods presents a genuinely dangerous ordeal. It may draw out latent power, but drinking it can kill.

The distinction is worth making because it prevents the scene from becoming a simple repetition of an earlier training trick. Toriyama has already used a fake shortcut to teach Goku that improvement comes from work. Now he gives him a perilous intervention with a real effect. The goal is not to replace effort with an effortless miracle. The body must survive something that is almost too much for it.

Goku's recovery brings him back into the world of the living as a more formidable opponent. When he arrives at the World King's palace, Tien is in danger from a demon named Drum. Goku defeats the monster with startling speed. The earlier hierarchy, in which Tambourine could defeat Goku, has been reversed. But the real object is still Piccolo, newly restored to his prime.

In a weaker story, the remainder would be an extended victory lap for the newly upgraded hero. Toriyama makes it a punishing fight. Piccolo does not obligingly demonstrate that his power has become obsolete. He destroys the surrounding environment, strikes with relentless force and eventually takes Tien hostage to restrict Goku's actions. The battle brings moral choice into the mechanics again: Goku cannot simply unleash everything he has without considering another person's safety.

The finishing attack is one of the great physical images in early *Dragon Ball*. Injured and nearly spent, Goku uses a Kamehameha directed toward the ground to propel himself upward. He gathers what remains into his right arm and drives through King Piccolo's body.

The physical mechanics are legible even in a world that has exceeded normal physics. A blast has recoil. Recoil can be used for movement. Movement can produce one final attack. We understand how the components fit together because we've watched Goku learn to improvise with martial techniques rather than merely announce his superiority.

There is no clean triumphal elegance to the outcome. The boy is badly hurt. The enemy has used hostages and terror. A victory that might once have ended with a smiling pose in a tournament ring ends with a body pierced by another body. The story has changed the violence it is willing to show and the emotional cost that violence can carry.

## The enemy leaves an egg

King Piccolo dies, but before the end he produces an egg carrying a successor, Piccolo Jr. This matters for the structure of what follows. The original demon king and the younger Piccolo whom Goku will encounter at the next tournament are connected, but not simply interchangeable characters. The offspring inherits a purpose of revenge while existing as someone who can make different decisions.

That is a striking way to end the reign of an apparently absolute villain. Piccolo attempted to destroy the institutions and people who might threaten him, but he cannot seal the future into the shape he wants. The story refuses to let his death be the last fact about him. His legacy remains a problem to be met later, in the form of a child who will grow.

This creates a complicated connection to the earlier tournament themes. Tien entered as the pupil of a cruel master and changed the direction of his life. Piccolo Jr. will carry an inheritance more demanding than a teacher's instruction: the unfinished intentions of his own creator. The possibility that an heir may diverge from a predecessor has become part of Dragon Ball's long narrative grammar.

The dragon's destruction also proves not to be the last word. Goku learns that Kami, the guardian who created the Dragon Balls, may be able to restore Shenron. He travels toward Kami's Lookout, and restoration becomes possible again. The manga has not abandoned its taste for miraculous reversal. It has made the route to reversal longer and connected it to the existence of a new part of the world.

A cynic might say that this simply cancels the losses. The objection deserves to be heard. Once the audience believes a magic resurrection is always somewhere beyond the next obstacle, each death risks becoming less final. In this particular arc, however, the interval of uncertainty has already done its dramatic work. Friends acted without certainty that they could be restored. The villain's destruction of Shenron altered their choices. Their attempts to protect one another remain meaningful even when another miracle eventually becomes available.

The larger franchise will have to keep renegotiating that bargain. King Piccolo is one of the places where we can see the bargain being formed rather than taken for granted.

## Terror inside a comic world

It is often said that King Piccolo is where *Dragon Ball* becomes serious. The description captures a real change in tone but can make the earlier material sound too slight. It was already a story about friendship, obligation, the misuse of power and the pain of losing people. The Red Ribbon Army included murder. Baba's ridiculous contest ended in an encounter with the dead. Tien's introduction showed cruelty inflicted in a place built for fair competition.

What King Piccolo does is concentrate those existing capacities and remove some of the cushioning around them. Comic enemies had been allowed to fail in absurd ways. The demon king's failures do not arrive quickly enough to protect Krillin, Roshi, Chiaotzu or a terrified population. A fight can end with a death rather than a lesson. A magic wish can belong to the tyrant rather than the child. Even Shenron can be destroyed.

The arc's villain is not psychologically intricate. His desires are simple: regain strength, eliminate threats, dominate the world. He does not challenge Goku with an elaborate philosophy, a sympathetic grievance or a tragic social vision. Yet that simplicity serves the moment. The force of the story comes from what he does to the world and the people already living in it. We know the value of Krillin's companionship. We know Roshi's flaws and the dignity of his better actions. We have seen Tien begin to learn responsibility. The villain's lack of interest in any of those individual lives makes him terrifying.

And Toriyama does not lose his capacity for absurd invention. There is still Yajirobe wanting to eat. Pilaf is still a fool playing with authority he does not understand. The demons still have names that sound like a peculiar percussion ensemble. The old cartoonist's humor persists inside a story that has acquired consequences harsher than the reader once expected.

That continuity is essential to understanding how *Dragon Ball* can later travel so far beyond its beginnings without becoming a wholly different creation. Toriyama's changing interests alter the balance of jokes, adventure and action, but the earlier artistic instincts continue to supply surprises. Someone can interrupt the grandiosity of a tyrant with a practical complaint. A brilliant battle maneuver may still grow out of a simple comic physical idea about recoil. An apparently finished antagonist may leave behind an egg.

## What the boy can and cannot save

King Piccolo is finally defeated through Goku's extraordinary effort, but Goku's success cannot make the earlier deaths meaningless or transform him into the kind of person who can prevent every loss. The fight has demonstrated the opposite. He can arrive too late. He can choose the wrong moment to pursue an enemy. He can need another person to carry him to safety. And he may have to put his own body at risk to protect someone who cannot protect himself.

This is a different heroic education from the one Jackie Chun offered in the first tournament. There the lesson was that the strongest-looking boy should remain humble because there is always someone stronger. Here the lesson is that strength, however remarkable, is not an exemption from dependence, fear or responsibility. Friends matter before the hero wins and after he has lost.

The arc also sets up a question that will continue to trouble the series as the threats become planetary and then cosmic. What happens when the person we rely on to save everyone is driven partly by the joy of encountering a stronger opponent? In a tournament, that delight produces wonderful competition. With King Piccolo, an enemy who refuses the rules, delight is no longer an adequate principle. The suffering of other people must count.

Goku learns that not through a sermon but through a friend found dead and a world threatened. It is a hard-won piece of maturity, and it does not erase his appetite for adventure.

At the beginning of the arc, the world martial-arts tournament had just taught Tien to care how a victory is won. At the end, Goku has defeated someone for whom victory meant nothing but power over others. The contrast is sharp. One fighter learned to respect a rival; one tyrant could not even imagine a rival as a person.

For Toriyama, the achievement is not that the story has finally become respectable by ceasing to be funny. It is that the same fictional world can make an old joke about a fortune teller, a young man's first honorable win, a friend's murder, a demon king's reign, and a boy's one-handed final attack feel like connected parts of one adventure.

The child with a tail has grown, and the world has grown more dangerous around him. The readers have grown with both.
