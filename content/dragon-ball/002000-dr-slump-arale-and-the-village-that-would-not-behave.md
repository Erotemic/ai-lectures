# 002000 — Dr. Slump: Arale and the Village That Would Not Behave

**Track:** Dragon Ball: From Penguin Village to the End of Z. **Audience:** Fans and curious general listeners. **Prerequisites:** None. **Scope:** The premise, characters, comic construction and cultural history of Akira Toriyama's *Dr. Slump* (1980–1984) and its first television adaptation. **Spoilers:** Major recurring character premises and the identity of Dr. Mashirito; a brief description of Arale's later appearance in early *Dragon Ball*. # Dr. Slump: Arale and the Village That Would Not Behave

Somewhere in Penguin Village, a scientist is trying to persuade the world that his new invention is an ordinary little girl. This is a reasonable ambition, if by reasonable you mean something only a very intelligent fool could think was possible. The girl needs glasses. She is so strong that the normal scale of everyday life is useless to her. She is inquisitive, enthusiastic, and almost entirely innocent of the little agreements by which adults pretend to be in control. Her name is Arale Norimaki, and the scientist is about to discover that building a person is much easier than living with one.

The question in the first installment of *Dr. Slump* is not whether the machine will work. It does work, spectacularly. The question is whether the world can survive having it work.

This is an excellent joke about invention. Fiction is full of scientists who dream of creating life and then face terrible consequences because they have crossed a forbidden boundary. Their creations are misunderstood, isolated or frightening, and the scientist must pay for the sin of arrogance. Toriyama takes the same premise and makes a comic about a man who has engineered himself into a permanent state of inconvenience. There is no thunderstorm of moral reckoning. There is Arale, delighted with the world and perfectly willing to go see more of it.

You can feel the central joke in the mismatch between her appearance and her capabilities. Small body. Round glasses. An expression of delighted curiosity. An engine of destruction so powerful that the usual distinction between large things and small things ceases to apply. She can do damage without intending harm and defeat a threat without understanding why it needed defeating.

If you only know Akira Toriyama for *Dragon Ball Z*, it can be difficult to understand why he spent years with a character whose strength had no levels to climb and no ultimate enemy to overcome. But *Dr. Slump* was not a lesser kind of *Dragon Ball*. It was Toriyama's first enormous success, and it reveals the almost inexhaustible range of his talent for making a cartoon world misbehave.

## The inventor is not the star

Senbei Norimaki is an inventor of astonishing ability and unreliable judgment. The title, *Dr. Slump*, points toward him. The premise allows him to construct things that would transform civilization if he lived in a story interested in transforming civilization. But he does not. He lives in a comedy, and his creations are far better at complicating a person's afternoon than at making him wise.

The doctor's first major invention is his triumph and his humiliation: Arale. She is not introduced as a monstrous machine secretly plotting against humans. She looks like a child, speaks with the enthusiasm of a child, and approaches the world with a child's impatience to try everything at once. The doctor wants everyone to think she is normal. We immediately understand that this plan requires Arale to cooperate in a very complicated deception, and Arale is the last person we would trust to behave according to anyone else's complicated deception.

The glasses are an especially wonderful detail. This is a superhumanly capable robot who cannot see properly without assistance. The limitation is not an action-adventure weakness, the little flaw that a clever enemy can exploit to win the final fight. It is a character joke. The robot has been built by an alleged genius, and she still needs to visit the ordinary world for a basic accommodation. She can outperform a machine or a warrior at their own task while needing what a human child needs simply to look around.

That design makes Arale sympathetic without the familiar science-fiction question of whether a robot has earned the right to be treated as a person. The comedy acts as though the question has already been answered by the fact that she has become a member of the village. People have to make room for her, not because a philosophical debate has concluded but because she is there. Her personality is not a defective imitation of humanity. It is what the rest of Penguin Village is forced to reckon with.

In the early chapters, Senbei tries to present this situation as evidence of his genius. His creator's vanity is a wonderfully vulnerable target. He can build extraordinary technology, but he cannot arrange the world so it will admire him in the proper way. The more Arale flourishes, the more the inventor has to adjust to the fact that he is a supporting character in the life of his creation.

That is not just my interpretation of how the story reads. It is built into the history of the comic. Toriyama originally imagined Senbei as the protagonist. His editor, Kazuhiko Torishima, saw that Arale could carry the series. They disagreed about it, and Torishima later recalled a wager concerning reader response. He says he won. Toriyama retained the title, which is why we still call a comic centered on Arale *Dr. Slump*.

You could not wish for a better historical example of a creative idea escaping its initial plan. The inventor loses the spotlight inside the fiction; the cartoonist's initial title character loses it outside. Neither defeat is a catastrophe. Both make the comic better.

## A village with room for anything

Penguin Village is not a rigorously designed futuristic city. It is a place where ordinary buildings and impossible inhabitants seem to have signed a treaty. There are schools, households, neighbors, animals, visitors, strange machines and occasional invaders who appear to have arrived in the wrong genre. A reader can accept a perfectly ordinary errand as the start of an extraordinary event, because the location permits both to exist.

There is an important difference between a world that has no rules and a world that has comic rules. Penguin Village belongs to the second category. The adult characters possess expectations: respect the community, go to school, behave sensibly, avoid embarrassment, act as though danger is proportional to size. The joke usually works by showing one of those expectations colliding with a personality, an invention or an interloper who does not understand it.

Arale is an ideal instrument for this. She has almost no instinct for preserving adult dignity. She is not deliberately cruel, and she is not conducting an experiment in social rebellion. She wants to do things. If she sees something interesting, she investigates it. If she hears a claim, she may accept it literally. If an obstacle is in front of her, her physical capacities make it very difficult for the obstacle to remain there.

Because she is so strong, Toriyama does not need to spend a great deal of time manufacturing suspense over her safety. That freedom lets him concentrate on reactions. What does a scheming adult do when a cheerful child does not understand the scheme? What happens when an invading alien expects its victims to be frightened, and discovers that the victim is more interested in the new arrival than in the invasion? What becomes of the proud inventor when the most exciting person in the room is a child he does not fully control?

The supporting characters form a rich network of comic expectations. Some are foolish, some vain, some lustful, some heroic only in their own imaginations. They can be schoolchildren or aliens or outright parodies. The same reader may encounter a sophisticated gadget and a deliberately childish drawing of excrement. The coexistence is part of the work's personality. A cartoonist who can draw a very convincing machine is also the cartoonist who knows that an impeccably rendered machine may be funniest when its operator is behaving like an idiot.

It is worth paying attention to the names. Senbei and Arale both refer, in different ways, to kinds of Japanese rice crackers. Their surname, Norimaki, points toward seaweed-wrapped food. Even the family naming feels like a gag someone found too enjoyable to throw away. English readers can miss that layer because translation often leaves names untouched. The ordinary sound of a name in one language can hide a small absurdity in another, and *Dr. Slump* is full of that pleasure in the sound of words.

The villagers themselves are designed with similar generosity. A face can have the proportions of a mascot, a few simple marks can establish a temperament, and a ridiculous outfit can tell a little story about the wearer. Toriyama is economical without being plain. He may draw a vehicle with great attention to detail while reducing a human reaction to a single startled expression. He knows that visual density and visual simplicity are tools to be alternated, not rival philosophies.

The result is a world you remember even when you cannot remember the plot of a particular chapter. *Dr. Slump* is full of events, but Penguin Village is what gathers them into something that feels like a series.

## When power is funny

One of the pleasures of rereading *Dr. Slump* after *Dragon Ball* is noticing just how early Toriyama's fascination with strength appears, and just how differently he uses it.

A conventional action hero begins with limited ability. The action plot gives that hero an opponent who requires more ability, then a mentor, a hardship, a breakthrough, and the pleasure of victory. You can call this a growth story. The rules of strength matter because the reader is asked to anticipate whether the next opponent can be beaten.

Arale is a terrible candidate for such a structure. She begins on the wrong end of the scale. Her strength is funny precisely because there is no meaningful contest between her and most of what she encounters. She may outrun an extraordinary vehicle, send an enemy flying, or do something physically outrageous with the casual air of a child trying a new game. The question is not whether she can win. The question is what the absurd imbalance will do to the people who thought they understood the situation.

This is the difference between strength as suspense and strength as punctuation. In an action story, a powerful attack can be the reward for a long sequence of preparation. In a gag manga, an overwhelming feat can terminate a scene as decisively as a punch line. The defeated opponent may have spent pages establishing a reputation. Arale can erase that reputation in the time it takes to react to her.

But this is not a world with no emotional content. The comedy needs you to like the people whose routines are being overturned. Arale is not an impersonal explosion. She has a recognizable way of greeting the world, a delight in physical sensation, and a capacity for friendship that makes it easy to forgive her obliviousness. When she disrupts the village, she is disrupting a place that has become home to her, not smashing scenery for its own sake.

This is where the comparison with Goku becomes productive. In the original *Dragon Ball*, Goku is ignorant of ordinary conventions, physically astonishing, and often completely sincere. These traits sound familiar. But the structure around him changes their use. Goku eventually wants to test himself against people stronger than he is, and the story gives that desire a shape. Arale has no equivalent requirement. Her adventures do not depend on the promise that she will someday surpass a rival. The gag and the character are already complete enough to generate new situations.

Toriyama learned how entertaining a powerful innocent could be before he built an adventure around a powerful innocent who could grow.

## The respectable men are the worst at being respectable

It would be a mistake to picture *Dr. Slump* as a world inhabited only by sweet children and friendly animals. Its humor frequently takes aim at the adults, and not all of the jokes wear well.

Senbei is capable of inventions far more sophisticated than most people could imagine. He is also vain, embarrassing, frequently distracted by romantic and sexual fantasies, and sometimes deeply unappealing in his behavior. The contrast between intellectual achievement and social foolishness is fertile comic ground. The doctor may know how to build a robot and be incapable of conducting an ordinary human conversation without humiliating himself.

At its best, this is an egalitarian sort of nonsense. Expertise gives you no guaranteed authority over anyone else's life. An official, a superhero, an inventor, a powerful visitor: all can discover that their position has less influence in Penguin Village than they imagined. Pretensions of dignity make excellent targets, especially when the person with the greatest force in the scene is too innocent to recognize an impressive title.

At other times, the original work leans on voyeurism, leering adult humor, crude bodily jokes or gender assumptions that a contemporary listener may not find charming. We do not need to pretend the series is innocent of these habits because its central character often is. Nor must we spend the whole essay issuing verdicts about jokes made in a particular magazine culture decades ago. The artistic fact is that the manga's freedom includes pleasures that travel well and others that become more difficult to share across time and audience.

It is also a mistake to think that the scatological jokes and broad gags exist because Toriyama lacked technical sophistication. The artist who makes an exquisitely composed machine deliberately puts that competence beside something childish. He likes the collision. The polished and the vulgar are not alternating phases of development. They inhabit the same page.

Then there is Dr. Mashirito, whose relationship to the real history of *Dr. Slump* is almost too fitting to be true.

Mashirito is an evil scientist who treats Arale as a problem to be eliminated and becomes obsessed with inventing a mechanical answer. He is capable, egotistical and repeatedly drawn back into a rivalry that has little to do with improving his own life. His name and appearance are based on Toriyama's editor, Kazuhiko Torishima; the official *Dragon Ball* site states this explicitly. Torishima also recalled, with considerable amusement, that when he told Toriyama to imagine someone truly unpleasant, the artist responded with a character who looked like him. He says the manuscript arrived too late for him to insist on a different design.

There is no need to imagine that their collaboration was a secret war of hatred. The prank is more interesting as evidence of a working relationship in which criticism and retaliation could become part of the comic. The editor pushes the creator to make the villain more memorable. The creator draws the editor as the villain. Readers love the villain. The editor has helped make himself successful as an object of ridicule.

Mashirito's rival constructions can be technically impressive. One of them, the robot Obotchaman, is much more than a mere weapon in a plot against Arale. He becomes an innocent personality with his own relationship to her. Another invention escapes its maker's intentions. That is the recurring victory of *Dr. Slump*: the comic imagination is more interesting than the inventor's plan for controlling it.

## How a weekly comic becomes a cultural presence

*Dr. Slump* started its magazine run in 1980 and finished in 1984, filling eighteen original collected volumes. The television adaptation began in 1981. Toei's catalog describes a 243-episode animated series, an enormous afterlife for a story built around short comic eruptions. Arale became recognizable far beyond readers who could recite a particular chapter. Her round glasses, lively expression and distinctive greetings could evoke the whole work.

There is a practical lesson in this success. A serialized gag manga does not always have a cliffhanger or a carefully escalating villain to bring readers back. It has something else: a world in which readers enjoy spending time. The characters become familiar, and familiarity changes how a joke lands. A new arrival may be funny on its own; a familiar character's reaction makes it funnier because the reader knows how desperately that person wants to avoid looking foolish.

For Toriyama, the same structure could become exhausting. Torishima later recalled the relentless pressure to invent fresh, self-contained material every week. An ongoing story can sometimes postpone resolution by moving toward the next confrontation; a gag manga must deliver the pleasure in the current installment. The explanation is Torishima's retrospective assessment, but the formal distinction is real. Every kind of serial storytelling borrows time from the future in different ways.

The anime introduced Arale and Penguin Village to a much wider audience and showed that Toriyama's visual inventions could travel into motion, music and voice. Adaptation also created a new artistic problem: how do you translate a comic's sudden panel-to-panel joke into an animated sequence that unfolds over seconds? A comic can force you to turn a page before you discover what a character has done. Television can use timing, sound and performance, but it must arrange the joke differently. A gag that takes one second to read does not automatically fill the time needed by a weekly broadcast.

Torishima later expressed dissatisfaction with aspects of how the first *Dr. Slump* adaptation was managed and described changes he pursued for *Dragon Ball*. That is useful production testimony, not a reason to tell generations of viewers they were wrong to love the anime. The manga and the television series reached people in different ways. Their different strengths are part of the history.

Arale was also a character who could cross from one of Toriyama's worlds into another without requiring an elaborate explanation. When Goku's early adventures take him to Penguin Village during the Red Ribbon Army story, he meets her. The official franchise account highlights a delightful comparison: she can keep pace with his flying cloud by running, and she disposes of a threat with spectacular ease. Goku's response is essentially to recognize a good martial-arts prospect. Arale's response is not to begin a training arc.

That small encounter is a meeting between two narrative systems. Goku lives in a world where an opponent can be stronger or weaker and that difference can define an adventure. Arale lives in a world where the stronger-looking opponent can be a joke waiting to happen. Their friendship is easy because both are cheerful, capable children; their fictional purposes are different.

The cameo is not just a nostalgic wink. It reminds us that the man who could draw the escalation of a serious fight could also step outside that seriousness at any moment, and enjoy being a cartoonist again.

## The future that was already there

What did *Dr. Slump* give to *Dragon Ball*?

The least interesting answer is a checklist: big eyes, compact cars, strong children, colorful aliens, crude jokes, odd costumes, talking animals. Those things are present, but a list makes the connection seem merely decorative.

The deeper inheritance is a way of making a world available to the reader. When a character arrives, you can understand something important about them before anyone describes their history. When a vehicle appears, it has a shape and a personality. When the story needs a surprise, the author has the confidence to create a new person or an object that changes the scene. Physical comedy is precise enough to become physical action. The apparent limits of a character are often set up so that the next event can reveal how little those limits meant.

And perhaps most important, the world is not embarrassed to be silly. The future of Goku will contain intense fights, losses, longing, sacrifices, and villains who can destroy whole planets. Those events can be moving and frightening. But if the series had to renounce the childish freedom of its earliest creator's work in order to become grand, it would lose part of its particular appeal. A tremendous battle can still make room for an absurd expression. A great villain may have a name that sounds like food. An extraordinary warrior may think first about dinner.

Arale does not need to be recast as the secret prototype of every *Dragon Ball* hero. She deserves her own comic, with its own rhythms and peculiar pleasures. The point of starting with her is not to subordinate *Dr. Slump* to a later success. It is to see how much of Toriyama's artistic imagination was already present before the first Dragon Ball was collected.

Picture Penguin Village one more time. An inventor has built the impossible and cannot persuade his creation to act normal. A tiny girl runs through a world in which every respectable adult is only one badly timed encounter away from becoming the punch line. The village survives. The invention continues to surprise its maker. And the reader has a reason to come back, because another ordinary day is about to begin.

There is no need for Arale to become stronger. She has already made the world large enough for anything to happen.
