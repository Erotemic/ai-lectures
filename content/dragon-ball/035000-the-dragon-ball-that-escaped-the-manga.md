# 035000 — The Dragon Ball That Escaped the Manga: Films, Games, and Other Lives

**Track:** Dragon Ball: From Penguin Village to the End of Z. **Audience:** Fans and interested newcomers. **Prerequisites:** The original Dragon Ball and Z premise are introduced; knowledge of individual films or games is not required. **Spoilers:** Original manga ending, the Bardock television special, the 1993 Broly film, and their later reinterpretations. **Scope:** The 1980s and 1990s theatrical and television offshoots, major approaches to Dragon Ball video games, selected international broadcast histories, and the difference between a finished story and an enduring franchise. **Perspective:** These works should be taken seriously as works, without pretending they all fit one continuity or that a large commercial audience demonstrates artistic merit.

# The Dragon Ball That Escaped the Manga: Films, Games, and Other Lives

There is a moment in an old Dragon Ball Z television special when the story seems to be moving toward the worst possible ending. A Saiyan named Bardock learns that Frieza intends to destroy his people. Bardock is Goku's father, but this is not a warm portrait of a family man separated from his child. He is a soldier in the machinery of conquest, accustomed to the same violence that is about to consume him.

He tries to warn the other Saiyans. They will not listen. He fights his way toward Frieza's ship, desperate and outmatched, and makes his last attack beneath an enormous threat that the audience knows he cannot stop. The planet is destroyed. The boy who will someday defeat Frieza has already been sent elsewhere.

This story is not an installment of Toriyama's original manga published as a complete chapter-by-chapter account of Bardock's last days. It is the 1990 television special commonly called *Bardock: The Father of Goku*. Its tragedy belongs to the television franchise that had grown around the manga. And yet for many fans, Bardock became indispensable to their understanding of Goku long before later works revisited his origin.

That is a useful starting point for thinking about Dragon Ball's life beyond its central story. A supporting figure can become the protagonist of a film. A villain can become more famous through repeated movie appearances than through anything the manga originally did. A video game can let players prevent a defeat, create a fighter, or face a hero against an opponent he never met. A dubbing studio can give a local audience a set of voices that become more familiar than those of the original broadcast.

By the time Toriyama's manga ended in 1995, Dragon Ball already existed in many forms. The point is not to collect them all and somehow fit them into a single timeline. The more interesting question is what kinds of experience these other forms make possible, and what they risk losing when they turn a long story into an assortment of reusable images.

## A story has a direction; a franchise has a repertoire

The original manga moves forward. Goku meets Bulma, learns from Roshi, finds friends, confronts Piccolo, discovers his Saiyan origins, fights Frieza, and eventually reaches the world of Majin Buu. Characters change. A ridiculous childhood encounter can gain meaning decades later. A moral decision can return in another form. The story accumulates a past.

A film franchise does not have to obey that direction. It can visit an episode in the middle of the chronology and invent a new enemy for the heroes to face. It can build a spectacular confrontation around an idea that would require months of weekly serialization if it were developed in the manga. It can make a successful villain return because audiences want to see that design and hear that voice again.

That freedom is both the reason the movies are pleasurable and the reason continuity arguments become endless. Some films include combinations of characters, transformations, and circumstances that are difficult to reconcile with the surrounding manga events. You can spend hours asking exactly where they belong, only to discover that you have replaced the movie's dramatic question with the puzzle of fitting it between two already overcrowded weeks in another story.

It is usually more productive to ask what the film is trying to do as a film. Is it a horror story, a chase, a comedy, a showcase of villain design, a compressed adventure, or an excuse to stage a battle on a scale the weekly show rarely manages? Does the short format help? Does the compression turn the characters into a series of familiar poses? The film can succeed or fail on those terms even when it cannot be inserted into a tidy chronology.

Toei was already producing animated Dragon Ball theatrical adventures during the original television era. Later Z films continued throughout the early 1990s, and their release history ran alongside the television series and the ongoing manga. The 1990s movie catalog contains more creative variety than the phrase “non-canon fights” suggests. A theatrical adventure can be an experiment in how Dragon Ball feels when its familiar ingredients are rearranged for a different duration and viewing occasion.

The important boundary is attribution. Toriyama's work is the source of the franchise, and he contributed designs or ideas to some offshoots. That does not mean every film plot, every animated scene, or every later reinterpretation expresses what he secretly intended when he drew the original manga. Screenwriters, directors, animators, producers, actors, and musicians made the other works. Giving them credit is more accurate and more interesting than stretching one creator's authorship across every derivative product.

## Bardock: a tragedy at the edge of a comedy

The Bardock special is an instructive case because it makes a different promise from the main Dragon Ball story. Goku repeatedly meets people who can be turned into friends, rivals, or allies. The possibility of growth is one of the manga's most comforting commitments. Bardock's story begins in a system where such a transformation is not guaranteed and has little time to occur.

His comrades are participants in conquest. Frieza's empire exploits their strength and disposes of them. Bardock's opposition is driven by the realization that the authority they served has marked them for destruction. He is not presented as a boy who learns kindness by meeting Bulma. He is a fighter who awakens too late to the structure of his own exploitation.

The special also uses the audience's knowledge in a cruel way. Goku will survive and one day fight Frieza. Bardock does not know the shape of that future in the way the viewer does, even though the special gives him disturbing glimpses. The audience waits for a victory it already knows cannot arrive in this episode. Tragedy and foreknowledge replace the manga's usual suspense about whether strength can be increased enough to win.

There is a reason a story like this can attach itself powerfully to the main canon without being the main canon. It changes how a familiar image feels. Goku's encounter with Frieza is no longer only about a hero and a tyrant on Namek. The enemy once destroyed the hero's entire people. The television special supplies a face and a last failed attack for that loss. You can remember the scene when watching Goku, whether or not the continuity of every later account agrees.

Later works revisited Bardock's character and circumstances. They did not merely reveal pages that had been hidden inside the 1990 production. Some are retellings or reinterpretations with different emphases. A critical history should let the old special remain its own work, with its own bleak tone, instead of editing its meaning to match the newest version.

## Broly and the power of a figure who will not stay put

Broly offers a different kind of franchise life. The original 1993 Z theatrical film, internationally known as *Broly – The Legendary Super Saiyan*, creates an overwhelming opponent whose design can communicate danger before he moves. There is the scale of his body, the contrast between forms, the force of his entrance, and the promise that ordinary martial-arts exchanges may not be enough to answer him.

This is the attraction of Broly in concentrated form: the image of a fight that has become too large for the established scale of fighting. The movie's plot and character motivation can be criticized, and they should be. A fearsome design does not automatically produce a persuasive personality. But the spectacle is not an insignificant achievement. Dragon Ball has always understood that the body of an opponent can make an argument before anyone delivers a speech. Broly takes that technique to an extreme.

The character returned in additional Z movies, and later a different cinematic treatment arrived in *Dragon Ball Super: Broly* in 2018. The later version is not evidence that the earlier film's characterization secretly meant something else. It is a new work with a new understanding of what Broly might be. Comparing the two reveals how a franchise can retain a dramatic silhouette while revising the story attached to it.

The contrast also reveals the peculiar power of animation and character merchandise. A fighter's design can be portable. It can become a toy, a selectable game character, a poster image, an animation challenge, and a recognizable silhouette that crosses languages. The design can survive much longer than a viewer's memory of the exact plot that introduced it.

That portability has a cost. A character originally associated with a particular fear, tragedy, or moral situation can become shorthand for a strength tier. The name becomes a promise of a certain kind of battle rather than a person in a specific story. Fans may enjoy that promise. It is also possible to miss what made the character more than a collection of attacks. Broly's several incarnations make that tension visible.

## A movie changes the length of an adventure

Toriyama's manga often earns its climaxes by delaying them. Goku trains. An opponent demonstrates an unexpected ability. Side characters discover that the situation is worse than anticipated. Strategies fail and are revised. Time is spent letting the audience understand why the conflict is difficult.

A theatrical film is compressed. It needs to establish a setting, introduce its opponent, assemble the characters, escalate danger, and deliver a satisfying confrontation within a relatively short running time. That encourages immediately recognizable silhouettes and clear conflicts. A returning audience already knows the cast, so the film can spend less time introducing them. The strength of the format is its economy; the weakness is how quickly familiar heroes can become merely the people who take turns losing before Goku arrives.

Consider a movie built around a returning villain such as Cooler, or a spectacular otherworldly threat such as Janemba in *Fusion Reborn*. These figures can work extremely well as visual and tonal inventions. Their stories do not always have room to give them the slow, disturbing accumulation that Frieza or Cell receives in the main narrative. The movie may give you a wonderful fight without an equally rich reason to care about its outcome.

A different film can use the short form to highlight emotion. The television special about Future Trunks, for example, draws on the alternate future in which Gohan teaches a desperate young fighter while a devastated Earth is hunted by Androids. It adds a concentrated atmosphere of helplessness and responsibility to a future that the main manga approaches from another angle. Its relationship to the manga's own Trunks material is that of an adaptation and expansion, not an extra uninterrupted chapter quietly inserted into the main story.

The best of these offshoots are not trying to beat the central manga at its own length. They find what a specific premise can do in the time available. The weaker ones mistake recognition for a substitute for storytelling. And a fan can love a spectacular fight while admitting that the narrative around it is little more than an excuse for the fight.

## When the spectator becomes the fighter

Video games reveal an even greater change in the relationship between Dragon Ball and its audience. In the manga, we watch Goku make a decision. In a fighting game, we can make a decision for him, or for Vegeta, Piccolo, Frieza, Krillin, or a character whose battle was never possible in the printed story.

The earliest pleasure is straightforward. You press a button and something that used to belong to an animated fighter happens because you chose it. The dramatic beam, the sudden dash, the movement into the air, the transformation: these are no longer only events to witness. They are actions to perform.

That changes the meaning of strength. A character may be invincible in the story and perfectly beatable in a game. Competitive fairness may require abilities to be limited, simplified, or balanced. A game that tried to obey every claim about power would risk becoming a miserable game in which the strongest character dominates by definition. So the game builds a new rulebook. It must represent the feeling of Dragon Ball without reproducing all its fictional arithmetic.

The PlayStation 2 *Budokai* fighting-game series introduced a generation of players to this translation of recognizable techniques into a playable system. Its success also demonstrates how much pleasure lies in choosing a character and learning a move set, rather than simply reenacting a favorite narrative result. The closely associated but mechanically different *Budokai Tenkaichi* series, known as *Sparking!* in Japan, emphasized the arena's scale, high-speed movement, and enormous cast of playable figures.

A roster can make an argument about what fans value. A character who appears briefly in a special or movie can stand beside the central heroes. A costume, a transformation, or an almost forgotten technique can become a reason for someone to pick one fighter over another. The playable franchise expands sideways, away from the chronological story and toward the pleasures of collection, experimentation, and impossible matchups.

This is often where a person encounters a character before seeing the movie or episode that introduced them. A fighting game may teach a child Broly's name or Bardock's face and only later send them searching for the story. In that case the supposed supplementary product has become the entry point into the original work.

## Different games discover different Dragon Balls

Not all Dragon Ball games are primarily about recreating a match. *Dragon Ball FighterZ*, released in 2018, uses an intentionally stylized fighting-game presentation to produce the illusion of animated action with the responsiveness demanded by competitive play. The challenge is not to let an impossible power hierarchy decide every outcome. It is to create a readable contest in which spectacular gestures remain under the player's control.

*Dragon Ball Xenoverse* and its successors find a different pleasure. Instead of choosing only from an established cast, players make a character and intervene in historical battles. The events that were fixed in the manga become situations that can be defended, repaired, or threatened. The fantasy is no longer simply “I am Goku.” It is “I have entered a story that I know and can matter inside it.”

*Dragon Ball Z: Kakarot* takes still another direction, organizing a game around living through important events and places from the story. Its appeal is not only in achieving victory. It is in lingering within a world that manga and television sometimes traverse quickly: visiting locations, traveling between conflicts, and inhabiting the intervals around legendary fights. The same material that a fighter compresses into a selection screen becomes a landscape to explore.

And the more recent *Sparking! ZERO* returns to the appeal of the vast 3D arena and the improbable battle between familiar combatants. These games do not all compete to be the final, definitive translation of Dragon Ball. Each emphasizes a different desire in the audience: to master the moves, to control the spectacle, to participate in history, to live in the world, or to rearrange the cast.

There is also an instructive contradiction. The original Dragon Ball repeatedly argues that training is meaningful because Goku loses, learns, and tries again. A video game can turn that dramatic structure into literal practice. You lose a match. You discover a timing problem. You revise your approach. You become better by repetition. In that small sense, the player's experience may be closer to Roshi's lessons than a perfect reproduction of Goku's latest transformation would be.

Of course, a game can also reduce Dragon Ball to a catalogue of purchasable forms, moves, or costumes. The commercial incentive to keep adding material can turn every narrative surprise into an item. A series whose best moments depend on unexpected change now has to manufacture more changes indefinitely. That is not necessarily the player's preferred experience, even when the collection is appealing.

## The series travels through different gates

The circulation of Dragon Ball outside Japan was never a single worldwide premiere. Manga editions, television licensing, dubbed broadcasts, home video, films, and games reached places on different schedules. A viewer in Mexico could have encountered a recognizable Goku through a Spanish-language television performance while a viewer in the United States was still meeting a different edited English release. A French or Brazilian fan may have an entirely different history of familiar theme songs, remembered names, and local performers.

This makes “the international version” a misleading phrase. There are many international versions, and the histories of France, Spain, Mexico, Brazil, Italy, and English-speaking countries cannot be folded into one generic overseas audience. A particular broadcast schedule affects which transformation is remembered as an event. A dubbing cast affects the personality of a character. A local publisher's edition affects whether the reader experiences the manga as one continuous work or as separate titles.

The North American case is instructive but not universal. The early U.S. dubbed Z episodes arrived through syndication, and Toonami later helped make the show widely visible on Cartoon Network. Games and home video then gave viewers new ways to revisit or catch up. That history is important, especially for the audience that grew up with it. It should not be mistaken for the day Dragon Ball began influencing the rest of the world.

Latin American audiences have their own strong relationships to the material, reflected in the continuing importance of their dubbing casts and the availability of regional releases. Contemporary official distribution makes that plurality easy to see: the same Dragon Ball streaming catalog can advertise Japanese, English, Latin American Spanish, and Brazilian Portuguese tracks. The franchise has become something viewers can recognize across languages without agreeing about a single definitive sound.

The broader commercial system is equally real. Publishers and rights holders license films, figures, trading cards, games, broadcasts, and other products. Bandai Namco's corporate reporting explicitly describes Dragon Ball as a franchise whose manga and animation support coordinated worldwide products and services. That can keep a story available across generations. It can also push every beloved character toward permanent market visibility, regardless of whether another story about that character is needed.

Global reach therefore deserves neither automatic celebration nor automatic disdain. More people can encounter Toriyama's art. More translators, actors, musicians, programmers, and illustrators can bring their own craft to the world. Fans can discover friends through a shared vocabulary of attacks and characters. At the same time, the franchise can become so familiar as branding that newcomers meet the hair, the aura, and the marketable transformation before they meet the mischievous boy whose first adventure was with Bulma.

## What survives when the original story ends?

There is a powerful temptation to judge every Dragon Ball product by whether it is canon. The word seems to settle everything in a single move: the manga is real, the others are not. But canon can answer only a restricted kind of question. It can help establish what happened in a particular continuity. It cannot tell us whether a film is moving, whether a voice performance is good, whether a fighting game captures the speed of Toriyama's panels, or whether a viewer's attachment to an invented villain is somehow counterfeit.

The opposite temptation is to declare that popularity settles every argument. Broly is famous, therefore every Broly film must be excellent. A game has a large roster, therefore it must understand the characters. Dragon Ball is an enormous global franchise, therefore every new product proves that the original story was perfect. None of that follows. Economic reach and artistic achievement should be discussed separately, even when one helps sustain the other.

A better approach is to ask what each medium makes available. A manga can make an impossible blow feel instantaneous through one panel turn. Television can make the same blow last in music and performance. A film can put a whole new threat around a concentrated spectacle. A fighting game can let you discover what mastery feels like through repeated failure. A role-playing game can make a fictional place feel like somewhere you have spent time. Dubbing can let a character take on an emotional identity within another language.

These are genuine forms of creation. They are not equally successful, and they do not all carry equal authority about the original manga. But their existence tells us why the franchise could outgrow the calendar of its original serialization. Dragon Ball had more than a plot. It had a world that people wanted to enter, a design language easy to recognize, a cast built on rivalry and attachment, and a set of impossible actions that invited imitation.

You can see the result in the gap between the Bardock special and a later game. In the special, a soldier cannot prevent the destruction of his people. The tragedy depends on the limits of what he can change. In a game, a player may take control of Bardock, try the attack again, and perhaps win a battle the story insists he lost. The pleasure comes from precisely the boundary the original work imposed. We want to touch the lost possibility.

That does not invalidate the tragedy. If anything, it shows how deeply the tragic image has entered the shared imagination. We remember the planet, the doomed attack, the child sent away, and the enemy who will one day be defeated. We can replay the battle because we know the story cannot really be undone.

## The boy behind the icon

There is a final cost of success that Dragon Ball makes especially clear. A character can become so recognizable that people stop seeing his origins. Goku's hair, orange clothing, and impossible energy attack are icons. The boy who once wondered about a girl's strange possessions, learned from a turtle hermit, lost tournaments, and traveled through a world full of impractical machines is easier to overlook.

That is why the franchise's many versions are worth studying alongside the original manga rather than in place of it. A Bardock special can turn Frieza's evil into family history. A Broly movie can make an imposing design into a cultural memory. A game can teach the grammar of a fight through the hands of the player. A dubbing performance can become a permanent part of someone's sense of childhood. Those are real achievements, some greater than others. They do not replace the artistry that made the world desirable in the first place.

The best later works understand this. They know that Dragon Ball is not merely a list of power levels or a series of louder explosions. It is a collection of characters whose strengths can be foolishness, stubbornness, kindness, pride, curiosity, fear, and the willingness to keep learning. A game that makes defeat instructive, or a film that gives a neglected character a compelling choice, can carry forward that deeper grammar even when its plot lies outside the manga.

The weakest later works remember the icon and forget the person. They reproduce the power-up and assume the audience will supply the meaning from an older story. Sometimes we do. Nostalgia is generous. But generosity has limits, and a new work still has to earn our attention.

Toriyama's manga had an ending. The television series had endings. Individual films and games had release dates, credits, constraints, and conclusions. The franchise, however, became a repertoire: a set of stories, movements, designs, voices, and possibilities that people in different countries could continually rearrange.

That is why the fallen Saiyan in the Bardock special still has a place in the imagination of somebody who first met him in a game. The character is both a tragic figure with a particular story and a figure whose form can travel into others. A continuity chart cannot contain both experiences, but a good history of Dragon Ball should.

The boy with the tail set off looking for seven magic objects. He became the center of something much larger than a quest. The remarkable thing is not that the story acquired more merchandise, more movies, and more games. It is that, at its best, those other forms keep finding new ways to make people want to enter the world he discovered.
