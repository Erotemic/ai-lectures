# 030000 — How Toriyama Draws a Punch

**Track:** Dragon Ball: From Penguin Village to the End of Z. **Audience:** Fans and interested newcomers. **Prerequisites:** Familiarity with Goku, the tournaments, Vegeta and Cell helps; the essay explains its examples. **Spoilers:** The first three World Martial Arts Tournaments, the Saiyan and Namek battles, and the Cell Games. **Scope:** Toriyama's original black-and-white *Dragon Ball* manga, with limited comparisons to television. **Perspective:** Reading direction, bodies in space, timing, force, comedy, and the difference between a clear action drawing and a merely complicated one.

# How Toriyama Draws a Punch

Imagine trying to explain a fight to somebody who can see only one still picture at a time. You can choose the pictures. You can decide how large they are, how close together they sit, and what part of the action each one contains. But you cannot move the people between pictures. You cannot play the sound of a foot hitting the floor. You cannot order the reader to spend three seconds on a face before turning to the next one.

And yet a good Dragon Ball fight has speed. It can make an opponent feel impossibly fast, an impact heavy, a recovery desperate. In some of the best exchanges, you can tell which way a fighter is moving before you have quite registered what the fighter is doing. This is a remarkable achievement for marks printed on paper.

When readers talk about Akira Toriyama's fight scenes, they often praise the drawing as fluid. That is a useful impression, but it names the effect rather than the method. Paper has no fluid motion. What Toriyama can control is the arrangement of successive changes. A punch is prepared, displaced, delivered, received, and answered. Even when the action seems instantaneous, the page offers the reader enough evidence to reconstruct an interval of time.

The result is a collaboration. Toriyama draws selected instants. The reader supplies the movement. When that collaboration works, a complicated battle feels easy to follow. When it fails, an elaborate action scene can turn into a collection of people pointing their arms through clouds of dust.

I want to understand why the former happens so often in Dragon Ball. We will start in a tournament arena, follow the story into the air and across entire planets, and eventually return to the simplest problem in action drawing: how to make one person hit another person while the reader knows exactly what happened.

## The arena teaches the eye

The first Tenkaichi Budokai is an excellent place to study Toriyama because it gives the fights a floor.

A floor may seem a modest technical innovation in a story about a boy who can fire energy from his hands. But the tournament has a platform, recognizable edges, spectators, and an agreed direction of travel. Fighters begin on the same stage. They can be knocked toward a boundary. They can jump back into the space. Victory and defeat are visible as changes in position, not only as declarations of strength.

Picture Goku confronting Jackie Chun, the mysterious elderly participant who is really Master Roshi in disguise. The tournament has already trained us to read feints, departures, and returns. When one fighter comes forward and the other yields ground, we do not need a narrator to announce whose initiative has changed. Toriyama can show feet, torsos, the space behind a body, and an edge that suddenly seems too close.

Roshi's disguise adds another layer. The audience knows more than Goku. The combat is also a lesson: the old master is trying to keep the boy from believing that he has reached the top of the world. A shift in visual advantage can therefore carry two meanings at once. It tells us who is winning the current exchange, and it tells us what the older man is teaching.

The fight also has a wonderful relationship with scale. A small motion may be enough to demonstrate superiority. A Kamehameha is spectacular partly because it arrives in a sequence where ordinary blows and evasions still have shape. An immense blast would mean less if the surrounding panels were already filled with equally immense visual noise.

The Dragon Ball Official Site's retrospective of the original magazine serialization singles out the panel arrangement during Goku and Jackie Chun's beam struggle. That is a useful starting point for looking at the actual printed pages. Two fighters stand opposed; the energies create a visible line of conflict; their positions remain intelligible even as ordinary hand-to-hand distance becomes irrelevant. The scene can grow impossible without becoming geographically confusing.

The crucial achievement is not just that Toriyama can draw a beam. It is that the reader knows where the beam came from, what resists it, and what would happen if the balance broke. The shapes create a little diagram of a contest, dressed as an astonishing event.

This is why a tournament is such a powerful training ground for an action cartoonist. It gives the artist recurring constraints. We learn what a win looks like. Later, Toriyama can violate that stability, break the ring, remove the ground, or let fighters move so fast that the ordinary boundaries cease to help. He has first taught us what the boundaries were.

## The missing instant

A surprising amount of good fighting occurs between panels.

Suppose a character is balanced on one foot in the first image. In the next image an opponent is airborne, having been struck. A third image shows the first fighter turning away. Not every muscle movement has been shown. The reader must infer a kick. If the drawings establish the positions clearly, that inference is almost effortless. We feel the speed precisely because we were not required to watch the kick unfold in twelve intermediate steps.

Toriyama repeatedly exploits this economy. He does not always show a projectile's entire path or a fighter's full change of direction. Instead, he selects what is needed to orient us, then trusts us to join the points. When a hero disappears from one position and appears at an opponent's flank, a reaction drawing can be more useful than another portrait of the hero moving through the air. The opponent's expression announces how impossible the maneuver has felt.

That reaction is one of the manga's most efficient devices. A face turned toward empty space, a body bracing too late, a head snapping to follow something already gone: these are drawings of movement that the page does not actually contain. They are also drawings of judgment. A fighter who believed the situation was under control discovers that it was not.

Consider the early tournaments again. Goku is not frightening because the narration keeps reminding us that he is strong. He becomes frightening when experienced fighters are surprised by a movement that the reader can reconstruct faster than they can respond. Toriyama lets our eyes briefly outrun a character's mind. That is a special pleasure available to sequential art.

The risk, of course, is losing orientation. If every transition simply teleports fighters to a new point in a featureless void, speed stops feeling impressive and starts feeling arbitrary. A useful manga artist must decide when the reader needs a landmark and when the reader can do without one.

For much of Dragon Ball, Toriyama has a strong instinct for this distinction. When a fight leaves an arena, a rock face, a crater, a tower, a road, a bend in the ground, or the angle of a distant figure can restore our bearings. The landmark does not need to be detailed. It must only tell us how the space connects to the last space we saw.

This also explains the value of blank paper. Negative space is not a failure to draw the landscape. A mostly empty background isolates the body, shows its direction, and asks the reader to attend to the change. The landscape can return when its presence becomes useful again. A dense background in every panel would compete with a moving silhouette for attention.

The reader's eye is limited. Toriyama's pages are often generous enough to acknowledge that fact.

## An action line is not the action

Manga has a vocabulary of speed lines, impact marks, stretched expressions, and shattered surfaces. Toriyama uses it, sometimes extravagantly. But none of those conventions is a substitute for a leg extending from a hip or a shoulder rotating behind a fist.

Try to imagine a comic in which every character is surrounded by lines pointing in every direction. You might conclude that everyone is moving very quickly. You would have less success deciding toward what, away from whom, or with what result. Motion marks become persuasive when they support the positions and intentions of bodies.

Toriyama's fighters frequently have strong silhouettes. Goku's hair and compact stance, Piccolo's angular cape and broad shoulders, Vegeta's sharply contained posture, Cell's patterned body and tail: these designs help us recognize a person instantly even at a difficult angle. In battle, recognition is a practical service. You are not reading a police lineup. You are trying to understand an exchange that can turn on one motion.

A silhouette can do more than identify a character. It can express their relation to the moment. A body compressed before a leap is different from one extended in flight. A fighter leaning into a blow is different from one thrown backward by it. A character drawn small against a vast cliff may look vulnerable even when their numerical power has not changed.

By the time the Saiyans arrive, combat can take place above the ground, through rock, and over distances that would have seemed absurd at the first tournament. The series has gradually loosened its physical frame. That makes Toriyama's ability to reestablish positions even more important.

The early battle against Vegeta is instructive because it is not one clean series of exchanges between equal opponents. Goku strains his body with Kaiō-ken. Vegeta changes the terms of the contest by transforming into a giant ape. Gohan, Krillin, and Yajirobe become relevant in different ways, from different positions, at different moments. The battle changes scale repeatedly without losing the narrative question of who can reach whom.

The giant ape provides an especially obvious visual contrast. A huge body should not automatically make a fight more interesting. It can make the choreography cumbersome. Toriyama uses the difference in scale to create new problems: small figures must avoid the giant's reach, find openings, and attack in ways that no longer resemble a tournament exchange. Our eyes compare the little figures with the overwhelming one. The proportions themselves communicate danger.

The best action drawing does not merely make power visible. It makes a change in power change what the characters must do.

## The comedy has not left the page

A lecture about Toriyama's action drawing would be incomplete if it treated his early comedy as the thing he outgrew.

The artist of Dragon Ball is also the artist of Dr. Slump, where absurd physical events and mechanical contraptions can be jokes in themselves. Even in his more severe battles, Toriyama retains an eye for the indignity of the body. Someone looks fierce and gets knocked flat. An important speech is interrupted. A character takes a pose that is impressive right up until its result becomes silly. A fighter's face can suddenly remind us that under the cosmic energy and impossible strength, they are a person who did not expect to be kicked in the stomach.

Comedy and action share a dependence on timing. A joke often needs preparation, expectation, and a reversal. So does a good feint. The reader sees the setup, predicts the next image, and discovers that the prediction was wrong. The difference may be whether the consequence is laughter or danger.

Remember the tournament habits: a confident opponent makes a judgment about Goku, and Goku does something that renders the judgment useless. This can produce a laugh and a demonstration of ability simultaneously. The character is revealed not only through what they can do but through how absurdly their opponent has misread them.

The same technique survives when the opponents stop being funny. In the Namek story, Vegeta's deception, surprise attacks, and sudden reversals depend on information that different parties possess or lack. A villain's confidence can be punctured by an unexpected movement; a hero's confidence can be exposed as similarly misplaced. The page has to make the reversal legible at the moment it arrives.

Sometimes the visual joke is the scale of an overreaction. A tiny gesture is met by an explosion of embarrassment. Sometimes the body itself becomes a punch line, as when a dramatically overbuilt pose cannot produce the hoped-for speed. Dragon Ball's later fascination with expanded muscles and proud declarations of superiority preserves something of the gag cartoonist's ear for the ridiculous.

Even the most solemn fights can become wooden if every image insists on solemnity. Toriyama's changing facial expressions, abrupt shifts in emphasis, and willingness to let a character look foolish allow an extended fight to breathe. The comic artist remains present inside the action artist.

## When being stronger is the wrong way to move

The Android and Cell material offers one of the clearest examples of drawing as storytelling argument.

During the training toward the Cell Games, the Saiyans discover ways to push the Super Saiyan body beyond its familiar form. The swollen muscles of a more extreme grade are visually striking. The figure announces extraordinary strength, but the body also looks harder to move. An impressive static shape becomes a tactical problem. Trunks can display power and still discover that power does not make him an effective opponent for Cell.

In prose, this is easy to state: strength without speed is insufficient. In a comic, the artist can make the distinction visible before the explanation arrives. Compare a heavy, expanded figure with a relatively composed opponent. The page asks which body seems capable of changing direction. When the fight proves the point, the drawings and the story agree.

This is a good moment to resist the temptation to convert every visual choice into a hidden technical theorem. The manga is not offering a biomechanics paper. It is dramatizing a common intuition about combat: a fighter must be able to land the blow. Toriyama exaggerates bodies until the intuition becomes almost comically obvious.

That ability to turn an argument into a shape is central to the craft. A character design should not just announce a new merchandise-ready appearance. At its best, it tells us what a fighter values, misunderstands, or has finally learned.

The Cell Games then return the cast to a contest with a ring, after years of battles in wild terrain and on dying planets. But the return is unstable. Cell has the ability to destroy the ring and make the contest mean something else. The shift is a visual announcement that the conventions of the tournament cannot contain the situation.

In the climax, the conflict between Gohan and Cell is organized around more than two beams of energy. It also depends on the distance between an injured young fighter and the people trying to help him. Vegeta's intervention and Goku's encouragement give the last exchange an emotional geography. We understand what each contribution costs, and why Gohan must finish the encounter himself.

The extraordinary blast at the center of the page has its force because the story has spent time arranging the people around it.

## A manga page is not a storyboard

It is tempting to praise Toriyama by saying that his drawings already look like animation. They do invite motion. But a manga page is not simply an unfinished cartoon waiting for a camera and soundtrack.

The reader can look back. A spread can be taken in at once and then inspected image by image. A large panel can dominate the page without requiring it to last a certain number of seconds. Two drawings separated by a narrow gutter can suggest a transition that occurs faster than speech. A character's face may stop the reader for an emotional reason no animator could specify with complete control.

The artist can also choose when a movement is absent. Toriyama sometimes skips the intermediate action and leaves only the consequence. In animation, the studio must make a new decision: should it show the missing motion, imply it with a cut, fill the interval with a held pose, or use sound and reactions to create its own rhythm? Each decision can succeed, but none is identical to the page.

In a television episode the director may prolong a stare before a fight, or allow music to carry an approach. That can be exciting. It can also dilute an abrupt page turn whose power came from the fact that it arrived so quickly. Conversely, animation can make an impact feel tremendous through timing, voice, and a sudden cessation of music. Calling one version better without naming the scene and the effect usually evades the interesting question.

Toriyama himself described changing his drawing style as the story changed. In an interview published in the 1995 *Daizenshuu 1* art collection, available in translation, he explained that the increasingly prominent fights called for more simplified, angular strokes than the rounder approach of Dr. Slump. We should not convert that recollection into a complete theory of every panel. It does confirm that the changing line is not merely a fan's imagined pattern.

Look at early Goku beside later Goku. The rounded, compact boy in a world of bulbous cars and comic animals becomes a fighter drawn with sharper facial planes and stronger directional lines. The style has not lost its playfulness, but a reader can feel that the artist has shifted the weight of his attention. The fights occupy a different percentage of the page, and their momentum increasingly organizes the story.

This evolution makes the manga worth reading in sequence rather than sampling famous still images. Toriyama learns what sort of movement his world demands while gradually teaching the reader how to follow it.

## The experiment of reading slowly

Here is a way to revisit a Dragon Ball fight without reducing it to nostalgia. Choose a sequence you remember for its animation or for the outcome of the battle. Read the printed manga slowly, not to make it less exciting but to see what you habitually overlook.

At the beginning of each exchange, work out where both bodies are. Then notice what the next panel asks you to infer. Is the missing action clear from the consequences? Is the background doing any work? Are you being directed toward an impact, a reaction, a new landmark, or a change in the fighter's confidence? When a panel feels enormous, is it enormous because it contains detail, or because it makes one change impossible to miss?

A revealing comparison is the first tournament beam contest with a late, planet-threatening confrontation. Both use an imaginary force, but the early scene relies on a stable arena while the later one has to establish danger with far less ordinary geography. The same artist has a larger vocabulary, and a harder problem.

Another revealing comparison is Goku's childhood battles with the fight against Vegeta. In the first, the comic often takes pleasure in a small fighter surprising a bigger one. In the second, the scale changes inside the fight itself, and victory passes from one person's strength to several people's contributions. The drawings now have to track a team across a battlefield without making everyone seem interchangeable.

Or return to Cell, where speed and mass are explicitly in conflict. Ask why the bloated body reads as a failure even before the narration explains it. The page can carry a physical intuition while the characters debate its meaning.

These are not secret codes hidden by an author for devoted fans to decipher. They are the ordinary grammar through which action becomes readable. Toriyama is memorable partly because that grammar so often disappears from our awareness. We are not thinking, what an effective change of scale. We are thinking, how is Goku going to survive this?

And that may be the highest compliment to a fight drawing. It can be technically controlled without feeling calculated, economical without feeling empty, exaggerated without becoming incoherent.

A good Dragon Ball punch is not the fist. It is the relationship between the body that throws it, the body that discovers it has been thrown, the space that lets us understand the difference, and the missing instant our imagination supplies. On the printed page, a single impact can be a joke, a defeat, a lesson, or the end of a friendship.

The paper never moved. We did.
