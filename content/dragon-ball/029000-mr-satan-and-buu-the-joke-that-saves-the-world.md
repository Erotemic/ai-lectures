# 029000 — Mr. Satan and Buu: The Joke That Saves the World

**Track:** Dragon Ball: From Penguin Village to the End of Z. **Audience:** Fans and interested newcomers. **Prerequisites:** Explains Mr. Satan's reputation after Cell and the premise of Majin Buu. **Spoilers:** The Cell Games, Mr. Satan's encounter with the good Majin Buu, the gunmen and Bee, the evil Buu split, and the final defeat of Pure Buu. **Scope:** Toriyama's original *Dragon Ball* manga, especially volumes 40 and 42, with specific cautions about anime-expanded domestic scenes. **Perspective:** Celebrity, fraud, courage, the ethics of friendship, public trust and the surprising place of ordinary people in a series about superhuman strength.

# Mr. Satan and Buu: The Joke That Saves the World

The fate of the Earth has come to depend upon a man who is very good at pretending to save it.

His name is Mr. Satan, the reigning martial-arts celebrity and, according to a substantial portion of the public, the champion who defeated Cell. We know better. We watched Gohan do the fighting. We watched the powerful warriors deal with a threat that Mr. Satan could scarcely comprehend. We saw the champion explain away evidence that should have shattered his reputation. He is a magnificent comic fraud.

And now he is standing before Majin Buu.

This is an opponent who turns people into sweets, survives violence that would destroy a planet, and sometimes behaves with the excited innocence of someone encountering a toy. The world's strongest fighters have learned to fear him. Mr. Satan arrives with the confidence of a man professionally obliged to be seen having confidence. He has neither the power to win a duel nor a convincing way to escape the expectations attached to his name.

What happens next is one of Toriyama's best decisions. Instead of merely humiliating the fraud, he allows him to become genuinely important.

The mechanism is almost offensively simple. Mr. Satan talks to Buu. He spends time with him. He watches the monster become attached to a puppy. And for a while he persuades the creature to stop killing people.

Later, when the planet's strongest fighters cannot finish the final battle, the public figure whom readers have spent years laughing at persuades ordinary people to give them what they need.

To see why these scenes work, we need to revisit the joke that created Mr. Satan in the first place—and ask what it means when a false reputation becomes a real responsibility.

## A champion of the world, if you believe the announcer

Mr. Satan enters Dragon Ball during the Cell Games, when Cell invites the world to watch a tournament that will settle the fate of humanity. Cell's ambitions are apocalyptic. The public spectacle around them, however, still belongs to the recognizable world of professional sports: cameras, commentators, excited crowds and a champion familiar from the ordinary martial-arts circuit.

The champion is theatrical. He boasts. He performs confidence as if confidence itself were a combat technique. From the point of view of the audience at home, he seems entirely plausible as the representative of human strength. He is large, loud, muscular and self-assured. He has the look of a man who expects to be photographed at the exact moment the crowd starts cheering.

The reader knows how ridiculous this is. We have followed Goku, Gohan, Piccolo, Vegeta and the others through battles that have exceeded ordinary human understanding many times over. A familiar sporting champion is entering a contest in which the units of measurement have already broken down.

Toriyama draws a brilliant comedy from the mismatch. Mr. Satan is ridiculous because we know the answer to a question he doesn't understand. What the public calls a great fighter belongs to one category; what Cell is belongs to another. The champion's explanations and bravado are an attempt to keep the categories from colliding.

After Gohan defeats Cell, Mr. Satan takes the credit. In a story of cosmic warriors, this is a spectacularly small human act: a man preserves his brand. And in another sense it is a large one, because the crowd believes him. The popular account of who saved the world becomes detached from the actual event.

There is an obvious satire of celebrity here. An audience sees what its media can show and what its familiar institutions can interpret. A being capable of destroying the planet is difficult to place in the usual language of sports. A famous fighter claiming victory is easy to understand. The fraud succeeds not because everyone is stupid, but because a ready-made story arrives before an accurate one does.

As a gag, this could have remained a throwaway. Every time an informed fighter enters the room, Mr. Satan's stature looks absurd. Every time an ordinary citizen calls him the world's savior, the gap between public reputation and actual history becomes funny again.

But Toriyama doesn't throw the character away. He returns, with the same reputation, at the moment when the Earth's survival requires a different kind of ability.

## A coward's courage

It is tempting to call Mr. Satan a coward and stop there. He is often terrified of things that are genuinely terrifying. He tries to avoid dangerous contests when he can, invents explanations when he cannot, and makes a living presenting himself as fearless. These are not admirable qualities.

But cowardice is not the same thing as the complete absence of courage. A character who is genuinely afraid has more room to surprise us by acting anyway.

When Mr. Satan agrees to confront Buu, he arrives under pressure from the public image he has built. His plan is not a noble hand-to-hand battle. It relies on deception and tricks. That makes the first phase of the encounter comic: the world's celebrated savior facing a monster who doesn't behave according to the assumptions behind the champion's performance.

Yet there is also an important asymmetry. Mr. Satan understands a great deal more about ordinary human weakness than Buu does. He knows people lie. He knows how fear works. He knows how to win attention and maintain the impression of control. Those skills are morally ambiguous, but they are skills, and they have mostly been absent from the latest series of fights between beings who can destroy landscapes with their hands.

Buu has immense force and very little social knowledge. He has been used by cruel masters and behaves as if the things they taught him are ordinary rules for living. He does not possess the moral vocabulary that the adults around him take for granted. He seems to understand amusement much more readily than harm.

That doesn't excuse the people he has killed. The deaths in the story remain deaths. But it does make a different response possible. Mr. Satan can ask what Buu believes he is doing, and the answer may reveal a capacity to learn.

The champion who cannot win a fight discovers an opponent with whom a conversation might have an effect.

## The dog that isn't afraid

One of the most affecting episodes in the Buu storyline begins with something very small: a puppy with an injured leg.

Buu finds the animal. It is hurt and vulnerable. He heals it with the same impossible power that has made him so dangerous. The puppy does not flee from the monster's appearance. It follows him. He receives a response he does not seem to have expected. A creature smaller than he is behaves as if being near him is a good thing.

Mr. Satan observes the relationship. The dog is later called Bee. For readers who have seen worlds threatened, towers destroyed and warriors resurrected, the attention given to a puppy is a remarkable change of scale.

It is also precise storytelling. Most of Buu's previous interactions have involved controlling, threatening, hurting, consuming or being commanded. Even his pleasures have been solitary. The puppy offers a different pattern. Buu performs a helpful action. The animal responds through attachment. Nothing about the exchange requires Buu to demonstrate that he is stronger than the dog. Strength is irrelevant to the relationship except as the ability to heal.

The scene makes kindness observable. A character is not changed by a speech about the abstract value of life. He encounters a living creature whose response contradicts his experience of the world. Then Mr. Satan explains, as a person might explain something simple to a child, that killing is wrong and that people can be friends.

This moral simplicity can sound embarrassing if paraphrased as a lesson. The scene itself has more dignity because it remains anchored in what Buu does. He sees the dog; he heals it; it stays. He learns that a life can become a source of affection rather than an obstacle or a snack.

Mr. Satan sees the change. His own motives start to shift. He has arrived looking for a way to remove the threat, preferably without having to fight it. But his time with Buu gives him a reason to care about the being in front of him. His knowledge of deception no longer exhausts what he can offer.

We can read this as a comic's very hopeful claim: some forms of cruelty are sustained by ignorance, and being given a different experience can interrupt them. The claim does not cover every villain in Dragon Ball, or every real-world situation. Frieza cannot be talked out of his empire by handing him a puppy. The point is that Buu is not Frieza. Treating two dangerous beings as morally identical would be a refusal to look at what the story actually shows.

## What friendship does that strength cannot

Mr. Satan's importance becomes easier to see if we compare his efforts with the heroics already attempted. Vegeta has poured his entire life into an attack that fails to destroy Buu. Goku has revealed a spectacular transformation, Super Saiyan 3, and still has to return to the afterlife. Goten and Trunks are working toward Fusion; Gohan is being prepared for another extraordinary increase in power.

These efforts are not pointless. The fighters need strength and technique to protect the world. But the assumption behind them is that Buu can be treated as a physical problem: find the right attack, the right form, the right level of energy, and the threat will be removed.

Mr. Satan is asking a different question. What if one of the creature's habits can change?

There is no comparable transformation sequence for his success. He does not grow golden hair or develop a new aura. He remains a man whose body is vulnerable to ordinary weapons. The shift is relational: two beings begin sharing time, food, amusement and an animal they both care about.

This is especially funny because of who Mr. Satan is. A comic fraud famous for claiming victories over monsters discovers that a genuine achievement may be invisible to the audience that loves him. He cannot show off a defeated enemy at his feet. The achievement, while it lasts, is a monster deciding not to hurt people.

It would be easy for Dragon Ball to make Mr. Satan suddenly strong, revealing a hidden martial-arts mastery that put his career in a flattering light. Toriyama does something much better. He leaves him physically outclassed. The character becomes useful by acting in a capacity the heroes have overlooked.

We should also be careful with a common simplification: Mr. Satan does not permanently tame every version of Buu, nor does his friendship solve every danger alone. The Buu storyline is interested in division and transformation. The discovery of kindness opens a possibility; it does not repeal the capacity for violence that has already been established.

That is why the interruption that follows is so devastating.

## The gunman

The danger reenters this small domestic world through people with guns. Their power is negligible beside Buu's, yet they are capable of cruelty. One of them shoots the puppy. The ordinary fragility that made Bee's friendship meaningful now makes the scene frightening. In a world where mountains can be destroyed, a bullet is still sufficient to injure a small animal.

Mr. Satan responds with anger. He is more than capable of fighting these ordinary men, and his outrage is real. Buu heals the dog. For a moment it appears that the relationship has survived the intrusion.

The gunman returns and shoots Mr. Satan. Buu can heal his friend too, but the emotional shock has a different effect. His anger grows beyond his ability to contain it, and an evil counterpart is expelled from his body. That darker being overpowers and consumes the gentler one, producing the more threatening form commonly called Super Buu.

The transformation is spectacular, but its cause is painfully small. The monster's divided nature is triggered by an attack against the friends he has finally learned to love.

There is a grim irony here. An ordinary human being's willingness to hurt for pleasure wrecks a fragile peace between a supposedly monstrous creature and the people around him. The story refuses a comforting division in which humans are always civilized and monsters are always wicked. The men with guns are not able to destroy a universe. Their power is modest. Their choices are terrible.

The scene's emotional logic works because Bee and Mr. Satan have been allowed to matter as individuals. Without the time spent on the relationship, Buu's rage would be merely a plot device for introducing a new form. With the relationship, the transformation resembles the destruction of a hard-won possibility. A creature that had begun to learn gentleness suffers a wound it cannot process without splitting apart.

The television adaptation expands some of the household comedy around these events. A listener remembering long domestic scenes with Buu and Mr. Satan may remember an experience that occupies more time than the corresponding manga pages. The essential sequence—friendship, injury, rage, separation—belongs to the original story. The extra television time can make the peaceful interval more tangible; it can also change the rhythm of the return to catastrophe.

## The champion who cannot save his friend by punching

From this point forward, Mr. Satan's role is difficult to categorize. He is not the protagonist of the cosmic battle. The fighters continue to invent new ways to confront the changing monster. But neither is he merely comic relief, standing to the side and misunderstanding every event. His attachment to the good Buu is a fact that can affect the decisions of others.

That matters during the final crisis. The story has made one part of Buu capable of friendship. When the monstrous forms change again and the good Buu survives apart from the purely destructive one, the moral problem is no longer simply to erase everything that shares the name. A living being who was implicated in terrible harm has also learned to choose differently.

Mr. Satan cares about that individual. In a universe where names and transformations can overwhelm identity, his devotion is almost stubbornly particular. He doesn't love an abstract category called Majin Buu. He loves the creature who cared for Bee and sat with him in a house, the creature with whom he has shared a strange, frightened companionship.

This is a form of discernment. The strongest warriors excel at determining an opponent's energy, speed, and technique. Mr. Satan has discovered something about a creature's capacity for relationship. He knows it because he was there.

It is worth asking whether Dragon Ball has made that knowledge available to its central heroes in the same way. Goku is frequently generous to enemies and rivals. Piccolo has been changed by raising Gohan. Vegeta has been changed by living with Bulma and Trunks. The theme is not alien to the series. But Mr. Satan brings it into sharper focus because he has almost no alternative. He cannot make friendship the second step after defeating an enemy. Friendship is his only workable approach.

In that sense, he is a peculiar fulfillment of one of Dragon Ball's oldest patterns: an opponent becomes a companion, and the story grows because of the change. Yamcha, Tien, Piccolo and Vegeta have all occupied variations of this pattern. Mr. Satan and Buu push it into a setting where the conventional confrontation would be absurd.

## The problem with a hero's reputation

There remains the question of public trust. Mr. Satan did not earn his fame in the way the public believes. His reputation is based on self-promotion and a false account of Cell's defeat. Ordinarily, we would expect a story interested in truth to expose him and move on.

Dragon Ball takes a more playful route. The reputation becomes both an accusation against him and a tool that can save people. When Vegeta comes up with a plan to gather enough energy from Earth's inhabitants to defeat the final Buu, the inhabitants have to be persuaded to share it. They cannot see Goku's battle in person. They do not have a detailed understanding of the fighters, the energy being collected, or the stakes in the realm of the Supreme Kai. They have learned to be suspicious of unexplained voices demanding their cooperation.

A collection of the world's greatest warriors cannot simply issue a technical instruction and expect the population to obey. The task is social. Someone has to make the request credible.

That someone is Mr. Satan.

For years, the audience has believed that he is its protector. The people recognize his voice. They know the story attached to his name. The very fame that the reader found ridiculous becomes a way to reach ordinary people in a moment when they need to act together.

This is not a simple endorsement of propaganda or celebrity power. The trust is founded on an inaccurate story, and the manga makes no effort to pretend otherwise. Its comedy depends on our remembering the discrepancy. But the story explores what happens when a reputation outlives the deception that created it. A person can be held to the responsibilities of a role he entered dishonestly.

Mr. Satan, faced with the choice, uses his influence for the genuine survival of the people who believe in him.

There is an almost fairy-tale inversion here. The strongest fighter has a task that only the crowd can help him perform. The man who cannot fight the monster knows how to reach the crowd. His great weapon is not a secret technique but being believed.

## A victory with many owners

The final Genki-Dama, or Spirit Bomb in many English versions, is one of Dragon Ball's grandest displays of strength. But its design is different from the straightforward escalation of an individual body. Goku gathers energy willingly contributed by others. Vegeta works to make the plan possible. The Dragon Balls help restore people and their world. The ordinary inhabitants contribute. Mr. Satan makes them listen.

The attack dramatizes dependence. Goku's individual ability is necessary, and it is not sufficient. Vegeta's planning is necessary, and it is not sufficient. The population's energy matters, but the population would not know what to do without a trusted voice. The final blow belongs to a structure of cooperation.

That structure also redistributes credit. The public may continue to celebrate Mr. Satan as if he were the singular champion. The reader knows the truth is messier. Yet the truth now includes a real contribution by the man who previously took credit for someone else's. The joke has not been erased. It has matured.

A comic can get a surprising amount of emotional force from this arrangement. We have spent hundreds of chapters learning to recognize power through bodies: who stands, who falls, whose posture changes after an injury, whose aura erupts across a page. Now the story asks us to recognize a different sort of power. A familiar voice can bring millions of people into action. A relationship with a puppy can interrupt a monster's habits. A father can choose to protect a child instead of proving himself superior to a rival.

The scale of the final attack is cosmic. The behaviors that make it possible are not.

## The joke grows up

Mr. Satan is never required to become an entirely different person before the story will reward him with significance. He remains theatrical, vain, frightened and capable of self-serving behavior. That continuity is important. A comedy character does not have to stop being funny in order to become moving. Indeed, some of the movement comes from watching the same habits operate under conditions for which they were never designed.

His courage is not the absence of fear; it is action taken while fear remains visible. His honesty is incomplete; his public reputation never becomes a perfectly accurate record. His friendship is not based on superior wisdom; he is surprised to discover that it works. He makes the decent decision with the limited tools he actually possesses.

That is a more generous and more interesting portrait than either a complete fraud or a hidden saint. Mr. Satan has participated in a lie about his greatest achievement, and he has also performed an achievement that the most powerful people in the world could not perform. Both facts belong to him.

The lesson is not that kindness can always defeat a violent enemy. Dragon Ball itself provides too much contrary evidence for that. Sometimes the monster remains monstrous. Sometimes the friendship is broken. Sometimes a friend with extraordinary healing power cannot prevent the catastrophe produced by rage. The final fight with Pure Buu still requires force, persistence and an attack on an astronomical scale.

The more precise observation is that force and care solve different problems. The fighters can stop a destructive body. Mr. Satan can give one version of that body a reason to stop harming. In the final battle, he can help ordinary people see themselves as participants rather than spectators.

When Goku eventually meets Uub, the reincarnation of the pure Buu, the series again imagines a future in which terrible power enters a relationship of teaching rather than destruction. That hope was always present in Dragon Ball: a defeated rival might become a companion, and a frightening stranger might become a student. Mr. Satan and Buu have given the hope its strangest and perhaps most ordinary expression.

A little dog follows someone who has helped it. A man who cannot win a fight sits with an enemy. A crowd believes a voice it recognizes and lends its strength to people it will never meet.

The world is saved by a battle. It is also saved by the things people do when they stop treating every encounter as a battle.

Mr. Satan, of all people, turns out to have something to teach the heroes.
