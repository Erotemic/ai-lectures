# 021000 — Namek: The Greatest Game of Keep-Away

**Track:** Dragon Ball: From Penguin Village to the End of Z. **Audience:** Fans and interested newcomers. **Prerequisites:** None; the Saiyan invasion is introduced in context. **Spoilers:** The Namek expedition, the Ginyu Force, wishes made on Namek, and the beginning of the battle with Frieza. **Scope:** Original manga, approximately Tales 243–296, with selective observations about its television adaptation. **Perspective:** Story structure, visual suspense, comedy, and the ethics of who gets to make a wish.

# Namek: The Greatest Game of Keep-Away

Imagine having crossed interstellar space in a borrowed spaceship, reached a planet you have never seen, and discovered that the strongest enemy in the universe has arrived first. You have two fighters, neither remotely powerful enough to beat him, and an inventor who was not expecting to spend her vacation in a war zone. You know that the planet holds the only objects capable of bringing your dead friends home. Unfortunately, the enemy knows that too.

This sounds like the beginning of a very long fight. It is instead the beginning of an astonishingly good game of keep-away.

The Namek adventure is often remembered by its destination. Frieza, the tyrant in the small chair. Goku, arriving with a new kind of strength. The first Super Saiyan. A planet about to explode. Those images deserve their reputation, but they can obscure how much pleasure comes before them. For a remarkable stretch of the manga, Toriyama builds suspense out of who knows where a Dragon Ball is, who has enough power to take it, who can hide without being detected, and who is prepared to help somebody else when helping may expose their position.

The crucial word is *position*. Namek is a conflict about position before it becomes a conflict about power. Where is the nearest village? Which direction did that energy reading come from? Can the soldiers see us? Can that child get away? How many of the seven balls does the enemy possess now, and can you reach one before he does? One fighter may be terrifyingly stronger than another, yet find himself helpless because a single ball is at the bottom of a lake and no one will tell him where.

That reversal is the distinctive pleasure of Namek. Dragon Ball has spent years making the fighter who is stronger the fighter who usually wins. Here Toriyama finds another answer: the stronger fighter may not be able to get what he wants at all.

## A trip made possible by loss

To appreciate the machinery, begin with the reason for the trip. The Saiyan invasion has left Earth's defenders battered, and several friends are dead. More seriously, Piccolo has died. Piccolo's life is bound to Kami's, and Kami's death means Earth's Dragon Balls have disappeared. The usual way out of a tragedy has been destroyed by the tragedy itself.

This is a good piece of fantasy plotting. A resurrection device is useful to the writer only while it has conditions. With no conditions, it erases consequences. With too many arbitrary conditions, it becomes the writer's permission slip for anything. Here the rule is simple, already connected to established characters, and severe enough to require a genuine expedition. The search for another set of Dragon Balls is not a whim. It is what the surviving characters can do for their dead.

And who goes? Not Goku, at least not initially. The expedition belongs to Bulma, Krillin, and Gohan.

There is something pleasingly old-fashioned about that group. In the early days of Dragon Ball, Bulma's radar and knowledge of vehicles made her central to the story. She could not knock a mountain down, but the adventure would not have happened without her. By the Saiyan invasion, Goku and Piccolo have become fighters who can reshape landscapes. Namek restores some of the balance. Bulma helps make the journey possible. Kami's old spacecraft, concealed on Earth since his childhood, provides a way to cross the distance; Mr. Popo's knowledge and Bulma's skill help put the machine into human hands. Later, Goku will travel in a very different ship, adapted by Bulma's father, with extraordinary training equipment inside. For the first voyage, however, the important thing is that someone remembers how to operate a machine and someone is willing to go.

Krillin is brave, experienced, and painfully aware of what would happen if he attempted to fight the wrong enemy. Gohan is a child who has already seen too much. He has tremendous potential, but he cannot make that potential appear whenever it is convenient. Bulma is inventive and impulsive, and she has good reason to be furious that these adventures keep putting her life in the hands of people who can fly away from danger.

Give any one of these three Goku's absolute confidence, and the story becomes worse. Their limitations make the journey into a story of observation. A hero who can win by arriving and throwing a punch changes the question from *what should we do* to *when will he get here*. A frightened child and a pragmatic monk, arriving with a scientist, cannot avoid the first question.

Toriyama also gives Namek a recognizable physical personality. Its landscapes are spare enough to be read quickly: expanses of water, low islands, round trees, clustered dwellings, a wide sky. In an audio essay one cannot reproduce the pages, but it is worth picturing what those uncluttered settings do. A character can see across an enormous distance. A streak of movement overhead is a threat. A small figure on a ridge can be a watcher. To hide, one must use terrain intelligently, not merely disappear into background detail. The scenery has the look of another world, yet it is economical enough to function as the board for a chase.

Then the newcomers discover that the board is already occupied.

## The four incompatible wishes

Frieza has come to Namek seeking eternal life. He commands soldiers, ships, measuring devices, specialists, and a terrifying capacity for violence. When he wants information, his men can search village after village and slaughter anyone who refuses to give it. He is the most powerful player and the least willing to be told no.

Vegeta has come for the Dragon Balls too. His defeat on Earth has not made him modest. If anything, learning about a greater power beyond his own has made immortality more appealing. He also knows Frieza's organization from the inside. He understands the behavior of its officers, the significance of a scouter reading, and how frightening the commander will be if he realizes one of his former subordinates is stealing from him. Vegeta is weaker than Frieza but much better placed to deceive his troops than the Earthlings are.

Bulma, Gohan, and Krillin have a different wish: restore Piccolo and the people who died defending Earth. One can say that this group is searching for Dragon Balls too, and thereby flatten all three parties into competitors. But their motives alter the stakes of every decision. They are not bargaining for personal dominion. They are trying to undo the human cost of an invasion.

And the fourth party is the one least likely to be named in a summary of the arc: the Namekians themselves.

These are not convenient guardians placed beside magic objects for the heroes to interrogate. They are people living in scattered communities. They have families and elders, customs, an explanation for the Dragon Balls, and the obvious desire not to have their homes invaded by murderers. Their possession of the balls is not an obstacle to a treasure hunt; it is their right to make decisions about objects that belong to their world.

This distinction turns the adventure from an elaborate scavenger hunt into a story of consent. Frieza needs the Dragon Balls but believes that the power to take them grants the right to use them. The Earthlings need the same objects but cannot claim that their grief entitles them to brutalize their owners. Vegeta is perfectly capable of doing what Frieza does. For much of the adventure, these differences become visible not through speeches but through the treatment of strangers.

One of the most important early sequences begins with the search of a Namekian village. Frieza's men threaten its inhabitants and seek their Dragon Ball. The elder Muri realizes that the intruders' scouters enable them to find energy signatures, and he destroys the instruments. It is a moment of practical intelligence. He cannot defeat Frieza by force, but he can take away an advantage. The price of the village's resistance is horrific. The soldiers kill, and a young Namekian named Dende is in danger of becoming another victim.

Gohan cannot stay hidden while he watches.

This is an old Dragon Ball trait in new circumstances. Goku's willingness to intervene for someone powerless has always been part of the series' emotional grammar. Now his son reacts before a sensible plan has been made. Gohan strikes at Dodoria; Krillin helps, and the two flee with Dende. It is brave and almost catastrophically dangerous. The rescue exposes them to pursuit, and neither boy has the strength to make the pursuer go away by winning a clean fight.

But rescuing Dende is also the decision that changes the story. The child is not a token awarded for a good deed. He has knowledge of his community and can take his rescuers toward the Great Elder, called Saichoro. The journey begins to have a social network that Frieza does not understand, because Frieza regards the population as material to threaten. Gohan and Krillin gain an ally because they treated someone as a person.

The contrast is sharper if we resist the temptation to turn it into a simplistic moral mechanism. Rescue does not reliably produce rewards in life, and Toriyama is not writing a transaction in which kindness buys access to an elderly wizard. Dende is vulnerable, frightened, and capable of friendship. He chooses to trust the people who protected him. The story's structure makes that trust consequential. Its emotional force depends on us noticing the difference.

## Vegeta discovers the uses of being smaller

For anyone who met Vegeta as the invader who dismissed Nappa and nearly destroyed Earth, his behavior on Namek can be a surprise. He remains brutal. He kills people when doing so helps him get what he wants. Yet the narrative repeatedly puts him in the position of the clever underdog.

Watch how rapidly Toriyama varies the roles. Vegeta defeats a familiar Frieza officer, Cui, and establishes that his ordeal on Earth has changed his strength. Dodoria becomes vulnerable too, and the balance appears to be moving toward the rebellious prince. Then Zarbon changes form. The elegant officer becomes a muscular monster and beats Vegeta badly enough that he would have died without intervention. Frieza wants him alive for questioning, so Zarbon brings him to a medical chamber. The prisoner recovers, escapes, and steals his captor's Dragon Balls.

The sequence has the quality of a good caper. Each reversal grows from information one side possesses and the other side has misjudged. Vegeta assumes that his improved strength is enough, learns otherwise, and uses the enemy's need for information to survive. His opponents understand how to overpower him but not how to keep a cunning captive from exploiting their machinery. It is funny in a grim way that a character whose self-image is built around superior power gets some of his best material by being outmatched.

He begins using geography as a weapon. A Dragon Ball may be concealed in water; another may be carried off while his pursuers chase a false lead. Scouters offer data, but characters who can suppress their energy make that data dangerously incomplete. The episode exposes a contradiction within Frieza's empire. Its soldiers are disciplined enough to carry equipment and obey orders, yet their confidence in measurable power can make them poor observers of people who behave strategically.

There is a terrible limit to how far admiration for Vegeta's ingenuity should go. He also attacks a Namekian village and kills its inhabitants to obtain a Dragon Ball. This is not a rough-edged hero doing what must be done. It is an invader imitating the empire he despises. Later changes in Vegeta make this violence easy to gloss over, especially if one has grown fond of his family life and his rivalry with Goku. But the Namek story is stronger if we remember that the man clever enough to resist Frieza is, at this point, willing to reproduce Frieza's methods.

That is why his reluctant cooperation with Krillin and Gohan is so interesting. The groups do not suddenly discover a shared set of values. They discover a shared problem. An enemy too strong for any of them alone has forced them into the same narrow region of the board. Vegeta can be useful without being good. Krillin can be right to cooperate without pretending the danger of trusting him has disappeared. Gohan can detest Vegeta's cruelty and still understand the immediate threat.

Toriyama had already used the enemy-turned-ally device with Piccolo, but the two cases feel different. Piccolo's relationship with Gohan gradually created attachments that changed him. Vegeta on Namek is not changed by being welcomed. He is cornered into temporary arrangements, and his pride makes every arrangement uncomfortable. A partnership built from reluctant necessity has more dramatic friction than instant friendship would.

## A child, an elder, and a language nobody thought to learn

The Great Elder is one of the quiet successes of this story. He is not a fighter presented for a duel. He is ancient, grieving, and physically limited, yet his presence reframes the quest. The Dragon Balls were created within a community. The people who hold them understand both their promise and their responsibilities.

Krillin's meeting with the elder leads to an extraordinary development: the elder senses his intentions, trusts him, gives him a Dragon Ball, and awakens power dormant within him. Later Gohan receives a similar awakening. Notice how different this is from the scouter's measurement. The machines can detect energy. The elder attends to a person's history and purpose. It would be too tidy to claim that wisdom always beats technology in Dragon Ball. Bulma's technology has made the whole rescue mission possible. But the distinction is dramatically useful. Some of the most important information on Namek cannot be obtained by scanning a body from a distance.

Namek also reminds the Earthlings that knowledge of a device is not the same as knowledge of a culture. The Namekian Dragon Balls do not operate exactly like the ones they knew on Earth. Porunga grants multiple wishes, and summoning and addressing him require knowledge the invaders do not possess. You cannot simply steal the hardware and assume that the local language is irrelevant.

This creates one of the arc's best ironies. Frieza may have enough force to seize all seven balls, but he has no way to compel the dragon to answer an order he does not know how to give. He can bully a village. He can threaten Nail, the Great Elder's guardian. He can command an army. But the object of his campaign remains beyond him because he has mistaken domination for understanding.

The Earthlings benefit from Dende's guidance. Their plan changes as events develop: first bring Piccolo back, which also restores Kami and Earth's Dragon Balls; then bring Piccolo to Namek, where he can help fight. These are not abstract strategic choices. They are attempts to reconnect people who have been separated by death and violence. The names within the wishes carry histories. Piccolo, who once tried to conquer Earth, is now someone they are desperate to save. Gohan trusts him; Krillin needs him; even Vegeta may need his strength. The seven magical objects at the center of the story are still the Dragon Balls, but the actual currency of the story is accumulated relationship.

Then the Great Elder's failing life becomes another clock. The very objects everyone seeks depend on his existence. The plot's cleverness is that the villain cannot solve this problem merely by fighting harder. He can destroy a person, but he cannot command that person's life to last longer. Namek keeps producing questions that strength cannot answer.

## The most overqualified dance troupe in the universe

At about the point when the story threatens to become unrelentingly grim, the Ginyu Force arrives. Five elite soldiers strike poses, compete for the honor of fighting, and behave like a troupe of performers who have somehow been given the power to crush mountains.

This is not comic relief accidentally wandering into the wrong saga. The performance is part of the threat. Frieza's organization has such confidence in its power that its specialists can afford to be ridiculous. Recoome treats a desperate confrontation like an exhibition. Guldo's strange time-related powers make him dangerous despite his comparatively modest strength. The team settles questions of combat assignment with games. The jokes are funny because their victims cannot laugh them off.

Toriyama's earlier gag-cartooning talent is fully visible here. Think of the movement from *Dr. Slump* to the first Dragon Ball tournament: grotesque bodies, pompous announcements, improbable abilities, a visual joke delivered with a perfectly composed silhouette. The Ginyu Force is a late expression of that same imagination, now backed by consequences severe enough to make an outlandish pose frightening.

In the manga, this combination plays especially well through the speed of a reveal. A dramatic arrangement of bodies occupies a panel. A boast follows. A terrible blow proves the boast was not empty. The reader has time to enjoy the pose and then almost no time to react to the violence. In television animation, music, pauses, voice performances, and repeated poses can stretch that contrast into a different sort of spectacle. Neither is automatically superior. The two media create anticipation using different tools.

The Ginyu Force also temporarily ends the possibility that Vegeta can outwit his way through every encounter. Having a hidden Dragon Ball is useful against somebody who lacks the means to find it. Against fighters carrying replacement scouters and moving too fast for the plan to work, the advantage vanishes. When Recoome begins overpowering Vegeta, Krillin, and Gohan, the unlikely coalition is reduced to what the Saiyan invasion taught them: endurance, interruption, and the hope that a stronger ally will arrive.

And at last, Goku does.

He has been training under enormous gravity during his journey, using the spaceship built by Bulma's father. He arrives stronger than the others expect, just as he arrived during the invasion of Earth. But the situations are not copies. On Earth, Goku's late arrival was haunted by friends who had already died. On Namek, his arrival interrupts a desperate contest that has brought together enemies who cannot trust one another. His strength is a relief, yet it also removes one source of suspense. The old pursuit story can no longer remain unchanged when somebody enters who can defeat opponents previously untouchable.

Toriyama solves that problem by making even Goku's body an object that can be stolen. Captain Ginyu's Body Change technique is a wonderfully perverse joke on the logic of training. Goku has acquired his strength through years of practice; Ginyu imagines that the body itself will deliver it. In the borrowed body, he cannot use its abilities as Goku does. Movement, control, experience, the relation between intention and training: they are not portable just because the muscles are. The gag becomes a pointed answer to a question the series keeps asking. What exactly is power made of?

The answer is not a numerical reading. The body's strength is real, but so is the person who learned how to use it. A stranger with the keys to a vehicle is not necessarily its driver.

Ginyu's misadventure ends with an amphibian. It is difficult to imagine a more Toriyama-like punishment for a warrior who believes he can steal greatness by collecting bodies: the proud captain becomes a frog. The joke does not cancel the terrible fight that preceded it. It reminds us what kind of artist is making the fight.

## The treasure hunt runs out of room

As the search collapses into direct confrontation, Namek's earlier strategies continue to affect what follows. Piccolo is revived and brought to the planet. Nail's delaying action against Frieza has bought time for Dende to help the Earthlings use Porunga. Vegeta's efforts have brought the Dragon Balls into play but not secured his immortality. Frieza discovers that being the strongest person present does not mean getting the wish he came for. The Great Elder's death interrupts the remaining opportunity.

At that point the tyrant turns more openly to what he understands: violence. The plot begins to exchange hiding, pursuit, and the control of information for transformations, desperate fights, and the threat of planetary destruction. There is a real change of genre inside the same adventure. The stakes of a small concealed object become the stakes of a whole world.

It is easy, with hindsight, to call the first half of Namek the prelude to the important fight. But that reverses the achievement. The Frieza battle is terrifying partly because the earlier story has established what he has been doing to people who cannot fight him. We have watched children flee his soldiers. We know that villages have been destroyed. We know why the Namekians refuse him, why Vegeta fears him, and why Krillin and Gohan are no longer ordinary treasure hunters. When the story finally removes the possibility of running away, we have learned what being caught means.

What is especially satisfying is that Toriyama never entirely abandons the original comic's premise. A boy and a girl set out years earlier to find seven objects that could grant a wish. Their first journey was full of animals, thieves, robots, monsters, misunderstandings, and sudden friendships. The series has changed almost beyond recognition, but on Namek the action is still driven by the old problem: find the Dragon Balls before somebody else does.

The scale has expanded, not the fundamental curiosity. Who will they meet? Whom will they trust? What can be done with a wish? What are they prepared to risk for another person?

The Namek adventure gives perhaps the fullest answer to those questions in all of Dragon Ball. A single ball hidden out of sight can be more important than a brilliant attack. A child saved from a massacre can change the outcome of a war. An elder who understands people can do something a scouter cannot. A fighter who learns to suppress his energy can defeat the assumptions of an empire. Even a comic dance troupe can become an engine of dread.

And when the strongest being in the region finally holds all seven objects, he still cannot get what he wants.

It is tempting to call that a victory for the weak. It is not yet victory, and the weak have suffered terribly. It is, however, a victory for the story. The terrifying power of Frieza cannot make the world simple. On Namek, it takes intelligence, loyalty, memory, language, patience, and the decision to protect a stranger just to remain in the game.
