# 027000 — The Buu Saga Refuses to Be One Kind of Story

**Track:** Dragon Ball: From Penguin Village to the End of Z. **Audience:** Fans and interested newcomers. **Prerequisites:** The essay supplies its own setup and assumes basic familiarity with Goku, Vegeta and Gohan. **Spoilers:** The entire Majin Buu storyline, including Vegeta's sacrifice, fusion, the destruction of Earth, the final Genki-Dama and Uub. **Scope:** Akira Toriyama's original *Dragon Ball* manga, especially collected volumes 36–42, with brief distinctions from the 1990s television adaptation. **Perspective:** Changes of genre, comedy and horror, shifting protagonists, narrative excess, the final battle and why the saga's unevenness is bound up with its best ideas.

# The Buu Saga Refuses to Be One Kind of Story

For a while, at the beginning of the last great stretch of the original Dragon Ball manga, the strongest teenage boy in the world is trying not to be recognized at school.

Gohan, who has already defeated Cell and saved Earth, is late for class. He has the ordinary problems of an unusually gifted student: people notice him when he does things that other people cannot do, and he would prefer to get through the day without explaining any of them. So he puts on a costume, strikes poses, and attempts to manufacture a second identity. The result is the Great Saiyaman, a superhero whose confidence in his disguise exceeds everyone else's confidence in it.

This is a curious way to resume a manga whose previous major enemy had erased a city. It is also a wonderfully Dragon Ball way to begin. The boy who saved the world has become an embarrassing local crime fighter. A girl named Videl sees through his performance and refuses to let him keep the terms of their acquaintance entirely under his control. School, the city, ordinary spectators and a tournament are reentering the story. Goku is dead, or at least living elsewhere. The series appears ready to discover what comes after the greatest battle of a previous generation.

Then a magician comes looking for enough energy to resurrect a monster, Vegeta tries to recover the person he believes he used to be, a pink creature turns people into confectionery, boys fuse into a single fighter with a spectacularly inflated opinion of himself, a hero is swallowed, the Earth is destroyed, and the ending depends on a professional braggart persuading ordinary people to help.

Calling all of this the Buu saga is convenient, but the name disguises its most conspicuous quality. This stretch of Dragon Ball keeps changing the rules of what sort of story it wants to tell. At times the result is exhilarating. At others it feels as if an ending is approaching, then wandering away with the keys. To understand why readers and viewers can love it and complain about it in the same conversation, we have to take the wandering seriously.

## The strange luxury of beginning again

After Cell, the manga faces a genuine dramatic problem. It has staged an apocalyptic tournament, handed the decisive victory to Gohan, and allowed Goku to remain dead. Even in a world where death need not last forever, that looks like a generational transition. Goku's son has been pushed into the role his father previously occupied.

The transition isn't simply a question of whose power is highest. Gohan is different from Goku. Goku grew up treating training and competition as pleasures so central to his life that one can scarcely separate them from his sense of being alive. Gohan has been trained under desperate circumstances. He has fought brilliantly, suffered terribly, and repeatedly expressed interests that do not fit the role of professional warrior. He likes studying. He likes his family. He is capable of adolescent embarrassment. That gives his high-school story a subject beyond the novelty of seeing him in a helmet.

Saiyaman takes the visual language of the genre and turns it into a joke. His poses imply that heroic intention needs to be announced at enormous volume, even when the audience already knows he can defeat the criminals with hardly any effort. Toriyama's page can move from a neat street scene to a burst of silly theatricality and back again. The enormous gap between Gohan's physical ability and his social composure is the joke. He has inherited extraordinary strength without inheriting his father's ease in carrying it around.

Videl is important because she doesn't meet him as a legend to be worshiped. She has confidence, sharpness, and her own convictions about justice. She can be wrong, and so can he. Their uneasy acquaintance gives the new story an everyday texture. So does Mr. Satan, the man celebrated as Earth's champion despite the audience knowing that Cell's defeat was Gohan's achievement. His fame is a running joke about public knowledge: the world has found a version of events it can understand, and its chosen hero looks rather less impressive if you have seen Gohan in action.

For an interval, Dragon Ball seems interested in how the extraordinary becomes ordinary again. This isn't merely an intermission before the next villain. It opens the possibility that growing up is a separate adventure, with stakes that cannot be expressed by a blast destroying a mountain.

We shouldn't pretend the manga develops an entire school-life series and then abandons a completed project. It doesn't. What we get is a short, lively experiment. But the change in scale is purposeful on the page, whether or not we have a reliable statement from Toriyama about a long-term plan. It reminds us that Dragon Ball's oldest talent was always the ability to turn a familiar situation upside down.

## A tournament full of people who know too much

The twenty-fifth Tenkaichi Budokai returns the story to its most reliable institution. There will be brackets, spectators, an announcer, rules, a crowd, and fighters who ought to be concentrating on the match in front of them. It reunites characters who now live in different places and whose lives have taken very different shapes. Goku receives a day back from the afterlife. Goten and young Trunks enter their own competition, astonishingly strong children who still quarrel and show off like children. Vegeta is present in a family setting as well as a competitive one.

The tournament is one of Toriyama's oldest dramatic inventions. It puts violent possibility inside an agreed frame. A character may fight fiercely without starting a war. A defeat can lead to a new friendship. The event draws serious fighters, frauds, officials, spectators and opportunists into the same place. In the earliest tournaments, it gave the manga a structure and made strength visible through comparison. Here it also becomes a trap.

There is a new divine authority in attendance, the Supreme Kai, whose anxiety should make everyone uneasy. The heroes have long since passed the point where an impressive title guarantees impressive judgment. Yet the Supreme Kai knows something they do not: an ancient being called Majin Buu may be revived, and the energy of powerful fighters can be stolen for that purpose.

The trap operates through the tournament's own ordinary procedures. Videl's brutal match against Spopovich is especially hard to watch because it occurs in the place where a combatant is supposed to be protected by recognizable rules. The difference between a sports spectacle and cruelty becomes stark. Spopovich has unnatural endurance, and Videl is a brave human opponent. She can outmaneuver him, but her skill cannot make this a fair encounter. The story lets the audience feel the inadequacy of the institution it has just welcomed back.

Then Gohan's energy is taken, and the tournament begins to disintegrate into a hunt for an enemy concealed elsewhere. The Supreme Kai's plan relies on risks the audience is invited to question. A character who holds a higher place in the cosmic hierarchy does not necessarily know how to manage the people he has recruited. Dragon Ball has been adding ranks of gods for years, and it remains suspicious of the idea that rank is the same thing as practical wisdom.

Inside Babidi's spacecraft, the heroes behave as if they have walked into another tournament, choosing fighters for successive rooms. Babidi believes he is arranging a carefully calibrated sequence of challenges. Goku and Vegeta behave as if the competition is entertainment. The resulting comedy comes from a breathtaking mismatch of expectations. These men have survived so many stages of cosmic escalation that a wizard's fortress briefly looks like a child's amusement ride.

It is funny right up to the point when it isn't. Their confidence has a cost. The real danger isn't the minor combatants on the ship. It is a relationship between two of the heroes that Babidi can exploit.

## A prince who wants to go backward

Vegeta is not satisfied. He has a family, a place to live, and years of training behind him. He has also spent years being compared with Goku, who is now visiting from the afterlife. In an earlier story, this rivalry could have produced a respectable tournament match. Here it becomes a catastrophic interruption of an attempt to prevent disaster.

Babidi's magic finds a foothold in Vegeta's desire to recover a crueler identity. The mark on his forehead announces outside influence, but the confrontation is powerful precisely because Vegeta isn't presented as a blameless puppet. He wants the fight. His old pride demands it. He can reject some commands and still make a terrible choice of his own. His attack on the tournament spectators is an atrocity, carried out to force Goku into the battle he has refused to conduct on Vegeta's terms.

For a few chapters, Dragon Ball becomes a moral crisis inside a fight between two people who already know each other's techniques. In the background, their energy helps awaken Buu. The fighters have spent so long imagining a worthy opponent as an occasion for happiness that the story turns the idea against them. The dream rematch is taking place at exactly the worst possible time.

Vegeta will eventually recognize what he has done and attempt to destroy Buu at the cost of his own life. That gesture is moving because it has a specific origin in guilt, responsibility and attachment. It is not a neat cancellation of the audience he killed. And the attempted sacrifice does not kill Buu. Dragon Ball gives its proudest warrior a moment of moral growth without awarding him a clean tactical victory for it.

There is a separate, long story to tell about that decision. For the moment, notice what it does to the structure of the saga. The villain does not simply arrive because nobody is strong enough to stop him. He arrives partly because the heroes have desires they have not learned to govern. A moral weakness, not an insufficiently high power level, helps open the door.

## The monster whose face is a joke

Majin Buu emerges from his prison, and one of the strangest designs in Dragon Ball steps onto the page. After Frieza's compact menace and Cell's armored biological sophistication, the reader meets a round, elastic creature with soft features and a small tail rising from his head. He can look like a child delighted by a new toy. He can also destroy a person with hardly a change of expression.

The visual surprise is essential. If Toriyama wanted us simply to recognize a final boss, he knew how to draw one. Instead he gives us a being whose body is comic in both the artistic and the physical sense. It stretches. It rebounds. It performs impossible contortions. It can be punctured or blown apart and then come back together. A conventional fighter tries to read an opponent's guard, balance and injury. Buu's body does not reliably grant those signals.

This makes him terrifying in a new way. Cell was a scientist's accumulation of stolen traits. His transformations moved toward a declared ideal of perfection. Buu barely appears to know what he wants beyond pleasure, food, amusement and the immediate satisfaction of an impulse. Other people can be changed into candy. A population can vanish while he plays. His strongest moments are often frightening because they are so little connected to the dignity of a warrior's duel.

There is a childhood echo in this design. Dragon Ball began in a world where powerful beings might be silly, and where a gag could interrupt an act of violence. Buu brings some of that freedom into a story now capable of annihilating worlds. He is a Dr. Slump-shaped problem appearing at the end of a history of Super Saiyan transformations.

That doesn't make him harmless. The very freedom of his design allows a disturbing collapse of boundaries. Fighting, playing, eating and killing can become the same event for him. The creature is funny because his proportions and habits are funny. He is frightening because his lack of proportion extends to how he treats other lives.

He doesn't even remain a stable character design. The friendship he eventually forms with Mr. Satan and a puppy exposes the possibility of changing his behavior. The violence of others then helps divide his good and evil aspects. His evil side consumes his better counterpart and becomes Super Buu, more recognizably built as an action antagonist. Later changes through absorption reshape him again, before the final, smaller and more purely destructive form emerges.

Readers who enjoy that continual metamorphosis may experience the saga as an astonishing sequence of inventions. Readers who prefer an antagonist with one consistent personality and clear dramatic trajectory may find the shifts exhausting. Both reactions have real support in the work. The design keeps generating new kinds of confrontation; it also makes closure feel perpetually provisional.

## A children's game conducted at the end of the world

Goten and Trunks are taught Fusion, a technique that requires synchronized movement and close cooperation. It is almost deliberately ridiculous. To make a warrior of extraordinary power, two boys must dance correctly. The stakes are immense; the method looks like an elaborate playground performance. When it goes wrong, the resulting fused body advertises the mistake.

When it goes right, Gotenks is powerful, inventive and insufferable. His ego seems to have acquired the confidence of both boys without necessarily acquiring the judgment of either. He names techniques with the relish of someone inventing a game while playing it. His ghost attack is absurdly elaborate. He wastes time. He stages the appearance of difficulty. A child who can overcome physical limitations is still a child capable of poor decisions.

This is one of the Buu arc's most rewarding contradictions. Toriyama insists on the comic meaning of being young even while making youth a potential source of world-saving power. Earlier, Gohan's strength had always come with a painful disagreement between his willingness to fight and the expectations others placed on him. Goten and Trunks are often delighted by their abilities. They assume they can do the thing because being able to try feels so wonderful.

The Room of Spirit and Time becomes a theatrical space where their improvisations might be magnificent or catastrophic. Piccolo, a character once driven by revenge and later remade by guardianship, has to act as an adult supervisor in an impossible emergency. The mismatch is funny until the monster starts absorbing the people the heroes hoped would save them.

There is something rigorous underneath the silliness: technique isn't the same as judgment. Fusion supplies enormous power but doesn't supply a new ethical personality. The body has changed; the habits of the children are still there. In a story concerned with ever-higher transformations, that observation has bite.

And it creates an irritating dramatic effect, if you are reading impatiently. Every time the heroes discover a new answer, they find a reason not to finish the problem. Goku's Super Saiyan 3 looks decisive and consumes enormous energy. Gotenks arrives at a spectacular level and behaves like Gotenks. Gohan returns with his hidden potential released and seems prepared to end things, until the terms of the confrontation shift again. Vegito then makes the balance of power look ridiculous in another direction, but has a different rescue objective and enters Buu's body.

The arc's strength is its willingness to try almost anything. Its weakness is the same willingness exercised repeatedly after the audience has begun waiting for a conclusion. That is not a moral failing in a piece of popular entertainment. It is an artistic tradeoff worth naming.

## Endings, and the reluctance to end

At a certain point the saga seems to accumulate endings the way a child accumulates toys. Vegeta's last stand might have supplied a tragic finish. A fully realized Gohan might have delivered the victory promised by the Cell Games. Fusion could have served as the climactic invention. Instead the story continues, discarding one possible hero and one possible kind of resolution after another.

The effect is not identical in every medium. On a printed page, a new turn of the plot can arrive a few panels after the last one. A chapter's abrupt shift is part of the pleasure of serial comics, especially when the drawing moves with Toriyama's economy. In a television adaptation running weekly, the same chain of reversals can mean a much longer stretch of waiting, reaction shots and repeated explanations. That may make an already winding structure feel more winding. It would be inaccurate, though, to treat the anime's pacing as the sole source of the problem. The changes of direction belong to the manga too.

Eventually the story strips away most of the elaborate machinery. The Earth itself is destroyed. Goku and Vegeta face the smaller form often called Kid Buu, or Pure Buu, in the realm of the Supreme Kai. The fight retains enormous power, but much of the emotional attention has moved elsewhere. The question is no longer which new form can outclass the last. It's how a victory can be assembled when the two strongest fighters cannot produce it alone.

The answer involves the Dragon Balls, the people of Earth, the willingness to contribute energy, and the one public figure they trust to ask. Vegeta conceives a collective solution. Goku must gather and aim it. The ordinary people who have spent the series largely outside the battles are asked to participate. Mr. Satan lends his voice and reputation.

This is the strangest possible use of the fraudulent champion introduced during the Cell Games. A man who became famous for taking credit for a victory he did not win now contributes to a victory no one else can finish without him. His reputation has been comically misplaced, but it has become a real resource. The final attack is a collaboration between cosmic fighters and people who couldn't last a moment in the arena.

There is a lovely architectural symmetry here. Dragon Ball began with the Dragon Balls as objects for a treasure hunt. They could grant a wish and give a road story its destination. At the end, wishes are part of a complex social undertaking that restores a world and allows the people in it to help save themselves. The objects retain their fairy-tale simplicity; the society around them has grown much larger.

## The ending remembers the beginning

The manga's final movement does not leave Goku on a throne. Years later, at another tournament, he encounters Uub, the human reincarnation of the pure destructive Buu. Goku becomes excited about the possibility of training him. He departs with the boy to help him grow.

This can be read as a refusal to provide the conventional ending in which the hero finally stops. Goku's appetite for an interesting opponent has not vanished. In fact, his wish that the defeated enemy might be reborn so they can meet differently has given that appetite a new form. He is thinking about teaching as well as fighting. The adventure continues through someone who might once have been treated only as an enemy.

The connection back to the first Dragon Ball adventures is stronger than the mere appearance of a tournament. A boy's strength is again an invitation to a relationship and a journey. The strongest fighter is not simply guarding a completed world. He sees a future in a student.

The Buu saga does not manage this journey elegantly at every moment. It repeatedly suspends its own resolution. The power scale grows so large that secondary characters struggle to retain direct influence over combat. A sequence meant to be funny sometimes arrives when the reader is hoping for emotional seriousness, and a sequence meant to be frightening can appear just after a joke has made the world feel safe. The Gohan we were invited to follow at school does not ultimately get to finish the crisis that began during his new life.

But the disorder isn't empty. The strange changes of form allow the manga to revisit nearly every mode that brought it this far: classroom comedy, martial-arts tournament, cosmic horror, training story, family melodrama, madcap invention, magical adventure and the kindness of ordinary people. These modes don't always fit together smoothly. We can hear the joints creak. Sometimes that is exactly what makes the whole thing alive.

A perfectly streamlined Buu story might have been easier to summarize. It might have produced a clearer progression from the new threat to the final fight. It could also have lost the extraordinary interval in which a frightened famous man sits with a monster and discovers that the monster can learn to stop hurting people. Or the interval in which two boys save the day in their imagination and very nearly lose it through the same imagination. Or the scene in which a prince fails to defeat an enemy but finally recognizes whom he loves.

The best defense of the saga isn't that every turn was necessary. Many weren't. It's that the turns disclose a deeper truth about Dragon Ball as Toriyama practiced it: the series was never only a system for measuring increasing force. It was an elastic cartoon world whose inhabitants kept finding new reasons to travel, compete, laugh, grow and become attached to one another.

When the final enemy falls, the last important transformation isn't a new hair color. It is a crowd of ordinary people contributing to a fight they cannot see, because a man they believe in has asked them to.

And when that fight is over, Goku meets another boy who needs a teacher.
