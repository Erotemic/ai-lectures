# Two Boys, One Turtle Hermit: Roshi, Goku, and Krillin

There is an image from early Dragon Ball that contains almost none of the things the franchise would eventually become famous for. No glowing aura. No endangered planet. Nobody explaining a transformation. Just two boys running along a road with boxes of milk, trying to complete a delivery route while an old man makes their lives difficult.

This is martial-arts training, according to the old man. He is called Kame-sennin, the Turtle Hermit, or Master Roshi in many English-language versions. His reputation is formidable. His island house is small. His personal habits are deplorable. And for much of the training he prescribes, he appears to have confused the duties of a martial-arts teacher with the labor requirements of a very small rural business.

Yet when the story has finished making fun of him, the boys are stronger. Not merely more effective at fighting. They know each other. They have discovered what they can endure, what they can do when they are tired, and what it means for somebody else to be running beside them. They have become students in a sense that the solitary boy at the beginning of Dragon Ball could not have understood.

The milk delivery is more than a beloved piece of visual comedy. It is the point where Akira Toriyama makes a decision about the kind of martial-arts story he wants to tell. Training will not be a mysterious explanation delivered in a remote temple. It will be visible work. Strength will come from bodies doing things, day after day, and the characters will spend enough time together that their improvement means something beyond a higher number.

There is also a concealed joke. The teacher who seems to be wasting their time is the same man who will later arrange a very serious lesson in defeat. To understand why, it is useful to meet his second student.

## The newcomer who knows exactly what he wants

At the end of the first Dragon Ball hunt, the original collection of magical objects is complete, the promised wish has been squandered on a gag, and Goku has acquired more friends than he knows what to do with. He has also acquired an appetite for the world. Before Bulma arrived, he was a boy in the mountains, practicing with his staff and living with memories of the grandfather who raised him. After the journey, there are roads he has not traveled, people stronger than he expects, and an old master who might show him something new.

Then Krillin arrives at Kame House.

Krillin does not look like the natural companion for a hero whose strength is already impossible by ordinary standards. He is short, shaved-headed, ambitious, and more experienced in the social tactics of securing an advantage. He has come from the Orin Temple, where he has learned martial arts and, apparently, that martial-arts students can be treated very badly. Goku is physically gifted and startlingly direct. Krillin has survived by being clever in a world where he is not the strongest person in the room. Their introduction puts the two forms of competence beside each other.

Krillin arrives with a gift for the old master that tells us much more about Roshi than about advanced martial instruction. Goku has been learning the world by asking questions and following his impulses. Krillin arrives knowing that adults can be influenced. He is willing to flatter, bargain, and cheat. In another kind of story, those traits would establish him as a villain to be punished or expelled. Toriyama makes them the beginning of a friendship.

That distinction is important. Krillin is not introduced as the perfect friend who was waiting somewhere for Goku to find him. He begins as a competitor. He lies and attempts to outmaneuver his fellow student. He has the ordinary anxiety of someone accustomed to measuring himself against other boys and discovering that he is smaller. If Goku's innocence can sometimes make him oblivious to consequences, Krillin's self-awareness can make him dishonest. Both need to learn something.

Goku's reaction is not to deliver a lecture about virtue. He is frequently too interested in the immediate challenge to understand that one has been set up. He can be fooled, but he is difficult to corrupt into resentment. The rivalry gradually becomes the sort of companionship that does not require an appointment or a declaration. The boys eat together, struggle together, recover together. The student who wanted to win the master's favor finds that he also wants to keep up with his friend.

It is an excellent comic arrangement because neither child becomes identical to the other. Krillin continues to think tactically. Goku continues to surprise everyone through raw enthusiasm and an almost offensive ability to regard a new difficulty as good news. The friendship does not erase their differences. It gives those differences somewhere productive to go.

## A teacher who has not earned his dignity

Master Roshi was already in the first adventure. He appeared in a beach-house comedy, received a visit from a grateful turtle, and proved that the elderly fellow with sunglasses could produce a Kamehameha powerful enough to astonish everyone present. Those contradictory impressions are not a temporary disguise he eventually discards. They are the character.

The formidable martial-arts master is also habitually distracted by women, magazines, and his own vanity. Some of the gags involve sexual harassment or bargaining with women as though their bodies were his compensation. They can be ugly, and the fact that the series uses them as comedy does not oblige an audience to laugh. Bulma's treatment earlier in the manga makes clear that this is a persistent weakness in the work's treatment of its female characters, not an isolated lapse. It is possible to admire Roshi's instruction and object to the behavior with which Toriyama surrounds it.

In an important sense, the contradiction prevents the Turtle School from becoming a temple of perfect wisdom. Roshi is not presented as an adult whose every judgment is sacred. His students learn from somebody who knows a great deal about fighting and not nearly as much about being decent. This creates room for them to surpass him morally without first surpassing him physically.

There is another reason the contradiction is useful for a comic. A perfectly dignified master would make every lesson sound solemn. Roshi can be right for the wrong-looking reasons. He can stand next to a magnificent technique, then spoil the grandeur by doing something embarrassing. The reader is not being asked to choose once and for all between taking this man seriously and laughing at him. That oscillation is a source of the series' tone.

The arrival of Lunch, or Launch in many English releases, adds a character whose personality and appearance transform whenever she sneezes. One version is gentle; the other is dangerous, quick-tempered, and armed. In the domestic geography of Kame House she is an almost perfect Toriyama device: a completely unreasonable problem accepted as part of the household routine. Her transformations are a visual punchline and a warning about relying too heavily on first impressions.

Her presence also gives us a limitation of this phase. The boys get a training story. Lunch often gets to be a mechanism for interrupting it. The contrast between the potential of such a boldly drawn character and the narrow uses to which she is put becomes more apparent as the boys' inner lives grow. Toriyama's talent for inventing characters sometimes outruns the space he gives them to develop.

For the boys, though, Roshi provides what no amount of power could supply at the beginning of the comic: a structured experience of effort. He insists that simply receiving a secret move is not the point. They must build bodies capable of doing things that once seemed difficult.

## The curriculum of ordinary work

Milk first. The two students run deliveries through the countryside, handling the awkward cargo, negotiating distance, and taking instruction from a teacher who can still outpace them. A road is a useful training space because it is not arranged to flatter them. A hill remains a hill. A heavy load does not disappear when one student decides he is talented. The business of reaching a destination becomes the exercise.

There is agricultural labor, too. Plowing a field with their hands is an absurd job, but Toriyama makes the absurdity legible. We can see what the ground demands from a body. Later there are construction tasks, water crossings, and work that would be a hard day even without the turtle shells the boys wear on their backs. Roshi turns the surrounding landscape into a gym without needing to build a machine that explains how many times stronger they have become.

The shells are among Toriyama's finest pieces of practical costume design. They are an unmistakable symbol of the Turtle School and an immediate source of physical resistance. A shell is not aerodynamic or heroic. It is bulky, comic, and always there. Put a burden on a boy's back and the boy must alter the way he moves. Show him moving freely after that burden is removed, and the page can demonstrate an improvement without a numerical caption.

The idea is simple enough to be understood by a child and rich enough to sustain the later franchise. Training changes what counts as ordinary. A task that exhausts you today may become the task you do before breakfast tomorrow. The next difficulty does not prove that the earlier work was wasted; it proves that the body has learned. In the manga, the movement from impossible to routine can happen across a handful of drawn scenes. What the reader retains is not a set of performance statistics but the rhythm of repetition and recovery.

Roshi also insists that his students study and rest. Their education is not only physical, and training does not mean fighting every waking minute. The message is sometimes obscured by Roshi's own appetite for leisure, but the contrast is revealing: a master who treats martial arts as something to be practiced alongside ordinary life, not as a substitute for living it.

Consider how different this is from a familiar fantasy of martial instruction. The hero climbs a mountain. A sage with a long beard reveals a technique that nobody else could have known. The hero acquires the technique and departs. Toriyama's sage sends the boys to deliver milk. They can do it badly, then better. The miraculous element has been replaced by a sequence of comprehensible exertions.

That does not make the work a realistic fitness manual. Goku is already fantastically strong, and the physical rewards are exaggerated beyond any account of human training. The relationship between effort and capacity is emotional rather than physiological. We understand why the boys feel they have earned a new level of confidence because we have watched them perform work, not because the story has proved a theory about exercise science.

The comedy depends on the same visibility. When a boy has to move an enormous stone or traverse an impossible stretch of terrain, the reader can see the discrepancy between the task and the body. Toriyama draws physical problems with clean silhouettes and clear directions. It is the grammar he brought from visual gags into adventure. A person at one end of a field. A destination at the other. A burden between them. The anticipation of the trip is already funny before anything goes wrong.

## What Krillin learns beside Goku

In a story about two students, it would be easy for the gifted one to swallow the less gifted. Goku can often do things Krillin cannot. If that fact were the only organizing principle, Krillin would be a temporary yardstick, useful until somebody taller arrived. Instead, Toriyama repeatedly lets Krillin's perspective define how hard the work feels.

Goku can accept physical difficulty with enthusiasm. Krillin protests. He calculates. He looks for an advantage or a way around an impossible demand. His responses are funny because they are so understandable. Most people would not approach an absurdly demanding training exercise as a delightful surprise. Krillin is closer to the audience than Goku in that respect. He makes the same physical feat feel different depending on who is attempting it.

The distance between the boys becomes a relationship rather than a verdict. Krillin's ingenuity helps him when raw power would not. He is capable of embarrassment and of recognizing that he has behaved badly. Most importantly, he can change his attitude toward Goku without needing Goku to become smaller. This is not an instant moral rebirth; it is the gradual development of affection through proximity, friction and the shared memory of getting through something difficult.

One reason the friendship lasts in later Dragon Ball is that the story gives it a period before either boy is burdened with protecting a world. It is founded on meals, teasing, practice, and the astonishing fact that both of them are young enough to think an international martial-arts tournament sounds like an excellent next outing. When the story later becomes more violent, these recollections of ordinary happiness provide something worth protecting.

It is also worth noticing that Roshi's training does not attempt to make the two students move identically. The path is shared, but the boys bring different temperaments into it. Goku is often a person of immediate action. Krillin is a watcher and a planner. Both can be funny, brave, petty, frightened, and generous. A good training story gives different people the chance to improve without suggesting that improvement erases personality.

There is a consequence here for the audience's idea of strength. A martial-arts series can easily imply that the person who wins the fight has the most interesting life. This phase of Dragon Ball keeps locating interest in things that cannot be decided by a match. Krillin can be less powerful than Goku and still become indispensable as a friend. Roshi can be more powerful than both and still be a disgrace in other areas of his life. The comic offers an early way to separate the ranking of combat ability from the worth of a character, even as it grows increasingly interested in rankings.

## The disappearance of a miraculous shortcut

It is tempting to view the Turtle School as the point at which Dragon Ball abandons its original adventure for its future as an endless tournament. In part, it does change the direction of the manga. The first quest required the company to keep moving toward the next Dragon Ball. Now the new characters remain in one place for long enough to acquire habits. A journey organized by collecting objects becomes a period organized by repeated work.

But the energy of the old road trip is still there. The landscapes are varied, the assignments are ridiculous, and nobody knows what absurd creature or obstacle the next exercise will contain. Toriyama has preserved the pleasure of discovering what comes next while replacing the Dragon Radar with a teacher who can invent challenges at will.

The Dragon Balls themselves are absent from the daily schedule. Goku is not training so that he can make a wish. His grandfather's four-star ball still carries its own personal meaning, and the possibility of another search remains. But the immediate objective is a martial-arts tournament, something that cannot be solved by gathering seven magical objects. Roshi's students must arrive with the capabilities they have actually developed.

This change solves a narrative problem that Toriyama later discussed in an interview published in the mid-1990s. He remembered that the early Dragon Ball material was not attracting the response he hoped for, and that his editor, Kazuhiko Torishima, criticized the protagonist as insufficiently distinctive. Toriyama's retrospective account is that he returned to Goku's appetite for becoming stronger and looked toward a tournament format, drawing in part on successful competitive events from Dr. Slump. These are recollections about the creation of the manga, not proof of a perfectly planned editorial formula. But the formal result is visible on the page: the wandering adventure acquires a public test.

Roshi's instruction prepares the readers for that test by giving effort a history. Without these scenes, Goku's tournament performance would mainly confirm that he is inexplicably powerful. With them, we can enjoy watching him discover where his strength stands in a world larger than the forest of his childhood. Krillin, who began by trying to manipulate the terms of his apprenticeship, now has something real to show for it too.

The practical work also changes the meaning of surprise. In the opening adventure, the surprise is that Goku can do something incredible almost without trying. In the training story, it is that ordinary work, performed with enough persistence and absurdity, can lead to incredible results. The hero's astonishing body remains. But the story has found a second source of satisfaction: achievement that can be remembered.

## The teacher's secret examination

A martial-arts tournament offers a seductive and dangerous form of recognition. A clear bracket, announced opponents, spectators, rules, and a final winner make progress measurable. For a child, that clarity can be intoxicating. You either stand as champion or you do not. No more difficult question needs to be asked.

Roshi sees a problem with this, and it is not that his boys are weak. He sees it because they are becoming strong. If either wins too easily, he fears the student may decide there is nowhere left to go. A lesson framed as the end of learning would be a failure, no matter how impressive the trophy.

So Roshi prepares another lesson. He enters the tournament himself under the name Jackie Chun, concealing his identity behind an admittedly fragile disguise. The old man who sent the boys across fields and rivers now intends to become the opponent who prevents them from thinking they have reached the summit. He has been teaching them how to improve; now he needs to teach them that improvement does not imply completion.

The plan is comic because the disguise belongs to the same universe as Oolong's transformations and the hapless ambitions of Emperor Pilaf. It is also one of Roshi's most serious decisions. The teacher is willing to compete against his own pupils, to risk defeat, and to use a public performance to send a private message. He wants them to meet resistance that neither flattery nor raw talent can erase.

There is an arrogance in this, too. The master appoints himself the guardian of their humility. His students do not consent to the hidden lesson. He is manipulating a competition from which they expect honest instruction. The story makes his motives sympathetic without requiring that every method be ethically perfect. Roshi is a character, not a policy manual.

The boys, meanwhile, are ready to leave the period of repeated chores behind. They have trained, eaten, argued, and worked. The world no longer seems quite as large or mysterious as it did when a girl arrived in Goku's mountains carrying a radar. Yet it has become larger in another direction. There are other people who have trained just as hard, under masters with other philosophies, for reasons that may be as serious as survival.

A delivery route can end when the last bottle is put down. A martial artist does not get such a neat conclusion. He carries what he learned into the next contest, and the next contest may reveal something he did not know he needed to learn.

That is the quiet achievement of the Turtle School interlude. Its most enduring image is not a spectacular technique. It is two boys working alongside one another, discovering that getting stronger is something they can do together, and a teacher who understands enough about success to fear what an easy victory might teach them.

The tournament will test whether he is right.
