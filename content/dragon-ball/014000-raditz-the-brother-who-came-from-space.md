# Raditz: The Brother Who Came from Space

The first person Raditz meets on Earth has a gun. It is an ordinary sort of gun, held by an ordinary sort of man, and in the world of early Dragon Ball this is not a completely ridiculous threat. Goku has fought soldiers and survived bullets, but the comic has always made room for danger to come from familiar objects. This man has reason to believe his weapon is useful. A stranger has appeared in an unfamiliar craft, and the situation is frightening.

Raditz catches the bullet and sends it back. The man dies.

It's an ugly little introduction, all the more effective because the victim isn't a famous warrior. The visitor has not come to announce an upcoming tournament. He doesn't wonder whether Earth offers an interesting challenge. He behaves as though the lives of the people who live here are beneath consideration. And he is carrying a machine that can tell him how strong people are.

The Saiyan saga is sometimes remembered as the moment Dragon Ball began keeping score. That is true in a limited sense. A scouter makes strength legible as a number. But the number is not the first new thing that arrives. The first new thing is an attitude: the confidence of a person who has been taught that he belongs to a class of beings entitled to judge, conquer, and discard everyone else.

The stranger's name is Raditz. He will turn out to be Goku's older brother. He will also be one of the shortest-lived major villains in the story. Both facts are essential. Raditz arrives, overturns everything the audience thinks it knows about Goku, makes the strongest people on Earth look helpless, and dies before his new world has had time to become familiar. His brief appearance changes the very meaning of the story.

To understand why, we need to return to the end of the original adventure, where no one was waiting for a spaceship.

## A champion with a family

When young Goku first meets Bulma, he has a tail, extraordinary strength, a flying cloud, and an almost complete lack of knowledge about ordinary society. Those qualities make him an excellent comic protagonist. The world is a puzzle, and he approaches it with the confidence of someone who does not know he should be embarrassed.

Over the years the story builds explanations for some of his powers and relationships. He trained with Grandpa Gohan. He can transform into a gigantic ape under the full moon, although he does not understand the transformation at first. He learns to fight from Roshi and later from Kami. He makes friends, survives an army, loses people he loves, and eventually defeats Piccolo Junior at the 23rd World Martial Arts Tournament.

By the time that tournament ends, Goku has become a young adult. He has won the championship he spent much of his childhood pursuing. He has also recognized Chi-Chi's claim that they had once promised to marry. It is a comic conclusion and a genuine transition into a different life. The boy who had no idea how people behaved has an unexpected future with another person.

A few years later, he arrives at Kame House with his little son, Gohan. The child has been named for Goku's adoptive grandfather, a choice that quietly shows what family has meant to him all along. He is a father before the audience has seen him learn the details of how to be one. His domestic life has largely happened in the spaces between stories. It feels like a world carrying on outside the panels.

This is important because Raditz does not interrupt a tournament with the promise of a stronger fight. He breaks into the hero's new family life. The cost of his intrusion is not merely that the next opponent has a larger number. A child is placed in danger. A household has to become a battlefield. The possibility that Goku's recent peace might continue is taken away before it has had much time to settle.

The arrival is also a shift in how the world explains itself. Goku has spent years being an extraordinary person among extraordinary people. He has never needed his body's origins to justify his friendships or his choices. The revelation will offer a new answer to where his abilities come from. It will not offer an adequate answer to who he is.

## The machine on the stranger's face

Raditz wears unfamiliar armor and a device over one eye. The scouter reads battle power, registers strong fighters at a distance, and provides a kind of battlefield intelligence unavailable to the people of Earth. In a story built on training and surprise, it is a frightening invention. It turns a person into a measurement before the measurement's owner has to speak to that person.

This is more than a handy screen for telling the audience which fighter is ahead. A scouter lets an invader travel through an unknown world without treating it as a mystery. Raditz doesn't have to ask who rules the place, what people value, or why a fighter might be hiding. He looks for a signal. To him, Earth is a set of targets of different sizes.

The comic then begins to expose the machine's inadequacy. Energy can be concealed or released suddenly. Gohan's furious burst, for example, makes no sense if one assumes that an individual's potential is a single constant number. The device can report what it detects at a moment. It cannot tell Raditz what love, fear, courage, or desperation will make someone do next.

That limitation is not a scientific objection about whether a sensor could measure fictional energy. Within the story's rules, scouters do things that work. The important narrative distinction is between information and understanding. Raditz possesses a tool that seems to make the world transparent. He nevertheless misreads nearly everyone in it.

He meets Piccolo first. The demon king's son, once the villain Goku had to defeat to save the world, is still a dangerous and proud fighter. Raditz receives his attack and treats it as irrelevant. This is a perfectly judged introduction because Piccolo does not need to be explained from scratch. We already know what it took to overcome him. By making him seem inadequate, the story tells us how alarming the newcomer is without asking for a statistical table.

The scouter confirms an imbalance. The scene makes us feel it.

## A brother who knows the wrong name

At Kame House, Raditz names Goku as Kakarot and tells him that he is a Saiyan from another planet. He claims that Goku was sent to Earth as a child to eliminate the people living there, and that a blow to the head caused him to forget his intended mission. He has come to retrieve his younger brother for work that the surviving Saiyans have been doing elsewhere: destroying or conquering worlds for profit and power.

There is a particular nastiness to this kind of revelation. A distant relative arrives with information you cannot independently check, then tries to use it to define your obligations. Raditz offers Goku a heritage packaged as a command. Because they share blood, he suggests, Goku should belong to the same violent enterprise.

The name itself is part of the pressure. The audience has known this character as Son Goku, a name tied to an adoptive family and to a body of stories told on Earth. Kakarot is a claim from elsewhere. Raditz uses it as if the unfamiliar name should settle the argument. He expects a biological fact to override an entire lived life.

Goku doesn't accept the conclusion. He is not moved to abandon his friends because a man from space has identified his species. He rejects the proposition that being a Saiyan requires killing Earthlings. We should not imagine that he has already worked out a sophisticated philosophy of identity. He is simply certain about the people he knows, the world he lives in, and the behavior of the person making the demand.

This is a revealing continuation of young Goku, not a repudiation of him. From the start, he has often been ignorant of rules that other people considered obvious. Some rules are trivial conventions. Others conceal cruelty. His refusal to obey a strange authority has been comic and occasionally heroic. Now the strange authority is his own brother, and the stakes are a planet.

Later Dragon Ball works would revisit the origins of the Saiyans, Goku's parents, and the circumstances of his departure. Those later stories can be interesting. But they should not be silently imported into this first encounter. At this point Raditz is delivering information that Goku and the audience have barely had time to test. The narrative asks what Goku does with the claim now, not how every detail will fit with decades of subsequent additions.

Goku answers the essential question by refusing. Raditz answers him by taking Gohan.

## The hostage who cannot fight

The kidnapping of Gohan is a brutal change of genre. A hero who has repeatedly protected friends is now unable to protect his own child. Raditz threatens the boy and demands proof that Goku will join the Saiyans' work. One part of the threat is a test of obedience. Another is an act of humiliation: the older brother can reach directly into the life Goku has made and seize the person Goku cannot bear to lose.

Consider how early Dragon Ball often used children. Goku himself was small, powerful, ignorant, and endlessly ready for adventure. His youth was part of his freedom. He could approach a monster as a possible source of food and an adult as a possible new friend. Gohan is a different kind of child. He is sheltered, more frightened, and not yet trained to understand this world of violence. The familiar comic premise of an impossibly strong little boy is now displaced into a child who desperately wants an adult to save him.

This difference gives the saga an emotional register the early adventures could not easily have used. Goku may have been a child hero, but the people around him generally learned that he could survive extraordinary danger. Gohan cannot be assumed to cope. He has powerful potential, but he does not have control over it. Watching him endure the first encounter is frightening precisely because we have been given so little reason to think he wants any part of a warrior's life.

Raditz treats Gohan as leverage. The story will eventually reveal that the child is also a threat. Those two perspectives are never equal in moral weight. Gohan's violent potential is not a justification for what his uncle does to him. It is an unexpected complication in the criminal act.

Goku has been faced with strong enemies before. This time he cannot solve the problem simply by being the person willing to take them on. He needs help from the one fighter on Earth he has good reason to distrust.

## The alliance that should not exist

Piccolo approaches Goku with a proposal. Neither of them can defeat Raditz alone. The villain of the previous major confrontation and the hero who stopped him must fight together.

There is a funny structural irony here. Piccolo had been created, in part, to carry forward King Piccolo's grievance. He was a surviving instrument of revenge. His identity seemed to bind him to a violent inheritance just as Raditz now claims Goku must be bound to his Saiyan one. The two characters who ought to be defined by family history and revenge make a choice that moves them beyond their assigned positions.

This is not yet the redemption of Piccolo. He has not become a kind uncle or a reluctant teacher. He still has reasons to want Goku out of the way. His new technique, the Makankosappo, often called the Special Beam Cannon in English, was developed to kill Goku himself. The alliance does not work because old hostility was secretly a misunderstanding. It works because both fighters recognize the immediate situation and choose cooperation over pride.

That choice has practical consequences. Goku's strength and agility can keep Raditz occupied. Piccolo's new attack can concentrate enough force to threaten an opponent who shrugs off their ordinary blows. But the technique takes time to charge. A battle that would be unwinnable as a straightforward exchange of strikes becomes a problem of timing, positioning, and trust.

This is one of the great early examples of Toriyama's ability to make a power gap interesting. If Raditz is simply stronger, then nothing dramatic can happen until Goku acquires more strength. Instead the fight asks what the weaker people can arrange. One warrior buys time. The other prepares an attack. The enemy is powerful enough to punish almost any mistake. Victory must be engineered from a set of limited opportunities.

For a moment it appears possible. Goku grabs Raditz's tail, exploiting the vulnerable spot associated with Saiyan physiology. Raditz begs him to relent. He promises change, appeals to their relationship, and pretends to have been brought to his senses. Goku lets go.

Raditz attacks him again.

This is a painful scene because it forces Goku's best and worst impulses into the same gesture. The boy who trusted strangers, spared opponents, and welcomed a rival's better self is now a father who cannot afford to be deceived. His willingness to believe that someone might change is one of his most attractive qualities. In this situation it becomes a liability others have to survive.

Raditz understands the possibility of mercy only as something to exploit. He is, at the most intimate level, a bad reader of the Dragon Ball he has entered.

## Gohan moves

Raditz's brutality finally produces the event his machine could not anticipate. Gohan, furious and terrified, erupts with strength and strikes his uncle. The attack is not a glorious masterstroke. He is a little boy trapped in a situation he does not understand, acting under emotional pressure. The resulting force is astonishing, but it is not reliably repeatable.

This is a different way of introducing a powerful character from the adult Goku's debut. Goku was already physically capable and socially naive. Gohan is emotionally overwhelmed and physically unpredictable. The distinction will shape nearly everything the story does with him later. His power becomes an object of hope for the people who want to survive, but also a burden placed on a child whose preferences are not the same as theirs.

The blow creates an opening. Goku restrains Raditz from behind, holding him in place while Piccolo charges his attack. This time the situation offers no comfortable escape. Goku understands that his brother cannot be trusted and that the attack will pass through both bodies. He stays where he is.

The Special Beam Cannon kills Raditz. It also kills Goku.

On a page-by-page level the reversal is startling. The person who has carried this series since the first meeting with Bulma dies while the new story is still introducing itself. The very beginning of what television calls Dragon Ball Z contains the death of its protagonist. There is no long farewell tour, no final championship, no satisfying closure to his years of training. Goku makes an immediate decision to save his son and the world, then is gone.

We know that death in Dragon Ball need not be permanent. The series has magical objects that can return people to life, subject to rules and circumstances. But the familiarity of resurrection does not cancel the choice. Goku has to decide without a guarantee that the others will succeed in bringing him back. His family still loses him. His comrades still have to act without him. The story has rearranged the cast's responsibilities.

And Piccolo, the villain, has participated in saving Goku's child. That consequence will keep growing long after Raditz has vanished from the stage.

## Victory is a warning

Raditz's final disclosure is almost insulting. He was not the most terrifying Saiyan in existence. Two more are coming, and they are much stronger. The device he wears has transmitted useful information about Earth and its Dragon Balls. What the fighters have just accomplished is not the end of an invasion. It is notice that the real invasion will happen later.

The power scale has shifted again, but more important, time has changed. Earlier Dragon Ball arcs often allowed the hero to go somewhere and discover what happened there. Raditz establishes a deadline. The defenders know roughly when danger will arrive. They must train for an event that will not wait for the pleasure of their wandering. In this structure, preparation becomes the plot.

It is also an oddly cruel legacy for a man who has barely existed in the story. Raditz dies, but his assumptions survive him. The incoming Saiyans expect a weak population, a useful planet, and objects that can grant wishes. They have learned enough about Earth to treat it as something to exploit. The heroes must build a defense without fully knowing the limits of the enemy's strength.

Goku will pursue training in the afterlife with King Kai. Piccolo will take Gohan away to prepare him for the arriving warriors. Krillin, Tien, Yamcha, Chiaotzu, and others must become stronger than they have any reason to believe is possible. In a narrative sense, the entire Earth is assigned the uncomfortable task Raditz refused to undertake: learn to understand people before circumstances force the lesson upon you.

The episode has produced an astonishing amount of story. It has made space real, made Goku's origin strange, introduced his son as a person with independent potential, redefined Piccolo as a possible ally, killed the hero, and installed a clock beneath every future training scene. It has also left us with a disturbing question: how much of what we thought we knew about the world was simply a limitation of where we had traveled?

## The brother who cannot come home

Raditz is often treated as a minor villain because his fighting career is brief and later characters become incomparably stronger. In a story whose audience enjoys comparing fighters, that is an understandable way to remember him. It is also a poor measure of his importance as a character.

His significance is not that he is a particularly subtle person. He isn't. He is arrogant, cruel, and lazy in his certainty that birth and strength grant authority. His contribution is to make the people around him reveal themselves. Goku chooses the family that raised and loved him over the one Raditz claims through blood. Piccolo chooses cooperation with his enemy over fidelity to a vendetta. Gohan responds as a frightened child whose body can perform something that his mind is not ready to command.

The most important fact Raditz gives Goku is that he came from somewhere else. The most important fact Goku gives Raditz is that coming from somewhere else does not decide what kind of person you have to become.

There is a sadness in the encounter, although the story doesn't pause to turn it into a sentimental tragedy. This is Goku's brother. They will not share a meal, train together, or exchange childhood stories that make them understand each other. Raditz has arrived with an answer to a mystery Goku never particularly needed solved, and with no desire to know the brother he actually found. He is so certain he understands the relationship that he destroys any chance of having one.

And yet, through his intrusion, Goku's world becomes much larger. The mountains, villages, deserts, tournaments, and cities of the old Dragon Ball still exist. Their hero has not ceased to belong to them. He has simply learned that the sky above them contains people who will try to tell him what that belonging means.

At the end of the encounter, Goku is dead, Piccolo is changed without admitting it, and a frightened little boy is about to learn the price of becoming a warrior. The story that began with a girl meeting a boy with a tail has crossed into another genre. It has not changed its central question. It still wants to know what people do when they meet someone whose world they do not understand.

Raditz thinks the answer is to measure the stranger and make him obey. Goku's answer is harder and far more costly: hold on to the people you love, even when the price is your own life.
