# 031000 — How to Draw a Transformation

**Track:** Dragon Ball: From Penguin Village to the End of Z. **Audience:** Fans and interested newcomers. **Prerequisites:** The major characters are briefly reintroduced. **Spoilers:** Goku's origins, Namek and the first Super Saiyan, the Cell Games, the Buu saga, and fusion. **Scope:** The visual and narrative grammar of transformation in Toriyama's original manga, with clearly identified differences in the animated presentation. **Perspective:** Design, silhouette, power, cost, invention, and why not every new form improves a story.

# How to Draw a Transformation

One of the most famous changes in the history of comics consists of a man standing up with different hair.

There is more to the moment, of course. Krillin has been killed on Namek, Goku is furious, and Frieza is discovering that a legend he has feared might be real. The manga has spent years teaching readers what extraordinary strength can do. But the image that survives beyond its original circumstances is almost absurdly simple. Goku is still recognizable as Goku. His hair rises and changes color, his expression hardens, and his whole body seems to announce a different relationship to the fight.

That simplicity is one of the reasons the transformation works. A reader who has never read the explanation can see that something has changed. The exact amount of increased power is secondary. Even on a black-and-white page, the lighter hair and more severe face make a new Goku legible at a glance.

Dragon Ball did not invent transformation as an idea. Japanese popular entertainment had long been fascinated by metamorphosis, disguise, giant monsters and heroes with new identities. Toriyama also began as a gag cartoonist who could make an absurd change of shape feel completely at home in a story. What Dragon Ball does over time is turn bodily transformation into one of its principal ways of organizing suspense.

That raises an interesting design problem. A character's appearance is a promise. Change too little, and the new strength may feel like an arbitrary claim. Change too much, and the audience may lose the person whose achievement it is supposed to celebrate. Make the new form effortless, and the fight might become less interesting. Make every form carry an obvious, identical cost, and the novelty dies in a different way.

Toriyama's solutions are not consistent, and that inconsistency is part of the series' appeal. He produces frightening monsters, embarrassing balloons of muscle, elegant hair shapes, wildly theatrical fusion costumes, and transformations that turn out to be mistaken strategies. Some are among his finest visual ideas. Others expose the trouble with an action story that must keep discovering what comes after the impossible.

The best place to begin is not with Super Saiyan. It is with a little boy who looks at the moon.

## The giant monkey inside the little boy

Early Dragon Ball gives us Goku as a compact, cheerful child. He has a tail, which initially belongs among the many comic oddities of his world. Bulma has technology that can compress a house into a capsule. Animals talk. Dinosaurs can appear beside cars. A tail is strange, but not beyond the imagination of the story.

Then a full moon changes Goku into a gigantic ape.

The effect is startling because it changes several things at once. The boy's size becomes monstrous. His ordinary face and voice are displaced by the features of an enormous creature. His friends, who have learned to regard his extraordinary ability as something usually helpful, face a new kind of danger. The transformation reveals a fact about him that he himself does not understand.

The Great Ape is important precisely because it is not a prize Goku has earned through discipline. It is an involuntary event, a loss of control, and a threat to people he loves. In later Saiyan stories, the same form can be deliberately exploited. Vegeta understands its military value in a way that young Goku never could. An image that began as a startling interruption to a comedy becomes part of the fictional machinery of conquest.

There is a visual economy in this transformation. The large ape is not just a taller Goku with more muscle. Toriyama borrows an immediately understood animal shape. Huge limbs, muzzle, fur and size do much of the narrative work. We do not need a training chart to understand that Bulma and the others have a problem.

The ape also gives the story an early lesson about the body: a change in size can create danger without creating wisdom. When Goku later learns how to use strength with judgment, that achievement is not redundant with the ability to become enormous. The series has planted a distinction it will return to repeatedly. Physical superiority and self-command are separate virtues.

That distinction is easy to forget when the hair colors begin multiplying. The earliest transformation was already asking the question.

## Kaiō-ken: a transformation that has a bill

During Goku's preparation for Vegeta, the techniques taught by King Kai promise a way to reach beyond ordinary limits. The Kaiō-ken is not a new species, nor a permanent biological stage. It is a technique, a way of driving power upward at severe physical cost.

In animation, the Kaiō-ken is strongly associated with red coloration and an aura. On the printed black-and-white manga page, its meaning has to come from the body's strain, linework, dialogue and the reactions of opponents. That distinction is worth keeping clear. The red glow many viewers remember is a successful part of the television's visual vocabulary, not literal red ink on every original manga page.

What makes Kaiō-ken dramatically useful is the invoice attached to it. Goku can do more, but doing more hurts him. The body becomes the battleground as much as the opponent does. He may land a better blow, yet every increase puts the continuation of the fight at risk. A desperate solution can make the next minute less survivable.

This is a strong form of suspense because it resists the simplest power fantasy. The heroic character does not simply discover that the ceiling was higher all along. He discovers that he can temporarily push through it, and that the price may arrive before the battle ends.

A good transformation can be defined by a tradeoff. Speed for force. Survival for control. A new resource for a new weakness. There is no reason every power-up must follow that template, but Kaiō-ken illustrates how dramatic clarity comes from showing what the ability makes possible and what it makes dangerous.

The Saiyan invasion also offers the opposite kind of bodily change. Vegeta becomes a giant ape to overwhelm his opponent. Unlike Goku's internalized, punishing technique, the Oozaru form announces dominance through scale. The two changes produce entirely different pictures of power: one body in agony as it tries to exceed itself, another body enormous enough to rearrange the battlefield.

Both are transformations. They are not interchangeable narrative tools.

## Frieza and the cruelty of changing shape

Before Goku's legendary golden hair, Frieza turns transformation into a form of theater.

The tyrant introduced on Namek is small and composed. He has the authority to order atrocities and the confidence to be polite while doing it. His first body is not especially intimidating by the usual standards of a muscle-bound fighting manga. The calm is the threat. Here is someone whose appearance does not need to advertise his full capacity.

When Frieza changes forms, he reveals that the body we have seen is only one possible arrangement. Some forms enlarge and exaggerate his menace. One becomes elongated, grotesque, closer to a monster from a horror comic. Another is compact, smooth, and unnervingly controlled.

This sequence reverses an easy assumption: bigger ought to mean stronger, and more elaborate ought to mean more dangerous. Frieza's final form is more economical than some of the bodies that precede it. His compactness looks less like an absence of power than the absence of unnecessary effort.

That is a design lesson disguised as villain escalation. The most frightening thing is not always the largest thing. A form can become more alarming by removing visual fuss. The enemy who no longer needs enormous protrusions or theatrical bulk may be closer to the force of nature the heroes feared.

Frieza's successive bodies also manipulate information. As the heroes learn to handle one version of him, he can reveal another. Each transformation forces the cast and the reader to revise the scale they thought they understood. This is effective suspense for a while. It also reveals the device's danger: if the audience learns to expect another unannounced form every time an opponent is cornered, victory can start to feel like a delay in the villain's next costume change.

The transformation must eventually cash out. Frieza's final body has to become the setting for decisions that cannot be answered by another change in silhouette. Goku's arrival, Vegeta's death, Krillin's loss and the eruption of Super Saiyan restore an emotional basis beneath the sequence of forms.

Frieza is showing us several versions of a terrible power. Goku is about to show us what one loss has done to one person.

## How yellow hair works in black ink

On the manga page, the first Super Saiyan is a triumph of contrast.

Most readers know the image in color: Goku's hair becomes golden, his eyes become a different color, and the anime gives him an immense atmosphere of light and sound. In the original monochrome manga, the light hair is largely white paper where a familiar black mass would have been. Against the darker forms of Goku's previous appearance, that absence is conspicuous. The changed hair outline and the harder expression make the new face immediately identifiable.

There is an often repeated production story attached to the design. In a translated interview from the *Super Exciting Guide*, Toriyama explained that leaving the hair light spared his assistant the work of filling Goku's black hair with ink, while also making the new strength recognizable at a glance. It is an appealing account because it joins economy of labor with economy of design. But it is best treated as Toriyama's retrospective explanation, through translation, rather than proof that every other artistic consideration was irrelevant.

In another interview, this one from the sixth *Daizenshuu* volume, Toriyama gave a different but compatible piece of testimony. He said that he had not been planning the Super Saiyan transformation from the beginning, and that when the story demanded a new level of strength he needed Goku's appearance to change. He also remembered worrying that the harder, angrier face might look too villainous for a hero. Those are revealing concerns: the form needed to signal power immediately, but it also needed to remain emotionally connected to the person who had earned it. The familiar design now seems inevitable. To its creator, at least in that later telling, it was a solution to an artistic problem that carried a risk.

The scene would still fail if it were only a clever time-saving measure. The hair works because of its dramatic placement. The legend of a Super Saiyan has been discussed. Frieza has inflicted suffering that the story does not allow us to dismiss. Krillin's death strikes Goku in a way that routine tactical setbacks have not. The transformation is not a cosmetic accessory offered before a scheduled fight. It is the shape of an emotional rupture.

That is why the expression may be as important as the hairstyle. Goku, who frequently approaches combat with eager curiosity, now looks grim. The new body has a different emotional temperature. The character is recognizable, but the familiar enjoyment is gone.

When animation takes over this image, it can give the transformation duration. The sound can build. Hair and clothing can shift. The landscape can respond. A voice can carry strain that a printed panel implies. These are legitimate additions, not merely ways to reproduce the manga. At the same time, the anime's extended interval changes the abruptness of the printed reveal. A reader may encounter the form in a turn of the page. A viewer may wait through an accumulating spectacle.

Neither medium owns the definitive emotional experience. They are arranging different materials around the same design.

The first Super Saiyan is powerful not because it looks the most complicated, but because it is immediately legible and attached to a loss we have already felt.

## When the new body is the wrong answer

One of the cleverest uses of transformation arrives during the Cell crisis, when Trunks learns to increase his Super Saiyan musculature dramatically.

The visual claim is obvious. The form is big. The muscles dominate the figure. The body seems to contain strength beyond what the earlier Super Saiyan appearance announced. If more visible mass were always better, the new state would be the obvious next step.

But Cell can exploit its lack of speed. The power-up advertises exactly the wrong priority. The form becomes a critique of the idea that advancement means maximizing one quantity. An exaggerated body makes an exaggerated mistake.

This is Toriyama's gag sensibility returning to a serious fight. The form looks impressive in a way a proud young fighter might find irresistible. Its inadequacy is almost comic, except that the stakes are terrible. Strength, divorced from effective movement, is not mastery.

Goku and Gohan's training points toward another approach: making the ordinary Super Saiyan state less exhausting and more natural to inhabit. The visual drama is quieter. Instead of constantly threatening to tear the body apart, the characters attempt to normalize the transformed state. That has an interesting implication for a series built around breakthroughs. The next improvement may not be another extravagant visual shock. It may be the ability to live comfortably with strength already acquired.

Gohan's own further transformation at the Cell Games changes the problem again. The deeper power emerges through rage and grief, after Android 16's destruction and Cell's attacks. The hair becomes sharper; the body and expression change; in animation, electrical effects and sound help mark the difference. But the excitement is inseparable from the danger in Gohan's state of mind. The boy can now win, yet his anger briefly keeps him from finishing the job.

A transformation does not guarantee moral improvement. In Gohan's case, strength and judgment become misaligned at the worst possible moment. It takes pain, assistance and a new decision to finish what the form alone cannot resolve.

The lesson is more interesting than a numbered chart. A person can become stronger without becoming better at choosing when to stop.

## Three stages up, and what is left to invent?

The Majin Buu arc arrives after so many revelations that a new transformation has an inherited problem. The first Super Saiyan was a legend. The later grades became techniques to analyze and surpass. Super Saiyan 2 has already arrived in a moment of great emotional significance. What can a third named stage offer other than a taller numeral?

Toriyama chooses an almost ridiculous answer: extraordinary length of hair, a changed brow, and a body that looks as though the first transformation has been pushed past elegance into mythic excess. Super Saiyan 3 is instantly memorable. In animation, Goku's extended transformation can become a display of the medium's capacity for escalation, with sound and atmosphere drawn out on a scale that printing cannot prescribe.

It is also a form whose practical limits prevent it from solving everything. Using and sustaining it is costly. The Buu conflict repeatedly refuses to end simply because somebody has unveiled the most impressive possible appearance.

This refusal can be both irritating and productive. Irritating, because viewers may reasonably ask how many more stages and interruptions one story needs. Productive, because it pushes the manga toward answers that are not only personal transformations: training other people, cooperating, separating what is good from what is monstrous, and finally persuading an entire planet to lend its energy.

The new hair shape is no longer enough to be the conclusion of the story.

There is another complication in the Buu arc. Fusion allows two characters to become one fighter, and this changes the visual meaning of power.

The Fusion Dance combines choreography with comedy. Goten and Trunks must coordinate positions, movements and timing. The resulting Gotenks is powerful, vain, theatrical and sometimes spectacularly incompetent. His design is a composite, and his personality is one too. The body does not simply add two power totals. It expresses the absurd mixture of two overconfident children.

The Potara fusion of Goku and Vegeta gives us a different composite. The fused character's appearance carries familiar elements from both men. This can make the impossible union visible before anybody explains its mathematics. It is an extraordinary dramatic proposition, too: two rivals who have spent years measuring themselves against each other agree to become one person because there is no other reasonable choice.

Fusion creates a problem for the artist and the writer together. A successful combination must be easy to recognize as neither of its components alone, and it must make the audience care about a person who, in the story's terms, has only just appeared. Toriyama can draw the merged silhouette immediately. The personality has to follow.

## Transformations as promises and traps

Step back from these forms, and a pattern begins to appear. Different transformations answer different questions.

The Great Ape asks whether strength can escape its owner. Kaiō-ken asks how much a body can bear. Frieza's stages ask how much danger can remain concealed inside appearances. The first Super Saiyan asks what grief can release. The heavy Super Saiyan grades ask whether raw force is the same as fighting skill. Gohan's next breakthrough asks whether rage can be controlled. Super Saiyan 3 asks what spectacle can still accomplish when the story is already overflowing with spectacle. Fusion asks whether a new identity can solve what isolated ambition cannot.

This is not a hidden system Toriyama set out in a production manual. It is a way to read the effects his designs have when placed in particular scenes. Some forms are more elegantly motivated than others. Some are more satisfying in animation, where color and sound give them another life. Some work splendidly as pictures but complicate the drama by offering new strength too cheaply.

It is worth being skeptical of the notion that each later transformation automatically improves on the last. Many fans cherish the first Super Saiyan not because no later form could defeat it, but because it arrived with exceptional clarity. Its body, emotion, and narrative timing coincided. An image did not simply say more. It said the right thing at the right time.

The proliferation of transformations also changes how an audience watches. Early in Dragon Ball, a sudden new body is alarming because it might reveal a secret or a loss of control. Later, we may expect the protagonist to discover another state at the decisive moment. Surprise becomes a tradition. The writer must find new ways to make the familiar breakthrough feel earned.

Sometimes Toriyama does that by withholding the transformation. Sometimes by making it inadequate. Sometimes by making the new form absurd. And sometimes, as with Mr. Satan and the final battle against Buu, by turning away from visual escalation entirely. A man without a spectacular battle form does something essential because other people believe him.

That is not an argument that transformations have been a mistake. They are some of the series' most wonderful inventions. It is an argument that their true value is dramatic rather than numerical.

Consider the earliest image again: a small boy turning into a monstrous ape when the moon rises. And the famous later image: an adult fighter, suddenly still and terrible, with hair that no longer needs to be filled in. One makes him lose himself. The other makes an unfamiliar side of himself visible. Between those moments lies an entire history of a cartoonist exploring how a body can announce a story.

A transformation is good when it transforms more than the drawing.
