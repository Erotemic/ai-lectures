# Comedy Under the Clock: Subtitles, Dubbing, and the Shape of a Laugh

**Course:** Comedy: Theory, History, and Craft  
**Section:** Audiovisual translation and comic timing  
**Status:** Substantive audio-first manuscript

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

A television character looks at a room filled with filing cabinets and says, with impressive confidence, "We have simplified the process." The camera cuts to a filing cabinet labeled "Requests to Simplify the Process." A clerk unlocks it. Inside is a second key, attached to a form. The clerk reads the form, crosses the room, and opens a drawer containing a third key. The joke has been made in language, in a prop, in the delay between actions, and finally in the clerk's calm refusal to acknowledge any contradiction. Someone translating the program must decide what information the audience needs at the moment it appears. If the words are subtitled, viewers must read while watching the keys. If they are dubbed, a new voice must fit an existing performance. If the program is shown to an audience familiar with the bureaucracy being mocked, the scene also carries a local satire.

It is tempting to say that comedy depends on timing and leave it there. Timing, however, is not one thing. It may mean the length of a pause, the point at which a key word arrives, the relation between speech and a gesture, the speed at which a line is read, the amount of time viewers need to inspect an image, or the expectation established by several earlier scenes. In audiovisual translation, the translator is working with some parts of time that cannot be moved at will. The original image and edit may be fixed even while the language changes.

Patrick Zabalbeascoa's 1996 study of dubbed television situation comedies addressed this precise kind of problem. His examples involved English-language comedy translated into Catalan and Spanish, and he argued for criteria that account for the translator's actual working environment. A dubbing assignment is not simply a test of whether an isolated joke can be paraphrased. The translator faces voices, characters, recurring relationships, production constraints, and decisions about the whole program. Delia Chiaro's work on screen translation and audience responses to dubbed or subtitled humor similarly treats humor as something received by viewers under particular media conditions, not just as a sentence stored in a dictionary.

## The same joke has several clocks

Picture the filing-cabinet scene again. Clock one is the sentence. The character announces simplicity, and the next image contradicts the claim. Clock two is the physical operation. The clerk retrieves increasingly absurd keys without ever breaking professional composure. Clock three is the viewer's processing time: one must read the label on the cabinet before the next action changes its meaning. Clock four is dramatic history. Perhaps the character has been promising simplification for an entire episode. A subtitle translator may preserve the sentence but still lose the second clock if the audience has to read the translation of the cabinet label at exactly the moment the clerk reveals the next key.

A good production has options. The subtitle can be brief. An on-screen graphic can be recreated if the production permits it. The speech can be shortened. The caption can arrive a moment before or after the visible action, within the limits of intelligibility and editorial rules. None of these moves should be described as universally available. A translator working with a licensed broadcast may not have authority to alter graphics or edit the shot. A film festival version, a streaming subtitle, a live interpretation, and a locally remade advertisement may have different freedoms.

There is also a difficult question of where the joke ends. Suppose the original clerk makes an audible sigh that conveys awareness of the absurdity. In one version, the translated line could name that absurdity explicitly. The result may become funnier as dialogue while weakening the silent performance. The actor's sigh was already an interpretation. Adding a caption that says, "This paperwork is ridiculous," asks the viewer to process a second interpretation and may flatten the original restraint. Sometimes the best translation is to leave room for the body to do work.

## Subtitles ask the eye to divide its labor

Subtitles allow the original voices to remain audible, but reading is an additional visual task. The viewer is no longer looking only at faces, props, and spatial relationships. Even a short subtitle occupies attention and has to remain long enough to read. In comedy, a quick reaction shot may contain the entire punch line. A person who reads the words just as the reaction appears might miss the look that makes them funny.

It would be careless to propose a universal number of characters or seconds that solves this problem. Subtitle conventions vary by language, platform, delivery specification, age of intended audience, and accessibility goals. A viewer's reading speed varies too. Technical guidelines are useful production constraints, not a scientific law of laughter. The central artistic question is whether the sequence of noticing remains possible.

Imagine a fictional exchange. A manager says, "I promised to reduce waiting." The camera reveals an enormous line of people standing behind a sign: "Waiting Reduction Registration." A subtitle that faithfully reproduces the manager's entire sentence may compete with the image. A compact version, "I said I'd cut the wait," can establish the expectation with less reading. The exact choice in a real language would require competent translators and context. In this English demonstration, the lesson is how the compression changes the time available for viewing, not that one English phrase is guaranteed better.

Now reverse the problem. The original dialogue contains a double meaning crucial to the plot. Shortening may remove the ambiguity and cause a later callback to make no sense. We cannot optimize only the immediate laugh. An audiovisual joke may be a promise the narrative cashes in later. The translator needs a map of future occurrences, consistent character language, and perhaps a glossary or stylebook that records which motifs must survive. Zabalbeascoa proposed attention to realistic criteria and the usefulness of a stylebook to bridge principles and particular assignments. That does not mean every production possesses a well-maintained document. It means that tracking decisions is often part of translating a sustained comic world.

## Dubbing changes the speaker's body without changing the visible one

Dubbing creates a different set of constraints. The translated voice should seem to belong to the visible person, and the line has to fit within the performance's time. A close shot of a speaking face may demand tighter lip synchronization than an exchange heard over a wide view of the room. The voice actor also makes interpretive decisions about tempo, hesitation, emphasis and emotional stance. These can shift a joke's apparent target without changing the image at all.

Suppose the manager says, "Naturally, we have anticipated every possible delay." In the original performance, the actor stresses "naturally" with a slight panic, signaling that the manager knows the system is failing. A dubbed performance delivered with triumphant certainty makes the character sound more arrogant than anxious. Both may support comedy, but not the same character. The translator and performer are jointly shaping a social action: an attempt to reassure, to deceive, to evade, or to maintain dignity. A technically synchronized line can still change the dramatic meaning.

Dubbing also permits a line to be spoken instead of read. It may allow viewers to concentrate on the visual details that subtitles compete with. Yet it can create new mismatches when an on-screen sign remains in the source language, or when a translated phrase no longer matches a character's gesture. There is no single media hierarchy in which dubbing always improves humor and subtitling always damages it. Each redistributes attention and changes what must be coordinated.

Try a simple thought experiment. The clerk holds up a tiny key and announces, "This opens the big drawer." In the original performance, the humor comes from the mismatched size. A subtitled version may preserve the line exactly while letting the image do the rest. A dubbed version might need a longer expression to fit the actor's mouth movements. If the longer expression identifies the key as ridiculously small, it steals information that the original audience was supposed to infer. A strong adaptation may seek a line that fills the time while leaving the visual contrast unexplained. That is a writing problem, not only a lexical one.

## Live interpretation and the problem of anticipating the punch line

Live interpretation reveals the timing issue even more sharply. An interpreter may need to understand a speaker's full sentence before knowing which earlier word was part of a joke. The audience hears a delay, a repair, or an explanation after the moment the original audience laughs. This can happen even when every statement is accurately conveyed. Some language structures reveal a verb, negation, or ambiguity at different points, so an interpreter cannot always mirror the order of information without risking error.

An original fictional presenter says, "Our government now has a department of the future. It is already behind schedule." A translator who anticipates the second sentence might prepare a concise equivalent. But a joke constructed from a delayed grammatical revelation may not permit such anticipation. When the interpretive delay is visible, the interpreter could choose to reproduce the information accurately and allow the humor to arrive later. Another strategy might convey the function through a different syntactic arrangement. Neither should be judged without asking what the assignment requires and what the interpreter actually knew at the time.

This demonstrates a fundamental difference between writing at a desk and performing across languages in real time. A writer can revise the entire sequence after seeing its end. A live interpreter often cannot. We should not treat a missed punch line as evidence of insufficient talent when the information needed to make the decision had not yet arrived. Likewise, we should not praise a risky invention merely because it caused a laugh in one performance. The interpreter has obligations to meaning, speakers, and audiences that may exceed the particular joke.

## The culture of the image and the culture of the sentence

An audiovisual work often keeps an image from one social setting and changes the language around it. This can produce a strange split. The translated characters may use an idiom familiar to the receiving audience while standing in a government office whose dress, architecture and procedures belong unmistakably to the source country. Sometimes this combination is simply the ordinary pleasure of watching foreign media. Sometimes it produces a contradiction the translation did not intend.

Suppose the cabinet joke is an attack on a specific civil service policy. A translated line that replaces the policy with an unrelated local scandal may create a laugh, but the set design and plot still point to the source institution. A viewer may wonder why the character is suddenly talking about the wrong government. The solution may be to keep the foreign policy recognizable and let the scene teach the relevant detail through action. Another solution may be an explicit adaptation in which the setting is remade, not merely dubbed. The categories matter: translation, adaptation, and local remake grant different kinds of artistic control.

This is why we should think of audiovisual comedy as coordinated channels. Speech, vocal performance, image, editing, soundtrack, and the audience's prior knowledge can reinforce one another or compete for attention. Not every joke uses every channel. A wordless gag might be easy to carry across languages but culturally delicate. A verbal pun might require complete rebuilding despite an unchanged image. A comedian's distinctive accent might be essential to character; dubbing it into a different regional stereotype could distort the social hierarchy of the story.

## An edit-room experiment

Take our imaginary filing-cabinet scene and prepare three English-language adaptation briefs. For subtitles, write the shortest speech and sign text that preserve the contrast between promised simplicity and actual complexity. For dubbing, write a line that leaves room for the original actor's hesitation and does not explain the cabinet before viewers see it. For live interpretation, decide what can safely be announced before the speaker's punch line arrives and what cannot. Do not translate into a language you do not know and call the result authentic. Work in English first to make the constraints explicit, then have qualified language practitioners test real versions.

For each brief, annotate the moment when the audience first receives the information necessary for the joke. Is it in the speech? On the sign? In the clerk's expression? In the prior episode? Now imagine moving a subtitle two seconds earlier, shortening the speaker's line, or cutting away from the clerk. Identify which alteration changes comprehension, which changes rhythm, and which changes character. These are distinct consequences that a single humor score would conceal.

A final production question is whether the audience has room to notice the repair. Suppose the clerk finally opens a cabinet and finds a card that says, "Please return to the beginning." If the original actor smiles at this command, the scene becomes a portrait of someone delighted by a system the audience finds absurd. If the actor closes the cabinet in defeat, the scene becomes an expression of institutional exhaustion. A translation that preserves all words but makes those performances sound identical has lost a meaningful distinction. Comic timing is not merely the instant before a laugh. It is the entire organization of attention and interpretation that allows an action to become a joke.

## Companion: sources and production boundaries

All characters, government offices, dialogue, image descriptions, and adaptation briefs are original. Numerical subtitle limits are deliberately not stated, because specific delivery standards differ and require separate production specifications. This lecture describes professional constraints and scholarly approaches, not the outcomes of an experimental comparison among dubbing, subtitling and interpreting.

### Workshop

Use a 20- to 30-second original comic scene. Mark when verbal, visual and contextual information becomes available. Draft a captioning version and a dubbing brief, including the actor's intended emotional stance. State what the translators may and may not edit. Assess changes to character and target separately from the immediate laugh.
