# The Harold and the Game of the Scene: Del Close, iO, and the Long-Form Argument

A performer enters an empty stage carrying an imaginary plant. She tells an unseen visitor, “This fern is the only employee who has never requested a raise.” Her colleague replies, “It's also the only employee in management.” The audience laughs. A few minutes later, a different scene features a town hall debating whether garden soil should be eligible for a pension. Later still, a character describes their grandmother as the only person in the family who receives sunlight for doing nothing. No one has planned a linear story about one fern. Yet a pattern begins to appear: institutions repeatedly mistake plants for members of the workforce, and people repeatedly behave as though being alive is equivalent to being employed.

A short improvisational scene might have ended after the first laughter. In a long-form performance, the ensemble can return to the idea in altered settings and discover relationships nobody wrote in advance. The pleasure is not just that a joke is repeated. We begin to recognize an underlying preoccupation that appears in different disguises. We may also feel an emerging structure: scenes that did not seem to belong together become parts of a larger argument about what it means to count as useful.

This aspiration is central to the long-form improvisational traditions associated with Del Close and Charna Halpern. It also illuminates a difference between two senses of the word game. The first is a theatrical game played under visible rules. The second is the repeating logic that makes a particular scene funny. Both are useful. Confusing them, however, turns a living method into a set of slogans.

## How a long form becomes possible

Del Close was a performer, director, and teacher who worked in several American theater communities, including the Compass Players, The Second City, and the San Francisco company The Committee. Versions of a long-form structure called the Harold developed around Close and collaborators at The Committee in the late 1960s. Close later worked with Charna Halpern at Chicago's ImprovOlympic, subsequently known as iO, where the Harold became an influential teaching and performance form. A standard reference is the 1994 book Truth in Comedy, co-authored by Halpern, Close, and Kim “Howard” Johnson.

The history matters because a genre often retroactively acquires a single inventor in stories told by its descendants. Close was a central architect and evangelist of long form, but performers, colleagues, teachers, directors, and institutions also shaped the form. A training manual is evidence of a method as taught, not proof that every company performed identical structures or shared a single artistic doctrine. The Harold itself has varied across ensembles and periods.

In a familiar classroom description, a Harold begins with a suggestion and an opening that generates associations. Scenes develop from the opening; group games or interludes connect parts of the performance; later rounds return to and transform material. A compact schematic may name three rounds of scenes and two group games. But this is a teaching map, not a universal mechanical obligation, and stage groups often bend or abandon the arrangement. A musician learns scales in order to play; a Harold performer learns a form in order to discover where form can carry something unexpected.

A central demand of this kind of performance is memory. The actor must remain intensely present in a moment while retaining enough of earlier moments to recognize an opportunity for return. One person may remember the fern. Another may remember that the phrase “management” had unexpectedly acquired a literal agricultural meaning. A third may notice that every scene has involved an institutional category applied to the wrong kind of being. None is required to announce the theme. The theme can be made visible through selective recurrence.

## The first sense of game: a theatrical activity

Imagine the ensemble plays a round in which every character must describe their workplace by comparing it to a garden. The constraint might lead to a series of exchanges. A librarian says the catalog needs weeding. A mechanic complains that the apprentices have not put down roots. A chef calls the restaurant compost. This is a game in the obvious sense: there is a rule, participants follow it, and their inventive responses generate pleasure. We could time the round or award points if the performance called for it.

The downside of a simple game is that everyone may understand the rule after one turn. The remaining lines then compete only to be the cleverest variation. An audience can enjoy that, but it may be difficult to sustain for a full dramatic scene. More serious difficulties appear if the comparison is imposed from outside while the characters have no reason to care about one another. The rule generates language, not necessarily relationships.

The second sense of game starts from something more particular. In our fern scene, a manager is sincerely treating a plant as an employee. The behavior is strange, but the character's attitude is coherent. They may be delighted to promote the fern, terrified of its union representative, or ashamed that their quarterly figures depend on photosynthesis. The game of this scene is not “say plant words.” It is “apply workplace bureaucracy earnestly to plant life, and discover what that commits the characters to.” The performer can now generate consequences that are specific to the world.

For example, the fern has never requested vacation, so management awards it all the unused leave from the other employees. That creates a staffing crisis. A colleague tries to prove that the fern has secretly been absent for years, but the time clock records perfect attendance because someone waters it every morning. The scene can heighten without becoming merely louder. Each invention deepens the institutional absurdity and changes someone's practical problem.

## Close's larger ambition

Long-form advocates did not simply want to produce the largest number of punch lines per minute. Close and Halpern sought a form in which improvised scenes could have thematic and emotional reach, with performers discovering connections over time. That artistic ambition gives the Harold a different objective from a rapid-fire contest of isolated games, although a long-form show can be extremely funny and a short-form game can be emotionally revealing. The distinction is a difference of emphasis, not a ranking of artistic worth.

A useful comparison is musical variation. The first appearance of a motif creates a memory. The second introduces a relation. A later appearance can change how the first is understood. If a scene about the fern returns unchanged, it is a callback. If it returns after a scene in which a human worker is treated like an object, it may become a critique of the same institution from the opposite direction. The plant is promoted precisely because it cannot complain; the human is punished precisely because they can. The two scenes illuminate one another.

This is not the same as claiming the audience always infers a philosophical thesis. Some people will simply enjoy the escalations. Others will notice a theme, and the theme might have emerged accidentally. The value of long form is its ability to make such relations available, not its power to guarantee a shared interpretation. A writer using the method on the page should therefore resist explaining every recurrence. The structure can do some of the explanatory work.

## The Upright Citizens Brigade and an explicit scene game

A later instructional lineage associated with the Upright Citizens Brigade made the “game of the scene” an especially teachable concept. The UCB Comedy Improvisation Manual, by founding members Matt Besser, Ian Roberts, and Matt Walsh, gives sustained attention to discovering a scene's comic pattern, exploring its logic, and heightening it. The school's association with the word game does not mean that UCB invented the idea of repeating comic behavior. Its contribution lies in a distinctive, explicit pedagogy within long-form improvisation.

Take a scene in which an ordinary office employee treats a tiny inconvenience as if it were a major public emergency. Their stapler is empty, so they demand a formal inquiry. Their coffee is lukewarm, so they order the evacuation of the building. The game is not “workplace comedy.” It is the more specific behavior: this person responds to trivial office conditions using the machinery of state crisis management. Once an audience recognizes the pattern, a subsequent variation creates anticipation. What will they do when a printer jams? Perhaps they convene a tribunal at which the printer has legal counsel.

Heightening does not merely mean selecting a larger noun. If the first problem is a stapler, the second must not automatically be a hurricane. The scale of reaction may grow, but the essential absurdity is the mismatch between minor workplace problems and grand official responses. The game fails when it abandons the original relationship. A sensible writer can test each new beat with a simple question: is it another manifestation of the same behavior, or only a stranger event?

Sometimes a performer identifies the pattern too quickly. They decide in advance that the manager “is always dramatic,” then repeat dramatic statements without noticing the partner's resistance or the particular circumstances. A general personality label is not yet a usable comic mechanism. The precise behavior matters because it predicts a direction for the scene. The partner also matters: a calm employee who repeatedly interprets emergencies as routine requests gives the game a different shape from an employee who becomes an enthusiastic deputy.

This is a pedagogical simplification with real benefits. Beginners learn to recognize what an audience has been invited to expect. But one can overtrain pattern recognition. Some scenes are funny because of a delicate relationship, a sudden truthful admission, a physical discovery, or a one-time reversal. Forcing every successful improvisation into a named game can obscure those qualities. A school provides a lens, and a lens may become a blindfold if treated as total theory.

## The productive disagreement between structure and discovery

Consider two ways to continue the fern material. In version one, the actors deliberately heighten its institutional treatment. It receives a performance review, demands a better window, chairs a committee, and finally fires the manager for insufficient oxygen. The progression is coherent and may build to a strong climax. In version two, the fern disappears after the first scene. A later scene about a family struggling to care for an aging relative introduces a very different kind of duty. Only near the end does someone return to the fern, and the audience suddenly sees a relation between care and employment. The second version may be less predictable and more emotionally complex. It also carries more risk: the connections may never become legible.

This is not an absolute divide between UCB and iO, nor a claim that their practitioners always choose separate strategies. Both training lineages have used structures, callbacks, games, relationships, and unexpected connections. The useful tension is between making a pattern explicit enough to develop reliably and leaving it open enough to discover something unplanned. Writers need both skills. Sometimes the draft needs clearer mechanics; sometimes it needs room to surprise its author.

Research on group creativity offers a complementary perspective. R. Keith Sawyer argues that creative products can emerge from interaction rather than from the plan of a single controlling mind. Performers build on and transform each other's contributions in real time. This does not mean every ensemble decision is good, nor that hierarchy disappears. It means the final scene can contain structures no participant intended at the beginning. A rehearsal can reveal a theme that cannot be located in any one person's initial suggestion.

For an individual writer, the analogy is instructive even without a cast. You can write successive scenes from different points of view, then read them for latent connections. Perhaps the clerk's response to the fern echoes an earlier joke about someone being called a replaceable part. Instead of inventing a master theme first, let a theme emerge from the material, then revise the draft to make the relation visible without turning it into a lecture.

## A long-form exercise on paper

Choose three apparently unrelated scenes. One is a customer returning a houseplant to a shop because it has no career ambition. Another is a teacher whose classroom is run by an elaborate attendance machine. The third is a family trying to schedule a birthday around a computer's maintenance window. Give each scene a plain human want. The customer wants a refund; the teacher wants to teach; the family wants to celebrate. Do not make them comment on each other yet.

Write each scene until a repeated kind of social behavior emerges. Then identify one comic game in precise language: perhaps somebody consistently treats living people as equipment or treats equipment as living people. Write a heightening sequence for just one of the scenes, preserving its human stakes. Next, find a connection between two scenes that is thematic rather than merely verbal. You might have the family behave with more warmth toward their computer than the school administrator shows toward children. Finally, return to an image from the first scene in a context that changes its meaning.

Read the sequence aloud. If every scene feels like another pun, the recurrence has been too narrow. If nothing connects, the recurrence may be too faint. If the audience understands the theme only because a character announces it, the shape may need revision. A long form asks for a delicate balance: enough repetition to create recognition, enough difference to make the recognition matter.

The Harold's enduring lesson is not a compulsory arrangement of rounds and games. It is the possibility that comedy can accumulate significance through pattern, variation, and ensemble memory. UCB-style game analysis offers a disciplined way to find and develop one pattern inside a scene. Del Close's larger long-form ambition invites us to hear what happens when patterns across scenes begin to converse. The best use of either method is to learn when its tools deepen a discovery—and when the discovery should be allowed to escape the tool.

## Companion: terminology and caution

- **Harold:** A family of long-form structures, not one immutable historically continuous template. The brief three-round description in this lecture is instructional, not normative.
- **Theatrical game:** A visibly constrained exercise or performance event.
- **Game of the scene:** A particular repeatable comic behavior and its consequences, emphasized in UCB teaching, not synonymous with the Harold's structural interludes.
- **Heightening:** Development of the established pattern, not merely louder acting or bigger nouns.
- **Original examples:** The fern-in-management, emergency-stapler, and houseplant-return scenes are new didactic fiction.
