# Who Gets to Be Ridiculous? Feminist Comedy, the Unruly Woman, and Queer Camp

**Course:** Comedy: Theory, History, and Craft  
**Section:** Modern schools of thought - feminist and queer comic criticism  
**Status:** Substantive, audio-first manuscript

Imagine a ceremonial awards dinner held by the Society for Perfect Composure. At the entrance, a sign requests that everyone speak softly, never interrupt, and express approval through one restrained nod. The evening's master of ceremonies is preparing to announce the prize for Most Appropriate Public Behavior. Before the announcement can begin, the guest of honor enters with an extravagant hat shaped like a small dining table. She tries to take her seat. The hat catches on the chandelier. She asks the chandelier to move. The chandelier does not respond. She accuses it of poor manners.

There are several jokes here. A large object occupies space intended for a small object. A person solemnly treats a chandelier as if it could choose to be polite. The audience also knows something about the event's rules. The organizers have defined their institution through quiet restraint, and their distinguished guest is spectacularly incapable of disappearing into the background. If we remove the setting, the hat may still be funny. But the challenge to authority becomes harder to see.

A considerable body of late twentieth-century feminist and queer criticism asks us to focus on that relationship. Who gets permission to take up space? Which bodies are made to look dignified, and which are treated as embarrassing? When someone exaggerates a social role, is the result only a joke about the performer, or does it expose how artificial the role was all along? A conventional account of incongruity can describe the mismatch between the hat and the ceremony. It cannot, by itself, tell us why the same mismatch might feel liberating to one audience and humiliating to another.

This is a lecture about two overlapping but distinct intellectual traditions. Feminist comedy criticism examines how laughter interacts with gender, representation, power, and the construction of respectable behavior. Queer approaches to camp examine exaggerated style, performance, and the instability of apparently natural identities. Neither is a single unified theory. Both have internal arguments. Their shared starting point is that audiences learn social expectations before a joke begins, and that comedy can put those expectations on display.

## The figure of the unruly woman

In 1995, film scholar Kathleen Rowe, later publishing as Kathleen Rowe Karlyn, brought together a set of arguments in a book titled The Unruly Woman: Gender and the Genres of Laughter. Her examples include performers and characters such as Mae West, Roseanne, and Miss Piggy. The book asks what happens when a woman makes herself conspicuous and refuses the rules of feminine containment. Rowe's unruly woman can be loud, excessive, physically imposing, sexually outspoken, and unwilling to defer to people who expect to regulate her behavior. Those features are not universal qualities of comic women. They are a critical pattern Rowe uses to explore a historical problem.

The history of comic bodies is also a history of who controls the joke: a performer may be turned into an object of ridicule without having any say in how the joke is told. In some scenes, the audience is invited to ridicule a woman for violating a narrow ideal. In others, the performer controls the violation, exposes the narrowness of the ideal, and makes the people enforcing it appear absurd. The same visible gesture can participate in opposite distributions of power.

Think of our woman with the chandelier hat. Suppose the camera repeatedly cuts to guests whispering, 'She should know better.' The music invites us to cringe at her humiliation. The ceremony's rules remain unquestioned. In a second version, the woman enters with confidence, discovers the chandelier problem, and persuades the guests that anyone who installed a chandelier at hat height should resign from the Society for Perfect Composure. Now the institution becomes the comic object. The prop is the same. The relation between performer, audience, and norm has changed.

Neither version must be politically innocent or politically transformative. The first could invite sympathy as well as embarrassment. The second could simply sell a reassuring story in which one heroic individual defeats a pompous institution. The point is that we can describe the direction of laughter with more precision when we ask who controls the scene and who is entitled to declare a violation.

Rowe connects such characters to a longer critical vocabulary about carnival, inversion, and the body. The broad idea is that a comic body can disrupt a hierarchy by eating, speaking, moving, or desiring in ways official decorum excludes. But carnival does not automatically produce lasting social change. An institution may tolerate one evening of inversion precisely because it expects order to return the following morning. A rebellious character can become a profitable convention. Criticism has to follow the joke beyond the moment when someone overturns a table.

A writing technique follows. Give a comic character a rule that other characters pretend is natural. Then let the character expose the labor needed to enforce that rule. For instance, at our dinner, the master of ceremonies insists that guests never raise their voices. When asked why, he must shout over a malfunctioning microphone. The person violating the rule may reveal the person whose authority depended on it. The joke does not require the audience to agree with every decision made by the rebellious guest. Its structure gives them something specific to compare.

## The performer who makes the category unstable

In 2004, Joanne R. Gilbert published Performing Marginality: Humor, Gender, and Cultural Critique, a rhetorical study of women performing stand-up comedy. One of the book's valuable questions concerns the stage position itself. The comic is speaking as an individual, but the audience may treat the comic as a representative of a gender, class, or community. Her personal material is therefore heard under constraints that other performers may not experience in the same way. A remark about ordinary domestic inconvenience can be treated as a statement about all women. A comic may exploit that expectation, refuse it, or shift between the two during a routine.

Consider a fictional performer who begins, 'People always ask me how I manage everything.' The line could introduce a conventional joke about juggling responsibilities. She continues: 'I tell them the secret is to have everyone else lower their standards. The house is immaculate if you only inspect the ceiling.' A listener may recognize a familiar expectation that a competent person must maintain a flawless home. The joke shifts the location of evaluation. Instead of proving competence under the original standard, the performer invents a ridiculous standard and evaluates herself with it.

Now consider delivery. With nervous self-mockery, the line might reinforce the idea that the performer is failing. With a ceremonious tone, as though announcing a Nobel Prize, the same words can ridicule the demand for total competence. On stage, one gesture or pause can signal whose viewpoint is being performed. A transcript alone cannot always settle the question.

Gilbert's analysis is not a rule that every joke by a marginalized performer is an act of resistance. Comedians can repeat familiar stereotypes, turn them against an audience, use them strategically to gain access to a stage, or reject the demand to discuss identity at all. Critics who assume that every woman comic must be politically instructive can reproduce another form of confinement. The performer may want to be silly, aggressive, sentimental, absurd, or technically experimental. A theory of comic freedom should have room for all of those choices.

## Camp as a way of reading the world

Now the guests at our dinner make an unexpected decision. They stop fighting the chandelier. The Society announces that gigantic furniture hats are now part of its ancient tradition. Everyone puts on a miniature cardboard dining table and poses for photographs. The rules of composure are still being enforced, but their artificiality is impossible to ignore. The institution has begun performing its own seriousness as spectacle.

This brings us toward camp. In 1964 Susan Sontag published Notes on 'Camp,' an essay describing a sensibility drawn to artifice, exaggeration, stylization, and things whose seriousness may be experienced in ways their makers did not intend. The essay helped bring camp into prominent critical discussion. Sontag was describing an attitude toward objects and performances as well as a set of examples. Camp may involve a loving response to an overdesigned building, an operatic performance, a flamboyant costume, or a spectacular failure to achieve the kind of dignity a creator imagined.

Camp is not a synonym for a punch line. An object can be campy without anyone telling a verbal joke. Some camp requires affection for the excessive object rather than a wish to defeat it. A performer can deliberately cultivate a camp style, and an audience can read an earnest artifact as camp even if its creator never intended that response. Camp therefore belongs partly to the audience's reading of an object, not merely to the intention of the person who made it.

There is a tension inside that description. If a critic treats camp primarily as a sophisticated way of enjoying artifice, what happens to the communities for whom exaggerated style has been a resource for living under hostile social expectations? Camp has histories in queer performance, especially in contexts where identity could not always be stated plainly and where parody or theatrical exaggeration could create recognition among insiders. Calling camp merely 'style' risks losing that history.

The 1994 collection The Politics and Poetics of Camp, edited by Morris Meyer, places such disagreements at its center. Its contributors ask whether camp is a detachable aesthetic manner or a form of cultural critique tied to queer discourse and performance. The question has no single answer that covers every object and audience. Mass entertainment can borrow camp signs while discarding their social context. Queer performers can also deliberately circulate those signs beyond a small community, often for their own purposes. A style can travel while its meanings change.

Return to our Society for Perfect Composure. One audience sees the enormous hats as a simple joke about ridiculous fashion. Another recognizes the hats as an exaggerated version of the ceremony's impossible demand to look effortlessly respectable. Still another finds genuine pleasure in the audacity of their design. A writer or performer can invite all three responses, but none is guaranteed. The joke's meanings depend partly on what the audience knows about the conventions being imitated.

## How quotation becomes a comic action

Camp often works through quotation without quotation marks. A performer can reproduce a familiar posture, gesture, or line with just enough exaggeration to reveal it as a performance. Imagine a fictional mayor walking onto the awards stage. He clasps his hands, bows to the audience, pauses exactly three seconds, and announces, 'I am humbled by how much humility I possess.' His movements are rehearsed with such precision that the pose of spontaneous modesty becomes the source of comedy.

A person can be pretentious without being camp. The camp reading becomes more plausible when the performance calls attention to its own stylization and invites us to appreciate the constructed image. Similarly, parody may criticize a specific original, whereas camp can preserve affection for the gestures it exaggerates. It is useful to ask two separate questions: What convention is being quoted? And does the performance invite ridicule, pleasure, identification, criticism, or several at once?

These are also questions about representation. The audience may laugh at a performer who is choosing to exaggerate an identity. Or it may laugh because it believes the identity is naturally ridiculous. A parody of a restrictive norm can be misread as proof that the people governed by the norm deserve mockery. Performance creates possibilities for alternative readings without granting the writer full control over reception.

The stakes change as contexts change. A theatrical performance among people who share a code can depend on elaborate implication. A short clip detached from the scene may make the same gesture look like an insult. The tension between in-group recognition and broad circulation is not new, but contemporary recording and distribution make it particularly visible. An intelligent criticism of camp listens for what has been removed as well as what survives.

## Two experiments in comic authority

Let us write two short scenes from the same situation. A person is instructed to dress appropriately for a formal interview. In the first scene, the interviewer's demand is the only visible standard. The applicant arrives wearing an extravagant cape and the interviewer laughs. As written, the applicant's appearance may be the entire joke. That is a familiar use of ridicule and might serve a particular story, but its social direction is narrow.

In the second scene, the applicant arrives in an ordinary suit. The interviewer announces that a serious candidate would have chosen the exact shade of gray that communicates ambition without arrogance. The applicant asks whether ambition comes in a paint catalog. The interviewer produces one. The suit becomes a gateway into an entire bureaucracy of respectable appearance. If the applicant later walks back in wearing a cape constructed from paint swatches, the cape is no longer a random sign of foolishness. It quotes the rules so extravagantly that the rules become visible as theater.

A third version can use camp rather than direct satire. Everyone in the office is delighted by the capes. They have elaborate names for the most magnificent folds of fabric. The applicant discovers a collective pleasure in overperformance. The story need not conclude with the destruction of the dress code. It may simply create a world in which supposedly stable categories are available for invention and play.

Notice that these versions demand different skill from the performer. The first needs a readable costume. The second needs a precise social rule, an escalating series of enforcement mechanisms, and an applicant who understands the absurdity before the audience gets bored. The third needs a style the audience can enjoy for longer than a single surprise. Repetition must vary the spectacle rather than merely increase the number of capes.

Feminist criticism will ask who writes the rule, who bears its cost, and who commands attention when it breaks. A camp reading may ask what pleasure is available in the rule's artificial signs, and what identities the signs make possible. A purely structural joke analysis may ask where the expectation is established and overturned. The approaches illuminate different layers of a single scene. Their coexistence is more useful than forcing one to swallow the others.

## Laughter, freedom, and the return of the rule

Our imaginary awards dinner ends with a final complication. The Society for Perfect Composure declares the extravagant-hat episode a triumph of its commitment to diversity. It establishes a mandatory annual festival of unruliness, with a dress code and an approved list of spontaneous gestures. Anyone who breaks the festival rules will be expelled for insufficient originality.

This ending introduces a possibility critical theory cannot ignore. A gesture of rebellion can be adopted by the institution it mocked. A comic image can challenge an authority in one setting and become branding for that authority in another. Conversely, a performer may appropriate a conventional costume and use it to create unexpected solidarity among people who were excluded from the old ceremony. The meaning resides in the evolving relation among gesture, institution, audience, and performer.

When studying gender and camp, therefore, do not stop at asking whether a joke is funny or progressive. Ask whose ordinary behavior is being treated as ridiculous. Ask whether excess is imposed on a character or chosen by that character. Ask which rules survive the laughter. And ask whether pleasure itself is part of the work, rather than a disposable cover for a political argument.

The most durable contribution of these modern critical traditions is a richer account of comic agency. A ridiculous image is not self-explanatory. Someone designs it, someone performs it, someone names it ridiculous, and someone decides what the audience is laughing at. Comedy can reinforce a boundary, walk across it, decorate it, or reveal that nobody knew who had drawn it in the first place. Those are different achievements, and we can write more carefully once we learn to hear the difference.

| Question | What to inspect |

| Who sets the standard? | Which character or institution defines normal behavior? |
| Who controls the excess? | Does a character choose to be conspicuous or become the butt of a joke? |
