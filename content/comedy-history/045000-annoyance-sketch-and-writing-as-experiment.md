# The Writer's Improv Laboratory: Annoyance, Sketch, and Competing Ways to Build a Joke

**Course:** Comedy: Theory, History, and Craft  
**Section:** Modern comedy writing schools, actor-centered choices, and revision  
**Status:** Substantive audio-first manuscript

A receptionist at an awards ceremony is told that the event has no winners. The organizing committee has concluded that recognizing anyone would create an unfair distinction. Unfortunately, the trophies have already been manufactured, the orchestra has rehearsed its triumphant music, and the audience has purchased tickets to watch someone receive a prize. The receptionist must keep the ceremony moving while ensuring that nothing happens that could be interpreted as achievement.

What should a comedy writer do with this premise? There are at least four plausible answers. One writer makes the receptionist's anxiety the center of the scene. Another looks for a repeatable game: every attempt to avoid ranking people creates a more elaborate ranking system. A third puts the actors in a room and asks them to improvise an actual awards presentation, discovering physical details that the outline missed. A fourth devises an escalating series of sketch beats on paper, polishing each line until the ending becomes unavoidable. Those approaches may produce noticeably different performances from the same starting point.

The competing schools of contemporary improvisation and comedy writing are best understood as collections of problems and methods, not a tournament with one winner. Chicago and New York institutions such as The Second City, iO, Upright Citizens Brigade, and The Annoyance share personnel and history but emphasize different aspects of craft. It is tempting to reduce each to a slogan, then declare one slogan correct. That destroys what is valuable about comparison: each method directs attention toward something a writer might otherwise miss.

## When an actor needs permission to be specific

One challenge of ensemble improvisation is that a performer may become too cautious. If every choice must be easily accepted by a partner, the actor can hover in a bland neutrality. “I think something might be wrong with the awards,” says the receptionist. “Perhaps we should discuss it.” The dialogue is compatible with almost any future direction, but it offers little that another actor can grab. No physical action, emotional position, or social pressure has been established.

The Annoyance Theatre in Chicago, founded by artistic director Mick Napier, has emphasized strong individual choices and the performer's point of view. Its official training materials explicitly say that they challenge conventional improv rules, and describe teaching influenced by Napier's book Improvise: Scene from the Inside Out. Its history also includes using improvisation to devise original scripted theater. The school's emphasis offers a useful corrective to a misunderstood version of ensemble agreement in which the actor waits for a partner to provide permission before being interesting.

Imagine the receptionist makes an immediate, specific choice: she is convinced that the only way to keep the awards ceremony fair is to personally wear all the trophies. She begins attaching imaginary medals, growing heavier with every announcement. Her conviction is visible even before the next line. A partner can accept or contest that behavior. Perhaps the chairperson solemnly congratulates her for absorbing the inequality; perhaps an audience member protests that wearing more trophies still constitutes winning. The scene now has a body, a point of view, and something to oppose.

This choice is not automatically superior. It could dominate the scene so completely that nobody else can contribute. It could also feel arbitrary if we never understand what the receptionist believes. The goal is not self-expression at any cost. It is an actor's responsibility to bring something specific enough to support genuine interaction. In improvisation, a weak offer may be as unhelpful as a blocked one. On the page, a writer can test whether a character has a concrete behavior beyond “be concerned” or “be funny.”

## Three meanings of a good first beat

Suppose we begin with the receptionist instructing nominees to place their own trophies into a communal box. A game-focused writer may identify the pattern as “the committee tries to eliminate competition by inventing new competitive procedures.” The next beat might require nominees to compete to prove they are equally deserving. After that, judges rate who has shown the greatest indifference to winning. The escalation is coherent because each new procedure contradicts the supposed principle more extravagantly.

A character-focused writer may ask why this receptionist obeys. Perhaps she has never won an award and is relieved that nobody else will either. The first beat is then not just a premise but the visible start of an emotional defense. At first she is officiously pleased. Later she realizes she has become the only person onstage making the event possible. Her insistence on fairness covers a longing to be noticed. An ending could have her quietly giving the smallest trophy to a stagehand who repaired the microphone. The emotional turn changes what the earlier absurdity meant.

An ensemble-devising writer may put performers in an imagined hall and watch what they do with space. A nominee attempts to walk to the podium, but the receptionist physically redirects them onto a treadmill so nobody moves farther than anybody else. The audience cannot see the treadmill unless the actors make it real through consistent bodily action. A second performer tries to clap and is instructed to distribute applause evenly across the room. Suddenly the scene contains physical comedy that an outline of verbal jokes might never have suggested.

These methods do not necessarily exclude one another. The game may reveal character. A physical discovery may provide the ending. But they enter the problem through different doors, and each can become a failure mode when used mechanically. Game writers may overvalue predictable repetition. Character writers may produce an affecting monologue that forgets to be comic. Ensemble writers may mistake an enjoyable rehearsal for a well-shaped audience experience. The practice of craft is partly learning which failure is appearing.

## Why a sketch needs a shape

A sketch is not a stand-up routine divided among several voices. In a conventional sketch, characters generally occupy a sustained situation and their actions let the audience observe a comic principle. There is no single mandatory architecture. Nevertheless, a useful way to examine a draft is to distinguish the establishing beat, the development, the shift, and the exit.

The establishing beat makes a promise to the audience. In our ceremony, the organization wants to celebrate achievement while denying the distinction that makes an award meaningful. The development shows repeated attempts to carry out that contradiction. A shift changes the stakes or interpretation. Perhaps the committee declares a winner after all: the person who most successfully avoided winning. The exit provides a satisfying final action. Perhaps the orchestra plays a fanfare for the only untouched trophy, an object left on the stage after everyone has gone home.

This sounds like a simple four-part outline, but the labels can hide bad writing. A shift is not effective merely because it arrives late. If the chairperson suddenly reveals they are an alien, the event has changed without becoming more meaningful. A stronger shift follows from the situation: in order to prevent winners, the committee must identify the best nonwinner. This is both an extension of the premise and a contradiction of its stated purpose. The ending should exploit what the audience has learned, not abandon it for a stranger subject.

A written sketch can also change shape through emotional recognition. If we realize that the receptionist is using procedural fairness to avoid admitting envy, the final gesture may be quiet rather than explosive. The writer's task is to decide which form of resolution the material has earned. Ending with the biggest laugh is one possible choice; ending with a meaningful reversal that leaves the room laughing a moment later is another. The scene can be edited toward either end.

## Scripted comedy and the myth of total control

Improvisational advocates sometimes contrast the living response of the ensemble with the dead certainty of a script. Written comedy advocates may return the insult: an improvised joke is only a first draft nobody had time to improve. Both caricatures miss the actual work. An actor can discover something meaningful through repeated performance of a fixed text; a written sketch can contain passages generated in rehearsal; and a highly structured improvisation can involve learned patterns no less than a scripted scene.

Researcher R. Keith Sawyer's work on group creativity examines emergence: the way a product can develop through interaction rather than unfolding from one person's fully specified plan. That observation helps explain why group rehearsal can reveal relationships a solo writer did not anticipate. But it does not establish that every group brainstorm is more creative than individual writing or that editing damages spontaneous invention. An improvised process can be collaborative and still produce a dull scene. Revision can be deliberate and still preserve surprise.

Generating possibilities and selecting among them are different phases of the work. If a writer edits every line while trying to invent the premise, they may suppress ideas too soon. If an ensemble refuses to edit because the first attempt felt spontaneous, it may leave a promising idea buried beneath repetition. Skilled writers vary the balance of generation and evaluation. They are not required to pledge allegiance to one phase as the authentic form of creativity.

## A comparison through three revised endings

Consider three endings to the award ceremony. In the first, the chairperson announces that every nominee is equally victorious and orders everyone to step onto the same small podium. The platform collapses. This is a physical consequence of an absurd policy. It may be immediately satisfying and can be staged with a clearly established imaginary weight, but it probably says little about the receptionist's motives.

In the second, the receptionist has been trying to distribute all trophies evenly. At last she notices that the stagehand has done all the work and received no recognition. She hands over a trophy, thus breaking the rule she has defended. The laugh might come from the chairperson's frantic attempt to define the gesture as “a noncompetitive allocation of symbolic metal.” The character's change makes the policy's absurdity more poignant.

In the third, a nominee refuses to accept any prize, insisting this would confer an unfair advantage. The committee creates an award for principled refusal. The nominee rejects it, and the committee invents another prize for maintaining consistency. The exit is the announcement of the world's first competitive championship in not competing. This is a game-centered escalation with little need for a psychological backstory. It can be excellent if the actors maintain their sincere bureaucratic attitudes.

These are not three stages in a hierarchy. They are three valid aesthetic directions. A writer ought to choose based on the desired experience. If the scene's purpose is an escalating critique of perverse incentives, the third ending is strong. If it concerns the human cost of conformity, the second may do more work. If it is a physical farce designed to culminate in spectacle, the collapsing podium gives the performers an actionable image.

## Feedback is a theory of the audience

Different comedy schools also carry assumptions about the audience. A rapid game makes a pattern legible, then rewards recognition with variation. A long-form scene asks the audience to remember and discover a relation. A character-driven scene asks viewers to care how a person responds when the situation presses on a belief. A satirical revue may expect the audience to recognize a public institution, then rethink something about it. A physical ensemble piece can communicate across language barriers that frustrate a verbal sketch.

Which method is right therefore depends partly on what the audience can perceive in real time. An audience cannot rewind a live scene to inspect a clever detail. A dense string of conceptual turns may overwhelm listeners who are still learning the premise. A line that reads as brilliant on the page may have no effect if the actors have not established the situation it requires. Rehearsal is partly a test of information flow: does the audience have enough facts, at the right moment, to recognize the surprise?

A practical revision method is to watch for three different forms of silence. First, a silence of confusion: people have not understood who is speaking or what is at stake. Second, a silence of anticipation: they understand the pattern and are waiting to see where it will go. Third, a silence of resistance: they understand the joke but do not want to laugh, perhaps because the target or situation feels wrong. These are different diagnostic possibilities, not infallible categories. A room's reaction can be affected by fatigue, acoustics, prior expectations, and social risk. Treat audience feedback as evidence to interpret, not a machine that returns a final verdict on artistic value.

That helps prevent a tempting but damaging conclusion: a joke that failed once is necessarily poor, and a joke that succeeded once is necessarily durable. Different performances, contexts, and audiences create different results. A writer still has to decide what sort of work they wish to make. Learning from laughter does not require surrendering every other aim to it.

## A repeatable workshop for one person or twelve

Take the same awards ceremony and draft three versions. For the first, follow the game-focused question: what particular policy contradiction keeps recurring, and how can each beat expose a new consequence? Write four beats in which the characters remain earnest. For the second, give the receptionist a strong personal commitment before writing any jokes. Let someone else oppose it, and allow the commitment to become costly. For the third, write almost no dialogue. Describe the trophies, podium, applause, orchestra, and movements of the actors. Ask whether the physical space itself can make the policy absurd.

Then read all three drafts aloud. If possible, recruit one other reader who has never heard the premise. Mark the first point where they understand the governing contradiction, the first point where they can predict its recurrence, and the moment when the sketch surprises them despite that prediction. Move the setup earlier if comprehension is slow; consider a more specific behavior if prediction is impossible; reduce pure repetition if prediction becomes too easy.

Now combine only the strongest discoveries. Perhaps the receptionist's personal anxiety gives us a sympathetic center. Perhaps the game of competitive equality supplies three escalating beats. Perhaps the unseen podium supplies the exit. The result is not a compromise between schools in which every doctrine receives equal representation. It is a scene built by using different tools at the moments when each solves a real problem.

The broader lesson is that comedy writing is not located exclusively in the first inspiration, the ensemble rehearsal, the script, or the final audience response. It moves between these stages. Spontaneity discovers possibilities. Strong character choices make those possibilities consequential. Games make an intelligible comic logic repeat. Revision determines what the audience will experience and in what order. These approaches are not a single school, and none supplies a master formula for amusement. Together they provide a disciplined way to ask why a scene is alive, when it has become merely busy, and what the writer can do about the difference.

## Companion: compare methods without caricature

- **Annoyance / Napier:** Actor-centered choices and personal point of view, while maintaining ensemble response; its historically documented work includes using improv to devise scripted theater.
- **Second City:** Ensemble workshops, topical revue, and processes that can transform discovered material into fixed scenes.
- **iO / Close-Halpern lineage:** Long-form improvisation organized around ensemble discovery, connections, and thematic emergence.
- **UCB manual:** Explicit teaching of a scene's comic game and of consistent exploration and heightening.
- **Sketch writing:** Not a single proprietary school. Structures offered here are analytical tools, not mandatory rules or institutional claims.
- **Original example:** The fictional award ceremony, three endings, and rehearsal variants were written for this lecture.
