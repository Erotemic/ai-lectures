# The Cognitive Turn: Incongruity, Resolution, and Benign Violation

**Course:** Comedy: Theory, History, and Craft  
**Section:** Modern schools of thought — cognitive and experimental theories  
**Status:** Substantive, audio-first manuscript

A caretaker leaves a handwritten note on a museum door: "The exhibition of perfectly synchronized clocks is running a little late." The sentence invites a smile without using a pun in the strict sense. We know what a synchronized clock is supposed to do. We know what it means for an exhibition to run late. The mismatch is not random; one half of the sentence makes the other half seem newly awkward.

Now imagine a different note: "The exhibition of synchronized clocks has been replaced by a herd of llamas." This is more surprising. Is it funnier? Not necessarily. A surprise can be vivid and still fail as comedy. A contemporary cognitive theory must explain not only how a mind notices a mismatch but also what the mind does next, why some mismatches are pleasant, and why the same apparent joke changes across audiences and occasions.

From the 1970s through the early twenty-first century, researchers developed several approaches to that problem. They were not one unified movement. Some studied comprehension. Some studied emotional evaluation. Some measured how context and social distance change responses. Their shared move was to turn broad claims about incongruity into questions one could investigate using particular stimuli and observable reactions.

## Two operations, not one

Psychologist Jerry Suls proposed an influential two-stage account of joke appreciation in the early 1970s. In simplified form, a listener first detects a mismatch between an expectation and a punch line. The listener then searches for a rule or interpretation that makes the punch line intelligible. Satisfaction can come from discovering how the odd final line fits the setup after all.

The synchronized clocks sign works along those lines. The initial phrase supplies a norm of precision. The ending describes lateness. We recognize a relation between the expected precision of clocks and the disorganization of the people exhibiting them. The resolution is not a logical proof that clocks can never be displayed late. It is an intelligible reason to interpret the line as a joke about human inconsistency.

To hear how important the second stage is, consider this invented exchange. "Why did the gardener bring a ladder to the concert? Because the moon forgot its passport." We can detect an incongruity. But the answer does not offer a satisfying relation to gardener, ladder, or concert. Someone who enjoys absurdist comedy might find that refusal itself comic. Someone expecting a conventional riddle may simply be puzzled. The two responses expose a limitation in theories that require every joke to resolve its incongruity successfully.

Researchers, including Willibald Ruch, have distinguished audience preferences for incongruity-resolution humor from preferences for nonsense humor, where the apparent incongruity is left unresolved or only partly resolved. This does not divide the world's jokes into two neat boxes. It shows that some audiences enjoy recovering a coherent interpretation while others also enjoy the mind being kept in play. In performance, a joke can begin as a resolvable puzzle and then deliberately dismantle the explanation it seemed to promise.

## Prediction, inference, and what the audience already knows

Suppose you are writing a short comic scene about a restaurant that advertises lightning-fast service. An impatient customer asks how long lunch will take. The waiter says, "Only three minutes, once we decide what decade to open in." The turn is partly about expectation. The advertisement establishes a prediction. The waiter's remark reclassifies "fast service" as an absurd claim about an organization that never moves forward.

Change the line to "Our average service time is very short because we count only meals we successfully serve." This version is less surreal and more administrative. The customer can reconstruct the restaurant's accounting trick. It also introduces a recognizable incentive problem: a metric may reward hiding failures. What counts as a satisfying resolution depends on the listener's background. Someone who has worked with performance dashboards may infer the trick almost before the waiter finishes the sentence. Another listener may need one more sentence explaining the denominator. Too much explanation flattens the joke; too little can leave people without the intended bridge.

This is why cognitive accounts connect naturally to craft. Every comic setup manages information. You can ask exactly which belief the audience is likely to hold, which piece of information will challenge it, and whether the listener has time to make the new inference. But predicting a listener's belief is not easy. Audience cultures, genre expectations, and individual experience change what feels obvious.

A well-constructed reversal is neither simply unexpected nor simply comprehensible. It has to occupy a narrow region where a new interpretation is available at the right moment. Even there, it may fail because of tone, hostility, repetition, or lack of interest. The mind can solve a puzzle without enjoying the puzzle.

## The benign-violation proposal

In 2010, psychologists A. Peter McGraw and Caleb Warren published work on what they called benign violations. Their proposal took a step beyond surprise. Some events are amusing because they seem, at once, to violate a norm and to be acceptable or harmless in context. An offense with no sense of threat may be dull. A threat with no sense of safety may be frightening. Humor becomes possible when those perceptions coexist.

Their experiments examined moral violations. They investigated circumstances in which a norm-breaking situation might still be judged benign, including weak commitment to the violated norm, an alternative interpretation that makes the act acceptable, or psychological distance. Those specific experimental findings support a narrower claim than a universal explanation of every laugh. It is important not to inflate a model's demonstrated domain into proof that it covers every comic form.

Try a harmless example. A small town commissions a statue honoring its most punctual resident. At the unveiling, nobody arrives on time, including the honoree. One can laugh because the failure violates the occasion's norm without seriously hurting anyone. Now change the scene. The honoree misses the event because a family member is seriously ill. The same absence is no longer benign in the same way. A writer who treated "punctuality violation" as a mechanical recipe would miss the human change.

A second variation moves the attention away from the person's misfortune and toward the town council's response. They propose postponing the ceremony until the statue itself apologizes for being late. The joke's object becomes institutional absurdity, not a vulnerable family. No single revision guarantees laughter, but the exercise shows that changing the target and perceived stakes can change what the audience is being invited to approve.

## Distance is not simply 'more is better'

The phrase psychological distance is easy to misuse. It can refer to whether an event occurred a long time ago, far away, to a stranger, or in a hypothetical situation. McGraw and collaborators later examined how distance interacts with the severity of a violation. Their research on tragedies and minor mishaps suggested that greater distance can make severe events easier to treat humorously, while minor mishaps may require some closeness to remain interesting. This is not a permission slip for jokes about suffering. It is a claim about how perceived threat changes within particular experimental contexts.

Imagine an actor in an obviously fictional scene being drenched by a theatrical rain machine while delivering a speech about indoor weather control. Spectators may perceive both the violation and the safe frame. If an actual production assistant is hurt by a broken machine, the frame has changed. A director who insists that the same visual action must have the same comic effect misunderstands the theory. Physical similarity is not enough; the audience's knowledge of risk matters.

Distance can also change over time. An event once too close to laugh at may become more discussable. But some harms do not become harmless merely because years pass. People affected by an event may carry a different perspective from observers. The model is most useful when it forces us to ask whose sense of safety is being measured.

## Experiments do not write scripts

Experimental psychologists often ask participants to rate brief scenarios or indicate amusement. Such designs allow researchers to compare conditions while holding other features steady. That is a strength. But they also strip away elements essential to performed comedy: the voice, the relationship between characters, anticipation built over a half-hour, a crowded room, a performer with a known reputation, and the option for an audience to refuse an invitation to laugh.

A laboratory finding that one category of prompt elicited higher mean amusement is not evidence that every individual laughed or that the higher-rated item was artistically better. Ratings of amusement, audible laughter, comprehension, and willingness to share a joke are different variables. A humor study may measure only some of them. This is not a defect when the research question is narrow. The defect appears when we ask the experiment to answer a question it was not designed to test.

There is also the difference between explaining an ordinary humorous event and claiming the conditions are necessary and sufficient. The benign-violation framework is illuminating when a comic scene combines norm-breaking with a safe frame. But a gentle observation about the way two old friends share a habitual misunderstanding may not feel like any substantial violation. Conversely, an audience may perceive a safe violation and find it repetitive. The theoretical vocabulary can help diagnose a failure without guaranteeing a cure.

## A three-way workshop

Take this original premise: a company introduces a new system to reduce administrative paperwork. Every employee must complete a fourteen-page form describing how much paperwork the system has saved. Write three versions.

In the first version, maximize the clarity of resolution. The audience quickly sees how an administrative burden was created by the project designed to remove it. The exact contradiction is the point. In the second version, lean toward nonsense: the final form asks employees to estimate their average annual consumption of paperclips in imaginary units. Here the incoherence itself becomes part of the pleasure. In the third version, use a perceived harmless violation: a team turns the absurd form into a mock awards ceremony, in which the forms receive medals and employees remain safely in control of the ritual.

For each version, ask four questions. What does the audience expect just before the turn? What new inference does the turn permit? What norm, if any, feels violated? And what allows the audience to experience the violation as safe, enjoyable, or deliberately troubling? Then introduce a listener who does not trust the company, or one who lost a job to a previous efficiency campaign. The social facts change. Some endings may now read as satire, bitterness, or a joke at the wrong person's expense.

The point of the exercise is not to place a moral warning label on every gag. It is to recognize the number of moving parts hidden by the phrase "a surprising punch line." The audience predicts; notices a mismatch; searches for a relation; assesses stakes; remembers prior experiences; and interprets the social invitation. Those operations need not occur in a single universal order, and different kinds of comedy give them different weight.

Modern cognitive research has not abolished older theories. It has narrowed and sharpened their questions. The most useful conclusion is not that every joke is incongruity plus resolution, or violation plus safety. It is that these are separate, testable proposals about parts of comic experience. A writer who understands their differences can construct better experiments. A listener who understands their limitations can enjoy a good joke without mistaking a clever explanation for a law of nature.

Compare a coherent reversal, an unresolved absurdity, and a harmless norm violation using a single original premise. For each, record the predicted expectation, possible repair inference, perceived stakes, intended target, and what an unfamiliar audience would need to know.
