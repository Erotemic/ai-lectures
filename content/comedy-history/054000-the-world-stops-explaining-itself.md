# The World Stops Explaining Itself: Beckett, Ionesco, and the Comedy of Waiting

**Course:** Comedy: Theory, History, and Craft  
**Section:** Postwar absurdist theater and comic form  
**Status:** Substantive audio-first manuscript

Imagine a small theater. On stage are two people and an empty chair. One of them says that an inspector will arrive any moment. They straighten the chair, wipe it, and argue about whether it should face the door. They have no evidence the inspector is coming. In fact, neither can remember what the inspection is for. Every time somebody decides to leave, the other insists that leaving would be irresponsible because the inspector might arrive. At the end of the scene, an announcement says the inspection has been postponed. The two people are relieved. They have succeeded, they say, in making the appointment run smoothly.

Nothing supernatural has occurred. The audience has watched people turn an absence into an institution. This is an original scene, not an episode from a historical play. It supplies a small model for an important comic possibility: what if the organizing purpose of a scene is not merely delayed, as in ordinary farce, but becomes less credible the longer everyone obeys it?

An ordinary farce can begin with a misunderstanding and proceed toward exposure. A character has the wrong suitcase. We know what is in the suitcase, or eventually will. In a comedy of waiting, the suitcase may never be opened. The people carrying it become more revealing than its contents. Their insistence on behaving reasonably can itself become unreasonable.

The historical term most frequently attached to such work is *the Theatre of the Absurd*. It is important not to hear that as the name of a company, a manifesto all the writers signed, or a general synonym for anything strange. The critic Martin Esslin used the phrase in a 1960 essay and developed it in a 1961 book to describe a disparate group of playwrights, including Samuel Beckett and Eugène Ionesco. Much later scholarship has questioned both the breadth and the limits of the classification. That history matters to the comedy writer because a label applied by a critic can conceal meaningful differences between artistic practices.

The conventional picture of dramatic action begins with a person who wants something. Obstacles intervene; choices change the situation; consequences arrive. Absurdist theater can preserve a person's wanting while disturbing almost every assumption that makes this structure reassuring. The character may not know whether an obstacle exists. A promise may not be reliable. A conversation may continue without increasing anyone's understanding. Time passes without delivering the result time was supposed to bring. In this environment, a small physical act—moving a chair, adjusting a hat, inspecting a shoe—can acquire the weight of a major event.

Consider the scene with the inspector again. Its crucial detail is not the unseen official. It is the choreography of commitment. One character holds the chair steady while the other wipes the floor beneath it. The first objects that the chair is now too clean for the room. Both agree to dirty it slightly. An audience might laugh at each new correction, but a more complicated emotion grows beneath the laughter: why must these people perform obedience to a demand whose origin they cannot establish? The scene does not tell us whether the inspector is imaginary. Its power comes from letting the characters remain exposed to that uncertainty.

## Waiting as an action

Samuel Beckett's *Waiting for Godot* premiered in Paris on January fifth, 1953. Beckett wrote the play in French, and its later English text is not simply a neutral transfer from one language to another. The work gives us Vladimir and Estragon, whose waiting is constantly interrupted by practical business, comic exchanges, bodily discomfort, and encounters with other figures. A superficial description might say that nothing happens. On stage, however, things are continually attempted. Plans start. Fragments of memory compete. Somebody needs help. A small decision requires consultation. The fact that these actions do not add up to decisive forward movement is part of the play's design.

Think about the difference between stillness and repeated attempted departure. A person standing still conveys one piece of information. Two people who decide, argue, prepare to leave, and nevertheless remain convey a structure of conflict. The pattern can recur while changing the emotional stakes. The audience is not merely wondering whether an awaited visitor will arrive; it is watching people sustain one another, irritate one another, and fill time with forms of cooperation that may be all they have.

This is why it is dangerous to say that Beckett's comedy is simply a joke about meaninglessness. The same gestures can be read as failures of purpose or as demonstrations of human tenderness. Michael Y. Bennett's work on absurd literature calls attention to readings that locate relationship and defiant persistence where the influential Esslin account emphasized metaphysical anguish. A performance may have audiences laughing and grieving without resolving which response is correct. The absence of a solution does not compel the absence of feeling.

For a writer, this gives a precise problem to solve. If a scene will not resolve the thing it promises, what changes? One answer is the relationship. In the inspector scene, let the first person begin by giving orders. During the third attempt to reposition the chair, the second person becomes the only one who remembers the sequence of decisions. The apparent subordinate now controls the rules. When the announcement postpones the inspection, one person celebrates and the other looks lost. The task has organized their companionship. Removing it feels like a loss.

The audience has experienced development even without a conventional resolution. Repetition and stasis are not identical. Repetition can expose a difference we could not see the first time.

## Language behaving as a machine

Eugène Ionesco's *The Bald Soprano*, first staged in 1950, explores a different kind of comic breakdown. Rather than reducing language to silence, Ionesco lets language proliferate while social exchange ceases to work in the way we expect. Familiar domestic conversation, formulaic politeness, and apparently self-evident assertions gradually lose the capacity to convey meaningful distinctions. The people continue talking; their sentences cease to help them meet one another.

The comedian's tool here is not a random word generator. It is an extremely recognizable social pattern made visibly automatic. Imagine a customer entering an office for directions. The clerk says, 'The directory is for directions. To find directions, consult the directory.' The customer asks where the directory is. The clerk replies that the directory's location can be found in the directory. Each sentence follows the office's preferred syntax. Each answer is unusable. The clerk can sincerely believe that excellent service has been provided, while the audience recognizes a system that values the form of an answer over its function.

Now change the clerk's delivery. If the clerk is a sneering villain, the joke becomes a satire of hostile bureaucracy. If the clerk is terrified of speaking without an approved phrase, the scene can become painful. If the clerk and the customer gradually begin copying each other's syntax until neither can ask a new question, language itself becomes the visible event. The same script mechanism can support satire, tragicomedy, or absurdist performance depending on human stakes and staging.

A frequent mistake in writing this sort of scene is to make every sentence nonsensical from the beginning. That deprives the audience of a stable operation to recognize. We need to learn how the machine is meant to work before we can appreciate its failure. Give the clerk one functional answer. Give the customer a believable need. Then let the policy become increasingly self-referential while the staff member grows more proud of having followed it.

This creates a useful kind of escalation. In many jokes, escalation means louder, larger, faster, or more outrageous. Here escalation can mean more rigorous application of one broken principle. The clerk has a beautifully organized set of procedures for finding a directory. The procedure requires a directory. To obtain the directory, the customer must first complete a location request. The location request asks for the directory's location. Nothing needs to explode. The logic is already doing the work.

## What the historical category hides

Esslin's framework was influential because it offered an explanation for why audiences could be amused by plays that frustrated established theatrical expectations. But not all writers so labeled saw themselves as participants in one artistic movement, and their contexts were not the same. Beckett's verbal and physical routines are not interchangeable with Ionesco's attacks on cliché, Harold Pinter's menacing silences, or Jean Genet's examinations of role and power. A broad label can become a shortcut that prevents close listening.

The geography is wider as well. Recent scholarship by Emilie Morin points out that histories centered on a small European canon overlook absurdist drama in Latin America, the Middle East, East Asia, and other contexts, including work addressing political and colonial conditions. Describing all absurdism as the isolated individual's despair in a meaningless universe misses theater built around the recognizable absurdities of public authority, state violence, social procedure, or imposed identities. A group of plays can share formal strategies without sharing a single philosophy.

Our imaginary inspection could be performed in a place where the feared official stands for a very real administrative threat. It could be performed by workers who risk losing housing when forms are misplaced. The absence of the inspector would then not make power unreal. It would reveal how people continue to organize their bodies around power even when no representative is present. A comedy writer cannot decide whether that reading is appropriate without specifying the world of the scene.

The distinction also protects us from a common confusion. Existential philosophy, the Theatre of the Absurd, and the absurd feeling that everyday language sometimes names are connected but not synonymous. A person waiting for a late train is not automatically performing Beckett. A bizarre object is not automatically surrealism. A character deciding life is meaningless is not automatically funny. The formal question is more exact: what expectation of language, action, or explanation has the work made available, and how does it disturb that expectation while keeping an audience engaged?

## A writing experiment with three endings

Return to the inspector, chair, and two employees. Give the scene three possible endings. In the first, the inspector finally arrives and explains that a different room should have been prepared. The ending reveals a clerical mistake and completes a familiar farce. The prior actions become the setup for a punch line.

In the second, the inspector never arrives. One employee begins rehearsing the official's imagined remarks so thoroughly that the other employee starts responding as if the official were present. By the end, one has become the inspector and the other has become the applicant. There is no announcement. The social relation created by expectation has become self-sustaining. The ending changes how the audience understands the entire scene.

In the third, the inspector calls to say there will never be an inspection. Both employees are deeply upset. The employee who complained most about the work asks whether they might continue preparing the chair tomorrow anyway. The other agrees, then carefully measures the distance between chair and door. The comic action persists even though its stated justification has vanished. This ending is not a statement that nothing matters. It asks what people do with an empty ritual when the ritual has become their shared life.

Different endings make different claims about time. The farcical ending says events were causally connected all along. The role-switching ending says a social structure can reproduce itself without its supposed source. The final ending says people may attach themselves to the action that once promised purpose. Neither is inherently superior. They are different artistic arguments performed through behavior.

In writing the scene, resist announcing the meaning. Do not have a character turn to the audience and say, 'We are prisoners of a meaningless bureaucracy.' The characters' fidelity to their task is more interesting than their capacity to summarize a graduate seminar. Give each a concrete desire: one wants approval, one wants the other not to leave, one wants an opportunity to sit. Rehearse the practical business until it produces a rhythm. The absurdity should emerge from how ordinary needs are trapped inside an unstable explanation.

## The comic actor's contribution

This tradition also reminds us that a manuscript is not the entire event. A tiny pause before moving the chair can make the audience suspect a change of mind. Repeating a sentence in exactly the same tone can imply a character is stuck; repeating it with a fraction more hope can make failure heartbreaking. The way performers look at an object or continue a task after a line has ended creates information the words do not carry. The history of absurdist theater is therefore inseparable from stagecraft and ensemble timing, even when a written work appears spare.

A performer has to know what each person is attempting, not simply that the script is supposed to be strange. When an actor plays 'absurd,' a scene risks becoming a collection of grimaces. When an actor plays 'I must have this chair ready before someone can criticize me,' an audience can recognize a human strategy in circumstances where strategy no longer secures its objective.

And that is the enduring lesson. Comedy does not always restore a broken order. Sometimes it invites us to stay with people who cannot make their world yield a satisfying answer. Their precise, foolish, touching efforts to continue are the event. The laughter comes not from a writer adding nonsense to reality, but from a form that makes the ordinary labor of continuing newly visible.
