# When One Electron Interferes: Quantum States, Probabilities, and Measurement

An electron detector clicks. Then it clicks again. A third electron arrives, and a fourth. Each event seems as definite as a tiny paint splatter on a screen. Leave the experiment running long enough, however, and the splatters build a pattern of alternating high and low detection rates. Where did the pattern come from if the electrons were sent through one at a time? And what does it even mean for an individual electron to interfere with something when there is apparently no second electron present?

This is not just a tale from an imaginary double-slit experiment in a textbook. In 1989, Akira Tonomura and colleagues reported a demonstration of the gradual buildup of an electron interference pattern using an electron microscope, an electron biprism and a position-sensitive counting system. The apparatus was not literally a pair of crude openings cut into a wall. An electrostatic electron biprism provided two coherent alternatives that could interfere. The measured pattern emerged from many individual arrivals, recorded one after another. What we need to explain is the combination: definite detection events and a distribution that behaves like interfering waves.

Quantum mechanics begins by refusing to collapse these two observations into a familiar classical picture prematurely. An electron is not a tiny billiard ball secretly taking one known narrow channel while its detector merely lacks the resolution to see it. Neither is it a patch of classical water wave that must spread its charge continuously across the whole screen at detection. In the standard theory, we represent the system with a quantum state. The state is a mathematical object used to calculate the probabilities of the outcomes of specified measurements. In some contexts it also predicts phase-sensitive interference between alternatives.

The most useful unfamiliar word here is amplitude. In ordinary probability, one might say the chance of rain tomorrow is forty percent, add it to other probabilities under the appropriate rules, and stop there. Quantum theory assigns probability amplitudes, which can be positive, negative, or complex numbers with a phase. If two indistinguishable alternatives lead to the same detection outcome and remain coherent, we add their amplitudes first. Only then do we take the squared magnitude to obtain a probability. This ordering is the source of interference.

Imagine two routes from the electron source to one selected part of the detector. Call their amplitudes A and B. The probability contribution for that detector position, when the routes cannot be distinguished even in principle by a retained record, involves the squared magnitude of A plus B. Algebra produces the squared magnitude of A, plus that of B, plus an interference term that depends on their relative phase. If the amplitudes point in the same direction in the complex plane, they reinforce. If they point in opposite directions, they cancel. Somewhere on the screen the two alternatives increase the detection rate, elsewhere they reduce it. The probabilities across all possible outcomes must still add to one.

This does not imply that a single electron deposits half a charge at one bright fringe and half at another. When the experiment detects an electron at a localized point, the click is one outcome. The pattern is a statement about the statistical distribution of many trials prepared in the same way. It is useful to say the quantum state explores interfering alternatives, but a story about the electron physically splitting into two ordinary particles is not required by the observed detector record.

Here is a crucial contrast. Suppose we modify the apparatus so that a detector or an environmental interaction records which alternative the electron followed. Even if nobody looks at the record, the quantum state of the electron becomes correlated with distinguishable states of the device or environment. If the path information is effectively available, the interference between those alternatives disappears from the electron's unconditioned screen statistics. The detector pattern becomes the sum of the separate path probabilities rather than the square of a coherent sum of path amplitudes. The experiment has changed physically. Human awareness is not the mechanism.

It is easy to abuse the phrase 'observing changes reality.' In quantum experiments, observation refers to an interaction that creates a measurement record or otherwise changes which alternatives remain coherent. Turning on a laser that scatters photons from the electron paths, installing a path detector, or coupling to uncontrolled environmental degrees of freedom can all matter. Consciousness is neither a required variable in the equations nor a demonstrated cause of interference loss. The real questions are which states are correlated, what information can distinguish them, and which measurements we perform.

This distinction also clarifies the quantum eraser. Sometimes an apparatus stores path information in a second degree of freedom, such as polarization or an entangled partner. By selecting particular outcomes of a later measurement, one can recover interference in appropriate conditioned subsets. But when all the outcomes are combined, the unconditioned data do not contain a usable message sent backward in time. The recovery of a fringe within a selected subset is not a device for rewriting an earlier detection or communicating faster than light. It reflects how quantum correlations are analyzed.

We can examine measurement with a simpler apparatus than an electron interferometer: a pair of polarizing filters. A horizontal photon polarization state is orthogonal to a vertical one. An ideal vertical polarizer transmits none of the photons prepared in the purely horizontal state. Now insert a polarizer angled forty-five degrees between them. A horizontally polarized photon has a one-half probability to pass the diagonal filter. Conditional on passing, its polarization has been prepared in the diagonal state. It then has a one-half probability to pass the vertical filter. The probability that it passes both filters is one quarter. Adding an extra absorbing element has created a nonzero chance of reaching the final detector.

There is no free light being manufactured. Many photons are removed at the diagonal filter, and the polarization state of those that pass changes. The example illustrates how quantum states depend on measurement context. The probability of a vertical result for a horizontally prepared photon is zero. But after an intervening measurement that selects a different state, the probability changes. In classical language it may look as though checking an intermediate fact should only reduce what you know about the light. In the quantum description, the intermediate apparatus is doing more than uncovering a preexisting answer.

The mathematics can be kept surprisingly compact. A two-outcome quantum system can be represented by a vector with two complex amplitudes. If the amplitudes for two mutually exclusive outcomes have squared magnitudes of one half and one half, the total probability is one. A measurement with those two outcomes yields one of them, with those probabilities. A change in measurement basis changes the decomposition. In the polarization example, the horizontal state can be written as an equal-amplitude combination of the two diagonal polarization basis states. The relative phases of amplitudes matter for later experiments, even though one two-outcome measurement reveals only one answer.

An electron has an analogous two-outcome property called spin. The name is historical and can tempt us to picture a charged ball literally rotating about an axis, but spin is not well described that way. Send suitable atoms through an inhomogeneous magnetic field in a Stern–Gerlach apparatus and the beam separates into distinct outcomes. For silver atoms, the relevant unpaired electron produces a two-way splitting in the familiar introductory treatment. Prepare the beam in the spin-up state along one chosen axis; immediately measuring along the same axis gives the same result. Rotate the measurement axis by ninety degrees, and the outcomes are equally likely. Measure the original axis again after selecting an outcome from the rotated axis, and the original result is no longer guaranteed.

That sequence is not a defect of the apparatus. Measurements of spin along different directions correspond to operators that do not generally commute. The measurement sequence changes the experiment. It is another way to see that quantum probabilities are not merely a spreadsheet of unknown fixed values for every possible question. The apparatus establishes which physical observable is being tested, and compatible and incompatible observations have different mathematical relationships.

What, then, is a wavefunction? In position representation, a quantum state can be expressed as a function whose value at each location is a complex amplitude. Squaring its magnitude gives a probability density for a position measurement under the usual Born rule. A wavefunction is not a literal photographic cloud of tiny material smeared over space. It is a representation of the state, and states can also be represented in momentum, energy or other bases. The mathematical representation changes, while physical predictions remain consistent.

The Schrödinger equation specifies how a quantum state changes with time when it is not being conditioned on a measurement outcome. It is a linear dynamical equation: if two states are possible solutions, appropriate superpositions are also solutions. That linearity makes phase and interference unavoidable features of the theory. If you want to know whether an electron is likely to appear on a particular region of the screen, the task is to evolve its state through the physical potential and apparatus, then calculate the outcome probabilities. The wavefunction is not an ornament attached to a quantum story. It is the object we use to compute the story's predictions.

Why don't we see a baseball interfering through two doors? We should not say quantum mechanics turns off for large objects. The objects that make up a baseball are governed by quantum physics. But a large warm ball is strongly correlated with its environment through light, collisions, internal vibrations and other interactions. Its alternatives rapidly lose the coherent phase relationships needed to see interference in the variables we can control. That process is called decoherence. It explains why quantum interference is difficult to preserve in macroscopic situations, though the detailed question of why one specific outcome is experienced remains part of the interpretational debate about quantum measurement.

Decoherence is not the same as proving one philosophical interpretation of quantum theory. Standard experimental practice uses quantum states, their evolution, measurement operators and the Born probabilities extremely successfully. Different interpretations may offer distinct accounts of what the state means or how individual outcomes should be understood. A careful lecture should not present one contested account—many worlds, spontaneous collapse, pilot waves, or a vaguely mystical role for mind—as if a double-slit pattern uniquely proved it. The interference pattern demands quantum amplitudes; it does not by itself settle every question about quantum ontology.

Another central feature follows from the mathematics of waves. A sharply localized packet of waves requires a wide range of wavelengths. Since a particle's momentum is connected to wavelength, a state localized sharply in position has a substantial spread in momentum. The uncertainty relation says that the product of the position standard deviation and the momentum standard deviation cannot be smaller than the reduced Planck constant divided by two. This is a limit on the distribution of outcomes available from a quantum state, not just a warning that the laboratory microscope is shaky.

Try a number. If an electron is prepared with a position uncertainty of approximately one nanometer, then its momentum standard deviation must be at least roughly five times ten to the minus twenty-six kilogram meters per second. In the nonrelativistic approximation, that corresponds to a velocity spread of at least about sixty thousand meters per second. An electron localized on the scale of a few atoms cannot also be treated as having one perfectly defined classical velocity. This scale is one reason atomic electrons are represented by states and orbitals rather than planetary trajectories.

A common misconception says the uncertainty principle means a scientist cannot measure position and speed accurately because measuring always kicks the electron. Measurement back-action is real and important, but the standard uncertainty relation is more general. Even an ideally prepared state can lack narrow distributions for both quantities. The disturbance caused by a particular measurement is an additional problem. Conflating preparation uncertainty and measurement disturbance hides the mathematics that predicts diffraction and atomic energy structure.

Quantum mechanics is not restricted to dramatic interferometers. The allowed energies of a bound system are often discrete. An isolated hydrogen atom emits or absorbs light at characteristic frequencies because its electronic state can change between particular energy levels. Quantum tunneling allows a particle to reach regions forbidden by a simple classical energy argument, with a probability that depends on the barrier. Alpha decay in nuclei and the operation of scanning tunneling microscopes depend on this effect. Semiconductor electronics depends on quantum states in solids, while lasers depend on stimulated emission and population arrangements that cannot be explained by classical light waves alone.

The phrase 'particle and wave' therefore does useful work only if we avoid turning it into a contradiction. Detection events can be localized and countable. Amplitudes evolve and interfere. Wavelengths influence the probabilities. Neither a fully classical trajectory picture nor a fully classical continuously distributed energy picture captures all the observations. Quantum mechanics is a better predictive framework than either analogy by itself.

The strangest-looking test involves two systems measured far apart. If they are prepared in an entangled quantum state, their joint probabilities need not factor into independent probabilities for each system. Certain correlations between measurements along differently chosen axes can violate Bell inequalities. John Bell showed that broad classes of local hidden-variable accounts, subject to particular assumptions, obey bounds that quantum predictions can exceed. Experiments by John Clauser, Alain Aspect, Anton Zeilinger and many later groups established such violations; their work was recognized by the 2022 Nobel Prize in Physics.

Be precise about what those results exclude. Bell tests rule out specified families of locally causal hidden-variable explanations under the tested assumptions and with carefully controlled loopholes. They do not logically prove that *all* imaginable hidden-variable theories are impossible. Some interpretations abandon locality of the relevant kind or modify other assumptions. Nor do correlations let one observer send a chosen message to a distant partner faster than light. Each local observer sees statistics that cannot be controlled by the distant observer's choice to send a signal. The striking effect appears when the results are later brought together and compared.

Entanglement is often described as if two particles exchanged instantaneous secret messages. But the operational theory does not require a usable superluminal message between them. It predicts correlations for a joint state and constraints on locally accessible information. The experiments are surprising enough without adding claims about faster-than-light messages that they do not establish.

Return to the single-electron experiment. We prepare a source, control an apparatus, and observe individual arrivals. We can change the phase difference between coherent paths and move the bright and dark bands. We can add path records and remove interference. We can alter the preparation and predict different distributions quantitatively. At no point must we appeal to a human witness creating the universe by looking at it. The experiment is strange enough without that embellishment.

Quantum mechanics changes the job of physics in a subtle way. Classical mechanics often asks, given exact initial positions and momenta, what trajectory follows? Quantum mechanics asks, given a physically prepared state and a measurement arrangement, what probabilities and correlations follow, and how does the state evolve between interactions? Its answers are precise enough to design lasers, transistors, clocks and detectors. Some philosophical questions remain open. But the core empirical claim is not open-ended: probability amplitudes interfere, incompatible observables have limits, and quantum correlations can exceed bounds imposed by particular classical models. The dots on the screen are definite. The pattern they make forces us to think differently about the rules that give those dots their probabilities.

## Technical companion: amplitudes and observables

The mathematical rules below summarize predictions rather than choose an interpretation.

For two indistinguishable coherent alternatives with amplitudes $a$ and $b$ for the **same** detector outcome:

\[
P\propto |a+b|^2=|a|^2+|b|^2+2\operatorname{Re}(a^*b).
\]

When a path degree of freedom becomes correlated with orthogonal environmental records, the interference term in the **reduced** local statistics vanishes on tracing out the record. More generally, partial distinguishability produces intermediate fringe visibility.

An ideal qubit state can be expressed as $|\psi\rangle=\alpha|0\rangle+\beta|1\rangle$, $|\alpha|^2+|\beta|^2=1$. A projective measurement in this basis gives probabilities $|\alpha|^2,|\beta|^2$. A different measurement basis yields a different decomposition.

For an initially horizontal photon, passage through an ideal $45^\circ$ filter then an ideal vertical filter has $P=(1/2)(1/2)=1/4$; no middle filter gives $P=0$. The intermediate filter selects a new state, not a hidden path from a classical mixture.

\[
i\hbar \frac{\partial}{\partial t}|\psi\rangle=\hat H|\psi\rangle,\qquad
\sigma_x\sigma_p\geq\frac{\hbar}{2},\quad \hbar\approx1.055\times10^{-34}\ \mathrm{J\,s}.
\]

With $\sigma_x=1\ \mathrm{nm}$, $\sigma_p\geq5.27\times10^{-26}\ \mathrm{kg\,m/s}$. Using $m_e\simeq9.11\times10^{-31}\ \mathrm{kg}$ gives $\sigma_v\gtrsim5.8\times10^4\ \mathrm{m/s}$ if the nonrelativistic relation $p=mv$ applies.

Bell inequality violations constrain models satisfying specified locality, measurement-independence and related assumptions. They do not by themselves select a unique interpretation of quantum theory or enable controllable faster-than-light signaling.
