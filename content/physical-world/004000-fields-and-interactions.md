# A Force at Every Point: Fields, Charges, and Interactions

Put a small compass on a table and bring a magnet near it, without touching the compass. The needle changes direction. Place a charged object near a light bit of material and you may see an attraction without contact. Let go of a stone and Earth accelerates it downward even though the stone is not connected to the ground by a visible string. These phenomena invite a question: what, exactly, mediates the influence?

One way to begin is to describe a force acting between two objects. The magnitude and direction can depend on their separation and other properties. But a different viewpoint is often more useful: describe what the source has established throughout the surrounding region, and then calculate how another object responds when placed there. That description is called a field. A field assigns a physical quantity to positions in space and, when conditions vary, to times as well.

The weather offers an analogy, but only an analogy. A map of air temperature assigns a number to each location. A map of wind velocity assigns a direction and a speed. The first is a scalar field; the second is a vector field. An electric field is a vector field too. At each position it records the electric force per unit positive test charge that would act there, under the usual idealization that the test charge does not significantly disturb the source. The field is not a different electric force for each object we happen to insert. It describes the source environment; the response depends on the inserted charge.

Suppose a region has a uniform electric field of three hundred newtons per coulomb pointing right. A positive charge of two microcoulombs experiences a force of six ten-thousandths of a newton to the right. The prefix micro means one millionth, so the charge is two millionths of a coulomb. The calculation multiplies the electric field by the charge. A negative charge of the same magnitude experiences the same force magnitude to the left. Changing the test charge changes the force, not the specified field. We also see the units cancel correctly: coulombs times newtons per coulomb leaves newtons.

Compare this to gravity near Earth. A mass of two kilograms has weight approximately nineteen point six newtons downward, because the local gravitational field is about nine point eight newtons per kilogram. Gravitational field strength can also be expressed in meters per second squared; those units are equal because a newton is a kilogram meter per second squared. This analogy is useful, but it is not identity. Ordinary mass is not available with positive and negative gravitational signs in the manner of electric charge. Electric charges can attract or repel, and electric forces on different particles can oppose each other.

A point charge creates a field whose magnitude decreases as the inverse square of distance in the electrostatic approximation. Double the distance and the field becomes one quarter as strong. Why inverse square? Imagine a sphere surrounding a source. Its surface area grows as the square of the radius. An approximately radial influence spread across larger spheres naturally leads to inverse-square geometry under the appropriate field law. That geometric picture is not a complete derivation of Coulomb's law, but it helps explain its spatial dependence.

The direction matters as much as the magnitude. For a positive source charge, the electric field points away from the source; for a negative source charge, toward it. With several sources, fields add as vectors. If two identical positive charges lie symmetrically on opposite sides of a midpoint, their electric fields cancel at that midpoint. That cancellation is a statement about the field at one position; it does not mean the two sources have ceased to exist, nor does it imply the field vanishes elsewhere.

How can we use a field to track energy? Consider two points in an electric region. We define the electric potential difference as electric potential energy change per unit charge. If a charge moves across a twelve-volt potential difference, the magnitude of the associated potential-energy change is twelve joules per coulomb times the magnitude of the charge. For a one-coulomb charge, that is twelve joules. For a charge of two microcoulombs, it is twenty-four microjoules. Which direction the energy changes depends on the charge sign and on which endpoint has the higher potential. Voltage is not force, and it is not energy by itself. It is energy per unit charge.

This separation between field, force, potential, and energy resolves many mistakes. Electric field strength may be measured in newtons per coulomb or volts per meter. Voltage is measured in joules per coulomb. The strength of a field is related to how potential changes with position, not to the absolute number assigned to a single location. The zero of electric potential can be chosen by convention in electrostatics. A physical energy change depends on a difference.

Magnetism introduces a different kind of response. A charged particle moving through a magnetic field feels a magnetic force perpendicular to both its velocity and the field direction. For an ideal point charge the magnitude is charge magnitude times speed times magnetic field strength times the sine of the angle between velocity and field. If the particle travels parallel to the field, the magnetic part of the force is zero. If it moves perpendicular to the field, the magnitude is maximal. The direction also depends on whether the charge is positive or negative.

Here is a surprising consequence. Because that ideal magnetic force is perpendicular to instantaneous velocity, it changes the particle's direction without changing its kinetic energy. In a uniform field perpendicular to its velocity, the particle can follow a circular path. The magnetic field bends the trajectory while doing no work on the point charge through this magnetic force. Electric fields, by contrast, can do work and change a charged particle's kinetic energy. Real devices can involve both effects at once, so the simple contrast has to be applied with care.

A compass needle does not contain a tiny charged particle visibly flying around its circle. Its magnetic behavior arises from magnetic moments associated with matter, and the torque on those moments depends on the surrounding magnetic field. This is an example of why the simple particle formula is one model among several. A good field theory can connect the models, but we should not swap their predictions indiscriminately.

The nineteenth-century development of electricity and magnetism changed what physicists believed a physical explanation could look like. Michael Faraday explored the effects of magnets and circuits, including electromagnetic induction. In 1831 he found that changing magnetic conditions could induce an electrical response in a circuit. James Clerk Maxwell later developed a mathematical field theory integrating electric and magnetic phenomena. Those discoveries were not simply about adding formulas to a list. They showed that changing fields could be dynamically connected and that electromagnetic disturbances could propagate.

Let's specify an experiment in a way you could actually reason about. Imagine a conducting loop connected to a sensitive current detector. Move a bar magnet toward the loop, then hold it still, then withdraw it. In a suitable arrangement, the detector responds during the changes and tends toward zero when the field and loop are stationary relative to one another. Move the magnet in the opposite direction and the induced current direction reverses. This is a proposed qualitative laboratory setup, reflecting well-established induction behavior, not an eyewitness description of Faraday's particular apparatus or results on a specific day.

The quantity that organizes this observation is magnetic flux through the loop: a measure of the magnetic field passing through the chosen oriented surface. The rate at which flux changes determines the induced electromotive force, including its sign. The induced current tends to oppose the change in flux that produced it, a principle known as Lenz's law. This does not mean a magnet and loop always repel. When the magnet moves toward the loop, the induction acts against that approach; when it moves away, it acts against the retreat. If the circuit is open, an induced electromotive force can exist without a sustained current around the loop.

An important caution follows. A field picture is not an invitation to assume influences cross arbitrary distances instantaneously. Modern electromagnetism describes field disturbances propagating at finite speed. Static inverse-square formulas are extremely useful in stationary or slowly changing arrangements, but they are not the complete story for rapidly changing sources. The distinction becomes essential when we study waves and light.

Another caution concerns field lines. Lines drawn on paper are not physical threads stretched through space. They are a visual convention: at each point the tangent indicates field direction, and a suitable density of lines can suggest strength. A different artist can draw a different number of lines without changing the underlying field. Confusing the drawing with the thing described would be like claiming roads on a weather map control the wind.

Fields let us think locally about interactions. We can calculate a field from sources, evaluate it at a position, and predict the force or energy change experienced by a probe. A field theory can also describe how the field itself changes and transports energy. That final step is the bridge from seemingly distant influences to propagating waves. The universe does not need a visible string between every pair of objects, but we still need equations with clear units, approximations, and experimentally testable consequences.

## Mathematical companion (display only)

- Electric force: \(\mathbf F_e=q\mathbf E\), so \([E]=\mathrm{N/C}=\mathrm{V/m}\).
- Point-charge electrostatic field (vacuum): \(\mathbf E(\mathbf r)=(4\pi\epsilon_0)^{-1}q\hat{\mathbf r}/r^2\), away from the source.
- Gravitational field near Earth: \(\mathbf F_g=m\mathbf g\), \([g]=\mathrm{N/kg}=\mathrm{m/s^2}\).
- Electric potential difference: \(\Delta U=q\Delta V\), \([V]=\mathrm{J/C}\).
- Magnetic part of Lorentz force: \(\mathbf F_B=q\mathbf v\times\mathbf B\), hence \(\mathbf F_B\cdot\mathbf v=0\) for point charges.
- Magnetic flux and induced EMF: \(\Phi_B=\int \mathbf B\cdot d\mathbf A\), \(\mathcal E=-d\Phi_B/dt\) for a single stationary loop; more general arrangements require the full law and motional effects.

### Exercises with answers

1. A positive charge \(q=2\,\mathrm{\mu C}\) is in a uniform \(300\,\mathrm{N/C}\) field. Force? **\(6\times10^{-4}\,\mathrm N\)** along the field.
2. A 2 kg mass is near Earth's surface. Weight? **About 19.6 N downward**, neglecting latitude/height variation.
3. An ideal charged particle moves exactly parallel to a uniform magnetic field. Magnetic force? **Zero**, though an electric field or other forces could still accelerate it.
4. Does a constant magnetic flux through a closed stationary loop induce a nonzero EMF from Faraday's induction term? **No:** it depends on the rate of change of flux.
