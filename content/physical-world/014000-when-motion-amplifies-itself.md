# When Motion Amplifies Itself: Resonance, Coupled Oscillators, and Feedback

In November 1940, a suspension bridge across the Tacoma Narrows in Washington twisted violently in the wind and collapsed. Film of the failure is difficult to forget. One side of the roadway rises while the other falls, and the bridge rolls as if some enormous invisible hand were wringing a ribbon. Physics teachers sometimes introduce the footage by saying that a wind struck the bridge at its resonant frequency, like a singer shattering a glass. That story is memorable, but it misses a central part of the failure. The mature engineering explanation involves an aerodynamic instability called torsional flutter: the bridge's motion changed the wind forces acting on it, and those forces supplied more energy to the motion. The bridge had become part of a feedback loop.

The distinction is worth a whole lecture. Resonance and feedback can both make small disturbances grow. They can coexist. But they are not identical, and the difference is vital in designing bridges, instruments, machines, electrical circuits and control systems. We need a model simple enough to solve, then we need to ask how real systems exceed it.

Start with a mass attached to a spring on a frictionless table. Pull the mass ten centimeters to the right and release it. The spring pulls to the left. The mass accelerates toward the center, passes through it with its greatest speed, and continues until the stretched spring and its inertia reverse the motion. This pattern repeats because energy moves between two forms: potential energy stored in the spring's deformation and kinetic energy in the moving mass. When the spring is stretched, most of the energy is potential. When the mass crosses the equilibrium point, most is kinetic. In the ideal model, the total stays constant.

The spring force is proportional to displacement and points toward equilibrium. We write it as force equals negative spring constant times displacement. Combine that with Newton's second law, force equals mass times acceleration. The acceleration must therefore equal a negative constant times displacement. This is the differential equation of a harmonic oscillator. It has sinusoidal solutions: position cycles smoothly between positive and negative amplitudes, with angular frequency equal to the square root of the spring constant divided by the mass.

Put numbers in. Use a one-kilogram mass and a spring constant of one hundred newtons per meter. The natural angular frequency is ten radians per second. Since a full cycle takes two pi radians of phase, the period is two pi divided by ten, or about zero point six three seconds. It completes roughly one point six cycles per second. If you release it from ten centimeters away, the spring initially stores one half times one hundred times zero point one squared joules. That is half a joule. At the center the mass has that half joule as kinetic energy, giving a speed of one meter per second. The smallness of the energy makes the example approachable; the same equation can describe much larger systems near a stable equilibrium.

In an ideal harmonic oscillator the period does not depend on amplitude. Pulling the mass twice as far gives more stored energy and larger speed, but not a different oscillation frequency. Real springs stop being linear when stretched too far, and a pendulum only approximately follows the same law when its swing angle is small. The restoring force for a pendulum involves the sine of the angle. For small angles, the sine is approximately the angle measured in radians, and the pendulum becomes approximately harmonic. At large angles, its period increases. A model's assumptions are not embarrassment; they are how we know which predictions to trust.

Almost every practical oscillator loses energy. The table has friction, the spring flexes internally, and the surrounding air carries away sound and heat. A simple model adds a resistive force opposite to velocity and proportional to its magnitude. With mass, spring and damping present, the equation contains an acceleration term, a velocity term and a displacement term. The damping continually removes mechanical energy. If the damping is weak, the mass keeps crossing equilibrium but each swing is smaller. If it is sufficiently strong, the mass returns without oscillating. Near the boundary between those regimes is critical damping: for a specified linear model, the fastest return to equilibrium without overshoot.

The distinction has practical consequences. A door closer should usually return a door smoothly rather than banging it back and forth. Vehicle shock absorbers limit bouncing, though real suspensions are more complicated than one linear degree of freedom. A measurement instrument may be designed to settle quickly after a disturbance. An oscillator in a precision clock, by contrast, may need very low damping because its stable repetitive motion carries timing information. We don't always want to eliminate motion. Sometimes we want to preserve it with a very carefully controlled loss rate.

To see resonance, now push the spring system periodically. The force has a frequency chosen by us. At very low forcing frequencies, the mass can follow nearly as if the force were static. At very high frequencies, the inertia prevents a large response. Somewhere near its natural frequency, repeated pushes add energy efficiently, and the amplitude can become much larger than the displacement produced by the same force applied steadily.

A playground swing supplies an intuitive example. Push at the wrong times and you may slow it down or barely affect it. Push once per cycle, with the force in a direction that does positive work on the swinging child, and the motion grows. The timing matters because mechanical work depends on force multiplied by motion in the force's direction. Repeating a push in phase with the velocity adds energy on each cycle. A push in the wrong phase can take energy away. The relevant variable is not just how often you push, but the relationship between the phase of the applied force and the phase of the response.

A driven, damped harmonic oscillator makes this precise. Its steady-state amplitude depends on the driving-force amplitude divided by a square root containing the mismatch between spring stiffness and inertial response, along with the damping term. We can calculate an example without learning a whole course in differential equations. Keep the one-kilogram mass and hundred-newton-per-meter spring. Apply a sinusoidal force with peak magnitude one newton. A constant one-newton force would displace the spring by one centimeter. Now set the driving angular frequency to ten radians per second and choose a damping coefficient of two newton-seconds per meter. At that frequency the spring and inertial terms cancel in the steady-state denominator; the damping term is two times ten, or twenty newtons per meter. The resulting steady-state amplitude is one twentieth of a meter, or five centimeters. It is five times the static displacement.

In the ideal undamped mathematical oscillator, forcing precisely at the natural frequency can produce unbounded amplitude growth as time passes. That is not a license to predict that a real spring stretches to infinity. Real systems develop nonlinear forces, losses, material failure or other changes that invalidate the ideal equation. Damping yields a finite steady-state amplitude. Moreover, when damping is appreciable, the precise frequency that maximizes displacement can lie slightly below the undamped natural frequency; 'the resonance occurs exactly at the natural frequency' is a good weak-damping approximation, not an exact universal theorem.

If you sweep a driving frequency and measure response amplitude, you obtain a resonance curve. Weakly damped systems tend to show a sharper and higher peak; strongly damped systems show a broader and lower response. In lightly damped systems, a quality factor often measures the narrowness of the peak and the number of cycles over which energy persists. High quality factor can be valuable in a radio-frequency resonator that selects one frequency from nearby signals, or in a quartz reference that preserves a stable oscillation. It can also make a structure more sensitive to sustained forcing near one of its natural modes. The same parameter can be a feature or a vulnerability depending on what the device is meant to do.

Frequency is only half of the response. Phase matters as well. Far below resonance the displacement tends to follow the applied force. Above resonance the displacement tends toward an opposite phase. Around the response peak, the phase relation changes rapidly, and at the undamped natural frequency in the familiar viscously damped model the displacement lags the sinusoidal force by roughly a quarter cycle. That phase lag lets the applied force do positive net work over a cycle, replenishing exactly the energy lost to damping in the steady state. Looking only at amplitudes while ignoring phase leaves the explanation of energy transfer incomplete.

So far we have one moving coordinate. But a real bridge has many, and a string has continuously many. Add a second mass, connected to the first by another spring, while each mass is also attached to an outer support by its own spring. When the masses move together in the same direction, the spring between them stays the same length; the system has an in-phase normal mode. When they move in opposite directions, the middle spring stretches and compresses more strongly; there is an out-of-phase mode with a higher frequency. A normal mode is a coordinated pattern in which all parts of a linear coupled system oscillate at one characteristic frequency, with fixed relative amplitudes and phases.

Here is a concrete calculation. Let each mass be one kilogram, each outside spring have a stiffness of one hundred newtons per meter, and the coupling spring between the masses have a stiffness of fifty newtons per meter. In the in-phase mode, the middle spring does nothing and the angular frequency is ten radians per second. In the out-of-phase mode, the effective restoring stiffness per mass becomes one hundred plus twice fifty, or two hundred newtons per meter. The angular frequency is the square root of two hundred, about fourteen point one radians per second. Two masses, two distinct collective ways to move. With more masses, there are more modes, and a continuous string or beam has an entire family of vibrational shapes.

This idea explains a surprising amount of musical sound. A string fixed at both ends can vibrate in different shapes with nodes that stay still and antinodes that move strongly. The fundamental mode has one broad arch. Higher modes have more nodes and higher frequencies. The particular mixture of modes helps give a plucked string its sound. The overtones do not arrive from little independent strings hiding inside the main string; they are different solutions of the same physical system's vibration equations. A piano soundboard, an instrument body and the air all couple to those motions and affect which modes persist and radiate.

Coupled oscillators also create beats. If two nearly equal frequencies sound together, their peaks align and then drift apart. The combined amplitude rises and falls at a rate related to the difference between the frequencies. A sound near one hundred hertz played together with one near one hundred two hertz produces an audible intensity modulation of about two beats per second, depending on the setup. The beats are not a third sound wave mysteriously generated from nothing; they arise from superposition and the slowly changing relative phase. Musicians can use this effect to tune instruments.

The same mathematics reaches electrical systems. An inductor stores energy in a magnetic field, a capacitor stores it in an electric field, and the two can exchange energy in a circuit. A resistor dissipates energy and damps the oscillation. Driving the circuit near its resonant frequency can produce a large response in some variable, depending on where the drive and measurement are connected. The specific circuit equations differ in their physical meanings, but the structure of the mathematical problem matches a mass, spring and damper. This is why engineers use the language of oscillators, modes, bandwidth and phase across mechanics, acoustics, electronics and optics.

Now return to the bridge. In ordinary driven resonance, an external force varies approximately periodically at a frequency that the structure responds to strongly. The energy arrives because a driver supplies it. But the Tacoma Narrows motion was not simply a matter of gusts hitting the bridge at a fixed matching rhythm. As the roadway twisted, it changed the pattern of aerodynamic forces. Those forces acted in ways that could reinforce the torsional motion. In the unstable regime, the moving structure extracted energy from the air flow, and the amplitude grew. The Washington State Department of Transportation describes the critical mechanism as self-excited torsional flutter, with earlier motion and vortex-shedding effects interacting with the deck's vulnerability. Treating the collapse as a neat demonstration of a single resonant frequency conceals the dynamic coupling that engineers needed to understand.

Feedback is the general idea: the system's output influences what happens to the system next. A thermostat measures temperature and turns heating on or off based on the deviation from a target. This is a form of negative feedback because the response tends to oppose the deviation. A microphone pointed toward a loudspeaker can produce squealing because the amplified output reenters the microphone in a way that reinforces certain frequencies. A bridge can be subject to aeroelastic feedback because motion alters aerodynamic loading. These systems are not identical, but all require us to trace the loop rather than only an externally imposed push.

Positive feedback is often described as intrinsically bad and negative feedback as intrinsically good. Neither statement is reliable without context. Positive feedback can drive an oscillator into a useful stable cycle when nonlinear mechanisms regulate its amplitude. Negative feedback can stabilize a system, but if it includes delay or strong phase shifts, it may produce oscillations or even instability. A controller that corrects too aggressively and too late can overshoot, correct back too aggressively, and keep swinging around its target. The mathematics of control therefore asks not just the sign of feedback, but how gain and phase vary with frequency, how delays enter, and whether perturbations decay.

A particularly revealing example is a tuned mass damper. Attach an auxiliary moving mass to a swaying structure and tune its motion so that the auxiliary system moves in a way that takes energy from the troublesome structural mode. The damper's own mass moves appreciably precisely so the main structure can move less. The goal is not to forbid all vibration but to redistribute and dissipate energy. In a tall building, wind and seismic response depend on many modes and on details of structural design, but the essential technique is a controlled use of coupling rather than a simple addition of stiffness everywhere.

Resonance does not always threaten destruction. It makes an organ pipe sing, allows a radio tuner to isolate a station, and helps a medical imaging system select a response. Yet saying a system 'resonates' does not automatically explain why energy accumulates. You must identify the mode, the driving or feedback mechanism, damping, and how the energy is supplied. A delicate glass can break if sufficiently strong sound excites a relevant mode and produces damaging stress; most human singing does not cause that result. A bridge can fail through flutter rather than a simple periodic driver. A building may amplify particular earthquake frequencies, but its actual response involves ground-motion spectra, damping, nonlinear behavior and many interacting structural modes.

There is a general lesson here about linear models. Near a stable equilibrium, many forces can be approximated by a term proportional to displacement. That makes harmonic oscillators appear everywhere. Coupling then produces normal modes. Add damping and forcing, and we can predict resonance curves and transients. Close the output back onto the input, and we enter the territory of stability theory. As amplitudes grow, nonlinearities may change frequencies, mix modes, saturate responses or destroy a structure. The simple oscillator is not the entire world; it is a mathematical microscope for recognizing the patterns the world builds on.

The image of Tacoma Narrows stays in our minds because the motion was so visible. But the deepest insight is almost invisible: the timing of force relative to motion determines whether energy enters or leaves a system. A tiny push, repeated correctly, can matter more than a large push delivered badly. Two objects can move together in a collective mode that neither could sustain alone. A response can feed its own cause. To understand things that shake, sing, ring, sway or destabilize, we need to ask what stores energy, what moves it, what dissipates it, and what returns the output to the input. Those four questions connect a child's swing to an orchestra, a bridge to a radio, and a feedback controller to the mathematics of stability.

## Technical companion: linear oscillator and modes

Single-degree-of-freedom damped and driven model:

\[
m\ddot x+b\dot x+kx=F_0\cos\omega t,\quad \omega_0=\sqrt{k/m},\quad
E=\tfrac12m\dot x^2+\tfrac12kx^2.
\]

When $F_0=0$, $dE/dt=-b\dot x^2\leq0$. The unforced undamped period is $T=2\pi\sqrt{m/k}$. For $m=1\ \mathrm{kg}$ and $k=100\ \mathrm{N/m}$, $\omega_0=10\ \mathrm{rad/s}$ and $T\approx0.628\ \mathrm{s}$. Starting at rest from $A=0.1\ \mathrm m$ gives $E=0.5\ \mathrm J$ and maximum speed $A\omega_0=1\ \mathrm{m/s}$.

Steady-state displacement amplitude for sinusoidal forcing:

\[
A(\omega)=\frac{F_0}{\sqrt{(k-m\omega^2)^2+(b\omega)^2}}.
\]

At $F_0=1\ \mathrm N$, $m=1\ \mathrm{kg}$, $k=100\ \mathrm{N/m}$, $b=2\ \mathrm{N\,s/m}$, $\omega=10\ \mathrm{rad/s}$, $A=0.05\ \mathrm m$, while static $F_0/k=0.01\ \mathrm m$. For weak damping, $Q\approx m\omega_0/b$; the exact peak in displacement can lie below $\omega_0$ when damping is nonzero.

Two masses $m$ connected to fixed walls with springs $k$ and connected to each other by a coupling spring $k_c$ have symmetric and antisymmetric normal modes:

\[
\omega_+=\sqrt{k/m}\quad(x_1=x_2),\qquad
\omega_-=\sqrt{(k+2k_c)/m}\quad(x_1=-x_2).
\]

For $m=1$, $k=100$, $k_c=50$ in SI units: $\omega_+=10$ and $\omega_-=\sqrt{200}\approx14.14\ \mathrm{rad/s}$. These formulas assume equal masses and springs, small linear displacements, and no damping.

For two close sinusoidal frequencies $f_1,f_2$, superposition produces an amplitude envelope with beat frequency $|f_1-f_2|$ under the usual convention for loudness peaks. For small pendulum angles, $T\approx2\pi\sqrt{L/g}$; larger amplitudes are nonlinear and produce longer periods.

A control system's stability cannot generally be inferred from the words 'positive' or 'negative' feedback alone. Closed-loop gain, frequency-dependent phase, delays and nonlinearities matter. Aerodynamic **flutter** is a self-excited instability and is not synonymous with externally driven simple resonance.
