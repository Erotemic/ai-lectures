# What a Battery Actually Does: Voltage, Current, and the Circuit

Place a small lamp, a battery and two wires on a table. Connect one wire from a battery terminal to the lamp and nothing happens. Connect the second wire to make a complete path and the lamp shines. At first glance the battery seems to send electricity into the lamp as a tank sends water through a hose. But the water image can mislead. The metal wires already contain mobile electrons before the connection is made, and the battery is not a warehouse full of electrons waiting to be used up. It is an energy source that can maintain a difference of electric potential while chemical reactions proceed.

We need three different quantities before we can explain the lamp. Charge tells us something about the electrical property of particles and matter. Current tells us how rapidly net charge crosses a selected surface: one ampere means one coulomb each second. Voltage means a difference in electric potential between two positions: one volt corresponds to one joule of potential energy change per coulomb of charge in the electrostatic description. Power tells us how quickly energy is delivered or converted: one watt is one joule per second. Mixing these quantities is like mixing the amount of water in a reservoir with its pressure and with the rate at which water flows through a pipe.

A useful starting image is a hill. A mass can lose gravitational potential energy as it moves downhill, and an electrical charge can change electric potential energy when it moves through a potential difference. But charge comes in positive and negative forms, so the sign matters. By historical convention, the direction of electric current is the direction in which positive charge would move. In a metal wire, the mobile carriers are usually negatively charged electrons drifting in the opposite direction. This convention is not evidence that early physicists secretly got every circuit wrong; the circuit equations work with a clearly chosen sign convention.

What does a battery do? In a galvanic cell, chemical reactions at its electrodes and transport through its electrolyte provide a route for transferring electrons through an external circuit while ions move internally to maintain approximate charge balance. For a classic Daniell cell, zinc metal can be oxidized to zinc ions at one electrode, while copper ions can gain electrons and become copper metal at the other. A salt bridge or other ion-conducting path allows compensating charge transfer. You cannot generally put two reactive substances beside each other, attach only a single wire and assume you have created a useful battery. The chemical, ionic and electronic paths must form a functioning cell.

The battery's open-circuit electromotive force, usually called its E M F despite not being a mechanical force, measures available work per unit charge under specified conditions. In a simple ideal model that becomes the voltage between terminals when no current flows. With a current, actual terminal voltage usually differs because of internal resistance and electrochemical losses. A battery marked one and a half volts will not always deliver exactly that voltage under every load, temperature or state of charge. Its energy and power capability are separate specifications.

Imagine an ideal nine-volt source attached to a resistor of three ohms. Ohm's law, for this resistor under the assumed conditions, says current equals voltage divided by resistance. The current is three amperes. The power converted in the resistor is voltage times current, twenty-seven watts. A real small nine-volt household battery would have significant limitations at that load; the numbers describe a mathematical circuit, not a recommendation to connect such a device. If we double the resistance while holding the source voltage fixed, the modeled current is cut in half. If we double the voltage with the same resistance, the current doubles and the heating power increases fourfold. Those consequences fall from the equations, not from an impression of what a brighter battery should do.

Resistance is not a mysterious appetite for electrons. In many conducting materials, moving charges respond to electric fields and exchange momentum and energy with the material's microscopic structure. That transfer can become thermal energy in the wire or resistor. In an ordinary metal, the microscopic explanation involves electron states and scattering, which are ultimately quantum mechanical. For the circuit, the simplified relation between voltage and current may be enough. Some devices are not ohmic: a diode, filament bulb over a wide temperature range, battery or solar cell cannot always be described by one fixed resistor.

A common puzzle is how a lamp can begin to respond promptly when the electrons themselves drift slowly through a long metal wire. The distinction is between a signal and a particular carrier. The conducting material already contains an enormous population of mobile charges. When a circuit is connected, electric and magnetic fields are established and change throughout the circuit at speeds determined by its electromagnetic environment, much faster than an individual electron's typical drift speed. The signal does not require one electron to race from the battery to the lamp and then back. Nor does it appear everywhere instantaneously. Real wires have finite propagation speeds, capacitances and inductances.

This leads to a subtler question: where does the energy travel? Introductory circuit diagrams draw arrows along wires, which is useful for calculating current. Electromagnetic theory gives a more detailed account in which energy can flow through fields in the space around conductors and be delivered into a resistor. Charges and currents are essential to creating the field configuration. It would also be wrong to say the wires do nothing or that charge transport has no role. The simple circuit model records the electrical work delivered at component terminals; the field picture explains how that energy transport fits within Maxwell's theory.

Now build a branched circuit. Put two lamps in series so the same path passes through each lamp. In steady direct current, the rate of charge flow through each series element is the same, because charge cannot continually accumulate between them without changing the state. The voltage drops across the elements add up to the source voltage, allowing for any internal loss. With two identical ideal resistors, the equivalent resistance doubles, the current falls, and each receives half the source voltage. For a source of fixed voltage, the total power supplied to that pair is smaller than for a single resistor of that value.

Connect the same two resistors in parallel instead. Now each branch joins the same pair of nodes and experiences the same voltage difference. The currents through the branches add to the source current. Two identical resistors in parallel have half the resistance of one; the ideal source supplies twice the current it would to one branch, and the total power doubles. This is why adding an electrical load is not always like adding extra friction in one long channel. Circuit topology matters. It tells us which parts share current and which parts share potential difference.

The two bookkeeping rules behind many circuit problems are called Kirchhoff's laws. At a junction, the total current entering equals the total current leaving when charge is not accumulating there. Around a closed loop in a lumped circuit, voltage rises and drops add to zero. This second rule is a powerful approximation for circuits where changing magnetic flux through the loop is negligible or accounted for appropriately. When fields are changing rapidly and electromagnetic induction matters, the more general field laws must replace the naive assumption that electrostatic potential differences alone describe everything. Again, a useful approximation is not a universal metaphysical fact.

Try a numerical puzzle. A twelve-volt ideal supply drives two resistors, three ohms and six ohms, in series. The total resistance is nine ohms. The current is twelve divided by nine, or about one point three three amperes. The voltage drops are four volts and eight volts. Notice that the larger resistance receives the larger voltage drop because both have the same current. If we wire the resistors in parallel, each receives twelve volts. The currents are four amperes and two amperes respectively, so the supply provides six amperes total, and the equivalent resistance is two ohms. The same parts yield remarkably different behavior solely because their connections changed.

Now put two conductive plates close together, separated by an insulator. This is a capacitor. Applying a voltage redistributes electric charge on the plates and stores energy in the electric field of the arrangement. The charges do not normally jump through the insulating gap. A simple capacitor's stored charge is its capacitance times its voltage, and the stored energy is one half capacitance times voltage squared. The energy need not appear as a chemical reaction, as it does in a battery. It is useful to distinguish the quantity of charge on plates from the energy associated with their arrangement.

Connect a resistor and capacitor to a constant voltage source. The capacitor does not charge in one instantaneous step. At first the current is relatively large. As the capacitor voltage approaches the source voltage, the voltage across the resistor shrinks, and so does the current. In an ideal series resistor-capacitor circuit, the characteristic time is resistance multiplied by capacitance. After one such time constant, the capacitor has reached about sixty-three percent of its final charge. That number is a mathematical consequence of exponential relaxation, not a universal timer for every electronic system. A capacitor's changing voltage is part of why circuits can represent delays, filters and memory of recent electrical activity.

A coil of wire introduces another kind of storage. A current through the coil creates a magnetic field, and the magnetic field stores energy. Changing the current can induce an opposing voltage, so a coil resists rapid changes of current in the relevant sense. This is inductance. If you have watched a motor spin down or heard an electrical hum, you have encountered systems in which changing magnetic fields and moving machinery matter alongside simple resistance. A circuit drawn with only battery symbols and resistors may be the right first model, but not the right last one.

Michael Faraday's work is central to this connection. In August 1831 he used coils around an iron ring and demonstrated that changing current in one circuit could induce a brief current in another. The preserved ring apparatus at the Royal Institution is a concrete artifact of the discovery. The important point is the change: a stationary magnetic field does not, by itself, induce a continuous voltage in a static neighboring coil. A changing magnetic environment or appropriate relative motion is required. Generators and transformers are technological descendants of the principle, but their operation depends on geometry, material properties and time-varying flux.

Why do power grids transmit energy at high voltage? Suppose a power line must deliver a fixed power to a distant load. In a simple model, electrical power equals voltage times current, so increasing transmission voltage lets us reduce the line current for the same delivered power, ignoring factors such as alternating-current phase for the moment. Resistive loss in a line scales as current squared times line resistance. If current falls to one tenth, the modeled resistive heating drops to one hundredth, provided the line resistance stays the same. The tradeoffs include insulation, equipment design, AC effects and safety systems. A high voltage is not automatically a high power, but it can be a practical tool for transporting high power more efficiently.

A battery is not an infinite store of charge, and it is not described by its voltage alone. Its energy capacity is often expressed in watt-hours, which is a unit of energy, not power. A battery that delivers ten watts for an hour has delivered ten watt-hours, or thirty-six thousand joules, in the idealized accounting. Its actual usable energy depends on discharge rate, voltage variation, temperature, age and the system's cutoff conditions. A specification in ampere-hours measures a quantity of charge; converting to approximate watt-hours requires voltage information. Two batteries rated at the same ampere-hours need not store the same energy if their operating voltages differ.

A rechargeable battery reverses the useful net electrochemical transformation by consuming electrical work supplied from outside. That is not the same thing as forcing used electrons back into a box. The microscopic structures and reaction pathways decide whether reversal is feasible and how many cycles can be achieved. Some chemistries can recharge efficiently; others undergo irreversible side reactions or structural changes. The field of battery design is therefore part chemistry, part materials science, part thermodynamics and part electrical engineering.

Return to the lamp. The battery maintains an electrochemical driving difference, the complete circuit provides a path for charge transport, fields establish the electrical response, and the lamp converts supplied energy into light and heat. Four concepts—charge, current, voltage and power—describe distinct aspects of the system. Their different units are not a nuisance to memorize; they are safeguards against confusing what flows with what drives it and with what gets transformed.

A circuit can be as small as a sensor or as large as a power network. In each case, a useful model starts by deciding its elements and its time scale, then applies conservation of charge and energy and the appropriate material laws. The impressive fact is not that every electrical system can be reduced to Ohm's law. It is that we know when Ohm's law is enough, and we have a hierarchy of deeper models for when it isn't.

## Mathematical companion (display only)

- **Units and definitions:** \(I=dq/dt\), one ampere = one coulomb/second. \(\Delta V=\Delta U/q\) for electrostatic potential energy per charge, one volt = one joule/coulomb. \(P=VI\) under the passive-sign convention with chosen voltage/current directions.
- **Ohmic resistor:** \(V=IR\), \(P=I^2R=V^2/R\). Hypothetical 9 V source, 3-ohm load: \(I=3\,\mathrm A\), \(P=27\,\mathrm W\), neglecting source internal resistance.
- **Two resistors:** Series \(R_{\rm eq}=R_1+R_2\). Parallel \(1/R_{\rm eq}=1/R_1+1/R_2\). For 3-ohm and 6-ohm components: series 9 ohms, parallel 2 ohms.
- **Node and loop:** \(\sum I_{\rm in}=\sum I_{\rm out}\) for a node without charge accumulation; \(\sum \Delta V=0\) in the quasi-static lumped-circuit limit.
- **Battery under load:** Simplified terminal voltage \(V_{\rm terminal}=\mathcal E-Ir\) during discharge, where \(r\) is internal resistance.
- **Capacitor:** \(Q=CV\), \(U_C=\frac12 CV^2\). In a simple series RC charge experiment, \(Q(t)=C\mathcal E(1-e^{-t/(RC)})\), \(\tau=RC\).
- **Power transmission:** At fixed real delivered power in a DC resistive model, \(I=P/V\); line loss is \(I^2R_{\rm line}\). In AC, power factor and reactive power require an extended model.

### Questions to test the model

1. How can a lamp react promptly if individual electrons drift slowly? **The electromagnetic signal propagates through a circuit already containing mobile charges; individual electrons do not need to traverse the whole circuit before it responds.**
2. If two identical ideal resistors are wired in parallel across the same voltage, does the supply provide more or less total current than for one resistor? **Twice as much.**
3. Can a battery with a high voltage have little energy capacity? **Yes.** Voltage is energy per unit charge, not total stored energy.
4. Why does a capacitor stop drawing steady DC current in an ideal RC charging circuit? **Its voltage approaches the source voltage, leaving no sustained potential difference across the series resistor.**
5. Why is an inductor not equivalent to a resistor? **It stores energy magnetically and responds to changing current rather than simply dissipating power.**
