# Autofiction, Documentary Form, and the Contract of Truth

A writer inherits a notebook from her father. The first page records the day he left his hometown. On the next page, in the same handwriting, is a date that cannot be correct: a year before he was born. She assumes it is a copying error. She plans to write a story about the notebook, using her own name and his. Then her sister reads the draft and says that the father wrote the second date as a joke. Their mother, she adds, used to tell it differently.

The writer has an immediate problem of form. If she publishes a memoir, readers may expect a sincere effort to report what happened. If she calls it a novel, they may allow her to invent a whole family. If she calls it autofiction, will readers know how much of the notebook is real? Does the category clarify anything, or does it create an attractive haze in which the author's obligations can disappear?

These are not purely legal questions or questions about marketing. They shape the reader's relation to every sentence. The claim that a scene happened produces a different kind of attention from the invitation to imagine that it happened. A remembered voice can be reconstructed with care and still not reproduce an exact conversation. An authentic document can be misunderstood. A fictional document can reveal a pattern of truth that a literal account has difficulty making perceptible.

The modern history of autofiction gives us a way to distinguish these forms without pretending they can be cleanly separated once and for all.

## A term born from a disagreement

The French critic Philippe Lejeune developed an influential account of the autobiographical pact in his 1975 *Le Pacte autobiographique*. In broad terms, he asked what sort of promise a text makes when its author, narrator, and central character appear to share an identity. A book does not become autobiography simply by using the first-person pronoun. Paratext, proper names, genre labels, and the relationship asserted between writing and lived events help form an agreement with readers.

In 1977, Serge Doubrovsky published the novel *Fils* and used the word “autofiction” in its paratext. Scholarship connects the term directly to Doubrovsky's response to Lejeune's scheme. His novel placed an author-like figure under his own name within a book presented as fiction. The term was not a neutral, timeless label discovered for an existing category. It emerged from a particular argument about how self-writing can violate the neat division between fictional and autobiographical contracts.

The label subsequently traveled into other national literary cultures, where it acquired broader and sometimes contradictory uses. A contemporary English-language reviewer may call almost any intimate, self-referential novel autofiction. A scholar working from the French debate may require a more specific relationship among the author's name, a narrator, and an invented literary form. There is no single uncontested checklist. A writer should know which sense is being used before making a strong claim about the tradition.

Our narrator with the notebook cannot solve the problem by changing the bookstore shelf. An honest account of what she found, a scene reconstructed from testimony, and an invented confrontation have different warrants even if a publisher might market all three as literary prose.

## What is the reader being asked to believe?

Imagine the opening sentence: “My father left home in 1958 with a cracked green notebook.” It looks simple. Yet each part can have a different status. Perhaps the year is documented in an official record. Perhaps the notebook exists and is blue. Perhaps the departure is remembered only through family stories. Perhaps the narrator's father is an invented composite. The sentence can be true, false, approximately correct, or fictionally effective, depending on the agreement the text proposes.

A writer need not attach footnotes to every narrative detail. The important thing is to know what the work asks of its audience. Does it invite readers to judge a historical claim? To share the limits of one person's memory? To inhabit a fully invented world? To consider what it means for an author to turn another person's life into art? These invitations can coexist, but their tension should be deliberate.

One version of the notebook story begins with a promise of evidence: “I can show you the page. The date is there in my father's handwriting.” A second opens in a novelistic mode: “Father left the station in the year before he was born, carrying the notebook that would prove he had escaped.” The second sentence announces impossible chronology. The reader may take the impossibility as fantasy, family myth, or a metaphor for reinvention. A third version allows the narrator to challenge her own recollection: “For years I remembered the notebook as green. My sister insists it was always blue.” Each version produces a different contract of trust.

The practical mistake is to treat the aura of documentary evidence as a free resource. A writer can paste a facsimile-looking letter into a novel, give it a date and an address, and make it sound authoritative. But the more a text resembles a record, the more it may encourage readers to imagine that the record actually exists. Fiction can use this ambiguity productively; it should not disguise its construction by pretending the reader's confusion is always a sign of sophistication.

## Memory is not a recording

Even when a writer sincerely recounts lived experience, remembering is an active process. People infer, combine, reinterpret, omit, and attach new meanings to old details. Fiction often dramatizes this instability. But there are two opposite temptations to avoid.

The first is to claim that because memory is constructed, no distinction between an honest recollection and a fabricated event matters. That is a convenient but defective inference. A witness can be mistaken without being deceptive. A historian can acknowledge uncertainty while still distinguishing a supported date from an invented one. A writer can transform a memory without pretending every claim about the past has the same standing.

The second temptation is to treat documentary material as unmediated truth. Our narrator has a notebook that unquestionably belonged to her father. It does not follow that every statement in the notebook is true. He may have copied an older text, exaggerated for an audience, or hidden a joke that everyone once understood. Even a certified record answers only particular questions. An archive must be interpreted, and the absence of a page is not proof of the story one wishes the missing page to contain.

Let us make the sister an active character rather than a convenient skeptic. She kept the notebook for twenty years. She knows why some pages were torn out. Perhaps she did the tearing. Her interest in the record may be unlike the writer's. The writer wants a coherent story about departure and self-invention. The sister wants their father remembered as someone who stayed and cared for a difficult family. Neither account automatically cancels the other. The story becomes interesting when each has a legitimate claim to some facts and a partial view of their meaning.

## The ethics of using a real name

The autobiographical form raises questions that cannot be settled by aesthetic enthusiasm. If the narrator publicly identifies her sister and describes a private family conflict, the sister may pay costs the writer does not. Changing a name may reduce the risk of identification in some circumstances, but a precise constellation of events can make a person recognizable regardless. A book can be aesthetically powerful and still expose someone in ways that deserve scrutiny.

We should distinguish legal permission, ethical responsibility, and narrative effectiveness. A publisher's legal review cannot decide how much care a family member deserves. Conversely, one relative's objection does not automatically establish that a work has no right to exist. A writer must weigh the consequences of disclosure, the accuracy of representations that read as factual, and the degree to which the narrative grants others independent motives rather than treating them as a resource for the author's growth.

Return to the notebook. Suppose the writer plans to invent a confession that her father had another family. In the novel, such a confession could produce a meaningful plot. But if the named father was a real person, and the packaging invites factual interpretation, the invented confession could alter how actual readers understand his life. The mere word autofiction is not a universal disclaimer that removes the effect of an accusation.

One solution is to make the boundary legible: describe the family archive accurately in a prefatory note, acknowledge reconstructed scenes, and place speculative material in a clearly fictional architecture. Another is to use a more radical invented world in which documentary questions are displaced into a form with no claim to be the real family's biography. A third is to keep the relationship to fact deliberately tense and accept that the ethical questions remain open. No strategy should be called universally correct; the consequences depend on context and degree of identification.

## Two versions of a discovery

Let us compare a report-like draft with a more self-aware narrative.

First version: “The notebook proved my father had lied about his departure. His lies shaped the family, and I understood that my sister had protected them.” This is decisive and satisfying. It is also epistemically careless. A wrong date in a notebook does not establish a lie about departure, much less a motive for another person's silence.

Try another version:

“My sister watched me turn the pages. She put one finger on the year I had circled. 'He wrote that after you were born,' she said. 'Why would he do that?' I asked. 'I don't know.' She seemed to wait for me to put the pen down. I held the notebook open. There was room beside the date for a correction, and I had already uncapped the pen.”

The second scene shifts the crucial action from the father's hypothetical deception to the writer's temptation to edit the record. The narrator wants evidence to behave. Her sister may understand more, or may merely distrust the way the narrator uses documents. The blank space next to the date becomes a moral and formal pressure point. The reader does not need to decide the father's motives before understanding the daughter's impulse.

There is no need for the scene to remain permanently unresolved. An actual letter may later establish the date, or a surviving relative may recognize the joke. But the answer to the historical question would not erase the more interesting question of how the narrator acted before she knew.

## Documentary fiction and autofiction are not synonyms

A story can incorporate court records, photographs, emails, maps, or invented archival documents without being autofiction. A writer can create an autobiographical novel without any dossier-like material. Documentary form concerns the representation and organization of evidence; autofiction concerns, among other things, the text's entanglement of authorial identity and fictional narration. Their overlap is large, but they are not interchangeable categories.

In contemporary practice, documents can interrupt a narrator's apparent authority. A family email may contradict a remembered argument. A photograph may confirm that two people shared a room without telling us what they said. A police report may make an official claim that the novel places beside an incompatible personal account. Documents can also create false confidence, especially when they appear to speak for people who never controlled their production.

The writer of our notebook story might put scans and transcriptions in a display-only appendix, letting readers examine what the narrator believes is important. Or she might dramatize the act of reading and leave the documents offstage. The first approach shares evidence with readers. The second makes the narrator's mediation part of the experience. Neither is inherently more truthful. Each makes a different kind of inquiry possible.

## A practical test of the contract

Choose one event from your own life that you are comfortable using, or invent a plausible event and label it explicitly as invented. Write a factual account that separates direct observation from secondhand information. Then write a scene that invents private thoughts and dialogue to make its characters emotionally legible. Finally, write a version in which the narrator reveals uncertainty about one important detail. Compare the three.

Where does the reader's trust come from in each case? From a narrator's confident voice? From identifiable evidence? From the consistency of characterization? From an explicit acknowledgment of ignorance? What happens if the work uses a real person's name? Does the scene imply a damaging factual claim that the writer cannot substantiate? Is the uncertainty a real subject of investigation or merely a stylish fog around a convenient invention?

If you decide to write autofiction, the decision should shape the work beyond a cover label. Ask what becomes possible because the reader cannot comfortably divide the author from the narrator. Ask what risks you are asking other people to bear. Ask whether the form can tolerate evidence that contradicts the best story.

The notebook remains on the table. The writer can correct its date, preserve its contradiction, or close it and write about the urge to make the past simpler. Every choice is a narrative act. Fiction's freedom to invent is considerable; that freedom becomes more interesting, not less, when the writer understands the promises surrounding the invention.
