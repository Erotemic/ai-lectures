# Irony, New Sincerity, and the Risk of Belief

At a reunion dinner, a woman named Lena has agreed to give a speech about the school her class attended thirty years ago. The building has since been demolished. She was asked to be funny because, when they were young, she made people laugh. She brings a stack of index cards filled with jokes about the teachers, the terrible lunches, and the school's spectacularly incompetent heating system.

Halfway through the dinner, she learns that a former classmate has died. His brother is in the room. She has a joke about him on card number four.

What should the writer do with this scene? One approach is to let Lena use jokes until the truth breaks through. Another is to make the speech entirely ironic, showing how a culture of performance has deprived everyone of a language for mourning. A third is to let her speak plainly, without a protective curtain of cleverness. None is guaranteed to work. The choice turns on what the audience knows, what Lena is afraid of, what the dead classmate meant to her, and what the scene is willing to risk.

Late twentieth-century arguments about postmodern irony and what critics call new sincerity give us a vocabulary for these choices. The point is not to decide that irony is bad and heartfelt speech is good. It is to understand what irony does, how it can become a habit of self-defense, and how difficult genuine directness can be when the writer and reader both recognize the tricks by which sentiment is manufactured.

## Irony as a useful instrument

Irony can reveal a distance between what is said and what is meant, or between what a speaker believes and what the story allows us to see. It can be tender, savage, liberating, defensive, or evasive. If Lena tells the room, “The school prepared us perfectly for adult life by teaching us not to trust the radiators,” she might be making a harmless joke. But if she adds, “It also taught us that anyone who complained could be told to wear a second sweater,” the same joke can expose a particular institution's contempt for its students.

A writer can use irony to disclose a truth that polite language conceals. A character who praises a cruel official in such extravagantly ceremonial terms that the praise becomes accusation is using the form of deference against power. A narrator who adopts the confident language of an advertisement can make its hollow promises newly audible. Literary modernism and postmodernism both found ways to make inherited language strange, though neither invented irony, and neither consists only of it.

Problems arise when the distancing gesture becomes automatic. Imagine Lena's narration begins every scene by mocking the feelings it could otherwise present. She is afraid to enter the reunion, but the story immediately points out that reunions are pathetic. She recognizes someone she once loved, and the story labels the moment a scene from a cheap romantic comedy. She remembers a death and adds a joke about the tasteful arrangement of grief. This prose may be clever at first. Eventually readers may understand that nothing can matter long enough to be tested. The tone rejects its own investments before another person has a chance to reject them.

That failure is not a flaw inherent in humor. A good comic scene often makes vulnerability more specific. Nor does an earnest tone guarantee serious feeling. A sentimental passage can coerce the reader by withholding complications, pressing every sentence toward the same emotional verdict. To understand the newer argument, we need its actual historical context.

## A diagnosis of television and self-consciousness

In 1993, David Foster Wallace published “E Unibus Pluram: Television and U.S. Fiction” in *The Review of Contemporary Fiction*. He argued that techniques of ironic exposure once associated with artistic resistance had been absorbed into American commercial television. Television did not need an earnest surface for artists to puncture; it could make fun of its own manipulations, invite the viewer into the joke, and retain the viewer's attention. The critic's skeptical posture could become one of the industry's own products.

Wallace's essay is a historical and cultural argument, not a demonstration that every postmodern novelist was insincere or every television comedy was empty. His target was a particular relationship among media, commercial self-awareness, and the writer's temptation to remain permanently above the material. Later critics have debated both the limits of this diagnosis and what fiction might do after it.

In 2010, literary scholar Adam Kelly published “David Foster Wallace and the New Sincerity in American Fiction.” Kelly developed a critical account of sincerity in a postmodern cultural environment. The phrase “new sincerity” is not simply a writer's manifesto invented in 2010, and it does not identify a single literary club with agreed membership rules. As a critical framework, it asks how an avowal of belief or feeling can become meaningful when the reader knows that language is staged, conventions are reusable, and professions of authenticity can themselves be performances.

This is a harder question than whether a novel makes us cry. Suppose Lena decides to throw away her index cards and deliver an unadorned tribute. The room applauds. A conventional uplifting story might end there. But a more searching story asks what changed. Did Lena abandon irony because she finally trusted the people in the room? Did she seize a dramatic moment to look good? Was the tribute accurate? What does the dead classmate's brother hear in it? If readers are asked to accept sincerity because the character announces sincerity, the novel has simply exchanged one unexamined convention for another.

## Three drafts of the reunion speech

Let us write the scene three ways, keeping the circumstances constant. In the first version, Lena leans on deflection:

“By now,” she said, “the school has been converted into what developers call an opportunity. Even as rubble it was better at preparing us for adulthood than the career counselor.” They laughed. She turned the next card over with her thumb. A name was written at the top. She read the opening line in her head and did not say it. “Anyway,” she said, “the lunches were terrible.”

This version uses irony to establish Lena's social fluency. The silence around the card introduces a crack without telling us exactly what she is feeling. Her retreat to a safe joke is not merely a tonal habit; it becomes an action under pressure.

In the second version, the narrator takes over and interprets her:

“Lena realized that humor had been her way of evading intimacy for decades. The news of the death taught her that human connection was more important than wit. She put the cards down and at last spoke honestly.”

Perhaps this is what the author's outline says. Yet the reader has not experienced any actual difficult speech. Abstract diagnoses have solved the emotional problem offstage. The passage also implies that humor and intimacy are natural opposites, which the first version did not establish.

In the third version, directness emerges through a specific risk:

“His name is on my next card,” Lena said. She looked toward his brother and then at the floor. “I wrote a joke about the time he locked me in the supply closet. I was going to tell it because he would have laughed. I'm not sure you would.” She waited. “That wasn't a good thing he did. He apologized afterward. He brought me an orange from his lunch, which was strange because we were both nearly eighteen.” She put the card face down. “I didn't know that was the last time I was going to see him.”

The speech is not wholly dignified. It does not turn the dead person into a saint. The orange is an awkward and resistant detail; its meaning cannot be compressed into an inspirational slogan. The speaker exposes a possibly embarrassing ambiguity in front of someone who has the right to reject her version. Her sincerity consists partly in giving up control over how the scene will be received.

This, too, can fail. The orange could become a manufactured symbol. The narrator could arrange a convenient approving smile from the brother. An audience trained by many sentimental endings may recognize the move at once. The task is not to remove craft. It is to use craft without making a spectacle of moral superiority.

## Sincerity is relational

A useful way to test a scene is to ask who bears the risk of the expression. If Lena tells the speech to an empty auditorium, she risks little beyond her self-image. If she tells it while the brother is present, she risks being corrected. He may remember a different apology. He may say that his brother remained cruel. He may thank her for not turning the story into a legend. The novel must treat his response as another human action, not a mechanism designed solely to certify Lena's authenticity.

Writers also bear an artistic risk when they commit to a feeling. An ironic sentence can protect an author against the reader who might call the scene corny. A sincere sentence must live under the possibility that the reader will not share the feeling. But the opposite risk is real too: an author who insists on vulnerable candor can treat skepticism as evidence that the reader is morally deficient. A novel is not a loyalty test. It invites attention and judgment; it cannot command either.

Contemporary discussions sometimes compress this history into a simple sequence: postmodernism was cynical, then new sincerity restored the heart. That slogan is poor history and poor craft. Postmodern writers could be deeply concerned with ethical life, love, grief, and community. Later writers who use sincerity often retain fragmentation, irony, metafiction, and an intense awareness of mediation. The newer emphasis describes a problem of address, not a universally accepted cure.

Even the word authenticity can be deceptive. Lena's unplanned speech may be rehearsed in the narrator's syntax. The speech she thought carefully about before the dinner could still be heartfelt. Spontaneity is neither a necessary nor a sufficient condition for sincerity. A person may mean something fully after revising it twenty times. What matters in fiction is whether the feeling encounters resistance from the scene's actual conditions and still remains intelligible.

## The scene after the speech

To test the ending, do not conclude at the applause. Follow Lena into the hallway. The brother approaches and says he remembers the orange. Lena waits for absolution. Instead he says his brother was allergic to citrus, and that he must have stolen it for her from someone else's tray. Suddenly a cherished memory acquires another edge. Lena laughs, then stops herself. He laughs too. This is neither a triumph of earnestness nor a return to mockery. A joke has become the means by which two people share a complication they can both bear.

Alternatively, the brother might say he does not want his sibling turned into material for speeches. The novel would then have to follow that refusal, not merely congratulate Lena for her vulnerability. A sincere attempt can still fail to respect another person's needs. Sincerity is a manner of address, not immunity from criticism.

This is an important distinction in the ethics of writing from life as well. When writers convert family memories into fiction, the decision to confess something painful may feel courageous to the writer. It may impose an unwanted exposure on someone else. The tone of honesty does not settle the question of consent.

## A practical revision test

Take a scene in which two characters have something difficult to say. Write one version in which both rely on jokes, quotation, and cultural references. Do not make their humor interchangeable: one uses jokes to invite closeness, while the other uses them to delay an answer. Write a second version in which both speak plainly. Notice how quickly the dialogue can become schematic when it exists only to announce the lesson. Then write a third in which one character risks a specific statement and the other does not provide the response the first character wanted.

Ask four questions. What is being shielded by wit? What would become embarrassing if the characters spoke directly? What does the scene allow an audience to disagree with? And what must remain true after the emotional peak, when the characters return to ordinary responsibilities? If your answer is that irony always defeats love, revise. If it is that a heartfelt declaration cures every misunderstanding, revise for the opposite reason.

Lena's most important moment may not be the speech at all. It may be the decision, two weeks afterward, to call the brother and admit she misremembered the orange. Or it may be a joke she tells him that no one else would understand, a joke that does not erase the grief it makes bearable.

A mature fiction can combine skeptical intelligence with the capacity for feeling. It can know that words are constructed and still ask something of them. The modern argument about irony and sincerity becomes useful when it directs attention away from stylistic allegiance and toward the precise relationship a sentence creates among speaker, reader, and world.
