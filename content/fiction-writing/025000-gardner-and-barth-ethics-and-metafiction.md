# Gardner and Barth: Moral Vision and Fiction That Shows Its Seams

We have a witness and a document. The witness is Mara, whose town's public bathhouse is closing. She believes that officials exaggerated the cost of repairs to justify selling the property. In her kitchen is a folder of meeting minutes with one page missing. She has until morning to decide whether to publish her accusation. A friend has warned her that the missing page might contain the reason the council acted as it did.

There are at least two obvious ways to tell this story. One is to enter Mara's immediate experience, giving us the objects in the kitchen, the tension in her hands, the phone she cannot bring herself to call, and the compromises she imagines. Another is to assemble the scene from an investigator's notes, incompatible testimony, fragments of a municipal report, and an editor's commentary that admits the missing page can never be recovered. The first invites absorption in a person's decision. The second repeatedly reminds us that decisions are known through unreliable records and narrative arrangements.

Those forms may appear to belong to opposite camps. John Gardner, a novelist and writing teacher whose On Moral Fiction appeared in 1978, insisted that serious art has an ethical dimension. In The Art of Fiction, published after his death in 1984, he became widely associated with the idea of fiction as a vivid, continuous dream: a reader enters an imagined experience and should not be jarred out of it through avoidable technical mistakes. John Barth, whose essays The Literature of Exhaustion in 1967 and The Literature of Replenishment in 1980 helped define a discussion of late twentieth-century metafiction, explored stories that make their own artifice part of the action. A Barth-like story may expose its narrator's manipulations, parody inherited conventions, or allow the teller to interrupt the tale.

That is a useful starting contrast, but it is too simple if it becomes a verdict. Gardner did not argue that every literary experiment is forbidden, and a continuous dream need not be plain realism. Barth did not propose that literature abandon emotion, human experience, or craft in favor of clever tricks. Gardner's moral prescriptions also provoked substantial criticism; a writer should not present his value judgments as if everyone in literary culture accepted them. We can learn more by identifying the different problems these positions were trying to solve.

## What the dream protects

Return to the kitchen. Mara has spread the documents beside a cup of tea that has gone cold. The light above the stove clicks each time its filament heats. She has circled the figure for roof repairs in red. Her father used to check the bathhouse boilers; she can still hear the tone he used when explaining why a pressure gauge was lying. She believes the estimate before her is lying in much the same way.

An immersive version of the scene lets us inhabit the rhythm of Mara's thought without immediately declaring whether she is right. When a number in the report contradicts her memory, her heart lifts. When the next line describes a second expense she does not recognize, the exhilaration recedes. She decides to publish anyway. Then her daughter, awakened by the kitchen light, asks whether the bathhouse really was unsafe.

If the author has attended carefully to perspective, objects, and transitions, the reader can feel how self-protection and public duty collide inside Mara. We may disapprove of her decision and still understand its interior logic. The continuity Gardner valued is not simply a refusal to mention authors or documents. It is the integrity of an unfolding imaginative experience. The rules of point of view should make the scene graspable. The physical setting should remain stable enough for actions to have meaning. Emotional changes should be earned by what Mara encounters, not inserted arbitrarily to steer the reader.

Imagine instead that the narration says Mara was furious, then casually states that a hidden camera outside the room recorded a stranger plotting against her, then describes the bathhouse roof collapsing a month later, then returns to Mara noticing the tea. These shifts might be intentional in a formally experimental novel, but if nothing prepares the reader to interpret them, the changes can seem like accidents. A sentence can break the imaginative continuity by being obscure, mechanically inconsistent, or careless. Gardner's account of craft is a defense of the reader's ability to experience a made world. It is not proof that every successful novel must hide its own construction.

The ethical question in this immersive version is harder. We are so close to Mara that her suspicion may become ours. If she is mistaken, will the novel give us evidence that allows us to revise our judgment? Will the clerk who authorized repairs have a life other than the role of denying Mara's theory? Does the author's intimacy with one character unfairly turn everyone else into an abstraction? The continuous dream can be generous, but it can also make selective perception feel like objective truth.

## What the seam reveals

Now let the narrative announce itself as a collection of records. The book opens with a note from an unnamed archivist: the documents were recovered after the building was demolished, and the reconstruction of events is incomplete. We read Mara's accusation, then a budget memorandum, then the notebook of a maintenance worker who was never asked to testify. A later section corrects an earlier date. Two witnesses remember the public meeting differently. A map of the building includes a corridor no longer present on the official floor plan.

At first this may look like a puzzle invented to entertain clever readers. It can become something more important. The form itself asks how a community remembers what happened. A missing page matters differently when the book explicitly shows all its surviving pages. The narrative cannot simply transform the truth into a revelation made by an all-knowing author. It has to dramatize the limits of records, and perhaps the temptation of investigators to fill gaps with their preferred story.

Barth's own work repeatedly explored the conventions and pleasures of telling. The Literature of Exhaustion was not a simple announcement that stories were finished. It considered the artistic possibility of working knowingly with forms whose conventions have become visible. The later Literature of Replenishment is often read as responding to simplistic accounts of the earlier essay by emphasizing that the achievements of narrative tradition can be renewed through imaginative reworking. The point is not that form-conscious writing automatically produces insight. It is that awareness of how stories are made can become material for a story.

Our archive novel can exploit that possibility. One witness claims to remember Mara standing in the third row. The next document shows the meeting was conducted by phone. A footnote does not resolve the contradiction; it explains that a later ceremony, held in the same hall, may have become entangled with the witness's memory. In an immersive version, this uncertainty might be narrated from inside the witness's mind. In the archive version, readers experience the effort to establish what can be known. The seams are not interruptions to the subject. They are the subject.

A bad version would provide irrelevant footnotes only to signal sophistication. It would remind us for three pages that documents are mediated while giving us no human or interpretive stakes. An artificial device cannot redeem itself by being labeled experimental. It has to alter the reader's encounter with the problem.

## Does moral seriousness require a stable storyteller?

Here the comparison becomes interesting. Gardner wanted fiction to resist indifference, to take life's possibilities and consequences seriously. His account of morality was more demanding, and at times more prescriptive, than the modest observation that stories affect how we see other people. Critics objected to the tendency to elevate one evaluative standard above diverse artistic aims and cultures. Some found his judgments about contemporary writers ungenerous. We can separate that controversy from a durable challenge: what does our chosen form do to the dignity, freedom, and complexity of the people it represents?

A documentary form can produce ethical seriousness precisely by refusing to certify one account as complete. If we see that the clerk signed an unsafe repair estimate under threat of dismissal, we can neither exonerate nor simply demonize him. If Mara's accusation rests partly on a memory she inherited from her father, we must ask how family loyalty shapes evidence. A narrator who admits uncertainty may be more morally responsible than one who describes the same events with impossible confidence.

But that conclusion is not automatic. A novel can refuse to make judgments so thoroughly that it treats real suffering as an elegant riddle. Suppose Ivo, a teenager without reliable bathing facilities at home, appears only as a name in an appendix of displaced patrons. The archive has acknowledged him without giving his problem weight. A polished experiment may leave the reader with a feeling of superior skepticism while the person's circumstances remain untouched. Formal reflexivity is not synonymous with ethical awareness.

Likewise, a tightly immersive novel can ask the reader to revise a prejudice. If we live through Mara's mistaken certainty and watch her slowly learn what the maintenance worker faced, the continuity of that experience can bring the moral revision home. It need not be didactic. The reader may understand why both acted as they did and still disagree about the result. The ethical achievement lies in the enlargement and complication of judgment, not necessarily in issuing a correct moral.

The conflict between Gardner and Barth is therefore not a contest between caring about people and caring about form. It is a disagreement about how representation works, and what dangers arise when an aesthetic becomes self-justifying. Absorption can obscure the terms of narration. Metafiction can become a game that obscures the suffering beneath the game. Both require artistic responsibility.

## Three drafts of the same disputed page

Let us make the comparison practical. In Draft One, the writer maintains close third-person attention to Mara. The missing page is a source of dread. She thinks she knows enough to publish, but the scene reveals her embarrassment at not having read the complete engineering report. Her daughter asks whether she could wait for the clerk's explanation. Mara imagines the hearing in which the town might discover her mistake. Finally she sends the accusation. The scene's force comes from the distance between her confidence and her fear.

In Draft Two, an archive narrator begins with the accusation as a document. The reader then sees two draft versions, one harsher than the other. A marginal note by Mara's daughter suggests she advised against publishing. The next item is a receipt showing that Mara paid for copies to be delivered before dawn. There is no continuous interior scene. Yet readers reconstruct a decision and may discover the pressure of their own assumptions while doing so. The writer has shifted the source of suspense from what Mara will do to what the available evidence permits us to say she did.

Draft Three combines them. We live inside Mara's kitchen for most of the chapter. At the moment she presses send, the prose stops. A later archivist supplies the message actually received by the council, which differs subtly from the version Mara believed she had sent. The difference could be caused by an edit, a software error, or a lapse of memory. The interruption should earn its cost: it must alter the reader's understanding of the scene, not merely display the author's authority to change formats.

Think about pacing. Draft One allows pressure to build without interruption. Draft Two creates rhythm from juxtaposition. Draft Three uses the expectation of continuity and then breaks it at a chosen point. All three may work. What makes them effective is not allegiance to Gardner or Barth, but whether the relation between form and experience is precise.

## Ethical failure can arise from what we omit

The novel's moral stance is partly a matter of what it includes. A writer may believe that the council is corrupt and build every scene to support that thesis. Then one witness who complicates the conclusion is cut for slowing the plot. Another who could humanize the budget director never receives a scene. A third, whose testimony contradicts the campaign's central number, is made ridiculous. The eventual verdict may feel obvious, not because the book has reasoned deeply, but because it has engineered agreement.

A different writer may retreat into the opposite error: every assertion is qualified, every document disputed, every act interpreted in six incompatible ways, until no one can identify whether the roof was dangerous or the closure harmed residents. That also controls the reader's response. It deprives us of the possibility of reaching any provisional conclusion.

To revise either version, ask two distinct questions. First, what does this reader have enough evidence to believe at this moment? Second, which human experience is missing because my narrative arrangement has no place for it? The first question guards intelligibility. The second guards against the false completeness of an attractive form. Neither question alone guarantees ethical art, but together they expose problems a purely stylistic review might miss.

It may also be worth allowing the novel to contain a fact that no one disputes. The bathhouse is closed. Ivo's family has lost an affordable place to wash. Whatever remains contested about budgets and political motives, the loss is real inside the fictional world. The writer can stage uncertainty without dissolving all consequences into uncertainty.

## A revision exercise in formal accountability

Choose a scene where a narrator believes something consequential about another person. Write an immersive version that keeps the point of view stable and allows the belief to change through observable action. Then write a document-based or self-conscious version in which the limits of available evidence become visible. Do not merely switch fonts or insert a footnote. Ask what each method allows the reader to know and what each makes inaccessible.

In a final pass, give each version a counterexample. In the immersive version, include a detail the protagonist cannot explain away. In the documentary version, include a person whose experience cannot be reduced to a disputed record. If the form can accommodate those challenges, it may be capable of supporting a serious novel rather than merely illustrating a thesis about novels.

We often talk about the continuous dream and the exposed seam as if one must be true and the other false. But a novel does not have to choose between caring whether its people are real to the reader and acknowledging that its own account is constructed. A story can let us inhabit Mara's kitchen, and later let us learn how the record of that night was altered. It can create an imaginative world with enough integrity to sustain a decision, and expose the ways in which decisions become stories.

Gardner's demanding moral vision and Barth's inventive awareness of form give writers two instruments of scrutiny. One asks whether the art deepens our relation to human possibility. The other asks whether we understand the means by which the art produces its effects. Neither question should be allowed to silence the other. In the space between them, a novel can be both absorbing and intellectually awake.

## Companion (display only)

**Historical boundary:** Gardner and Barth were contemporaries, but this lecture constructs an analytical comparison; it does not claim they conducted the specific debate staged here. Gardner's moral prescriptions were contested, and Barth's essays should not be reduced to claims that fiction had ended. All bathhouse documents are invented.

**Practice:** Tell a disputed-event scene once through close interior experience, once through a dossier of conflicting records, and once in a hybrid form. Record what each makes accessible and conceals. Preserve a concrete human consequence across all three.
