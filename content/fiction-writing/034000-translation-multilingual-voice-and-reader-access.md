# Translation, Multilingual Voice, and Who Gets to Understand

A daughter returns to a house that her family is preparing to sell. The kitchen light is on; most of the furniture is gone. Her mother opens a drawer and sees that the small brass key is missing. 'No pasa nada,' she says. Her daughter translates the phrase to herself as 'It's nothing.' But the mother continues looking into the drawer long after she has said it.

If we render the phrase simply as 'It's nothing,' the scene remains intelligible, but one of its pressures disappears. The words can reassure, deflect, insist that a problem is manageable, or close a conversation. The daughter has a fluent gloss and still may not know what her mother wants her to understand. If we preserve the Spanish without any context, some readers may recognize the phrase immediately while others infer a general mood from the action. The text distributes knowledge differently depending on what it chooses to translate.

This is not merely a problem of inserting foreign words into English prose. It is a problem of literary address: whom does the novel presume to be listening? Who may remain temporarily outside an exchange? Which forms of knowledge are carried by the narrator, which by a character, and which by a reader's own linguistic history? Fiction in more than one language forces writers to confront questions that monolingual prose can hide.

We should distinguish three related activities. Multilingual writing may incorporate several languages within the composition itself. Translation renders a text or utterance into another language, always by making choices about effect as well as meaning. A narrative can also represent translation as an action performed by a character: a person interprets a conversation, leaves something out, chooses a polite substitute, or refuses to speak on someone else's behalf. Those three activities often meet, but they are not the same technique.

## Language is not decoration

A thinly imagined multilingual scene often uses another language only when it wants to signal atmosphere. Characters speak in ordinary English until someone expresses an emotion; then a convenient word from another language appears, often italicized, as if the family's identity were a seasoning added to otherwise standard prose. Sometimes this is consistent with a character's actual speech. Often it is an authorial shortcut.

To deepen the scene, ask what the language change accomplishes in the relationship. The mother may switch to Spanish when speaking to her daughter because they share a private history of domestic instructions. She may stay in Spanish when the estate agent arrives because she refuses to let the agent dictate the conversation. The daughter may answer in English because she is angry, because the agent is present, because she has become more comfortable speaking English, or simply because that is how she habitually replies. Every explanation produces a different person and a different scene. It would be false to present one motive as universal for bilingual families.

The point is to portray competence and choice, not to make each switch into an allegory. Sometimes a change of language is meaningful because of power. Sometimes it is the least dramatic thing happening, a normal movement within a life lived in several languages. A writer who marks every switch as exotic may misunderstand the ordinary fluency of the people being represented.

In our kitchen scene, the daughter says, 'You didn't tell me you still had the key.' Her mother replies, in English this time, 'I didn't tell you a lot of things.' The change matters not because English is a language of truth and Spanish a language of concealment, which would be an unsupported generalization, but because this particular mother has chosen a phrase her daughter cannot quietly paraphrase away. The language choice belongs to the character's history, not to a fixed theory of two languages.

## Two writers, two political responses

The question of which language a writer uses has been especially charged where language was imposed through colonial power. Chinua Achebe's influential essay 'The African Writer and the English Language,' first presented in the 1960s and later collected in *Morning Yet on Creation Day* in 1975, argued for transforming the inherited English language so that it could carry African experiences and connect speakers across linguistic communities. His position was not that colonial history was harmless. It was a proposal for what writers might do with a language that had become part of their historical situation.

Ngugi wa Thiong'o offered a different argument. His *Decolonising the Mind*, published in 1986, examined the political force of language in education, imagination, and literary institutions. Ngugi's move toward writing fiction and drama in Gikuyu was a practical as well as theoretical intervention. The relation between language, audience, and power could not be reduced to changing a few words within an otherwise unchanged literary marketplace.

It is tempting to cast this disagreement as a tidy contest in which one author valued reach and the other valued cultural belonging. That would flatten both positions. For Achebe, reshaping English is itself a political and artistic choice. For Ngugi, choosing a language also reorganizes who the work addresses and which communities can create, circulate, and control its meanings. Neither argument provides an automatic prescription for a contemporary writer in a different linguistic situation. A 2024 scholarly reassessment of the debate is useful precisely because it records the continuing diversity of positions rather than declaring a final winner.

Think about the kitchen scene in this light. Who could read the first version without translation? Who could read a version written fully in Spanish? Who has access to the publisher, the audiobook service, and the classroom in either case? No decision about audience is neutral, but neither is every choice an act of betrayal. The practical question is what the work asks languages to do, and who bears the effort of crossing between them.

## Anzaldua and the form of the border

Gloria Anzaldua's *Borderlands/La Frontera: The New Mestiza*, first published in 1987, is a particularly important example of multilingual literary form. It is not simply a conventional novel whose dialogue occasionally changes language. It moves among essay, poetry, autobiography, history, and other forms, while drawing on multiple varieties of English and Spanish and additional linguistic traditions. Its changes of language belong to its account of social borders and to its challenge to the assumption that one standardized voice should command the page.

A craft lesson from this work is not that every novel should imitate its surface. It is that the reader's experience of comprehension can itself become part of the form. A passage can allow one audience to move with immediate recognition and ask another to listen through context. A book can decline to translate everything and still be generous, precise, and artistically coherent. Conversely, leaving words unexplained is not automatically radical. The choice has to function within the particular scene and its relationships.

There are useful debates about what a writer owes readers. Suppose a character says a sentence the narrator does not understand. Should we provide an immediate gloss? An italicized translation? A later response that makes the meaning apparent? Or should we allow the narrator's noncomprehension to remain part of the event? None is correct in every situation. A fluent bilingual reader and a monolingual reader may occupy different positions in the narrative. The author decides how much distance the work invites each reader to travel.

## Four drafts of the key scene

Let us test four choices with the same incident. We will not invent a dictionary of intimate phrases, because the household has a life larger than a list of glosses.

The first draft translates all dialogue into one continuous language. 'It's nothing,' the mother says, and turns the drawer over. The reader follows immediately, but the switch in language disappears. This version may be entirely appropriate if the story's focus is the mother's gesture and not the family's linguistic history.

The second keeps the phrase: 'No pasa nada,' the mother says. Then she moves every object from the drawer onto the table, including a bent spoon and a postcard. A reader without Spanish can infer that the reassurance is not the whole story, even if the exact wording remains unavailable. The physical action carries the dramatic contradiction.

The third has the daughter translate within the scene. 'She means it's fine,' the daughter tells the estate agent. The mother looks up sharply: 'That isn't what I said.' Now translation becomes a contested action. The daughter has used her position as interpreter to smooth an encounter, but her mother refuses the simplification. The conflict is not in a colorful untranslated word. It is in who gets to decide what a person's speech means in public.

The fourth gives us a narrator who understands both languages and never explicitly glosses the phrase. The narrator might describe the daughter knowing that her mother's 'no pasa nada' often arrived just before a difficult task began. This is neither a word-for-word translation nor a refusal of access. It translates by giving a remembered pattern of use. An interpreter might choose a different phrasing in a different context, which is exactly the point: language has meaning in situations, not only in dictionaries.

The four drafts have different virtues. The all-English draft allows speed and transparency. The retained phrase invites linguistic variety without requiring a lecture inside the scene. The interpreter draft dramatizes power. The remembered-pattern draft deepens characterization. A serious writer may need all four methods in the same book.

## Translation as a writing practice

When a book is itself translated, these choices change again. A phrase that remained untranslated in an English novel may be ordinary language in a Spanish edition. A title that sounds foreign to one audience may be familiar to another. Lawrence Venuti's *The Translator's Invisibility*, published in 1995, made influential arguments about fluency, domestication, and the visibility of translation in Anglophone literary culture. Those are critical tools, not a simple order to preserve every foreign word at all costs. Translators negotiate purpose, register, readership, literary texture, and the material circumstances of publication.

A writer collaborating with a translator has opportunities to make intended distinctions legible without demanding an impossible word-for-word equivalence. What matters about the mother's remark? Its literal content, its tone, the daughter's history of hearing it, or its public effect on the estate agent? The answer may differ by scene. A phrase can be translated faithfully while losing a joke. A literal rendering can preserve a metaphor and mislead readers about ordinary usage. Precision is not always identical to literalness.

A responsible multilingual practice also has a research problem. If you do not know the speech community you are portraying, do not assume a handful of search results gives you its rhythms. Listen to fluent speakers with relevant lived contexts, ask about register and region, and make room for disagreement. Avoid using speakers as anonymous certification for an invented identity. Even native speakers differ about what sounds natural, and one person's opinion is not a universal language rule.

For audio narration, another consideration appears. A human listener cannot glance backward at a glossary as easily as a reader can. An audiobook may need contextual clues, careful pronunciation, or a narrator able to handle the languages present. But the constraints of a playback device should not silently dictate which languages a story is allowed to contain. Audio is another act of interpretation that can be designed thoughtfully.

## Who does the work of understanding?

Try a practical exercise. Write a scene with two people who know different amounts of the same language, or with one person translating for another. Begin with a plain-language version in which everyone understands each other. Then write a version that does not translate every utterance but makes the stakes clear through action. Then write a version in which a translation is incomplete, disputed, or strategically altered. For each, identify whose knowledge the reader shares and whose knowledge the text asks them to infer.

If you can, ask readers with differing language backgrounds what they experienced, not whether the writing passed a test of exotic authenticity. What did each reader understand immediately? What did each infer? Where did confusion contribute to the story, and where did it merely obstruct? If an author has intentionally placed some readers outside one exchange, what does the story give them to follow besides a sense of exclusion?

At the end of our kitchen scene, the daughter finds the brass key hanging on a nail behind the open door. She had been searching the drawer because she remembered her mother always leaving it there. The mother had moved it when the estate agent arrived, worried he would lock the side entrance before the family finished collecting its things. They have been speaking about a key, but also about who still belongs in the house and who is allowed to close it.

Language gives that conflict its changing shape. A translated phrase, a withheld gloss, a daughter's paraphrase, and a mother's correction all become actions. The lesson is not to sprinkle another language into the manuscript. It is to recognize that fiction always distributes understanding, and multilingual fiction makes the distribution visible. The writer's task is to compose that distribution with care, so the people in the story remain speakers with purposes rather than symbols of someone else's fluency.

**Historical boundary:** Achebe's essay is associated with the 1960s African literature debates and the 1975 collection *Morning Yet on Creation Day*. Ngugi's *Decolonising the Mind* appeared in 1986. Anzaldua's *Borderlands/La Frontera* appeared in 1987 and is mixed-genre, not a conventional novel. Venuti's *The Translator's Invisibility* appeared in 1995. The kitchen conversation is invented; any specific language use should be reviewed for context and register before publication.

**Practice:** Make three versions of a bilingual or interpreted scene: fully glossed, partly inferred, and explicitly contested translation. Record which readers know what and when, and what each choice changes about the character relationship.
