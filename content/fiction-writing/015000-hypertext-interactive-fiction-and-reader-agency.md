# Hypertext, Interactive Fiction, and Reader Agency

A town keeps a public tree that flowers once a year. The tree is dying. The council has enough money either to preserve it through a costly treatment or to build a public shelter for people displaced by winter floods. A young clerk named Neri must record the council's decision. In a conventional short story, the author decides which proposal wins. The reader encounters one sequence of events, although the author can still use ambiguity and multiple viewpoints to complicate it.

In an interactive story, the reader might choose which meeting Neri attends first. They might read the tree keeper's testimony, the shelter organizer's records, or the city treasurer's report. They might be permitted to cast a vote, alter a budget, or decide whether Neri publishes evidence of concealed maintenance costs. Each action changes what the reader experiences, and some may change what happens in the fictional world.

The obvious question is how many branches to write. The more interesting question is what responsibility the system gives the reader. A meaningful choice is not necessarily a choice that produces a different ending. It can determine what a character knows, which values become salient, whom the player trusts, or whether the player must live with the consequences of incomplete information. Interactive fiction turns the relation between narrative information and action into an explicit part of craft.

## Two related traditions of digital literature

Interactive fiction has a long history that predates the contemporary web. Early text adventures, including Adventure and Zork, let players issue commands in simulated worlds. Commercial text games inspired later independent communities, and Nick Montfort's Twisty Little Passages, published in 2003, analyzed interactive fiction as both literary form and game. This tradition often treats the world as a space to explore, manipulate, and reason about.

Hypertext fiction developed a partly distinct community in the late 1980s and 1990s. Works built with tools such as Storyspace invited readers to follow links among fragments, exploring nonlinear structures that might not resemble puzzle-solving games. This historical distinction matters. A linked narrative can be interactive without offering a simulation full of objects and verbs. A parser-based game can offer a rich simulated world while following a relatively directed story arc.

Later tools such as Twine made it easier to create nonlinear stories as linked passages, with optional variables and conditions. The technology changes how a writer works. A draft can be a graph of passages rather than a line of chapters. Yet the artistic question remains the same: what experience of knowledge, commitment, uncertainty, or consequence does the structure produce?

## Choice is a claim about agency

Suppose we create the town-tree story with three choices at the start: visit the tree keeper, visit the shelter organizer, or visit the treasurer. All routes lead to the same public meeting. A naive critic may say that the choices are fake because the meeting occurs regardless. But the choice could still matter: the first witness encountered may shape the reader's interpretation of later evidence. If the player reads the treasurer's numbers first, they may suspect that the shelter campaign is financially impossible. If they begin with the keeper's testimony, they may see preservation as a duty to shared memory.

This is a choice about attention and inference. It can be artistically meaningful even if the physical plot converges. The writer should be honest about the promised degree of control. If the interface says 'Decide the fate of the town' but the decision is fixed, the experience may feel deceptive. If the interface says 'Investigate the arguments before the vote,' a convergent ending may be perfectly coherent.

Conversely, branching outcomes do not automatically create agency. Imagine the player is offered two doors, one blue and one red, with no information about what lies behind them. The blue door produces a good ending; the red a bad one. Unless the work is intentionally about unknowable risk, the result may feel arbitrary. A consequential choice requires a legible relation between available information, a value or intention, and what follows. It need not be easy, and the outcomes need not be fully predictable. But the player should be able to see why the choice was theirs.

## The problem of exploding branches

Every independent branch increases the amount of story a writer must maintain. If five decisions each yield two completely separate continuations, the number of possible paths can double repeatedly. A project that promised freedom may become unfinishable, or its endings may be so thin that none feels earned. The solution is not always to reduce choice. It is to decide which kinds of variation matter.

One design gives the town-tree story a shared spine. The public meeting will happen. Before it, the player can gather testimony in different orders and uncover different facts. During it, one major decision affects the budget. After it, the ending reflects both the decision and the relationships formed along the way. The work has branching meaning without demanding an entirely separate novel for every combination.

Another design uses state rather than many separate passages. The reader can win trust with the shelter organizer while losing it with the tree keeper. A later scene changes its dialogue according to that state. This can be powerful when it makes relationships persistent. It is weak when it amounts to hiding a few altered adjectives under an interface that promises deeper consequence. Track only the variables the story can afford to acknowledge.

A third design intentionally denies control. The player may be a low-ranking clerk who can assemble evidence but cannot cast the council's vote. The resulting frustration can be meaningful if the narrative is about institutional limits. The important distinction is between a deliberate limitation of agency and a system that invites choices it does not recognize.

## Reader knowledge can diverge from character knowledge

Interactive writing introduces a special problem of perspective. A player may restart a game and remember a secret from an earlier run. The character inside a fresh run may have no way to know it. What should happen if the player attempts to use that knowledge? The writer can enforce character-bound information, acknowledge replay as part of the fiction, or design a story where the gap becomes the subject.

In our tree story, a first playthrough might reveal that the treasurer falsified a maintenance estimate. On replay, the player may wish Neri to expose the deception immediately. But perhaps Neri needs to discover evidence through actions available in that version of events. If the story allows a shortcut, it can treat the reader as inhabiting a remembering role. If it refuses, it emphasizes that knowledge belongs to the character's life, not to the player's broader experience. Either choice can be deliberate; accidental inconsistency cannot.

This distinction matters in prose too. Dramatic irony gives readers facts unknown to characters. Interactive fiction asks what readers may do with those facts. The answer affects interface design, dialogue, branching, and the reader's emotional relation to a decision.

## Procedural meaning

Some interactive works communicate an argument through rules rather than exposition. Suppose the player tries to preserve both tree and shelter. The budget system rejects the proposal because the council can reallocate funds only in whole categories. The player searches for exceptions: private donations, volunteer labor, emergency grants, delayed construction. Each attempt reveals who controls the budget and what tradeoffs remain possible. The rules convey a model of institutional power.

But simulations are simplifications. The author decides which actions are allowed and which consequences the model represents. If no characters may organize a protest, the story has silently defined politics out of the world. If donations solve everything, the story may encode a belief that private generosity can replace public obligation. Such decisions are authorial choices as real as a novelist's decision to omit a character. They deserve the same criticism.

This is why a perfectly balanced game mechanic is not always an effective story. A decision that offers equal numerical rewards may erase a moral asymmetry. A difficult choice in fiction should be difficult for intelligible reasons, not merely because two options carry the same point value. Sometimes the right artistic choice is to let the reader discover that the available menu does not include the action they most want to take.

## A branching story with one theme

Build the tree story around a central question: what does a community owe to its past when present needs are urgent? The tree keeper argues that the tree is a living record of the town's history. The organizer argues that people who cannot afford housing are also part of the town's history and future. The treasurer knows that the published figures are incomplete. Neri initially wants only to keep an accurate record and avoid becoming part of the dispute.

Let the player's early choices determine which testimonies Neri hears. Let one later choice determine whether Neri publishes the incomplete numbers before the vote. This choice may expose a dishonest process while undermining trust in the council. Then allow the budget decision to produce a concrete outcome: the tree survives and the shelter is delayed, the shelter opens and the tree dies, or a compromise preserves a cutting while redirecting most funds. Do not award a simple good or bad label. Show who pays each cost and who carries forward the memory of the decision.

The ending can return to Neri's role as recorder. In one version she writes an official summary that omits the dissenting voices. In another she attaches the testimonies and risks her job. The interactive form has made readers participate in the very problem of selection that the fiction explores. They did not merely choose between two endings. They chose which accounts became visible and, finally, how the town would describe itself.

The central achievement of contemporary digital storytelling is not that software permits infinite alternatives. It does not. It is that the act of selecting, searching, deciding, and sometimes failing can become part of narrative meaning. A linear novelist chooses what the reader learns in what order. An interactive author designs a space in which those choices are partly shared, but never without structure. The art lies in making that structure express a consequential idea about the world.

## Companion (display only)

**Design distinctions:** Parser interactive fiction often emphasizes manipulating a simulated world through commands. Hypertext fiction emphasizes linked texts and nonlinear traversal. Choice-based fiction often exposes explicit decisions. These traditions overlap and cannot be separated solely by tools.

**Practice:** Design six passages and two persistent variables for the tree/shelter story. Include one information-only choice, one consequential decision, one convergent scene, and an ending that acknowledges both variables. Explain the purpose of each branch before adding more.

**Further reading:** Nick Montfort, [Twisty Little Passages](https://mitpress.mit.edu/9780262134361/twisty-little-passages/) (2003); Jill Walker Rettberg, [Hypertext Fiction from 1987–1999](https://elmcip.net/critical-writing/hypertext-fiction-1987-1999) (2010); [Twine project site](https://www.twinery.org/), including its documentation and history.
