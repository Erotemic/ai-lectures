# Narrative Time: Order, Duration, and Repetition

Twenty years after a flood, a woman named Mara finds a bus ticket in a coat she has not worn since the river rose. The ticket shows a departure time of six twenty-two. She has told her daughter, many times, that the last bus left before she reached the square. But the ticket is unused, and it is hers. Had she boarded the bus and then climbed off? Had someone given it to her? Or has she been telling the story with one event in the wrong place for twenty years?

That is a problem about memory, but it is also a problem about storytelling. A writer must decide not only what happened, but what the reader encounters first, how long the reader remains with each moment, and whether an event returns more than once in the telling. These choices alter what the same facts mean.

The useful starting distinction is between the time of the story and the time of its narration. Story time is the imagined sequence of events in the world. Narrative time is the order and pace in which the text presents them. The terms are analytical conveniences: a novel may describe a dream that resists dates, or a memory that refuses stable sequence. Nevertheless, even a work that questions chronology needs a relation to chronology for its departures to register.

In a study published within the French book *Figures III* in 1972, and in English as *Narrative Discourse* in 1980, Gerard Genette organized the study of narrative time around three questions: order, duration, and frequency. He did not invent every device he named. Flashbacks and compressed histories existed long before the terminology. His achievement was to give readers and writers a precise way to distinguish effects that often get lumped together under the loose name of pacing.

Let us build a chronological version of Mara's flood before breaking it apart.

## The story that happened

At six oh five on a rainy morning, Mara sees a maintenance light flashing near the river gate. At six seventeen, the gate mechanism fails. At six twenty-two, a bus turns into the square, its driver calling for passengers to board. Mara looks down a side street, thinking of her neighbor Elias, who cannot walk quickly. At six forty, a shelter opens in the school gym. At noon, Elias arrives there by boat. The ticket remains in Mara's coat. Twenty years later, she finds it.

These are invented facts for an invented story, not a reconstruction of any particular flood. We can place them on a straight line. That line is valuable even if we never show it to a reader. It stops us from accidentally giving Mara information she could not have possessed at six oh five, or from implying that the bus waited until after the shelter opened. It also identifies the human question beneath the sequence: when a route to safety appeared, what did Mara decide she owed another person?

Here is a plain chronological opening: Mara saw the warning light. Twelve minutes later the gate failed. Five minutes after that the bus reached the square. She had to choose whether to leave without Elias.

There is nothing intrinsically weak about that approach. It makes the sequence clear and locates pressure at the choice. A nonchronological version has to earn its complexity by doing work that the chronological one cannot.

## Order: the reader arrives somewhere else

Suppose the novel opens twenty years later, with the unused ticket. Mara's daughter asks why she kept it, and Mara says she does not remember taking it. We next see her at the bus stop during the flood. We do not immediately learn what she did. Only later do we return to the shelter where Elias arrived, and later still to the warning light that began the morning.

Now an object is a question before it is a fact. The reader first knows that Mara's present account is troubled; then the reader discovers the day that account concerns. The delayed warning light is no longer merely the first event. It may become evidence of what Mara knew and when she knew it.

Genette called a backward movement in narration an analepsis, often called a flashback. A forward movement is a prolepsis, often called a flash-forward. These words refer to the relation between the presented sequence and an established narrative position, not simply to whether a sentence uses the past or future tense. A story narrated entirely in the past tense can still travel backward and forward relative to its own main line.

Imagine a second opening: 'The ticket would remain in her coat for twenty years. On the morning of the flood, Mara did not yet know she would keep it.' That first sentence reaches forward from the flood toward a later discovery. It creates a promise of consequence. But it also takes a possible surprise off the table. If the narrator announces the long survival of the ticket, the reader may stop wondering whether Mara escapes with her possessions. Instead the reader may wonder why this particular object survives and what it eventually accuses her of.

Neither movement is automatically sophisticated. The important question is what kind of attention it recruits. Beginning after the event may create inquiry. Revealing a future consequence may create dread. Remaining chronological may create the force of an irreversible decision. These are different contracts with the reader.

One revision test is to put the same three scenes in three orders: warning, bus, ticket; ticket, bus, warning; and bus, ticket, warning. Then ask not which order sounds literary, but what each order makes the reader believe the story is about. The first might be about action under pressure. The second might be about memory and self-exoneration. The third might be about a decision and its delayed meaning.

## Duration: a page is not a minute

Narrative order concerns where the reader goes. Narrative duration concerns how much space a stretch of imagined time receives. The gate takes one second to fail. A writer could spend several paragraphs describing the lever, the vibration, a drop of water on its housing, and Mara's sudden awareness that the warning light has changed color. That second is elongated in the experience of reading. Or a writer could dispatch an entire month in a sentence: After the flood, the school stayed open as a shelter until autumn.

Genette distinguished four major ways a text relates the extension of the telling to the extension of events: scene, summary, pause, and ellipsis. In a scene, actions and dialogue unfold with a comparatively sustained relation to story time. In a summary, extended events are compressed. In a descriptive pause, narrative attention lingers while the represented action is effectively suspended. In an ellipsis, some span of story time passes without being narrated.

These are not stopwatch categories. Reading takes different amounts of time for different readers, and a scene does not have to last exactly as long as the conversation it presents. The categories describe relationships and effects, not precise equations.

Consider Mara standing beside the bus door. Summary could say: She hesitated, searched for Elias, and let the bus leave. That sentence compresses the decision. A scene might include the driver shouting over the engine, a child pressing against Mara's arm, and Mara attempting to see through the rain. The decision becomes lived pressure. A pause could linger over the painted route number and the water flowing through the grooves of the tire as Mara's attention catches on details. An ellipsis might cut from the driver calling her name to the school gym at six forty, omitting the act of choosing entirely.

Omission can carry moral weight. If the reader never sees Mara step away from the bus, is the absence a mystery the narrative will later investigate? Or is it a shortcut used to avoid writing the hardest scene? The answer depends on what other scenes do. An ellipsis can make a reader participate in reconstructing an action, but it can also leave the causal center of a story empty.

Likewise, slowing down a moment does not automatically deepen it. A paragraph devoted to every raindrop might intensify Mara's dissociation, or might merely distract from Elias's danger. The question is whether the chosen scale of attention exposes a change in perception, knowledge, or choice.

## Frequency: once, many times, every time

The third question is often overlooked. How many times does something happen, and how many times is it told? In Genette's terms, a singulative telling narrates once an event that occurred once. An iterative telling narrates once what happened repeatedly. A repetitive telling returns several times to one event.

Mara and her daughter have discussed the flood at every birthday dinner. We might summarize that recurring pattern in one sentence: Every spring, Mara told the story of missing the last bus. That is iterative narration. We might devote a full scene to one particular dinner, when the daughter begins to ask new questions. That is singulative narration. We might show the bus departure three times: once as Mara remembers it, once from the driver's notebook, and once from the daughter's reconstruction after finding the ticket. That is a repetitive presentation of what may be the same event.

The distinction matters because repetition can change rather than reinforce a claim. The first telling may foreground shame, the second a timetable, the third the daughter's anger at not being trusted with a complicated answer. A repeated event is not necessarily proof that one version is false. Different descriptions can be compatible, contradictory, or simply concerned with different things. The novel must decide which tensions are material.

Time in fiction therefore also has a social dimension. The calendar of a transport agency differs from the calendar of a household. A rescue crew records minutes since dispatch; a child remembers when the adults began to whisper. A public anniversary can compress decades of reconstruction into one ceremony while leaving an individual's experience of the morning unresolved. These are not separate physical clocks. They are different systems for making time meaningful.

## The nonlinear story's obligation

Recent fiction supplies many approaches to these problems. Jennifer Egan's *A Visit from the Goon Squad*, published in 2010, moves among characters and periods in ways that make a life legible through its scattered consequences. But invoking a celebrated nonlinear novel is not a reason to scatter one's own chapters. The purpose is not a fashionable timeline. The purpose is to change what the reader can infer about character, cause, and history.

Try a diagnostic rather than a rule. Draw two columns. In the first, list everything that occurs in the fictional world in chronological order. Include the events your manuscript currently conceals. In the second, list the order in which the reader receives the information. Mark what the reader knows after each section, what the reader only suspects, and what a character falsely assumes. Then mark the points where the narrative slows into scene, accelerates into summary, rests in a pause, or passes through an ellipsis. Finally circle every event narrated more than once.

Ask each displacement a question. If the reader knows the outcome before the cause, what becomes interesting in place of suspense about outcome? If an action is skipped, who benefits from the absence? If a day receives fifty pages and a decade receives three sentences, what value judgment is embedded in that allocation? If the same memory returns, what becomes newly available on its second appearance?

The diagram cannot write the sentences. It will not tell you whether Mara's first look at the ticket should be funny, humiliating, or frightening. It gives you a way to connect formal design to emotional and ethical effect, so the complexity is intentional instead of merely difficult to follow.

## What the ticket does not answer

Return to Mara twenty years later. Perhaps the last bus really did leave before she reached the square, and she was handed a ticket by someone gathering evacuees. Perhaps she boarded and came back for Elias. Perhaps the driver waited and she could not bear to leave. These are materially different histories. A writer can resolve the question, or choose to let some evidence remain ambiguous, but should know which uncertainty the reader is being asked to carry.

An ending might place the daughter with the old timetable and the ticket. She can establish when the bus was scheduled, but not what Mara's hand was doing when the door closed. The discovery shifts the story from solving a transportation mystery to recognizing a life lived under a memory that may have become too simple.

The essential craft principle is not that stories should be nonlinear. It is that time in fiction is composed. You decide what precedes what in the telling, what receives attention, and which events are allowed to return. Those decisions shape what counts as an explanation, which people remain visible, and how a reader experiences the distance between an event and the story later made from it.

**Source boundary:** Genette's account of order, duration, and frequency was published in *Figures III* (1972), with *Narrative Discourse* in English in 1980. He systematized earlier narratological study rather than inventing flashback or nonlinear fiction. The flood, timetable, bus ticket, and characters are entirely invented teaching examples.

**Practice:** Write a factual chronology of five fictional events. Present it in three orders. Mark which events are dramatized as scenes, summarized, paused over, or omitted. Tell one event twice and identify precisely what the second telling adds.
