# A Workshop in Competing Poetics: Four Revisions of One Story

At the back of a small electronics-repair shop, Elise finds an old tabletop radio with a handwritten tag: 'Return to Mrs. Vale before the weekend.' Mrs. Vale has recently died. Her daughter will collect the radio on Monday. Elise replaces a corroded contact, turns the dial, and hears the voice of a woman saying, 'Please don't give it back.' The voice sounds like Mrs. Vale. Elise turns off the radio, waits, and tries again. This time an ordinary weather forecast comes through.

That is the whole premise for today's workshop. The task is not to discover the one correct version. It is to see how different approaches to writing fiction ask different questions about the same materials. A short-story theorist may ask where attention should be concentrated. A speculative writer may ask what follows if the voice genuinely crossed a boundary. A theorist of narrative knowledge may ask what Elise can reasonably infer. A revision teacher may ask whether the very scene the author likes is obscuring the story's actual subject.

If these theories are useful, they should change what the writer does on the page. If they merely give us labels for a story we would have written anyway, their value is limited. So we will draft several openings, diagnose the costs of each, and make one substantial revision that does not simply combine everything. Combining all possible techniques is a reliable way to turn a small story into a manuscript that has forgotten what it wants.

The examples are invented. They are not written in imitation of any named author's characteristic sentences, and they are not presented as passages from an existing story. We are testing broad craft questions, not borrowing anyone's voice.

## Version one: the incomplete realist scene

Start by removing the apparently supernatural event. Elise repairs the radio and finds an old cassette taped inside its wooden case. The cassette contains Mrs. Vale's voice, recorded years earlier, asking that the radio not be returned. The daughter is due Monday, and Elise has no idea why her mother would have hidden that instruction.

A plausible opening is this: 'On Friday Elise tested the radio after replacing its corroded contact. It worked well enough to find the public station, where a man was reading tomorrow's weather. Inside the case she found a cassette labelled in a cramped hand: Not for Helen. Helen was Mrs. Vale's daughter. She had called twice to ask when the radio would be ready.'

This version turns the central question into an ordinary ethical problem. The instruction may reflect an estrangement, a private joke, or an outdated wish. Elise has a duty to return the repaired object and a possible duty to respect an unknown person's confidence. It is easy to imagine her motives without inventing a metaphysical rule: curiosity, kindness, a desire to be important in someone else's family history, or fear of being blamed for withholding property.

A compressed version might end after she places the cassette beside the radio and writes the daughter's name on both. The reader is left to consider what a repairer can restore and what is not theirs to fix. A longer story might include a conversation with Helen and reveal how the two women remember Mrs. Vale differently.

But notice the risk. We have removed the strange voice, yet preserved a highly convenient hidden object. If the cassette exists only to deliver the theme, it may feel manufactured. To make the realist scene convincing, give it ordinary resistance. The cassette might be unplayable. Elise may have no cassette player. The tag might be in someone else's handwriting. Helen might be more interested in recovering the radio's power cord than its history. The work becomes specific when its people are not waiting obediently for the author's revelation.

This version has a legible source for the message and perhaps a more modest ending. We should not call it superior realism just because it offers a physical explanation. It has chosen one way of connecting evidence and obligation.

## Version two: a speculative rule with consequences

Restore the impossible transmission, but resist the instinct to explain it immediately. The first question is not 'what is the science of ghost radios?' It is what conditions in this story make the transmission repeatable or consequential.

Suppose the radio receives one message from a person at the moment another person is about to give away something they once owned. The message may be from the dead, from a future memory, or from some unexplained feature of the object. This is a rule proposed by our invented story, not a claim about the real world. We need enough demonstrations to let the reader understand what the rule permits, while leaving room for the narrative to complicate it.

Try this opening: 'Elise heard the voice before she had connected the antenna. Please don't give it back, it said. The next day, when the baker donated his father's clock to the shop, the radio spoke again, though nobody had touched its dial. Keep the key, said a man's voice. Elise asked the baker whether the clock had a key. He looked at her for a long time before reaching into his coat.'

The second event has changed the story. The first voice might be a mechanical accident, a memory, or an eerie effect. The second suggests a pattern. It also creates a problem: if the radio can convey warnings, when is Elise obliged to listen? Can she refuse to be the town's secret messenger? What if a voice demands a cruel act? Who has authority to interpret a fragmentary message?

A weak continuation introduces more phenomena without developing their costs. The radio predicts storms, brings back lost pets, solves a murder, and speaks a different language every Tuesday. The list is colorful, but each new gift can dissolve the previous problem. A stronger continuation follows one restriction. Perhaps messages arrive only while a repaired object is being relinquished. Then Elise cannot investigate at leisure. She hears something at the moment of another person's vulnerable decision, and the story acquires urgency from that limitation.

The speculative premise may illuminate the real emotional conflict, but we must decide whether the reader is promised eventual knowledge of the mechanism. A mystery about who built the radio will need different evidence from a story about whether Elise has a right to carry a message. If we set up the former and end with the latter, we must make the change in the story's attention feel earned rather than evasive.

## Version three: the same scene through different voices

Now remove the question of mechanism from the center and alter the distribution of knowledge. Imagine three speakers describing the radio: Elise, Helen, and an older repairer who used to know Mrs. Vale. Each has a plausible, incomplete account. Do not make one speaker the author's spokesperson and the others merely mistaken witnesses.

Elise says the voice was clear and that she is certain of its words. Helen says her mother used to record messages for radio clubs and sometimes played them back at family gatherings, making people think a stranger had entered the room. The older repairer says the radio's receiver had been damaged long ago, so it should not have produced any recognizable broadcast. These accounts do not establish the truth by taking a vote. They reveal the different interests each speaker has in what counts as evidence.

Helen's perspective deserves care. She may not want a stranger to tell her what her dead mother wished. Perhaps she spent years caring for Mrs. Vale and is exhausted by people claiming special knowledge of their relationship. Her skepticism does not make her cold. Elise may be genuinely frightened and still enjoy becoming the person with a secret. The older repairer may know an important detail and still be wrong about the technical condition of the set.

Write the scene in direct dialogue. Helen asks what Elise heard. Elise answers. Helen asks why she turned the set off before recording the message. Elise says she was afraid. Helen says that if the message was real, her fear has now become part of the evidence. The older repairer points out that the power cable was disconnected. Each line changes the terms of the inquiry.

The difficulty is not simply giving characters different opinions. It is allowing each to possess a way of organizing experience that the narrative cannot dismiss without cost. Helen's remembered mother is not the same as Elise's disembodied voice. Elise's fear is not cured by Helen's family anecdotes. The repairer's practical knowledge is not a metaphor for moral truth. When we let these perspectives remain partly independent, the story can investigate the limits of testimony without declaring that every claim is equally probable.

The result might end without solving the voice. Helen takes the radio home and leaves the cassette, if there is one, with Elise. Or she asks Elise to repair it again, this time while both women listen. A shared act can move a relationship even when their accounts remain incompatible. That is a narrative change, not a substitute for one.

## Version four: revision begins by doubting the premise

The most disruptive revision is to ask whether the radio is the right object. Perhaps our real interest is not a supernatural message but the discomfort of deciding what to return to another person after a death. The radio may be valuable because it once carried Mrs. Vale's broadcasts. Or perhaps it is distracting, and the story would be stronger if the object were a repaired sewing machine, an annotated recipe book, or an uncollected coat.

This is the stage at which a writer can feel that changing the premise betrays the work already done. But earlier drafts are evidence of discovery, not a legal contract. Suppose we notice that every moving scene has involved Helen's conflicting desire: she wants the radio, yet fears that receiving it will require accepting other people's stories about her mother. Maybe the actual story begins when Helen enters the shop, and the ghostly voice is a memory Elise describes later. That new opening puts the human encounter before the puzzle.

Try a reversal. Open with Helen saying, 'You can keep it.' Elise looks down at a radio she has spent two evenings restoring. She expected gratitude and a payment, not permission to take possession. When Helen explains that the set was always louder than anyone in the house, the reader learns a family history through an apparently modest refusal. Only after this scene do we learn that Elise heard a voice. That information now complicates Helen's action instead of determining it.

This is a structural revision, not merely a line edit. The causal center has moved from the discovery of a phenomenon to the negotiation of an obligation. We may still use the voice. But the voice is now one pressure among several. If we decide that its inclusion overwhelms the human negotiation, we might remove it altogether. If its impossibility makes the negotiation newly difficult, we keep it and establish the minimum rule the story requires.

There is no prize for retaining the most ambitious combination. A draft with psychological realism, an occult receiver, three narrators, a timeline puzzle, and a public scandal may be rich; it may also be five separate good ideas refusing to cooperate. The writer must choose which discovery the reader is supposed to carry.

## A comparative map of revision decisions

Let's summarize the changes without pretending they are objective grades. Version one concentrates on a human obligation and uses a discovered recording to make it concrete. Version two offers a rule about impossible messages and must pay attention to consequences and reader expectations. Version three distributes evidence among speakers and allows disagreement to persist. Version four revises the premise itself to ask whether the story's strongest source of pressure lay somewhere else.

Each choice has an associated failure. The realist version may feel arranged toward a tidy lesson. The speculative version may explain too much or escape into arbitrary magic. The multi-voice version may give everyone a speech but nobody a decisive act. The structural revision may become so eager to improve the original that it removes the strange event that made the writer care. These are failure modes to investigate, not inevitabilities.

Now imagine trying to keep only two versions. We choose the speculative rule and Helen's resistant point of view. Elise hears a message that may be from the dead. Helen suspects a performance, yet agrees to listen because the radio contains a crackling background song she remembers from childhood. They test it at night. No voice comes. Helen says she is relieved. Elise believes her, although Helen does not sound relieved. The absence of the voice becomes an event that tests the relationship.

Would this work better if we added the cassette explanation from version one? Possibly. But doing so would change the epistemic promise: the question would turn toward whether Elise was tricked, rather than toward what it means for a daughter to encounter a stranger's claim about her mother. We need to choose consciously, not add the explanation simply because an editor asks for more clarity.

## The obligations of an honest workshop

A workshop can help identify these alternatives, but it can also impose its preferences as if they were universal truths. Matthew Salesses's work on craft and workshop conventions is a useful reminder that the imagined reader is not a culturally neutral object. A person who values causal closure may ask us to explain the radio. Another reader may be less troubled by ontological ambiguity and more concerned that Helen has been flattened into an obstacle. Neither response is worthless. Neither settles the artistic question alone.

For that reason, separate reports of experience from prescriptions. Ask: 'At what moment did you believe the radio was supernatural?' or 'What did Helen seem to want?' before asking 'Should I reveal the mechanism?' A reader can reliably report their interpretation even when their proposed fix would break the work. A writer can learn from criticism without surrendering to the critic's imagined ideal story.

One exercise is to ask readers for two different reverse outlines. The first records the sequence of facts as they believe those facts occurred. The second records the sequence of emotional pressures on the characters. If the outlines diverge, the divergence may be fruitful. Readers might misunderstand a fact because a speaker is unreliable, while understanding precisely what the misunderstanding costs. Or the factual gaps might be accidental and prevent the emotions from making sense. The difference tells us what to revise.

Another exercise is to read an entire scene aloud while pausing after each paragraph. Ask not whether the sentences are beautiful, but what the reader must do next: remember an earlier fact, infer a motive, revise a belief, endure uncertainty, or wait for information. A paragraph that performs none of these may still be necessary for texture or rest. If so, identify what kind of rest it offers. The test is a way to listen to the draft, not a machine for deleting life from it.

Finally, consider the ethics of the premise itself. Our fictional Helen is bereaved. The narrative could use her grief to justify an entertaining haunting without giving her any independent desires. We can guard against that not by avoiding supernatural fiction, but by creating circumstances in which her choice has consequences that would matter even if the radio never spoke. Perhaps she wants the set because she promised to donate it to a local museum. Perhaps she wants to destroy it because it filled the family house with a sound she hated. Perhaps she both misses and resents the mother whose voice everyone else is eager to hear. Those contradictions are the material of a person, not impediments to plot.

## A final draft and a stopping point

Here is one possible closing scene. Helen returns to the shop a week later. Elise has kept the radio unplugged. Helen asks whether the voice spoke again. Elise says no. Helen puts the radio on the counter between them and asks Elise to show her how the repaired dial works. Elise explains the switch, the tuning band, and the corroded contact she replaced. When she is finished, Helen turns the dial herself. They hear music, static, and a station announcing the time.

Helen says that her mother once insisted a radio was a thing you listened to with other people. Then she laughs because her mother almost never let anyone else choose the station. Elise does not tell her that she has written down the message in a notebook and crossed it out twice. Nothing supernatural happens in this scene. The earlier possibility remains, but the final action is a shared act of listening. Whether that ending satisfies depends on which story we have taught the reader to expect.

If the manuscript had promised an investigation into the source of the voice, this ending might feel evasive. If it had promised a struggle over who may claim to know a dead person, the act of listening may be enough. If it had promised a system of messages from departed owners, we might need to show the cost of refusing to interpret one. There is no ending that can be evaluated apart from the promises and developments that lead to it.

When is a draft finished? We cannot specify a number of revisions. But we can ask whether its major choices now support rather than contradict one another; whether the reader can encounter uncertainty without being deprived of essential causes; whether the people remain more than functions; and whether further changes open possibilities or only exchange one defensible version for another. At some point the writer must stop, not because a formula has declared success, but because the particular work has become coherent enough to release.

The point of studying schools of fiction writing is not to assemble a single master procedure. It is to acquire a richer vocabulary for making choices. Poe can sharpen the question of effect; Munro can widen the scale contained in a short narrative; Davis can test what a tiny act of language can accomplish; speculative hybrids can alter what counts as a rule; Saunders can focus attention on a reader's successive expectations; and critics of workshop defaults can ask whose ways of reading and living have been privileged. None of these tools absolves the writer from deciding what this story needs.

Elise switches off the radio after Helen leaves. She can still hear the sentence she thought came from Mrs. Vale. She does not know whether she has done the right thing. But she knows that she has finally asked Helen what she wanted, and waited long enough to hear the answer. The workshop ends there: not in a universal recipe, but in a particular choice that has become visible because we tested several ways of telling it.

## Companion (display only)

**Scope:** All radio, repair-shop, and family scenes are original examples. The named writers are discussed as sources of craft questions, not imitated or placed in a fictitious real-world debate. The final scene is one workable alternative, not a claimed canonical solution.

**Practice:** Draft a premise, then make four deliberately incompatible versions: human obligation, speculative rule, competing testimonies, and changed opening or point of view. Identify the distinctive promise and failure risk of each. Choose one hybrid and revise one complete scene, then compare two readers' factual and emotional reverse outlines.
