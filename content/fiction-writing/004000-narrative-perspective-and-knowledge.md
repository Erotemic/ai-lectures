# Narrative Perspective: Who Knows What?

At six in the morning, a municipal records clerk sees a red ribbon tied to the door of the flood archive. She knows the ribbon is used when a document must not be removed. Someone has tied it on the outside, where visitors can see it, rather than on the inside, where the staff would use it. She reaches for the handle. Across the street, a man who has been awake all night watches her reach. He is hoping she will open the door. A child waiting for a bus sees both adults and assumes they are arguing.

Three people have witnessed one small event. They have not experienced the same story. The clerk sees a possible breach of procedure. The man sees the last chance to recover evidence before a public meeting. The child sees a social conflict and may remember the ribbon only because it resembles the one on a birthday present. If we choose one of these people as the center of the narration, the event acquires different causes, stakes, and meanings without a single physical fact changing.

Narrative perspective is the set of decisions that controls whose experience is made available, what is known at a given moment, how events are interpreted, and how close the telling stands to its subject. Those decisions shape suspense and sympathy, but they also shape the truth that a reader is permitted to assemble. The aim of this lecture is to make perspective an instrument of story design rather than a label such as first person or third person.

## The speaker is not always the observer

A useful first distinction comes from narrative theory: the person or voice telling a story need not be the person through whose perceptions we experience it. Imagine a retired clerk telling us about the morning she found the ribbon. She speaks in the first person, years after the event. The young clerk who touched the door did not know why the ribbon was there. The older narrator might know exactly who placed it. One grammatical I therefore contains two positions: the person who lived the event and the person who now tells it.

Alternatively, an impersonal third-person narrator can stay close to the young clerk. We are given the gray paint lifting around the lock, the ribbon damp with river mist, and her unease at the incorrect placement. We are not given the watching man's thoughts. A different third-person narrator could cross the street and reveal that the man tied the ribbon himself. Both accounts use the same pronouns. Their difference is what the telling can know and disclose.

The narratologist Gérard Genette helped make this distinction precise by separating voice, the question of who speaks, from focalization, the question of whose experience or knowledge constrains the telling. You do not need these terms to write good fiction. They become useful when a passage feels confused. Has the author changed the speaking voice? Has the author moved to another consciousness? Or has the narrator simply reported information that the current character could not possess? Those are different editorial problems.

Consider this sentence: 'Lena hesitated at the door because she knew the ribbon had been tied by a liar.' If Lena has merely seen the ribbon, 'she knew' grants her evidence she does not have. The narrator may be omniscient and revealing an independent fact, but then the sentence should clarify whose certainty is involved. A closer version is: 'Lena hesitated. Whoever tied the ribbon had wanted the whole street to notice.' The second sentence is an inference. It might be right, and its uncertainty can become productive.

## The same incident, told three ways

Let the clerk be Lena, the watcher be Oren, and the child be Kiri. In the physical event, Oren has tied the ribbon because the flood archive contains an old inspection report. The city plans to demolish the archive that afternoon. Oren believes the report shows that a retaining wall was knowingly built below the approved height. He cannot enter the building because he was dismissed from his engineering post. Lena has not learned of the demolition order. Kiri knows that her grandmother lives behind the retaining wall, but knows nothing about engineering reports.

First, take Lena's close perspective. 'The red ribbon was on the wrong side of the door. Lena could picture her supervisor's mouth tightening when he saw it. She removed her gloves, folded them into her pocket, and tried the handle. Locked. Nobody had bothered to tell her the locks were being changed.' The immediate story is an institutional mystery: who altered the procedure, and why has the clerk lost access? Lena's attention to gloves, supervisors, and locks is characterization. The prose lets the reader encounter the system as a person who has learned to live inside it.

Now take Oren's perspective. 'He had spent the night choosing where to knot the ribbon. Too high and she would miss it. Too low and the cleaning crew would take it down. Lena stopped beneath the red strip. Oren's whole plan had led to this thin motion of her hand toward the lock.' We know he arranged the sign, but not yet why. The ribbon is an instrument of action rather than an alarming discovery. Lena is a source of hope. We may still wonder whether Oren is manipulating her and whether his cause is just.

Finally, Kiri. 'The woman in the green coat was touching the government building. The man across the road leaned forward as if a race had begun. Nobody spoke. Kiri had never seen two grown-ups argue so quietly.' Here the same scene is charged by a mistaken interpretation. The narrator can remain faithful to Kiri's sensory experience without making Kiri foolish. She recognizes tension and lacks its explanation. She might later tell her grandmother that a man threatened a woman at the archive. A child's account can be sincere, emotionally informative, and factually incomplete.

Notice what has not happened. We did not swap a few adjectives to make three decorative voices. We changed the questions the reader is able to ask, the evidence the reader receives, and the decisions that can matter. A perspective draft should be judged by those changes before it is judged by stylistic flair.

## Information creates different kinds of suspense

Suppose we remain with Lena. The reader knows only that the ribbon violates procedure. Suspense comes from investigation. We want to know what the sign means and whether she can enter the archive. If the prose stays honest about her observations, a later discovery can revise our interpretation without breaking trust.

Suppose instead we have followed Oren for a chapter and know he placed the ribbon to warn Lena. Now suspense comes from anticipation. The reader understands the danger and watches Lena misread a signal. When she pockets the ribbon as an administrative nuisance, that small action becomes painful. We are not asking what the ribbon means. We are asking whether she will learn in time.

This second arrangement is often called dramatic irony: the audience possesses relevant knowledge that a character lacks. But the phrase is not a command to reveal everything. We might know that Oren is trying to prevent a disaster while still doubting whether his evidence is sound. Knowledge is not a binary switch. A story can give a reader a fact, withhold a motive, and leave a consequence uncertain.

Another possibility is to know more than each character but less than the group collectively. Imagine alternating Lena and Kiri. Lena understands the archive's filing system; Kiri knows that the wall behind her grandmother's house recently began leaking. Neither has connected the two. The reader can connect them before the characters do. Alternating views then creates an emergent pattern rather than just a change of scenery.

## Distance changes the emotional experience

Perspective also has distance. The narration might report, 'Lena was irritated by another procedural mistake.' That sentence identifies a feeling from outside. Or it might move closer: 'Another ribbon, another policy no one had thought to tell the morning staff. Of course.' The irritation is now embedded in Lena's wording, though the sentence need not be in quotation marks. In third-person prose, this blending of narrator grammar with a character's idiom is commonly called free indirect discourse.

Close distance is powerful because readers infer a mind through word choice, habit, and selective attention. It also has risks. If every detail is filtered through immediate emotion, the reader may lose geographic clarity. A wider sentence can restore it: 'The archive stood on the old riverbank, between the courthouse and the ferry road.' Widening does not necessarily break a point of view. An experienced person can know that geography. What matters is whether the movement makes narrative sense and whether its voice is consistent enough for the reader to orient themselves.

Distance is not identical to affection. A narrator can remain close to someone who is cruel, mistaken, or tedious. A distant narrator can treat a character tenderly. Sympathy develops from the relationship among the narrated actions, the language used to present them, and the moral space left for judgment. A first-person confession can be cold; a panoramic account can be intimate.

Try an experiment with Lena. In a distant account, write that she had worked at the archive for nineteen years. In a close account, show that she notices a missing brass screw because she remembers when the building's sign was mounted, before Kiri was born. Both convey duration. The second connects time to attention. Neither is inherently better; one may serve an overview, the other a lived moment.

## Unreliability is more than a surprise lie

A narrator can be unreliable in several ways. The narrator might report an event incorrectly, interpret a correct observation badly, or judge conduct by values the broader story invites us to question. These are separable. Kiri can describe Lena's posture accurately and conclude incorrectly that she is being threatened. Oren can report his dismissal accurately while minimizing the mistake that led to it. Lena can record every document precisely while believing that authorized procedures are therefore ethical.

The interesting effect often lies in a gap the reader can detect. If Oren says he never cared about professional recognition, but he repeatedly recalls how his colleagues failed to thank him, his language gives us grounds to reconsider his self-description. The reader does not need a final chapter announcing that Oren lied. His ordinary habits can expose a blind spot without denying every fact he reports.

Unreliability should have a purpose beyond tricking the audience. It can reveal how someone protects their identity, how a community mistakes its own conventions for universal truths, or how trauma, innocence, prejudice, and limited information affect testimony. The author should know which discrepancies are deliberate. If the narrator cannot remember an event, that fact can be part of the story; if the writer accidentally moves a door to another wall, the resulting confusion is not a sophisticated narrative puzzle.

A useful private worksheet has three columns: what occurred, what this person believes occurred, and what the reader can presently infer. You do not need to publish the worksheet. It keeps the author from confusing a meaningful gap in knowledge with an inconsistent draft.

## The ethics of what the reader is allowed to know

A viewpoint can conceal someone else's life. Lena may describe Oren as a disgraced former employee because she has heard only the supervisor's explanation of his departure. If the novel never permits Oren's perspective, readers may accept that label as the story's judgment. This may be the author's intended portrait of institutional blindness. It may also be an unintended simplification. The craft question is not whether every character gets equal page time. It is whether the narration gives readers enough evidence to understand the limits of its own claims.

Nor does fairness require moral symmetry. Suppose the wall's defect puts residents in danger, and senior officials have concealed that defect. Their explanations can be internally coherent without deserving the same ethical weight as the residents' need for safety. A perspective can make a harmful decision intelligible while showing its victims, constraints, and alternatives.

Even apparently neutral omniscience has a stance. A sentence such as 'Everyone in the neighborhood feared the river' may flatten the people who depend on the river for work, worship, or pleasure. An author can deliberately speak in broad collective terms, but should know when a broad statement hides disagreement. Sometimes one sharply specific observation conveys a community more honestly than a claim about everyone.

## Perspective in nonlinear and interactive stories

A story told out of order makes knowledge management more visible. The older Lena may narrate the archive incident from a time when the wall has already collapsed. She could tell us the outcome immediately and make the story about culpability rather than suspense. Or she could withhold it, speaking from present knowledge while closely reproducing her earlier confusion. Either choice needs a reason in the voice: what is the older Lena trying to do by telling this story now?

In interactive fiction, the reader or player may control a character who cannot know everything the player has learned in another branch. A practical design distinguishes three inventories: facts true in the fictional world, facts a character has observed, and facts the audience or player has been shown. Those sets can overlap without being identical. If a character accuses Oren of hiding the inspection report, the accusation should follow from an in-world clue, not from information available only in a separate playthrough.

Different branches can reveal different facets of the same event, but it matters which observations persist after reconvergence. A choice that changes what Lena knows may change the meaning of her later decision even if the wall, the report, and the demolition date remain fixed. Narrative state is partly informational state.

## A practical revision: perspective as design

Return to the ribbon at the archive. A weak draft may jump between minds whenever the writer wants to explain something: Lena sees the lock, Oren remembers the report, Kiri fears for her grandmother, and then the narrator announces what the mayor secretly plans. The scene contains information, but no stable experience through which the reader can organize it.

Choose Lena as the center. Give her an immediate task: retrieve a ledger before the day's inspection. Make the ribbon troubling because she has spent her career protecting evidence and has never seen an official warning placed like a public advertisement. She tries the lock; it fails. She notices a fresh scrape on the plate. Across the street someone moves. The reader has enough to recognize a pattern, but not enough to resolve it. We can end the scene when Lena chooses to abandon a routine errand and seek the archive's spare key.

Then choose a later Oren scene, not a simultaneous paragraph, to reveal why the ribbon was external. He could watch Lena ignore it and conclude that his plan failed. When he subsequently sees her heading toward the key cabinet, he revises his judgment. This is an important principle: shifting perspective is most useful when the shift makes a new causal or emotional possibility available. A perspective change should do more than repeat the same information from the other side.

The story could also remain with Kiri throughout. Then it would no longer be primarily an institutional investigation; it might become a story about a child learning that adults can know parts of a danger and still fail to protect one another. That is not a lesser version. It is a different answer to the question of whose story the incident is.

## A writing experiment and conclusion

Choose a small event with at least three witnesses: a misplaced object, a canceled appointment, a sudden applause, or a person leaving a room. Establish five physical facts that remain fixed across all versions. Then write three short accounts of the event. In the first, give the focal character a private fear. In the second, give someone knowledge that changes the object's meaning. In the third, give someone an interpretation that is reasonable from limited evidence but ultimately wrong.

Afterward, ask not which passage is prettiest, but which story each account promises. What question is open? What next action feels possible? What does the reader know that the focal person does not? Where is the narration closest to a character's language, and where does it step back to orient us? If two versions create the same questions and consequences, their perspectives may not yet be doing distinct work.

Perspective is not the decorative camera mounted after a plot has been built. It decides which facts can enter the plot, which mistakes can persist, and whose costs become visible. Choosing who speaks, who perceives, and when a piece of knowledge becomes available is a way of designing the reader's experience of reality. The ribbon on the door was one physical object. The story began when we decided who could understand it, and who could not.

| Layer | Ribbon example | Continuity question |
| --- | --- | --- |
| World fact | Oren tied the ribbon to draw attention to a report | Is it true independently of viewpoint? |

| Audience knowledge | May know Oren's motive from a prior chapter | Does the resulting suspense depend on ignorance or anticipation? |

### Exercises

1. Rewrite the same 200-word event in first-person retrospective narration and third-person limited narration without changing physical facts.
2. Write a one-paragraph account that reports facts accurately but interprets them incorrectly. Add two clues permitting a reader to notice the gap.
3. Sketch an interactive branch where the player learns a fact that the protagonist cannot know, then adjust a later choice so it remains character-consistent.
