# Historical Fiction: Evidence, Imagination, and Silence

The municipal archive contains three items concerning a flood in a river city in 1912. First, a printed notice announces that the footbridge will close when water reaches a red mark on the upstream gauge. Second, an engineer's report says the bridge was still open an hour after that mark was passed. Third, a letter from a shopkeeper complains that customers could not reach the market on the morning of the closure.

This archive is fictional. The flood, the bridge, the city, and the documents were invented for this lecture, so we can study the reasoning without inadvertently presenting an imaginary event as history. But imagine that they were real. What could a novelist responsibly write?

We could write a scene in which a young bridge attendant looks at the rising gauge and hesitates to lower the barrier. The record supports the claim that the bridge was open after the stated threshold; it does not tell us whether this attendant existed, what they saw, or why anyone delayed. We could invent a supervisor who orders the gate left open for merchants, but then we would be assigning a motive the record does not establish. Or we could write the attendant hearing an argument about the bridge while still being unsure who has the authority to close it. That, too, is an invention, but one designed to preserve rather than erase the uncertainty in the documents.

The craft of historical fiction begins here: where an archive stops and a human scene must begin. The writer must create thoughts, sensations, conversations, and choices that no surviving record can completely supply. But imagination does not cancel evidentiary responsibility. It makes that responsibility more difficult, because fiction can give inventions the emotional force of lived experience.

Our problem is not simply how to get the clothing and street names right. It is how to construct an inhabited past without converting the dead into obedient actors in a story designed by people who know what happened afterward.

## The historian and the novelist are doing related but different work

Historical scholarship evaluates sources and makes arguments whose evidentiary basis should remain open to scrutiny. Historical fiction creates an experience that may incorporate established facts, disputed interpretations, and invented events. Both may handle evidence with care. Both can distort it. But they make different promises to the reader. A scholarly claim about the flood delay ought to say what documents establish it and which inferences remain tentative. A novel need not interrupt a scene to footnote the attendant's hand on the gate lever. It should nevertheless avoid making that sensory invention appear to settle an unresolved historical accusation.

Hilary Mantel explored this balance in her 2017 BBC Reith Lectures, particularly the lecture 'Can These Bones Live?' She described the historical novelist's work as reconstructing lived experience while refusing to flatten the stubborn shape of the available record. Mantel wrote historical fiction centered on figures for whom substantial documentation exists; her advice should not be treated as if every period and every person supplies the same abundance of evidence. She was also explicit that the novelist must confront contradictory testimony rather than quietly turning what is convenient into fact.

The bridge packet gives us a small model of that task. The engineer's report may be accurate about the hour, but who measured the gauge, with what instrument, and when was the report written? Was the printed notice still in effect? Does the shopkeeper's complaint refer to the same morning? Before we create the attendant's motives, we must know whether the apparent contradiction is one the historical record actually contains.

Notice how questions of source criticism become questions of scene design. If the timing of the river's rise is uncertain, a scene narrated from the attendant's limited viewpoint might legitimately register confusion. If we later discover that the engineer had been ordered to submit a report before the city's inspection, the report becomes testimony produced under pressure. But the novelist cannot simply invent a bribe or a conspiracy because those would make the plot exciting. A plausible invention is not automatically a documented event.

## Build an evidence ledger before a scene

A useful discipline is to separate three columns. The first holds what the historical sources explicitly establish. The second holds interpretations that the sources make possible but do not prove. The third holds inventions introduced for narrative purposes.

For our imaginary bridge, the first column contains the text of the closure notice and the fact that an engineer later reported a delay. If this were a real case, we would record the document dates, repositories, and possible contradictions, not merely our paraphrase. The second column might include the possibility of confusion about who could order the closure, or the possibility that a broken gauge made the threshold hard to recognize. The third contains a character named Ruth, her wet cuffs, a supervisor who is also her uncle, the sound of the iron chain, and the private shame she feels when a merchant calls her afraid.

Those inventions are legitimate tools for fiction, but they need not all be admitted to the story. The uncle might produce melodrama. The wet cuffs might make the scene bodily and specific. Ruth's shame could turn a bureaucratic delay into a recognizable moral choice, but only if the narrative has earned her emotional history. An evidence ledger prevents a detail imagined for the scene from quietly migrating into the column of established fact when the author writes a historical note or discusses the book in an interview.

The ledger is especially valuable when writing about identifiable real people. Inventing the thought of a documented public official and inventing the thought of an unnamed person whose record exists only in a punishment register are not equivalent decisions. The first may face a substantial counter-record; the second may risk reproducing the very silencing that shaped the archive. Fiction does not remove this difference. It requires us to be conscious of it.

## The temptation of retrospective knowledge

We know that the flood happened. The people on the bridge do not. The reader may recognize the ominous river, but Ruth cannot honestly think, 'This is the moment the city's great disaster begins,' unless the story is explicitly framed as hindsight. Most historical actors do not experience their days as chapters in a later history book.

This is one of historical fiction's central technical challenges. We must distinguish what happened, what we now believe happened, and what people living at the time could reasonably know or expect. A politician may give a speech that later historians regard as decisive, while most people in the market were thinking about bread prices and damaged shoes. A new law may look like a turning point in a timeline, yet its consequences may arrive gradually and unevenly in households.

Rewrite the flood from Ruth's viewpoint. At noon she is irritated that the gauge numbers have been painted over. She has been told that no one will lose their wages if the bridge closes, but she has never seen that assurance in writing. She is also waiting for her sister to cross before the road is barred. Those details produce conflicting pressures. Ruth may keep the gate open for a defensible but dangerous reason. She may close it early and later discover that her action stranded someone on the wrong side of the river. Both scenes take place inside the character's uncertain present.

Now imagine a narrator who knows everything that the flood will cause fifty years later. That is a valid formal choice too, but it changes the work. The narrator may contrast a mundane afternoon with later commemorations, or expose the limits of Ruth's knowledge. The craft requirement is to make the temporal perspective deliberate. A character should not suddenly know the results of historical events merely because the author has read the last page of a textbook.

## A historical world is more than period decoration

Descriptions of hats, streetcars, and shop signs can make a scene vivid, but they can also become a museum display through which modern characters wander unchanged. The deeper task is to reconstruct constraints: what people can legally own, where they can go, which authorities they recognize, what work pays, how households distribute care, what different speakers believe they owe one another, and which words for their situation are available.

The bridge attendant's wages matter. If Ruth will be dismissed for disobeying a superior, her hesitation is not merely temperamental. The route to the hospital matters. If the bridge is the only dependable crossing, lowering the gate has consequences that differ by neighborhood. The notice's language matters. Perhaps the rule is written clearly for engineers but ambiguously for the people who must obey it. These questions transform historical context from exposition into causality.

Research then becomes selective. If you are writing the one scene in which Ruth must pull the barrier, you need to understand the bridge mechanism, the source of the water reading, the chain of command, and the specific forms of labor on the quay. You may have read a hundred pages about the city's mayor and use none of them. That research can still have been useful because it helped you decide which facts are relevant. But an informative paragraph is not automatically an effective fictional paragraph. Information matters when it changes what a character can perceive or do.

You can test exposition with a simple question: would a person in this place and time have a reason to notice this detail now? Ruth does not need to explain the city's whole transportation system to a coworker who uses it every day. She might notice a rule changing because the official she relied on has left, or because the closure now prevents her sister from getting home. Change makes customary things visible.

## When the archive has been built by power

Some records preserve the voices of administrators and owners more readily than those of workers, enslaved people, colonized people, prisoners, or people excluded from formal institutions. This is not merely a shortage of facts that an imaginative writer can patch. The pattern of surviving evidence can itself be a consequence of power. Who had the resources to write, the right to testify, and the institutional standing to be believed?

Saidiya Hartman's essay 'Venus in Two Acts,' published in *Small Axe* in 2008, examines the violence of an archive in which the lives of enslaved women often appear through documents produced by systems that harmed them. Hartman's project is not a general fiction-writing recipe and should not be appropriated as a promise that a novelist can simply invent what a silenced person 'must have' thought. Her discussion of critical fabulation makes the gaps, the limits of knowing, and the conditions of archival appearance central to the form of the work.

We can learn a caution from that method without claiming that our fictional bridge story is comparable to the histories Hartman studies. Suppose Ruth's name appears only in the engineer's report as 'assistant.' A novelist could write a first-person confession explaining why she left the bridge open. The prose might be moving, but its authority would be constructed entirely by the writer. Another approach would make the limited evidence visible: a later character searches the records, finds conflicting descriptions of the assistant, and confronts the fact that even the name is uncertain. A third approach might invent a wholly fictional character informed by the known conditions of work, clearly distinct from a real person whose voice cannot be recovered.

None of these strategies is automatically the only ethical one. The choice depends on the historical subject, the kind of book, what is known, and whom the fiction risks misrepresenting. But the claim 'the archive is silent, so anything may be imagined' is not a responsible historical argument. Silence can be a formal feature rather than a license to speak without limits.

## The boundary between fact and invention is a craft resource

Writers sometimes worry that acknowledging uncertainty will drain suspense. The opposite can happen. A scene in which Ruth does not know whether the river has crossed the mark may be more tense than a scene in which the author invents a villain ordering her to ignore an obvious warning. An unsigned notice, a damaged dial, and a supervisor who refuses to give a clear instruction produce conflict because the mechanisms of responsibility are incomplete.

A book may also place an author's note outside the narrative clarifying which major people and events are documented and where it has taken liberty. That note cannot repair every misleading scene, and it should not become a substitute for care in the writing itself. But it can keep readers from mistaking a fictional composite for an established historical actor. There is no obligation to annotate every imagined cup of tea. The need for clarification increases as inventions become consequential claims about identifiable people or contested atrocities.

Anachronism deserves a similarly careful treatment. A character's words may sound modern because the novelist wants them immediately understandable. Historical fiction is always a form of translation between eras. But some contemporary terms can smuggle in categories that the person would not have used. Instead of treating every modern phrase as forbidden, ask what concept the character could have formed and what social institutions made it thinkable. A modern metaphor can be an explicit narratorial bridge; accidental modern psychology may flatten a historical person's choices.

There are also forms of deliberate counterfactual and alternate-history fiction. They can invent different outcomes openly and explore what follows. The ethical question then becomes whether readers can identify the counterfactual premise and whether the invented consequences obey the book's rules. A counterfactual that announces its departure from history makes a different promise from a realist novel that presents fabricated accusations as documentary certainty.

## Revision through the evidence ledger

Let us draft Ruth's scene twice. In the first version, she watches a man in an expensive coat point at the clock and demand that the bridge remain open. She obeys because she is afraid. It works as a simple scene, but it gives us a convenient villain, a convenient motive, and a resolution the documents do not require.

In the second version, she reaches the barrier and finds a chalk mark on the gauge where the red line should be. The official notice is damp and half unreadable. Her uncle, who supervises the crossing, is not present. One merchant says the river is still below the threshold. Another says it passed the mark an hour ago. Ruth asks the engineer for the time of the last measurement. He tells her that the instrument was checked at dawn. She must decide whether to close a crossing on an uncertain reading while people are still coming across.

Now the scene has a specific technical problem, competing witnesses, and a choice that could have been made under pressure. It remains fiction; none of these details can be assigned to the imaginary archive as fact. But the revision is more historically disciplined because it does not use invented certainty to solve the source's ambiguity. It also makes the action more interesting. Ruth cannot merely identify the villain. She must interpret incomplete evidence and act before she knows the result.

Try this on a real period that interests you. Choose one small documented event with at least two sources. Write a source ledger: what each document states, who produced it, what it omits, and where it conflicts with other records. Then invent a fictional viewpoint character whose daily obligations intersect with the event. Write one scene entirely within what that character could know at the time. Finally mark every sentence in which the narrator claims a fact about the event. Can you distinguish documented history, informed inference, and deliberate invention? If a major assertion rests only on your wish for the plot, either substantiate it or change the plot.

The river rises, and Ruth reaches for the chain. Somewhere behind her a vendor calls that there is still time to cross. The ledger does not tell us whether this happened. Fiction may make the moment vivid, but only the writer can decide what promise the scene makes to history. We are not entitled to turn uncertainty into whatever answer is easiest. We are entitled to find human choices inside it.

Historical fiction is strongest not when every object has been researched and placed on display, but when the writer knows which claims the evidence can bear, which silences remain, and how to make a reader feel the pressure of a time that was once as unsettled as the present.

## Companion (display only)

**Evidence boundary:** The 1912 river city, bridge, notice, report, shopkeeper, and Ruth are original inventions. They are *not* examples of a real flood. Mantel's fourth 2017 BBC Reith Lecture, 'Can These Bones Live?', is a practitioner account of historical-fiction craft. Hartman's 'Venus in Two Acts' (2008) is a scholarly and ethical examination of the archive of Atlantic slavery; its method is not a generic authorization to invent marginalized people's testimony.

**Practice:** Prepare a three-column ledger of documented statements, plausible but unproven interpretations, and inventions. Check every consequential claim about identifiable people. Write a scene from a character's situated present, with no retrospective knowledge they could not possess.
