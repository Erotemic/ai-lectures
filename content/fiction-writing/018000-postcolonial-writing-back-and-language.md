# Writing Back: Postcolonial Fiction, Language, and Narrative Authority

A new teacher arrives at a school whose oldest building is about to be demolished. In a locked cupboard she finds exercise books written by children fifty years earlier. The books are full of family stories, names for the river, accounts of a harvest, and arguments over who owns a hillside. A typed school history identifies the period as the beginning of proper education in the district. The notebooks tell a more complicated story: the children already knew a great deal, and much of what they knew had to be translated into the language the school recognized.

The teacher decides to write a novel about the building and the people who passed through it. The immediate craft question might be where to open: at the demolition or in an earlier classroom. But a more consequential question arrives first. In whose language will the novel tell its story? Who will be allowed to explain what those notebooks mean? What happens when the official record and the surviving testimony disagree? And who is imagined as the reader who needs the district explained?

These are central questions in many traditions of postcolonial fiction. The term postcolonial does not describe a single period, one political attitude, or a uniform style. Colonial power took different forms across Africa, the Caribbean, South Asia, and other regions; formal independence did not instantly end the hierarchies it created. Writers responded with realism, satire, historical revision, experimental narration, speculative fiction, and many other forms. They did not all agree about language, nationalism, tradition, or literary form. What joins several major debates is a concern with how power shapes who can speak, what counts as knowledge, and whose life becomes legible in a novel.

## A narrator is an arrangement of authority

Imagine the teacher, Adi, beginning with the school history. The original author, a district inspector, records that the first headmaster brought literacy to the valley. That sentence might be factually defensible if the inspector meant literacy in one official alphabet; it could be radically misleading if readers understand it to mean that the inhabitants had no complex systems of memory or written record before the school arrived. A narrator can make a narrow proposition seem like a complete account simply by refusing to name its limits.

Adi could quote the inspector and then correct him. But the correction would still make the official history the center around which other voices must arrange themselves. A different novel might begin with a grandmother teaching a child a song that encodes the old paths to the river. The school appears later as one institution among many, not as the origin of an entire world. The change in opening position alters what needs explaining. Readers no longer assume the people of the valley await the arrival of history.

This is one meaning of writing back: contesting an authoritative story by changing the center of narration. The phrase has become associated with postcolonial literary criticism and with rewritings of colonial or European canonical texts. Jean Rhys's *Wide Sargasso Sea*, published in 1966, is a prominent example. It reimagines a life connected with a character presented in Charlotte Brontë's *Jane Eyre*. The later novel asks what a character's restricted place in one book conceals about history, subjectivity, and power. Its own representation has also been criticized. Restoring voice to one person does not automatically solve the problem of representing others.

An inexperienced writer might think the goal is always to reverse the hierarchy: dismiss the inspector and declare the grandmother fully truthful. But replacing one unquestioned authority with another can flatten the fiction. A compelling novel might let the grandmother remember accurately in some respects and inaccurately in others. What matters is that she is allowed reasons, history, and interpretive life rather than serving as a symbolic corrective to the official record. The reader should be able to understand competing claims without assuming one document contains the entire reality of the place.

## The language problem is not one problem

Our teacher speaks one language at work, another with a parent, and a third poorly but affectionately with a neighbor. She can write a whole novel in the official language, translate conversations into it, leave certain phrases untranslated, or write primarily in a language the original district history did not recognize. These choices are not just decorative details. They select readers, preserve or transform rhythms, and can reinforce or challenge the prestige assigned to different kinds of speech.

Consider the debate associated with Chinua Achebe and Ngũgĩ wa Thiong'o. Achebe used English to write fiction rooted in Nigerian and African experience, arguing that an inherited colonial language could be made to serve new expressive purposes. He did not argue that everyone should abandon African languages. Ngũgĩ, who had written major early work in English, later championed writing in Gikuyu and insisted that literary language was inseparable from systems of schooling, power, and cultural continuity. His *Decolonising the Mind*, published in 1986, is a landmark statement of that position. The positions should not be reduced to a simple contest between pragmatism and purity. The writers were responding to concrete histories of education, suppression, publication, and access.

The debate remains active, and there are more than two views. Literature can circulate in several languages, be self-translated or collaboratively translated, and create new mixtures or registers. A language used by colonizers may also have long histories of local adaptation and many speakers who consider it their own. A language excluded from formal institutions may have rich living literary and oral traditions. The choice to publish in one language may be a means of reaching neighbors, crossing boundaries, earning a living, resisting a hierarchy, or negotiating all of those goals at once.

In Adi's novel, a child asks what a word in the grandmother's song means. One approach provides an immediate parenthetical translation. Another allows meaning to become clear through context. A third leaves the word unresolved because none of the available translations carries the relationship or historical memory it names. These are craft choices that also express an imagined relation to the reader. If the author translates everything, readers outside the community may feel welcomed but the fiction can begin to resemble a guided tour. If nothing is explained, readers may be asked to inhabit a productive uncertainty, or they may be left without information the story genuinely needs them to have. There is no universal setting on the dial.

A useful test is to distinguish necessary comprehension from exhaustive explanation. If a word names the tool a character uses to save a child from drowning, readers must understand enough to follow the rescue. They need not learn a dictionary definition before the action. If a word names a social obligation that resists easy translation, the story might dramatize the obligation through choices and consequences. The reader then understands a relation even if no single English term matches it.

## The difference between setting and viewpoint

Some fiction depicts places outside the presumed reader's experience through familiar tourist logic. Food, clothing, landscape, and ceremony appear as exhibits. Characters become guides to a culture, speaking as if they are explaining their ordinary lives to strangers. Yet people rarely narrate their familiar surroundings to themselves as a travel brochure. The result can be a world whose inhabitants lose their own habits of attention.

Return to the schoolyard. Adi may notice the loose tile by the gate because children have stumbled over it for years; she might not notice the mountain view that visiting officials praise. A former student might remember the assembly bell as a threat. A younger student might use the abandoned classroom as a place to rehearse a song. These viewpoints allow the school to become a lived place rather than a collection of emblematic details. Exposition emerges from what people need and what interrupts them.

This principle is useful beyond specifically postcolonial writing. All narrators are situated. Their knowledge depends on work, age, language, class, memory, and relationships. But in writing about histories of colonization, the question of who has been trained to regard their own knowledge as secondary takes on particular force. A child's recollection may have been dismissed in the archive not because it was inaccurate but because it was recorded in the wrong language or in a form officials did not count as evidence.

## The nation is not the narrator

A novel about colonialism or national independence may be expected to represent an entire people. That burden can be artistically distorting. A nation has no single interior monologue. People sharing a history can disagree about class, gender, religion, land, politics, and the meaning of liberation. The novel gains strength when it does not force characters to serve as tidy delegates of collective identities.

*Things Fall Apart*, published by Achebe in 1958, helped transform international expectations about who could narrate African history and social life. Yet it is also a carefully built story of a particular character, his family, and a community facing pressures from within and without. Its importance does not license treating every later African novel as a commentary on that book. In another context, Salman Rushdie's *Midnight's Children*, published in 1981, makes individual narration and national history collide through an expansive, inventive voice. These books do not belong to one school of technique. They show different ways a novelist may resist having a place explained from the outside.

In Adi's story, a government representative wants to save the old school as a heritage monument. Some former students agree. Others want the building replaced with a clinic because the school has represented humiliation rather than cultural pride. A foreign researcher wishes to collect the notebooks. The grandmother fears the collection will remove them from the community again. Nobody needs to be evil for the scene to contain severe conflict. The claims have different material consequences. A richly situated novel does not avoid politics; it makes politics specific enough to become action.

## Translation is also a narrative event

Suppose one notebook survives in a script that Adi cannot read. She asks two people to translate it. One renders the passage as an accusation against the headmaster. The other hears a joke, pointed but affectionate. Their disagreement can arise from dialect, historical context, wordplay, or competing interests. Suddenly translation is not an invisible service performed for the reader. It is an action within the plot, and it has consequences for who will be believed.

This is one reason multilingual fiction can be formally inventive even when its surface prose appears conventional. The novel can acknowledge that speech passes through institutions and interpreters. It can represent silences where a translation is refused or impossible. It can let characters switch registers to mark intimacy, defiance, embarrassment, or danger. The writer must treat such choices as part of character and causal design, not simply insert words from another language to provide local color.

There are real limits to any account that encourages the writer to choose freely among other people's voices. Research, relationships, and humility matter. The fact that fiction permits imagination does not mean every representation is equally informed. The central question is not whether a writer has received permission from an entire culture; no one person could grant that. It is whether the work has attended carefully to the specificity of the lives it invents and whether it notices when a dominant story is quietly being repeated.

## Rewriting the archive

Here is a practical exercise. Write two hundred words from the perspective of the district inspector who composed the official school history. Let him think he is doing useful work. Then write the same afternoon from the perspective of a pupil whose family story has been edited out of the record. Do not make the second voice omniscient or morally flawless. Finally, write a short scene in which Adi must decide what to include in a public exhibit. She has room for only two documents. What are the costs of the two she chooses, and what is lost with those she does not?

Notice that the strongest question may not be which narrator is right. It may be who had access to the power to make an account durable. A story acquires a public life when someone can print it, teach it, archive it, translate it, or turn it into policy. This extends the craft of viewpoint beyond the single sentence and into the institutions through which testimony survives.

The old school building will be demolished on Monday. Adi has not finished the novel. The notebooks are still in a cupboard. She can decide whether to tell a tale of education, loss, transformation, or all three, but she cannot evade the choice of whose world forms the center of her prose. In that sense, the postcolonial challenge is also a general challenge to writing fiction: every story has a position from which the world becomes visible, and every position leaves someone outside the frame. Strong fiction does not have to abolish that limitation. It has to use it knowingly.

## Companion (display only)

**Historical caution:** Postcolonial is not the name of one movement or a single shared African, Caribbean, or South Asian position. The Achebe–Ngũgĩ disagreement is one influential debate, not an exhaustive taxonomy. Many writing traditions in African and Asian languages predate twentieth-century independence movements.

**Practice:** Rewrite an archive scene from three positions: the recorder, the person recorded, and the translator. Preserve the event but change what is considered worth recording. Identify the reader each version implicitly assumes.
