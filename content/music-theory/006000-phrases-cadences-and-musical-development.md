# Phrases, Cadences, and Development: Making a Musical Idea Go Somewhere

Imagine speaking two sentences. The first begins confidently but ends with your voice slightly lifted, as though you have more to say. The second repeats the opening words and then settles into a full stop. You have created a relationship between statements. The first makes a shape that the second completes. Music can create a similar relationship without words, not by translating grammar into a rigid system, but by shaping expectations about length, return, arrival, and continuation.

A musical phrase is a span that listeners can hear as a relatively complete unit. Its beginning may be marked by a new motive, an entrance, or a change of texture. Its end may involve a held note, a rest, a harmonic arrival, a drum fill, a breath, or a change of lyric. In one style, a particular chord progression may mark the ending. In another, a repeated rhythmic cycle may provide the boundary. Phrase is a useful description of organization in time, not a demand that everything be divided into four-measure boxes.

Still, four beats per measure and four measures per phrase give us a manageable workspace. A measure, or bar, is a recurring unit of counted time. For our examples, there will be four quarter-note beats per bar. We will work in C major, whose basic pitches are C, D, E, F, G, A, and B. The tonic is C: the pitch and harmony we are treating as a home base. A dominant harmony built on G contains G, B, and D, and in this tonal context can prepare an arrival on C. These names let us discuss the example precisely; the idea of shaping a departure and return is more general.

## A phrase is more than its length

Start with a short melodic idea: C, D, E, G. Give each note one beat. It rises by small steps and finishes with a larger leap from E to G. If you tap a beat and imagine or sing those four pitches, the figure lasts exactly one bar. It might serve as a complete miniature. It might also be the opening gesture of a much longer statement. Its duration alone does not decide.

For the second bar, make a complementary shape: A for one beat, G for one beat, and F held for two beats. The line goes up at the start, descends, and then has room to breathe. The first bar was active on every beat. The second settles into a longer sound. Together, the bars suggest a small question followed by an easing of motion.

Now add a third bar: G, B, A, G, again four single beats. Its jump to B makes the upper register active. Under this bar we can place G major harmony. For the fourth bar, use B held for two beats and D held for two beats, still over G major. The notes B and D belong to the G chord. But because the tonic C has not returned, the larger phrase can feel suspended. In common-practice tonal terminology, ending a phrase on dominant harmony is one way to make a half cadence: the music arrives somewhere, but not at its primary home.

That distinction is important. A half cadence is not a failure to finish writing a measure. Our fourth bar has exactly four beats. The melody ends on a stable chord tone. The phrase can pause completely. What remains unfinished is a particular tonal expectation: the dominant has become a possible point of departure for a later arrival on the tonic.

You do not need to hear a perfect recording of this example to inspect its design. Write down the four bars. Mark the first bar with an arrow rising, the second with an arrow descending and a long end, the third with a peak on B, and the fourth with two sustained notes. The contour becomes gradually less busy as it approaches the pause. The harmony provides a different kind of information: C is introduced, and G is emphasized at the close. Those two levels—melodic motion and harmonic destination—cooperate without saying exactly the same thing.

## Answering a phrase without copying it

We can build a second phrase from almost the same ingredients. Repeat the first two bars exactly: C, D, E, G, then A, G, F held. The listener can recognize the beginning without learning a completely new motif. Repeat the third bar, G, B, A, G. Up to this point, the answer seems very close to the question.

Change only the last bar. Put C in the melody, held for all four beats. Under it, put a low C and a C major harmony. Now the G chord in the preceding measure leads to C, and the melodic line lands on the same scale degree that we are treating as home. In common-practice tonal analysis, G major going to C major at a phrase ending is an authentic cadence. In our explicitly realized ending, the bass moves from G to C, and the upper melody lands on C. That gives the final dominant and tonic chords in root position with scale degree one on top: the conventional ingredients of a perfect authentic cadence, provided the passage is heard as a real phrase ending in this style. A chord-symbol progression by itself does not establish those details. These are formal labels for a particular harmonic grammar, not requirements for a compelling ending in every genre.

The two phrases together form a simple model of antecedent and consequent. An antecedent is a phrase that ends less conclusively; its consequent responds with a more conclusive ending. When the phrases begin alike, analysts often call this a parallel period. The word parallel here refers to the recurring melodic beginning. It is different from the term parallel motion between two simultaneously moving lines.

What did we really compose? Eight measures, yes, but the crucial decision was to repeat nearly everything and change the destination. The second phrase earns its role through comparison with the first. It supplies an answer because the first phrase made a recognizable statement whose ending left room for a second one. If we changed every note in the second phrase, listeners might hear contrast rather than reply. If both endings landed on C with equal force, we might have made two repeated statements rather than an antecedent and consequent.

Notice also that we do not need the melody to sit on chord tones at every instant. In bar two, the movement A, G, F over an F chord includes G, which is not one of the F triad's three basic members. It is a passing tone moving between neighboring chord tones. The line acquires shape from notes that connect stable destinations. A composer who restricts every melodic event to chord roots and thirds can still write music, but will lose a useful source of direction.

## Cadence is a role, not just a chord pair

The language of cadences is sometimes taught as a small table to memorize. In C major, G to C is called authentic; F to C is called plagal; G to A minor can create a deceptive arrival; a phrase ending on G can be called a half cadence. That list is helpful as a map of conventional tonal behaviors. The map becomes misleading if we classify every G-to-C change as a dramatic final cadence regardless of whether the music is starting a section, continuing a loop, or moving through a busy texture.

A cadence is an event perceived as an arrival or phrase boundary within a context. If G-to-C occurs repeatedly on weak beats in the middle of a repeating accompaniment, it may not sound like a final punctuation mark. Conversely, a melody can come to a clear stop over a long-held harmony without a traditional cadence. The rhythm, register, texture, melodic ending, and larger form all influence the result.

Consider the deceptive alternative in our eight-bar piece. Keep the final bar long and quiet, but substitute A minor harmony for C major. Its notes are A, C, and E. The preceding G chord had suggested a return to C. Ending on A minor turns that expected arrival aside, even though the new chord shares C and E with C major. You can keep a final melodic C over the A minor chord. Then C is not the chord's root but its third. The note itself has not changed, yet its harmonic role has. A deceptive ending often invites further continuation, though it can also be an intentional final color.

Try the plagal option. Replace the preceding G harmony with F major and then move to C major. The sequence F to C establishes a different type of motion from dominant to tonic. In some contexts it sounds like a gentle settling; in others, especially when repeated, it can be a strong and emphatic gesture. The word gentle is a descriptive possibility, not a property guaranteed by the labels. Dynamics, voicing, style, and timing decide how the cadence is experienced.

The practical lesson is to compose a boundary rather than merely to select a cadence name. Ask what a listener has been prepared to anticipate. Ask where the phrase seems to end. Ask whether its final pitches, rhythm, and harmony agree about that ending—or deliberately contradict one another.

## Develop a motive without losing its identity

Our opening C, D, E, G is a motive: a short recognizable musical idea. It has four attacks, three steps or leaps, and an upward contour. We can develop it by changing one attribute while preserving others.

The easiest transformation is transposition. Move the entire pattern upward by a scale degree in a given key, or by the same semitone distance for each note. These are not always the same operation. Starting on D in the C-major scale and preserving the sequence's diatonic step relationships gives D, E, F, A. Starting on D and preserving the exact semitone distances of C, D, E, G gives D, E, F sharp, A. The first keeps the phrase within C major. The second is a literal chromatic transposition. They sound related, but their harmonic implications are different. The choice is made for the piece, not for an abstract exercise.

A second transformation is rhythmic augmentation: extend the durations of the motive's notes. The four notes C, D, E, G, originally one beat each, could become two beats each. The contour is unchanged, but the idea now occupies two full four-beat bars. A related transformation, diminution, compresses the durations. Half-beat attacks on C, D, E, G occupy only two beats. Put the shortened version after a rest and it can feel like a quick recollection of the larger statement.

A third transformation is contour change. Our pattern rises C, D, E, G. Suppose we instead write C, D, F, E. The rhythm and number of attacks remain, but the peak arrives earlier and the line turns downward. We retain a family resemblance without exact repetition. This can make a return sound remembered and revised rather than merely replayed.

Finally, develop the motive by fragmentation. Use only C, D in one voice, then E, G as a response in another. A bass line could play the first two notes while a singer or another instrument answers with the second pair. Or a guitar could alternate a low two-note figure and a high two-note figure. The material remains recognizable because its small parts have been established. The more you fragment, the more important it becomes to keep a clear rhythmic or melodic link.

## Sentence structure: continuation and acceleration

There is another common-practice approach to a phrase that differs from a question and answer. A musical sentence often begins with a short idea, repeats or sequences it, then continues with material that becomes more active and drives toward a cadence. Its key characteristic is not a spoken grammatical sentence. It is an organizational shape: presentation, repetition, continuation, arrival.

Imagine an eight-bar sentence. In the first two bars, state C, D, E, G, then hold G. In bars three and four, repeat the same pattern, perhaps beginning on a different pitch. In bars five and six, reduce the material to a shorter two-note fragment and repeat it more quickly. In the final two bars, use the resulting momentum to prepare a harmonic ending. The listener first has time to recognize the material, then encounters it in a compressed form, then reaches an arrival.

This is a flexible compositional technique rather than an eight-bar law. A rhythmic groove could make its continuation through denser percussion rather than shorter melody notes. A singer could repeat a lyric phrase and then break it into overlapping fragments. An instrumental ensemble could introduce an imitative response before the cadence. The same principle of establishing, compressing, and directing material works with many sound sources.

## When repetition does not lead to a cadence

Music built around a repeated chord loop often does not behave like our dominant-to-tonic example. A four-chord loop can return to its beginning without making that return sound final. The bass may never stop, the rhythm may keep repeating, and the phrase boundary may be marked by the singer's words or by a change in the arrangement. In that context, the best question is not always which cadence has occurred. It may be how repetition creates continuity and how changes in texture distinguish one large section from another.

There are also musical traditions in which pitch organization, formal timing, and repetition do not map neatly onto the C-major cadence vocabulary. The point of studying a period is not to claim every piece contains one. It is to learn a generalizable choice: repeat a recognizable idea, then make its second destination different from its first. A composer can use that choice within tonal music, adapt it to modal or rhythm-centered composition, or reject it in favor of a continuously changing form.

Now return to our eight measures. You can state exactly why the opening returns, what changes at the end, and which harmonic expectation is satisfied. You can also create three variants—dominant pause, tonic arrival, and diverted arrival—without rewriting the whole piece. That is the advantage of having a small, explicit model. Musical development stops being a vague request to add variety. It becomes the controlled alteration of material whose identity the listener has time to learn.

### Alternatives to compare

- **Deceptive:** bar 8 A minor bass A2; melodic C5 held; chord notes A–C–E. The melodic pitch stays but its harmonic function changes.
- **Plagal:** bar 7 F major instead of G major, followed by C major in bar 8. Adjust the melody if desired so it fits the new harmonic expectation.
- **Less final:** bar 8 G major with a held D5 instead of C5. Both phrases end away from the tonic; the relation is less conclusive.
- **Motivic:** change the second appearance of C4–D4–E4–G4 to C4–D4–F4–E4, preserving four attacks while shifting the contour.

### Exercises

1. Label every bar's harmonic root and the first beat of each phrase. Identify exactly where the second phrase begins to depart from the first.
2. Double the motive's note durations; calculate how many 4/4 measures it occupies.
3. Compose a different consequent ending using bar 7 G major and bar 8 C major but a different melodic rhythm. Explain whether the tonic arrives on a strong or weak beat.
4. Sketch a four-chord loop where the start of a new cycle does not behave like a final cadence. Say which musical feature, if any, marks phrase boundaries instead.
5. On guitar, sing the melody while playing only the bass roots, or use a second player for the melody. Without an instrument, tap four beats per bar and read the pitch contour aloud.
