# 023000 — Timing, Phase, and Stereo Space: When Adding Tracks Changes the Sound

# Timing, Phase, and Stereo Space: When Adding Tracks Changes the Sound

Two guitar microphones record the same musician. Each recording sounds good when listened to alone. The engineer pulls up both faders, and suddenly the guitar sounds thin, almost as though someone removed its middle frequencies. Turn down either microphone, and the body returns. There is no broken cable. The computer has not run out of arithmetic capacity. It has added the signals correctly. The problem is that the two signals are related, but they are not aligned in exactly the same way. To understand what happened, we need to distinguish musical time, sample time, polarity, phase, and the space we create in a stereo mix.

A sample is an amplitude at a specific moment on a discrete clock. Forty-eight thousand samples per second gives us forty-eight thousand positions in each second of a mono audio signal. Stereo means two sequences of sample values at that rate. At an index of forty-eight thousand, we are one second after the index-zero starting point. If one file contains a drum hit at index ninety-six thousand and another file contains the matching hit at index ninety-six thousand, the stored hits are aligned by their declared sample positions. If the second file omits its first thousand samples when exported, simply starting both files at the first visible sound will move one relative to the other. That sounds mundane until we remember that fine timing differences are part of the sound, not merely part of where notes land on a musical grid.

One millisecond at forty-eight thousand hertz corresponds to forty-eight audio frames. Two milliseconds corresponds to ninety-six. Those are small numbers compared with an entire four-beat bar, but they are long enough to change the relative phase of some audio frequencies dramatically. A musician may not perceive a bass note as entering a whole beat late, yet two recordings of the same bass note can interact strongly when displaced by just a handful of frames. The effect depends on which frequencies they contain.

First imagine a pure sine wave. It traces a smoothly repeating cycle. At one hundred hertz, one complete cycle lasts ten milliseconds. If I add two identical one-hundred-hertz sine waves in phase and at equal amplitude, the resulting sine wave has twice the amplitude of either one. In decibels that is a rise of approximately six. If I delay the second copy by five milliseconds, exactly half a cycle at one hundred hertz, then one copy is positive when the other is negative. They cancel under our ideal, steady-state assumptions. If I delay it by ten milliseconds, a full cycle, the waves reinforce again. Nothing about the musical note changed. Only their relative timing changed.

That calculation does not imply that all sound cancels after five milliseconds. A guitar note has many frequencies, and each has a different period. At two hundred hertz, a five-millisecond delay is one complete cycle, not half a cycle. That component reinforces. So the same delay can weaken one frequency while strengthening another. The result is a pattern of peaks and troughs across the spectrum. Engineers call this comb filtering, because a graph of the response can resemble a comb with regularly spaced notches. The two microphones did not need to be poor microphones to produce it. Their paths through air and their electrical processing placed the same source at different times in the mix.

Let us quantify the idealized case. Add one signal to a second copy that is delayed by two milliseconds, with equal gains and no other processing. The first complete cancellation of a steady sinusoidal component occurs at two hundred fifty hertz. The next appears at seven hundred fifty, then at twelve hundred fifty, and so on. The reinforced regions include zero hertz in the mathematical model, five hundred, one thousand, and further multiples of five hundred. Real microphones, rooms, filters and sources make the notches less perfect, but that alternating structure is why the blend can have a different tone from either original recording. The important fact is not that two milliseconds is universally wrong; it is that timing differences interact with spectral content.

A simple polarity reversal is a different operation. Multiply every sample of one track by minus one, so every positive value becomes negative and every negative becomes positive. If that track is an identical, perfectly aligned copy of another track, summing them after polarity inversion gives silence. But changing polarity does not slide the track in time. For a single pure sine wave, flipping polarity is equivalent to half a cycle of phase shift. For a complex musical signal containing many frequencies, one fixed delay corresponds to a different phase angle at each frequency. This is why a polarity button sometimes improves a two-microphone recording and sometimes makes it worse, but is not a general substitute for correcting the actual time offset.

You may hear people use the word phase to mean several things at once. Sometimes they mean the relative position of two repeating waves. Sometimes they mean the phase response of a filter at different frequencies. Sometimes they mean an overall timing mismatch between two tracks. Sometimes they mean the listening impression that something sounds hollow or disappears in mono. These matters are connected, but identifying the precise failure helps choose the remedy. A phase-correlation meter is not a device for deciding whether a performance is good. It summarizes one aspect of the relationship between two channels, usually over a window of time. An unusual reading can be intentional for a wide texture or problematic for a mono-compatible lead. The musical role determines whether it matters.

It is also important to distinguish correlation from identity. Two independently played guitar takes may have similar notes and rhythm but different pick attacks, minute intonation shifts, and dynamic variations. Combining them may produce width and apparent size rather than the severe cancellation of two copies. They can still mask each other or cause inconsistent low-frequency energy, but they do not behave like an identical waveform with a fixed delay. If a producer asks for a doubled guitar, they might mean two separate performances. Duplicating one take, delaying it by a few milliseconds, and panning the copies apart can create a stereo effect, but it is not acoustically equivalent to a second human performance.

How does this connect to a DAW? Ardour shows regions on a timeline, but regions also carry metadata about where they begin, where their source audio begins, and how processing affects their output. The mixer may apply plugin delay compensation to keep paths aligned when effects report latency. A linear-phase equalizer or a lookahead limiter can introduce delay relative to another branch. If two branches of the same source are blended and one effect adds latency, the host may compensate for it. If the effect reports its latency incorrectly, or if you export the branches independently and later reimport them without matching offsets, the alignment may change. Plugin delay compensation protects one specific aspect of timing; it cannot correct every acoustic or performance misalignment automatically.

The distinction is easier to see in an offline processor graph. DawDreamer takes graph connections as dependencies, and each render walks through an ordered set of processors to produce samples. Suppose one branch reads a dry guitar recording and a second branch reads the same recording through a processor with an actual delay of ninety-six frames. The final addition sees two arrays at each shared output index. If the processor's latency is not compensated at that junction, our two-millisecond comb-filter example becomes real. If a host aligns the two paths or you explicitly delay the dry branch to match, the effect changes. No real-time audio deadline is needed to make this an audible correctness issue. Offline rendering removes the deadline, not the signal-processing mathematics.

Now consider a source recorded through two microphones. A microphone one meter farther away receives the direct sound roughly three milliseconds later, if we use sound traveling through air at about three hundred forty-three meters per second at room temperature. This is only an approximation; temperature, geometry and reflections matter. Still, it is enough to understand why the same instrument can arrive at different points on the recording timeline. The two microphones also hear different blends of direct sound and room reflections. You may align their leading transients and improve one frequency region while changing the room character elsewhere. Perfectly aligning all their components is generally impossible because their waveforms differ for physical reasons. The producer must decide which relationship matters to the music.

A helpful exercise is to imagine a kick drum close microphone and an overhead microphone. The close microphone captures a strong direct transient; the overhead captures the cymbals, the drum kit and room sound, with the kick arriving slightly later. Shift the overhead forward until the kick transient lines up exactly. Does the entire drum kit improve? Not necessarily. The snare and cymbals may move into different relationships, and the overhead's room information may lose some of its intended space. A time shift chosen to maximize one correlation score does not automatically optimize a multi-instrument recording. It is a candidate edit to listen to, not an objective proof of quality.

Stereo adds another layer. A conventional stereo mix has left and right audio channels. Panning often means distributing a mono source between them with a chosen gain law, though a DAW can also use richer channel mappings or multichannel panners. If you send a source equally to both channels, it is usually perceived as centered when played through a balanced stereo speaker or headphone setup, subject to room and listener. If you send it only to the left, it appears strongly on that side. The perceived position is not encoded by a literal physical coordinate in the sample data; it emerges from level differences, timing differences, spectral cues, the playback arrangement and the listener's hearing.

A pan law is a rule for adjusting channel gains as the control moves. One common equal-power approach makes a centered source roughly three decibels lower per channel than when placed fully on one side, under the model of two equally powered, uncorrelated speaker contributions. Other laws are possible. The term equal power does not mean all room geometries and headphones produce identical perceived loudness. It encodes an engineering design choice. If two agents export a mono source after different pan laws, one may be several decibels higher than the other even when both controls visually say center. Document whether panning is applied before stem export or after the stems are imported into the mix.

For a concrete stereo picture, imagine a central vocal or lead instrument, a guitar somewhat to the left, a complementary texture somewhat to the right, a bass near the center and a broad reverberation return. These are not prescriptions for every tradition, instrument or aesthetic. They are a way to allocate attention. If both the left and right backing parts are bright and busy, the lead may still be obscured even though nothing occupies its apparent pan position. Spectral masking, timing overlap and musical arrangement all matter. A wide stereo picture is not synonymous with a clear arrangement.

What happens if the stereo recording is played through one speaker? A simple mono fold-down might compute the average of left and right channels. If both channels contain the same centered signal at equal amplitude, the average retains that signal at its original per-channel amplitude. If one channel contains a source and the other contains an equal-amplitude polarity-inverted copy, the average cancels it. A mix can therefore sound impressively wide in stereo and lose important parts when folded to mono. We cannot make a universal rule that all stereo content must correlate strongly, but we can test whether essential musical events survive common playback transformations.

There is a second kind of spacing that mixes often use: reverberation. A sound in a room arrives through a direct path and then through many reflections. Early reflections may give clues about a source's position and room size. Later, dense reflections contribute a tail that can make an instrument seem to occupy a shared space. Artificial reverb models some aspects of this behavior, using delay networks, convolution with an impulse response, or other methods. A convolution reverb can be computationally expensive depending on the impulse response and algorithm. An efficient partitioned convolution can spread work across blocks, but it still adds processor state and often some latency. This is one reason a common reverb bus can be more efficient than several separate, identical long reverbs, though the bus also introduces a shared dependency in the processing graph.

The choice of pre-delay, the short interval between the direct sound and the first prominent reverberant energy, is musically meaningful. A lead line can remain intelligible when its consonant-like attacks appear before the reverb grows, even as the sustain gains room character. If the reverb begins immediately and is very dense, the source may become indistinct. Different instruments may need different send amounts. The point is not to make the mix simulate a historically accurate room at all costs. It is to choose the spatial relationships that support the piece.

Reverberation also complicates stem interchange. Imagine two instruments share a single reverb bus in Ardour. Export the dry instrument tracks separately, and they omit the common reverb. Export each track with its own processing and accidentally include the shared reverb in both, and you might duplicate the reverb contribution when you later combine them. Export a reverb-return stem separately, and now you have an explicit shared effect that should be added exactly once. That final option is often attractive for interchange, but it requires a clear contract about what each file includes. The words separate stems do not, by themselves, guarantee that the files are disjoint musical contributions.

Let's return to the CPU question. Panning a mono track into stereo is just a small number of multiplies per sample. A fixed delay is mostly moving samples through memory, though very long delay lines can require significant storage. A high-quality time stretch needs to analyze and reconstruct structure, and may perform far more work. A convolution reverb runs a much larger filtering problem than a constant gain. A spectral processor may compute many overlapping transforms, and a nonlinear effect with oversampling creates more samples internally before returning to the session rate. Those differences explain why a session with only a few tracks can be expensive and another with many dry tracks can be easy to render. The number of colored rectangles visible on a DAW screen is not a reliable CPU metric.

There is also a difference between a processor causing *latency* and causing *high CPU load*. A lookahead limiter may delay audio by a known number of samples even when it is computationally efficient. A zero-lookahead nonlinear effect may burn many CPU cycles while reporting little algorithmic latency. The DAW may compensate the first delay but still suffer dropouts from the second if it misses the real-time processing deadline. We should not use these words interchangeably. Timing alignment, buffer scheduling and total computational throughput need separate measurements.

We can design a repeatable diagnosis without needing sophisticated listening software. Choose two copies of the same short, original waveform. Keep one at sample zero. Offset the second by ninety-six samples in a forty-eight-kilohertz session and add them. Compare with the aligned sum. Then reverse polarity on the second copy without changing its timing, and compare again. If you inspect the result with a spectrum analyzer, you should see the comb-like structure for the delayed sum and a different pattern for polarity inversion. If your tool later plays the waveforms, the difference becomes audible; but the model and the expected notch frequencies are already testable on paper.

Try a second experiment with two *different* recordings of the same musical phrase. Do not assume a single sample offset will make them identical. Mark their obvious rhythmic landmarks, decide whether the human timing is part of the arrangement, and make one edit at a time. Maybe the correct fix is to leave both performances alone. Maybe their onsets should align more closely. Maybe the second performance should move to a higher register or a less busy rhythm. These are artistic judgments informed by measurements, not produced by the measurement itself.

Finally, apply these lessons to an agent handoff. Require a declared sample rate, channel layout, common timeline origin and treatment of tails. Record whether panning, polarity inversion, timing shifts, and shared effects have already been printed into each stem. If all stems are independently generated but share a precise musical timebase, you may simply render them to a common origin and combine them. If they are separate recordings, you may need to assess clocks, drifts, takes, latency and intended feel. And if they were generated with a shared spatial effect, decide how that effect is represented so it is not silently doubled or omitted.

A good mix is not always the one with the fewest phase interactions or the highest stereo correlation. It is the one in which the relationships support the musical argument. We can compute exactly what delay does to two idealized sine waves; we cannot decide the appropriate width of a chorus from that equation alone. Understanding the equation makes the artistic decision more informed. That is the essential link between the physics of audio and the practical work of production.

## Display-only companion: delay and comb filtering

Two equal-gain copies of the same source with one delayed by `tau` seconds have transfer function

\[
H(f)=1+e^{-i2\pi f\tau}, \qquad |H(f)|=2|\cos(\pi f\tau)|.
\]

For nonzero `tau`, ideal magnitude nulls occur at `f=(2k+1)/(2*tau)` and reinforcement maxima at `f=k/tau`. At `tau=0.002 s`, nulls appear at 250, 750, 1250, ... Hz. This assumes an unchanging delayed copy, equal gain and no additional processing; it does not predict every room-mic combination.

| Event | At 48,000 Hz |
|---|---:|
| 1 millisecond | 48 frames |
| 2 milliseconds | 96 frames |
| 5 milliseconds | 240 frames |
| Half period of 100 Hz | 5 milliseconds |
| Air travel over approximately 1 meter at 343 m/s | approximately 2.92 milliseconds |

## Display-only companion: inspection sequence

1. In Ardour, import two identical copies of an **original or authorized** mono file and preserve the common start index.
2. Duplicate once with a known two-millisecond offset. Sum through a bus with gain chosen to avoid clipping, then inspect waveform and spectrum.
3. Restore alignment. Invert the polarity of one branch to verify ideal cancellation, recognizing that plugin paths must remain equivalent.
4. Return to the two-millisecond offset and perform a mono fold-down; note that mono compatibility is a different question from stereo width.
5. Test a common reverb send and record whether the return is exported once as a separate stem or included in each rendered source.
6. For a generated source in DawDreamer, treat any configured processor delay as explicit graph state. Do not assume offline rendering automatically resolves every dry/wet alignment question.
