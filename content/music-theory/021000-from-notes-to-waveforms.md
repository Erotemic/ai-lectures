# 021000 - From Notes to Waveforms: MIDI, Sampling, Synthesis, and Recording

**Track:** Music Theory and Composition. **Audience:** Composers who have a sequence of notes, recorded instruments, or agent-produced parts and want to understand what a DAW must turn into sound. **Prerequisites:** Familiarity with musical beats, basic pitch names and tracks; no keyboard or programming experience assumed. **Core question:** What exactly does an audio engine receive when someone says, "Here is a track"?

# From Notes to Waveforms: MIDI, Sampling, Synthesis, and Recording

Suppose you ask four collaborators to compose a piece. One sends a melody as a list of notes and durations. Another records guitar. A third programs drums. The last designs a shimmering sustained sound and sends you a preset. They all say the same thing: my track is finished. Yet the four deliveries are not the same kind of object. One is a score, one is a recorded waveform, one might be a stream of instructions addressed to a drum instrument, and one is a recipe that produces sound only when loaded into an appropriate synthesizer. If you put all four in a DAW, the DAW has to resolve those differences before it can make a single mix.

The first distinction is between an *event* and a *signal*. A note event can say that pitch C begins at a particular musical time, with a given velocity, lasts for two beats, and may carry other performance controls. By itself, this does not tell the speaker how the note sounds. Does it have a sharp plucked attack? Does it swell? Does it bend? Does it sustain after the key has been released? A PCM audio track, on the other hand, supplies numbers representing the evolving signal at a particular sample rate. The numbers have already committed to one particular performance, timbre and timing, even if we later process them.

Think about the simplest case: a composer gives you a note described as MIDI number sixty. Under the common twelve-tone equal-tempered mapping with A four, MIDI number sixty-nine, tuned to four hundred forty hertz, number sixty corresponds to middle C at approximately two hundred sixty-one point six hertz. This conversion is a convention, not a law of music. Other tunings exist, and pitch bend, tuning tables and expressive performance can change what a particular event eventually produces. More importantly, the frequency of a nominal note describes only a possible fundamental or pitch reference. It does not describe a complete sound.

Imagine that a sustained tone contains only a single smooth sinusoid. It would have a fairly spare timbre. Add harmonics, adjust their amplitudes, change their relative phases, and the timbre changes. Make the sound start abruptly with a brief noisy attack, and the listener may hear the difference even if its later spectrum resembles the original. Give it a gentle onset and a long fade after note release, and it behaves differently again. This is why the words "play a C" do not specify a reproducible recording. They omit the instrument, its configuration, the dynamics, and sometimes the pitch system itself.

An audio engine commonly renders a note through a chain. First, a sequence or a live performer supplies events. An instrument interprets them, allocates a voice or voices, and generates audio samples. The samples pass through effects, track gain, routing and possibly further shared processing. The output is then collected into a buffer for playback or written to a file. If you are using Ardour, a MIDI track can hold events and feed an instrument plugin. If you are using DawDreamer, a render graph can host an instrument plugin or a Faust synthesizer and render its output offline. The two tools have different work styles, but the logical boundary between note events and audio remains the same.

Here is a numerical timeline. Choose one hundred twenty quarter-note beats per minute, four quarter notes to the bar, and forty-eight thousand samples per second. One beat lasts half a second. One bar lasts two seconds. An event beginning at beat three, if beats are numbered from zero at the beginning of the piece, starts one and a half seconds into the piece, at audio frame seventy-two thousand. An eight-bar passage occupies sixteen seconds, or seven hundred sixty-eight thousand frames per channel. Those numbers are not aesthetic decisions; they are a means of stating timing without ambiguity. If another contributor instead counts the first beat as beat one, that fact needs to be declared. Otherwise, two participants can agree on a tempo and still disagree about where an event should begin.

A sample rate tells us how frequently samples occur, not how many notes must fit into a bar. A MIDI note can begin between sample boundaries when expressed in musical time; the rendering system needs a rounding or sub-sample treatment policy. If a recording arrives at forty-four thousand one hundred samples per second and the session runs at forty-eight thousand, simply pretending those original samples occur at the new rate will change both duration and pitch. The correct routine is to resample the audio, calculating a new sequence of samples intended to represent the same time-varying signal. A good resampler must filter appropriately before or during conversion to limit aliasing, and different algorithms make different compromises in fidelity and CPU cost.

Aliasing deserves a careful explanation because it affects both sampled recording and synthesis. At forty-eight thousand samples per second, the Nyquist frequency is twenty-four thousand hertz. A sinusoidal component above that limit cannot be uniquely represented by the samples at that rate. During recording we use anti-alias filtering before sampling. During synthesis we must also pay attention to the new harmonics a generator creates. A naive digital sawtooth that instantaneously jumps between values produces very high-frequency components. If those components are not treated, some can fold back into the audible spectrum as frequencies that were never part of the intended analog sound. Increasing sample rate or oversampling a processor can help, but at additional computational cost. It is not a guarantee of good sound. A bandlimited oscillator may perform well at the session rate without brute-force oversampling.

Now suppose the collaborator sends you a guitar recording rather than note events. It may be a mono microphone recording, a stereo pair, or a direct electric guitar signal before amplifier simulation. A recorded string has pick noise, fret sound, mechanical irregularities, and an acoustic or electrical response that is not fully captured by note numbers. An electric guitar's direct signal may sound thin or strange until an amp and speaker model shapes it. An acoustic-guitar stereo recording might already contain natural room reflections. These are not details to be scrubbed away in pursuit of a neutral instrument. They are part of the audio object you have been given.

A guitar example clarifies another separation: pitch and *fingering* are not the same thing. Several locations on a guitar fingerboard can produce the same named pitch at similar frequencies, but the string and fret selection changes attack, sustain, color and what other notes can sound at the same time. A MIDI stream containing only pitches and note lengths does not preserve the intended physical fingering. An agent should not claim that its MIDI rendering represents the performance nuances of a fingerstyle guitarist unless those nuances have actually been modeled. Likewise, a piano is a convenient diagram for semitone positions, not a universal standard for how every instrument must be expressed.

At the other end of the spectrum, a synthesizer can take a small number of parameters and create a large variety of sounds. Consider a simple oscillator with a pitch, an amplitude envelope, and a low-pass filter. The oscillator produces a repeating signal. The envelope controls how its amplitude changes from note onset through sustain and release. The filter shapes the output spectrum. Modulate the filter cutoff while the note sounds, and the timbre evolves. Add nonlinear distortion, and new spectral components appear. Add a delay or reverb, and the sound can continue after the synthesizer has stopped generating the dry note.

That last point is easy to overlook when exporting independent stems. The MIDI event might stop at precisely bar sixteen, but the synthesizer's release tail may extend beyond that bar. A reverb might continue several seconds more. If the renderer simply stops at the last note-off time, the exported file can end with an abrupt cut. If two agents render separate sources with different tail conventions, their stems may have different lengths even though their musical parts end at the same point. The delivery contract should distinguish the *composition range*, the *render range*, and any extra room for release or effect tails. All parts should still retain the same session origin so they can be aligned on import.

The word velocity also causes confusion. In basic MIDI note events, velocity is a value associated with note-on, commonly from one to one hundred twenty-seven. It is not a measurement of acoustic sound pressure and not automatically equivalent to a decibel value. A sampler might use it to select a different recording layer. A synthesizer might use it to open a filter. Another plugin could ignore it. A rendered audio file no longer contains a simple, invertible mapping back to that velocity. If the intention is to produce a certain level, measure the audio rather than infer it from the event number.

MIDI may also be too narrow a description for certain kinds of music. A line that depends on continuous slides, variable intonation or detailed ornamentation may need richer pitch curves or instrument-specific articulation controls. Some instruments expose polyphonic expression; some expect automation on a separate channel; some musical systems require a notation that is not built around twelve fixed pitch classes. The solution is not to declare the musical practice impossible to represent. It is to choose a representation that actually preserves the distinctions that matter, and to recognize when direct performance or audio recording carries information that the current event scheme cannot.

There is an important computational distinction hiding inside this musical one. When you mix four recorded waveforms, many operations are vector arithmetic: read samples, scale, add and write. When you render four instrument tracks, the program may also need to synthesize many simultaneous voices. A sample-based drum kit may read and interpolate dozens of recordings. A physical-model string instrument may simulate a dynamical system. A synthesizer may oversample oscillators or run expensive filters. The CPU consumed before summation may dwarf the cost of summation itself. So if an agent claims it has delivered four independent tracks, ask: did it deliver four arrays of audio samples, or four descriptions that require rendering? These have very different production costs.

DawDreamer is particularly useful for making this difference explicit. It has audio playback processors that take existing signals and instrument processors that generate audio. Its graph connects processor outputs to later inputs. It can render without a real-time deadline, allowing you to measure cost or regenerate a part many times. When doing many renders in parallel, the documented safer pattern is to give each worker its own rendering engine and processor objects. That is a rule about mutable plugin state, not an argument that each instrument should be permanently trapped on a different processor core. Ardour, in contrast, gives you a visible timeline, instrument routes, monitoring and the ability to inspect how MIDI regions and audio regions differ in practice.

A practical exercise requires no particular instrument. Write four events: begin with one note for two beats; add a second note for one beat; insert one beat of silence; then let the final note ring for two beats. Write the event onset times in seconds at one hundred twenty beats per minute. Now imagine three renderers: a sine oscillator with no release tail, a plucked instrument with a short decay, and a sustained pad with a long release. The event list is identical, but the waveforms are not, and their useful export boundaries may differ. You could perform the same exercise with a human voice instead of any synthesizer, treating the note list as instructions to a performer rather than as the performance itself.

For a more revealing test, have two agents deliver the same event list and insist that both label their output exactly. One delivers MIDI plus a plugin name, preset and version. The other delivers a forty-eight-kilohertz stereo WAV with its exact sample origin. The first is editable at the level of note events but depends on instruments and rendering settings. The second is immediately mixable but less flexible for changing individual notes. A robust project often keeps both: the symbolic source as the *reason* for the sound, and a rendered audio stem as the *actual sound being mixed*.

That pairing is not merely archival thoroughness. It gives you a path to revise. If a chord voicing clashes with the melody, changing the MIDI and rerendering may be cleaner than pulling frequencies out of the finished waveform with equalization. If the tone is right but the part enters too soon, shift the region or event. If a percussion layer is too loud, you may need only a gain adjustment. Distinguish which problem you are solving before choosing a tool; otherwise an apparently simple mix can become a string of compensations for decisions that should have been made at the source.

There are a few further questions hidden in that seemingly modest note-event model. One is how an instrument knows when two notes are separate. Consider a note-on for middle C, followed by another note-on for the same pitch while the first is still sustained. If the instrument allocates a new voice, the two notes may overlap. If it retriggers the same voice, the old sound may be cut off. If the performance controller sends one note-off, which voice is released? The MIDI protocol and the receiving instrument's voice-allocation policy jointly determine what happens. A composer who exports only the pitch list and believes it is sufficient to reproduce a performance may never notice this ambiguity until the piece is rendered with a different instrument. For sustained pads, plucked-guitar emulations, and piano instruments with pedal modeling, it can be audible and musically important.

Similarly, the duration of a MIDI event is not the duration of its audible sound. The note-off marks a control transition. A short pluck may have decayed almost completely before note-off; a string ensemble patch may need a second or more to release afterward. A piano-like instrument may respond to a sustain pedal held through note-off, and overlapping sympathetic resonances can continue still longer. To specify a waveform unambiguously you may need the note events, pedal and control events, tuning assumptions, and all instrument state. This also changes how we should test an agent-produced part. Comparing its note-on times with the score is useful, but it does not prove that its audible attacks align with other stems. A sound with a slow attack can reach its perceptual prominence noticeably later than its nominal event time.

A second hidden issue is tempo changing over time. At a steady one hundred twenty beats per minute, converting musical beats to seconds is straightforward. But imagine a piece that accelerates. At one moment it is at one hundred twenty beats per minute; later it is at one hundred fifty. A note placed at the beginning of bar nine cannot be positioned by simply multiplying its beat index by a single fixed number of seconds. We must accumulate time across the tempo map. If the tempo changes abruptly at a beat boundary, compute the time in each constant-tempo segment and add the segments. If it changes continuously, calculate the elapsed time from the tempo curve. Different tempo-interpolation conventions can yield different absolute timestamps even with matching endpoint tempo values. Two collaborators who agree on the beginning and ending tempo may therefore still export waveforms that drift apart.

Let's do a simple two-stage calculation. For the first eight quarter-note beats, use one hundred twenty beats per minute, so each lasts half a second: four seconds total. For the next eight beats, use ninety beats per minute. Each lasts two thirds of a second, giving five and one third more seconds. The end of the sixteenth beat interval occurs at nine and one third seconds, not eight seconds. At forty-eight thousand samples per second, that is four hundred forty-eight thousand sample frames. The fractional second becomes an exact frame count in this case; in other tempo maps it may not, so the renderer needs a deliberate rounding policy. Counting a few beats may feel simple, but the clock that connects score time to audio time needs a clear definition.

Another question is whether all sources can be assumed to have the same timebase. A raw recording made with an independent interface could drift slightly relative to another clock over a long performance. Sample rate expressed as forty-eight thousand is a nominal rate; hardware oscillators have tolerances. A difference of fifty parts per million accumulates to three milliseconds over a minute. That may or may not be musically important, but it is distinct from a constant starting offset. Shift one track by three milliseconds and you may align the opening transient while still disagreeing near the end. To remedy drift, you may need timebase synchronization, resampling, or a careful stretch operation. Sliding the whole file cannot fix a changing error. Conversely, for digitally generated stems that share a precise sample clock and tempo map, we can avoid introducing unnecessary stretching.

When people say resampling, time stretching, and pitch shifting as though they were interchangeable, ask which property is being held fixed. Resampling from forty-four point one kilohertz to forty-eight kilohertz preserves the intended duration and pitch by producing a different sample sequence at the new rate. Merely reinterpreting the original sample sequence as forty-eight kilohertz changes playback speed and pitch. A time stretcher aims to change duration while approximately preserving pitch, usually by estimating and modifying local structure. A pitch shifter aims to change perceived pitch without proportionally changing duration. For speech, drums, or complex polyphonic music, each operation may produce different artifacts. The right tool depends on the reason the source is misaligned.

We can make the distinction concrete with a one-second sine wave at four hundred forty hertz. At forty-four thousand one hundred samples per second it contains forty-four thousand one hundred frames. If the sample values are simply played at forty-eight thousand frames per second, the sound ends in about point nine one nine seconds and the frequency rises to about four hundred seventy-nine hertz. A proper sample-rate conversion to forty-eight thousand frames per second produces forty-eight thousand frames for the same one-second musical event, still centered at four hundred forty hertz. The actual anti-alias filter and interpolation details matter for less simple signals, but the preservation target is unambiguous.

This helps us understand why a program can succeed as a note generator and still fail as an audio producer. It may emit all the correct pitches, but use the wrong instrument bank. It may write note-off events that overlap unpredictably. It may round onset times too early. It may omit the sustain controls that give a phrase its articulation. Or it may make a fine rendered part and then drop its last half-second of natural release. None of these is a failure of addition in the mixer. They are failures of the boundary between musical instructions and the actual signal. Treat them as separate stages with separate diagnostics.

A practical design for an agent pipeline is to require three artifacts for every generated instrumental part, when feasible. The first is the editable musical intention: note events, performance controls, tuning, and any arrangement rules. The second is the sound-production configuration: plugin identity, bank or preset, rendering parameters and a record of nondeterministic choices such as a random seed if relevant. The third is the rendered audio: its sample rate, channel count, sample origin, file length and gain convention. The three artifacts need not be equally portable; a human guitar recording will not magically have a perfect MIDI transcription. But separating intention from instrument state from final waveform makes the source of an error easier to identify.

For a small demonstration without audio playback, imagine two agents that claim to deliver the same four-beat phrase. Agent One gives the event sequence: C for one beat, E for one beat, G for two beats. Agent Two gives an array of ninety-six thousand samples for a two-second monophonic rendering at forty-eight kilohertz, beginning at the common zero. The first can be rearranged, revoiced, retuned and re-instrumented; it contains no guaranteed sound. The second can be summed immediately with other correctly aligned waveforms; changing its third note without changing timbre or transients is much harder. If you expect a full production workflow, retain both forms when possible and make explicit which one is authoritative after edits.

A DAW therefore sits between two kinds of knowledge. Upstream it has scores, performances, articulations and instrument design; downstream it has time-indexed waveforms, buses, processing and monitoring. Mixing cannot begin in earnest until those worlds meet, but the best workflow keeps enough information from both sides to change its mind. When the four collaborators say their parts are finished, you now have a better question: finished in which representation, with which timing, and with which sound-generating assumptions? That question often saves more work than buying another processor.

## Display-only companion: source types and time arithmetic

| Deliverable | What is fixed | What remains dependent |
|---|---|---|
| MIDI note list | Event positions, pitches, durations, velocities (if included) | Tuning, instrument patch, envelope, articulation, audio rendering |
| Plugin preset | Some sound-generating parameters | Notes/events, plugin and host versions, automation, rendering settings |
| Dry audio | Waveform at stated sample rate and origin | Downstream processing, arrangement editing, balance |
| Processed audio stem | Rendered instrument and included effects | Routing/mix processing, overlap with other stems, future editability |
| Session project | Graph/edit decisions and references to assets | Assets, installed plugins, engine versions, possible nondeterminism |

At BPM 120 with quarter-note beats: `beat_seconds = 60 / 120 = 0.5`. At sample rate 48,000 Hz, `frames_per_beat = 24,000`; four-beat bars take 96,000 frames. For beat index 3 (zero-based), event start frame is `3 * 24,000 = 72,000`.

The equal-tempered MIDI mapping under A4 = 440 Hz is `f(m) = 440 * 2 ** ((m - 69) / 12)`; this mapping is only a convention and does not encode instrument timbre or all tuning practices.

### Offline thought experiment

Create the event list `(pitch=60, start_beats=0, duration_beats=2)`, `(pitch=64, start_beats=2, duration_beats=1)`, `(silence_beats=1)`, `(pitch=67, start_beats=4, duration_beats=2)`. Render through a dry oscillator, a plucked sample and a slow pad, separately. Record each renderer's *nominal event end* and *actual waveform end*, including tails; the latter need not match.
