# 012000 — From Sketch to Piece: Variation, Contrast, and Musical Revision

**Track:** Music Theory and Composition. **Audience:** People beginning to compose or arranging music on any instrument, or on paper alone. **Prerequisites:** None; the necessary musical terms are explained where used. **Method:** Complete an original sixteen-bar composition in text, derive several variations, identify and repair a weak transition, and design an edition that is ready for eventual synthesis or live realization without requiring audio now.

# From Sketch to Piece: Variation, Contrast, and Musical Revision

A composition often begins as something too small to deserve the word piece. It might be four notes, a repeated rhythm, a chord movement, or a pattern of rests. The challenge is not only to find that initial material, but to decide how to make it worth following. What should return? What should change? How can a second section sound related to the first without merely repeating it? And when the result seems dull or cluttered, what exactly should be revised?

We will build a short work from a four-note gesture, then expand it into sixteen bars. You do not need to hear it to follow the logic, though ultimately the sound and the performers will tell us important things the page cannot. Every pitch, duration, and section boundary will be explicit, so the piece is playable in principle by voices, guitar, keyboard, electronic instruments, or some combination. The point is not to prescribe a particular tone color. It is to make the compositional choices inspectable.

Begin with four notes: D, F, G, A. Give each note one beat in a measure with four beats. The pattern rises by a minor third, then a whole tone, then a whole tone. Its rhythmic identity is very plain: four equally spaced attacks. This is our motive, a compact idea that can be recognized and varied. In our original setting, D is a proposed home pitch. We have chosen the D Dorian scale, whose notes are D, E, F, G, A, B, and C. Its F is a minor third above D, while the B natural distinguishes it from D natural minor. None of this guarantees what the music will feel like. It tells us which relationships we are trying to make available.

What would make the motive into a phrase rather than a list of notes? One answer is to give it a response and a destination. In bar one, play D, F, G, A, one beat each. In bar two, reverse the motion partially: G, F, E, D. In bar three, place E for two beats and B for two beats. That B is worth hearing in a later performance because it brings the Dorian sixth into the foreground. In bar four, use A for two beats and D for two beats. The entire four-bar phrase departs from D, moves away and back, reveals a distinctive pitch in the third bar, and closes on D. We can support it with D minor, G major, E minor, and D minor harmonies respectively. These are chosen from the D Dorian collection, but they are not rules dictating the only possible chords.

The phrase has a beginning and ending. Does it yet need to be repeated? Probably, because listeners benefit from recognizing a musical idea before we ask them to follow an elaborate transformation. Yet an exact repeat may not be the most useful choice. We can make the second appearance almost the same, but give it a little more motion. In the fifth bar, begin D and E as two quick half-beat notes, then F, G, and A each for one beat. We still reach A at the end, and the contour is largely familiar, but the phrase now contains five attacks rather than four. This is a rhythmic variation built from the opening motive.

In the sixth bar, lengthen G to two beats before moving through F and E in one beat each. The earlier downward response had four separate attacks, G, F, E, D. Now the response is more spacious and does not immediately reach D. Bar seven can give the distinctive B natural a full attack, followed by A, G, and E. In bar eight, move through F and E to a D lasting two beats. We have written a variation of the opening four-bar phrase. It still begins near D, still makes use of its close relatives, and still ends at D, but the attacks, durations, and placement of B differ.

Notice what has and has not changed. We have not asked for a louder instrument, a faster performance, or a complicated chord. We changed the local timing and some pitch order. That makes our revision easy to test. The first phrase has a straightforward answer; the second has a slightly more active beginning and a delayed settling. If those differences are too subtle when performed, we could increase the contrast. If they are too large, we could restore one familiar rhythm. Composition becomes manageable when each revision has a name and a purpose.

Now we need a contrasting section. We could make its melody jump more, thin the texture, or change the rhythmic grouping. For this example we will briefly change the harmonic direction. Our home has been D, and the B natural helped mark D Dorian. In bar nine, we introduce D dominant seventh, spelled D, F sharp, A, C. This F sharp is not part of D Dorian. Its presence creates a particular route toward G: the F sharp can rise a semitone to G. The chord D seventh is a dominant of G, so it makes G a temporary destination. The fact that the root is still D does not mean the chord serves our original D-centered role in the same way. Its new third, F sharp, changes the direction.

Write F sharp, A, C, A as four one-beat melody notes in bar nine. In bar ten, move to G harmony, holding G for two beats and B for two. This arrival does not have to establish G as a permanent new key. It is a local destination inside our larger form. In bar eleven, return to E minor harmony, with a line B, G, E, G. These three notes all belong to E minor. The result is calmer after the chromatic approach to G and gives us room for another preparation.

In bar twelve, use A dominant seventh harmony. Its pitch classes are A, C sharp, E, and G. The C sharp is not the C natural of our D Dorian collection. It is a leading tone to D. Let the melody move E, G, B, C sharp, one beat each. B is a non-chord tone relative to A seventh. It leaps up from G, then moves by step to C sharp. In a strict account, that is not an ordinary passing tone, which would normally be approached and left by step. Its role here is to direct attention toward the chromatic leading tone. Put the C sharp on the last beat, and now we have a clear melodic question: will the next strong beat arrive on D? In bar thirteen it does. We place D on the first beat, and the whole sixteen-bar design begins to turn homeward.

We have used two different chromatic gestures. F sharp in bar nine prepares G in bar ten. C sharp in bar twelve prepares D in bar thirteen. One directs attention away from the original center, while the other helps restore that center. A reader can confirm the semitone movements without hearing the piece. The phrase structure provides the larger reason for their placement: four bars of established material, four bars of variation, four bars of contrast, and four bars that return and conclude.

For the final four bars, let the opening idea return with a changed contour. In bar thirteen, use D, A, G, F, one beat each. This begins on the same home pitch as our motive but reaches immediately upward to A and then descends. It refers to the original ingredients without reproducing their order. In bar fourteen, recall the descending response G, F, E, D. In bar fifteen, use B, G, E, D. The B natural comes back so our characteristic Dorian sixth is present near the close. Finally, in bar sixteen, hold D for the entire four-beat bar. The ending now has a larger context: the return is not merely the first four bars copied, but a recognizable family of material that has moved through contrast and reached a more extended close.

A sixteen-bar design of this kind can be labeled A, A prime, B, A double-prime. The labels describe relationships among sections; they do not require that every piece have four equal sections. In our version, A introduces a melody, A prime varies it, B brings chromatic contrast, and A double-prime returns to the home pitch. The last section has an ending different from the first. That matters because a listener who has traveled through the middle of a piece may need a different sort of closure than a listener who has just heard the opening four bars.

At this point we have a complete score skeleton, but not a finished performance or even necessarily a convincing composition. That distinction is important. A page can be internally consistent while sounding crowded. A note can be a correct chord tone while falling awkwardly under a singer's breath. A guitar part can ask for a stretch that is impractical at the intended tempo. A register can be too low to be clear, or two instruments can mask each other. These are not failures of the theory; they are questions a more complete arrangement and an eventual performance must answer. We should mark what remains unknown instead of pretending the score guarantees a result.

How do we revise responsibly when we cannot hear the piece yet? First check what can be checked without sound. Count every bar. The opening has four quarter-note beats, so each line of the final score must use exactly four beats. Check that altered notes are named correctly. F sharp appears in D seventh, C sharp in A seventh. Check that the one-beat C sharp in bar twelve is followed by D at the opening of bar thirteen. Check that the original idea returns in a recognizable way. These are objective questions. Then identify the things that require judgment: how much contrast is enough, whether the return should be quieter, whether the second phrase's faster beginning feels energetic or fussy, and whether the closing D should linger after the other parts stop.

For one deliberate revision, suppose bars five through eight feel too close to bars one through four. We can leave the melody pitches intact and change only the supporting texture. In A, keep the melody alone in bar one and introduce one sustained bass pitch in bar two. In A prime, begin with the bass already present and add a softly articulated rhythm on each beat. If the rhythm makes the section too dense, remove attacks from the accompaniment instead of changing the melody again. This is the advantage of separating composition from arrangement: the same phrase can be tested in multiple settings without losing its basic identity.

For a second revision, suppose the chromatic middle section feels disconnected from the Dorian opening. We could bring back the original rising rhythm over the D seventh in bar nine, perhaps with new pitches F sharp, A, C, D, rather than the current F sharp, A, C, A. Or we could repeat the opening motive's rhythm in the bass while the upper melody changes. That would make the B section share a rhythmic fingerprint with A even while its harmony moves elsewhere. The choice is not automatically better. It gives us a clear comparison: stronger motivic continuity versus a sharper break at the section boundary.

For a third revision, consider the final bar. Our melody holds D for four beats over D minor harmony. If every part sustains a full chord for exactly four beats, the ending might feel solid. If all but the melody stop on beat one, the same written D can sound exposed. If an accompaniment repeats a quiet pattern underneath, the ending might suggest continuation instead of full closure. These are testable arrangement alternatives. We can write them as separate versions and later audition them, rather than silently replacing one with another and forgetting why.

The score also invites instrument-specific decisions without being owned by any instrument. A guitarist might alternate a low root with upper chord tones, or use a simple D minor voicing and a G major shape. But standard-tuning guitar places limits on which voicings can sustain independently, and a part that seems simple on paper may need two players if the melody and bass overlap awkwardly. A singer may change an octave for comfort. A keyboard player can sustain low notes while placing upper melody tones, provided the hands can reach. A sequencer can separate every line into events. The abstract note relationships survive those choices; the practical realization does not have to be identical across instruments.

One day an audio tool could turn this score into playback. We would need a representation with at least pitch, onset, duration, and a part identifier for each note. We would also need tempo, a tuning convention, and probably articulation, dynamics, and choices of sound source. Those last parameters are not merely cosmetic. They can determine which line is foreground, how phrases breathe, and whether a slow chord change feels connected or abrupt. The text score is therefore a reproducible starting point, not a substitute for sound. For now, writing it clearly allows us to reason about structure without inventing an audio example that does not exist.

Most importantly, do not let the score become an excuse to avoid composing. A first draft teaches you what the material can do. A second draft should answer an identified problem, not simply accumulate more notes. If a phrase is recognizable, decide whether to repeat, vary, or interrupt it. If a passage lacks direction, identify which arrival it is preparing. If the texture is crowded, remove or redistribute an event before adding another instrument. If the ending is weak, ask whether the musical motion has actually arrived or just stopped. These questions are available whether you are working with guitar, voice, notation software, or pencil and paper.

We began with D, F, G, A: four attacks and a rising shape. We now have sixteen bars with a clear central pitch, a modal signature, a variant phrase, two controlled chromatic approaches, and a closing return. None of those decisions individually makes a piece good. Together they give the composer a coherent object to test and revise. That is the real transition from theory into composition: not collecting labels, but using relationships to make a piece that can carry memory and change across time.

### Arrangement versions to compare

- **Version S — sparse:** Melody alone during bar 1; add a sustained root from bar 2; no rhythmic accompaniment until bar 9; silence except D4 on final bar.
- **Version P — pulsed:** Low root sustains each bar; a quiet rhythmic line attacks beats 1 and 3, except during the chromatic B section, where it rests. No instrument identity assumed.
- **Version G — guitar-compatible sketch:** One part plucks a chord root on beat 1 and a higher chord tone on beat 3, while a separate line handles the melody. This is a **two-part abstraction**, not a promise that one player can perform every voicing at tempo on a single guitar.

### Revision exercises

1. Rewrite bar 9 to retain the original four-quarter-note attack pattern while changing at least two pitch intervals. Keep D7 and prepare G in bar 10.
2. Remove all chromatic notes from B and compare the resulting harmonic motion. Explain which destinations become less explicitly prepared.
3. Write a new bar 16 that closes over the same Dm harmony but has two separate melodic events. Compare its sense of ending with the long D4.
4. Make a version of A′ in 3+3+2 eighth-note groupings, while keeping each bar four quarters long. Mark every attack and compute the sums.
5. Assign the melody, low part, and rhythmic accompaniment to three arbitrary sound sources. Discuss which events would have to be changed for a single acoustic guitar arrangement rather than three independent performers.
6. On paper, compare A, A′, B, and A″ by marking repeated pitches, distinctive intervals, harmonic root motion, and phrase endings. Separate changes that are already in the score from decisions deferred to performance.
