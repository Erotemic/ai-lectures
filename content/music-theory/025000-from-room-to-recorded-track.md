# From the Room to a Track: Microphones, Monitoring, and Recording Latency

A guitarist plays the same four bars twice. In both takes, the instrument, player, room, and musical notes are the same. In the first take a microphone is close to the instrument; in the second it is farther away. The two audio files do not merely differ in volume. Reflections from the room, the balance of strings and body, and the time at which sound reaches the microphone have changed. If two microphones are used at once, the resulting files may not even combine as expected. Before reaching for a compressor, we need to understand which decisions were already made by the act of recording.

The path from musician to sample file has several distinct stages. A vibrating string, vocal fold, drumhead, or loudspeaker excites the air. Pressure variations travel outward and reflect from boundaries. A microphone responds to pressure and, depending on its design, to the way pressure differs across space. Its transducer makes an electrical signal. A microphone preamplifier raises that signal to a usable level. An analogue-to-digital converter measures and encodes it as samples. An audio interface and driver deliver blocks of those samples to the computer; the recording application places them on a timeline. Every stage matters, and no single stage can be summarized as merely converting a perfectly pure musical note to numbers.

The microphone hears the instrument from a different position than the musician does. A guitar has multiple sound-producing surfaces. Close to the sound hole, the balance of low frequencies can differ greatly from a position near the twelfth fret. Farther back, more of the instrument and room contribute. There is no abstract microphone position called correct. Shure's recording-techniques guide treats orientation, directivity, and distance as choices for favoring some sounds and rejecting others. Its examples are not laws of guitar tone, and a microphone model's frequency response must not be confused with a guarantee about the final recording.

Consider directionality. An ideal omnidirectional microphone responds similarly to equal sound arriving from different directions. A cardioid favors the front and rejects sound arriving from behind, with real-world performance varying by frequency and design. That makes a cardioid useful when a monitor loudspeaker, another performer, or a ventilation system is behind it. But moving the unwanted source into the microphone's nominal rejection direction does not make the room silent. Reverberant sound reaches the microphone from many directions. The off-axis response may color the reflected sound as well. A smaller pickup pattern is not an electronic delete-background-noise command.

Many directional microphones also change their bass response when brought close to a source. This is called the proximity effect. Shure documents the effect explicitly for its cardioid SM58. It is not appropriate to assign every microphone the same number of decibels of bass boost; the magnitude depends on microphone type and distance. Nor is the effect a general law of sound getting bassier whenever anything comes closer. In its usual microphone sense it is a property of pressure-gradient directional transducers. An omnidirectional pressure microphone does not exhibit that same proximity-effect behavior.

Here is a thought experiment to separate direct sound from room sound. Record one line with the microphone a short distance from a singer, then move it substantially farther away while keeping the gain adjusted so the direct voice remains similar in level. Because room reflections have traveled different paths, the ratio and timing of reflected to direct sound change. The distant take may make the singer sound integrated with the room; the close take may be easier to place in a later artificial acoustic space. Neither is superior in isolation. If the room has an unpleasant resonance, the close position may reduce its influence. If the room is part of the musical idea, the distant microphone may preserve something the close one cannot.

Distance also has a time consequence. For an illustrative speed of sound of 343 metres per second, one extra metre of travel corresponds to approximately 2.92 milliseconds. At forty-eight thousand samples per second, that is about 140 samples. This is a physical arrival-time difference. It is not the same as an audio interface's buffering delay. If a second microphone is one metre farther from the same guitar, aligning both recorded files by their start timestamps will not erase the propagation difference. Depending on microphone placement, part of the same instrument arrives with different phase and with different reflection patterns. Simply shifting one file by 140 samples might align a particular transient, but cannot generally undo the different room responses.

Now imagine a player recording a new part over an existing stem in Ardour. The backing track travels from stored samples through plug-ins and the digital-to-analogue converter to the headphones. The live microphone travels through the analogue-to-digital converter and possibly through Ardour's processors before returning to the same headphones. Several kinds of delay have accumulated. Ardour's manual distinguishes sound traveling through air, conversion, driver and I/O delays, buffer processing, and plug-in processing. They must not be collapsed into a single knob called latency.

A numerical example makes the difference concrete. A buffer of 256 samples at forty-eight kilohertz corresponds to 5.33 milliseconds of audio. That is the duration of one block, not a guaranteed round-trip latency of 5.33 milliseconds. A live monitored signal may pass through capture and playback buffering, converters, a USB interface, plug-ins, and other scheduling stages. Its round-trip delay may be much longer, and the actual figure should be measured rather than guessed from buffer size. Halving a block size can reduce one part of the delay while increasing how often the computer must wake and process work. A quiet-looking CPU meter does not guarantee every buffer finishes before its deadline.

Ardour provides three broad monitoring strategies, described in its manual: monitoring outside the application, monitoring through compatible audio-interface hardware, and monitoring through Ardour itself. The first two can avoid much of the computer's processing latency, depending on hardware, but may bypass software effects the performer wants to hear. Software monitoring provides extensive routing and processing but adds its own delay. When a musician hears both an unprocessed direct path and a delayed software path, the combination may create an audible comb-filter effect. It can be tempting to diagnose that as bad microphone quality when the problem is duplicated monitoring.

Recording alignment is yet another issue. Imagine a performer listening to a backing beat and playing exactly in time according to what arrives at the ears. The microphone captures the performance and the computer writes a file. If the software simply stamps the received block at the instant it reaches the CPU, that would place the performance late relative to the backing track. Ardour's latency-compensation documentation explains its read-ahead approach and why capture and playback delays must be accounted for separately. Compensation can position the recorded take correctly on the session timeline even when the performer experiences some monitoring delay. That does not retroactively change how the performance felt while it was being played.

A robust procedure therefore begins with two measurements, not one. First, does monitoring feel tolerable for this performer and part? Second, is a newly recorded transient placed correctly relative to a known reference after recording? A loopback calibration answers the second question by routing a test signal from an interface output back to an input and measuring the round trip, with care to distinguish cable delay from converter and driver delay. Ardour documents the JACK and jack-delay methodology, while noting that the exact backend matters. The procedure should be adapted to the device and driver actually in use rather than copied as an incantation.

Consider the practical decision when adding a guitar part to stems produced by separate agents. If the guitar is recorded through a microphone, save the original unprocessed capture. Document the microphone model and placement, interface, sample rate, room condition when material, and any monitoring path that changes what the performer hears. This is not bureaucratic overhead: it makes it possible to distinguish a compositional disagreement from a recording artifact. If a guitarist complains that the rhythm feels late, inspect the monitoring and alignment before telling a composition agent to move the notes earlier.

If the guitar part is instead generated as MIDI and rendered through DawDreamer, the chain is different. There may be no microphone, no sound propagation through a physical room, and no capture converter. A virtual instrument computes samples from note events and internal state. Yet the rendered stem can still contain deliberate room simulation or plug-in latency, and it still needs a common session origin. An offline render may take more than real time without producing an audible dropout, but that freedom does not excuse a mistaken tempo map or an unrecorded plug-in preset.

We can now make a useful distinction between a recording, a performance, and a mix. The performance is what the musician does. A microphone recording is evidence of what happened at a particular listening position, filtered through real equipment and a real room. A mix is a set of later decisions about how recorded or generated sources coexist. Some decisions are reversible: fader levels can be changed. Some are partly reversible: a resonant room can sometimes be mitigated. Some information has never been captured at all: no equalizer can reconstruct the instrument sound at a completely different microphone position from a single ordinary recording.

For the composer, the lesson is more constructive than cautionary. Before collecting independent stems, specify whether they are symbolic notes, dry synthetic audio, close-miked performance, or deliberately room-colored sound. A stem is not just a waveform; it is the result of a chain of artistic and physical decisions. Once those chains are understood, the mixer can spend less effort guessing at the causes of problems and more effort deciding what the music should become.

## Display-only companion: two independent latency budgets

For an illustrative 48,000-Hz session:

- A **256-frame audio period** represents `256 / 48000 = 5.333... ms` of samples. It is not a complete round-trip measurement.
- A **1-metre additional air path**, using the illustrative speed `343 m/s`, adds `1/343 ≈ 2.915 ms ≈ 139.94 samples`.
- To test a recording offset, use a documented loopback path and compare recorded input to the emitted reference; independently verify monitoring behavior with the actual interface and backend.

## Source-to-claim guide

- **Physical placement, pattern, reflections:** [Shure, *Microphone Techniques for Recording*](https://www.shure.com/en-US/docs/education/microphone-techniques-for-recording), sections on directionality, recording distance, and placement. The first-person guitar scenario and quantitative metre example are **original illustrative reasoning**, not measurements reported by Shure.
- **Proximity effect and specific microphone behavior:** [Shure, *SM58 User Guide*](https://www.shure.com/en-US/docs/guide/SM58), “Proximity Effect” and “Applications and Placement.” SM58-specific figures must not be generalized to all microphones.
- **Monitoring route choices:** [Ardour Manual, *Monitor Signal Flow*](https://manual.ardour.org/recording/monitoring/monitor-signal-flow/) and [*Monitor Setup in Ardour*](https://manual.ardour.org/recording/monitoring/monitor-setup-in-ardour/).
- **Capture/playback latency, JACK calibration, and read-ahead:** [Ardour Manual, *Latency and Latency-Compensation*](https://manual.ardour.org/synchronization/latency-and-latency-compensation/), especially “The Latency Chain,” “Latency compensation,” and “Calibrating JACK Latency.” Backend-specific details should be applied only to compatible setups.
- **Non-destructive recording modes:** [Ardour Manual, *Track Modes*](https://manual.ardour.org/working-with-tracks/track-modes/).

## Exercises and falsification checks (non-spoken)

1. Write two alternative signal paths for recording guitar with direct interface monitoring versus software monitoring. Mark precisely which components can add latency. This is a diagrammatic reasoning exercise; no microphone needed.
2. Create a table separating physical propagation offset, unknown hardware conversion offset, audio block duration, and plug-in latency. Avoid adding any value not actually measured.
3. If a future sound renderer is available, test a direct guitar source plus a delayed copy by shifting one copy by 140 samples. This is a synthetic demonstration of correlation and delay, **not** a faithful physical two-microphone simulation.
