# Chord Voicing, Inversions, and Harmonic Color

Suppose a singer holds the note E. The pitch stays the same for four beats. Under it, imagine a C major chord: C, E, G. E belongs to the chord as its third. Change the accompanying harmony to A minor: A, C, E. The melody is now the fifth of the chord. Change the harmony again to F major seven: F, A, C, E. The same melodic E is now the seventh of the chord. You can identify these roles by reading the pitch sets without needing an audio demonstration. In performance, the note participates differently in each harmonic environment.

That is one of the most useful lessons in harmony. The identity of a note is relational. Its pitch does not by itself determine whether it feels stable, active, floating, poignant, or expected. The bass, the surrounding notes, the sequence of earlier harmonies, the register, and the style all contribute. A composer can therefore change the expressive direction of a melody without altering the melody at all.

We will work with a specific pitch system: twelve-tone equal temperament and the common chord labels used in much Western tonal, jazz, and popular music. Other musical systems organize pitch and vertical combinations differently. Nothing in this lecture depends on owning a keyboard. The examples can be checked on a guitar, another instrument, with a voice and a friend, or as written lists of events.

## A chord name is not the actual sound

A C major triad consists of the pitch classes C, E, and G. A pitch class groups together notes with the same letter and accidental regardless of octave. C3, C4, and C5 are different pitches in different registers, but all belong to the C pitch class. The chord name tells us its basic ingredients. It says little about their spacing, doubling, attack, or balance.

Imagine a low C, a middle E, and a high G. Now bring G close to E in the middle register, leaving the low C isolated. These arrangements contain the same three pitch classes, but one spreads its energy across a wider register while the other concentrates it. Add another C high above the others, and the tonic pitch becomes doubled. Move that doubled C down to the lowest register instead, and the listener's sense of weight may change again. All these versions can still be called C major.

The placement of chord notes in register is called voicing. A voicing is a concrete choice about which octave each note occupies and which notes, if any, are repeated or omitted. A keyboard offers one physical method for trying these choices. A guitar offers another: a given chord shape may double some strings, omit a fifth, or place an open string higher than a fretted one. A choir distributes notes among singers; a wind ensemble might give the melody note to one instrument and the same pitch an octave lower to another. Different physical solutions can express the same abstract chord.

This is why simply saying 'play C major' leaves room for composition and arrangement. A songwriter may only need a chord symbol above a lyric. An arranger must decide exactly which instruments play which notes. A solo guitarist must find a playable fingering. A composer for an electronic instrument can choose spread, register, sustain, and timbre independently. Those are different stages of specifying the music.

## A chord's root and its bass are not the same thing

The root is the pitch from which a chord is named. The bass is its lowest sounding note at a particular moment. When they are the same, we say the chord is in root position. If the bass is another chord tone, the chord is inverted.

For C major, put C in the bass and any arrangement of E and G above it. That is root position. Put E at the bottom and C and G somewhere above. That is first inversion, often written C slash E in popular chord notation. Put G at the bottom with C and E above. That is second inversion, written C slash G. In all three cases, the underlying chord is C major: C, E, and G. The difference is which member occupies the lowest part.

That bass difference matters for the movement from one harmony to another. Consider four chords in a row: C major, G major, A minor, F major. Their roots are C, G, A, F. If the bass plays those roots, it makes the larger jumps C to G and A to F. Instead, you might choose C, B, A, A beneath the same sequence of chord identities: C major, G major with B in the bass, A minor, then F major with A in the bass. The bass descends by step from C to B to A and then stays. The chord roots have not changed, but the bass has become a continuous melodic line.

You can verify every state. G major contains G, B, and D, so B is permitted as its inverted bass. F major contains F, A, and C, so A is permitted as its inverted bass. The second and fourth harmonies are inversions, not different chord species. If you hear or imagine the second version as smoother, it is because we have designed a clearer path for the low voice. A different rhythmic or dramatic purpose might make the larger root jumps preferable.

On a six-string guitar in standard tuning, the lowest open string is E. A common C major shape can include that open low E along with C, E, and G above it. In that case the lowest note is E and the sonority is C slash E, even though the chord's root remains C. The choice to include or mute the low string changes the bass without requiring a new scale. This is a guitar-specific illustration of a completely general harmonic distinction.

## What a seventh adds

A triad is built in thirds: for example C, E, G. Add another third above the fifth, and you have a seventh chord. The name comes from the distance between the root and the added note as spelled in the scale. The resulting sonority has four basic chord tones, although an actual voicing may omit or double some of them.

C major seven has C, E, G, B. In semitone terms, B is eleven semitones above C within an octave, one semitone short of the octave. C dominant seven, usually written simply C seven, has C, E, G, B flat. Its seventh is ten semitones above C. These are different chords. The distinction of a single semitone changes the potential movement of the seventh and the chord's possible function in a key.

The same pair of labels does not tell you everything about emotion. A major-seven chord is often used to create a spacious or sustained color, while a dominant-seven chord can create directed tension. But harmonic function comes from context. A C dominant-seven chord may function as the dominant of F major because its E and B flat make particular motions toward F and A. The same C seven sonority may appear as a stable member of a blues or rock progression, without resolving according to classical dominant rules. The pitches alone cannot tell you which interpretation is appropriate.

Now compare C minor seven: C, E flat, G, B flat. Lowering E to E flat changes the quality of the basic triad. The added B flat is still a minor seventh above C. In a different sonority, C minor-major seven consists of C, E flat, G, B natural. It combines a minor third with a major seventh. All four of these chords have C as root, yet their third and seventh combinations produce different internal intervals. You can compare them on paper by making a four-column table for root, third, fifth, and seventh, then circling the notes that change.

## Tendency tones and a concrete resolution

Some chord tones acquire a strong expectation of motion in a given tonal setting. In C major, the dominant seventh chord is G seven: G, B, D, F. B is the leading tone, a semitone below the tonic C. F is the seventh of the G chord, and in common-practice voice leading it typically resolves downward to E, the third of C major. When B rises to C at the same time that F falls to E, two voices move by one semitone in opposite directions. That pair of movements helps make a G-seven-to-C arrival feel directed.

We can make a full four-part example without depending on any particular instrument. Put G in the bass. Above it, place a second G, a B, and an F. This is an incomplete voicing of G seven because it omits D, the fifth. At the next harmony, move the bass G to C. Hold the upper G. Move B upward to C. Move F downward to E. The destination notes are C, G, C, E: a complete C major triad with the root doubled. Three of the voices have moved; one is a common tone. The two semitone resolutions carry the strongest local direction.

It helps to distinguish a tendency from a commandment. In classical four-part exercises, resolving the chordal seventh down by step is a major convention, and the leading tone often rises to tonic in a prominent upper voice. In jazz, blues, rock, film scoring, and many other settings, players may hold, bend, revoice, substitute, or otherwise reinterpret those notes. Musical rules tell you how a system produces a style-specific effect. Learning them creates options; it need not narrow your vocabulary to one historical repertoire.

If you have no way to play the example, use three arrows: B to C, F to E, G to G. Those arrows describe the dominant's resolution in the upper voices with complete precision. Once the relationships are clear, hearing them later will deepen the intuition, but the compositional decision is already represented.

## Added notes and extensions

Adding a seventh opens the door to larger chord labels. Continue stacking thirds from C: C, E, G, B, D. D is a ninth above C if you count letter names across an octave. The label C major nine ordinarily means a C-major-seventh sonority with an added D: C, E, G, B, D. The sound can be widely spaced. A practical voicing might omit G because the root, third, seventh, and ninth carry enough information in the given context.

But the label C nine usually means a dominant ninth chord: C, E, G, B flat, D. And C add nine normally means a C major triad with D added but no seventh required: C, E, G, D. The distinction between C major nine, C nine, and C add nine is not a notational trick. They are instructions for different collections of notes, with different possible relationships to surrounding harmonies.

Chord symbols also depend on genre, arranger, and instrument. Guitar charts often choose playable four-, five-, or six-string grips rather than every theoretical chord tone. A horn arranger may distribute the ninth and seventh across independent voices. A composer using a synthesizer might emphasize the extension as the melody and omit the root from the upper instrument because a separate bass supplies it. A chord's theoretical spelling is a starting point; its realization is an artistic choice.

Consider a second added note: F over C major. It can be understood as a fourth or an eleventh, depending on the harmonic and notational context. If the note F replaces E, a suspended-fourth sonority such as C sus four contains C, F, G and deliberately avoids the third. If E and F both appear, the close relationship between them can create a different tension. An eleventh chord label may imply the presence of a seventh as well, while an add-four label makes a different specification. Rather than memorize every symbol at once, learn to ask which notes are present and which have been deliberately left out.

## Reharmonize a melody while leaving it intact

Return to the held E from the opening. We will use it as the first beat of four successive bars, one chord per bar. Place C major under it. E is the third. Place A minor under it. E becomes the fifth. Place F major seven under it. E becomes the major seventh. Place C major seven under it. E returns to being the third, but the new B in the chord gives the surrounding harmony an additional color.

These four choices can all fit within the same seven-note pitch collection. You have changed the listener's harmonic reference point without transposing the melody or introducing chromatic pitches. That process is a basic form of reharmonization: choosing different supporting chords for material that already exists. Some reharmonizations deliberately change function, motion, or key. Others simply redistribute color and bass movement within a stable tonal center.

Now change only one bass note. Keep F major seven in the third bar, but place A in the bass instead of F. The same four pitch classes are available: F, A, C, E. We have F major seven in first inversion. The held melodic E remains the seventh, but the low voice connects differently to the neighboring A minor and C harmonies. A good exercise is to write only the bass pitches under the same melodic E, then compare whether the bass tells a descending, rising, or stationary story.

This perspective prevents chord vocabulary from becoming a contest for the most elaborate name. Adding three extensions to every harmony can crowd the middle register and make the melody harder to locate. A single seventh in one bar can be more consequential if it provides a new voice-leading path. A simple triad in the next bar may gain expressive force from the contrast. The harmonic question is not how many distinct chord symbols you can write. It is which relationships will be heard and why they matter at this point in the piece.

## The arrangement is where the idea becomes physical

Suppose you compose a sequence of chords on paper: C major, G over B, A minor, F over A. The abstract progression is portable. Two people could sing the upper voices while a third sings bass notes. A guitar player might arpeggiate the chord tones, finding shapes that keep the top note within reach. A small string ensemble could sustain the harmonies while one instrument carries a melody. A synthesizer could place the bass in one register, the seventh in another, and the melody in a bright timbre above both.

But no instrument is a neutral translation device. On a guitar, some open-string voicings ring easily and others require stretched fingers or muted strings. A wind player needs breaths. A singer has a comfortable range. A plucked string note decays differently from a sustained bow or a held electronic tone. The same pitches and durations can therefore produce different textures when realized. The score records the relationships; arranging also specifies how they become sound.

When working without audio playback, start by writing an event table: melody note, chord symbol, bass note, and the exact notes of each chosen voicing. Check that every required note has a place and that the intended rhythm adds up. Mark the semitone movements with arrows. When the plan is performed or rendered as sound, it becomes something you can audition and revise. The absence of playback limits immediate sensory feedback, but it need not stop you from making precise musical decisions.

A chord name is an address in a harmonic vocabulary. Voicing says who occupies which room, and voice leading says how they travel between rooms. Inversions change the foundation without changing the chord's identity. Sevenths and extensions introduce new relationships that can be sustained, resolved, or deliberately left open. Compose with these ideas as movements and choices, and harmony becomes more than a sequence of labels: it becomes a way to shape attention through time.

### Smoothly directed G7 → C example

| Voice | G7 event | C event | Motion |
|---|---|---|---|
| Bass | G2 | C3 | up perfect fourth |
| Lower inner | G3 | G3 | held common tone |
| Upper inner | B3 | C4 | up semitone, leading tone to tonic |
| Top | F4 | E4 | down semitone, chordal seventh to third |

The G7 voicing omits D and doubles G; the C voicing doubles C. This is one specific valid tonal realization, not a universal template for all seventh chords.

### Bass-line revoicing, independent of instrument

| Chord identity | Root-position bass | Alternative bass |
|---|---|---|
| C | C3 | C3 |
| G | G2 or G3 | B2 (G/B) |
| Am | A2 | A2 |
| F | F2 | A2 (F/A) |

The alternative bass C3–B2–A2–A2 makes a mostly stepwise line. Exact octave selection may be adapted for a performer.

### Exercises

1. Hold E4 as a four-bar melody. Choose C, Am, Fmaj7, Cmaj7 as the harmonies and identify E's chord role in each bar.
2. Transcribe C/E and C/G into pitch sets. Explain which note must be lowest for each label to be accurate.
3. Change G7's upper B3 to C4 and F4 to E4 while keeping G3 sustained. Confirm that the target is a C major voicing.
4. Take a C major triad and write two different voicings over the same bass: one close and one widely spread. State their absolute pitches, not just note letters.
5. If using guitar, compare a C chord with and without an open low E string. Check the actual lowest string that sounds before labeling the inversion. If not using an instrument, compare C3–E3–G3 with E2–C3–G3 on paper.
