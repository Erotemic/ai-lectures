# Who Is the People? Revolutions, Constitutions, and the Boundaries of Freedom

# Who Is the People? Revolutions, Constitutions, and the Boundaries of Freedom

Imagine a town in which nearly everyone agrees that the government has lost its right to govern. The tax collector speaks for a distant king. Soldiers enforce orders that local families regard as intolerable. Merchants complain that trade is being strangled. Craftspeople want protection from rising prices. Farmers resent dues. A group of property owners calls a meeting and declares that authority belongs to the people.

Who enters the meeting room?

The question seems small compared with the decision to overthrow a government. Yet it turns out to be central. Does the phrase the people include women? People who own no property? People whose ancestors were enslaved? People still enslaved? Communities whose lands the new state wishes to occupy? What of the men carrying guns for a revolutionary army who have no vote in its councils? And if the existing ruler loses authority, does the answer become a republic, a constitutional monarchy, an independent federation, or several rival governments claiming the same legitimacy?

Across the Atlantic world from the 1770s into the 1820s, the answers changed through declarations, petitions, riots, elections, uprisings, wars, and negotiations. A textbook often presents a neat succession: American Revolution, French Revolution, Haitian Revolution, Latin American independence. But this sequence hides how differently people understood the political future, and how events in one place altered the choices elsewhere.

The central problem was not simply whether liberty should replace tyranny. It was who could claim liberty, what kind of liberty they meant, and whether one people's freedom could be constructed by restricting another's.

## Sovereignty is a claim about the right to decide

Start with a word that sometimes sounds like legal decoration: sovereignty. It means, broadly, ultimate political authority—the standing to decide which commands are binding and what makes a government legitimate. Under a monarchy, that authority might be described as resting in a king, though actual rule depended on laws, councils, local rights, and institutions. Under a doctrine of popular sovereignty, authority is said to rest in a people who may authorize, alter, or reject their government.

The change is profound but incomplete. Saying that the people are sovereign does not identify which people, where their territory begins, or who can speak for them. An elected legislature claims representation. A revolutionary committee claims emergency necessity. An army claims to defend the revolution. A city claims its own historic rights. A community that has been excluded from representation asks why its consent was not requested.

Consider an imperial crisis. An overseas colony is governed by a monarch across an ocean. The colonists may ask for taxation only with local consent, yet some among them want to remain subjects of the monarch. When war begins, people must choose whether resistance means reform inside the empire or separation from it. Their choice is not merely philosophical. It may affect the property they hold, the allies they can recruit, the troops they fear, and the commercial networks on which they depend.

The historian Wim Klooster argues that the Atlantic revolutions should be understood comparatively as conflicts within societies, not tidy uprisings in which all ordinary people confronted a small ruling elite. Families and neighborhoods divided. People of similar economic standing fought for opposing causes. Some rebelled to expand rights; others rebelled to preserve local hierarchies against imperial officers. Sometimes the same person changed sides when the meaning of the revolution changed.

This perspective matters because a revolution has at least three histories. There is a history of stated principles. There is a history of institutions and military power. And there is a history of those who live with the outcome. The three do not move in step.

## Thirteen colonies, several kinds of independence

In Britain's North American colonies, disputes over imperial taxation and governance intensified after the Seven Years' War. The war had increased Britain's expenses and reconfigured its imperial position. Parliamentary measures and colonial resistance became entangled with older arguments about rights under British constitutional traditions. Many colonists originally demanded the rights of British subjects, not a new national identity. When independence was declared in July 1776, that transformation had been fought over.

The Declaration of Independence articulated a broad principle of human equality and government resting on consent. It also listed grievances against the king, including a denunciation of Indigenous peoples in language that reveals a central contradiction in the document's political world. Read the document both as an ambitious claim and as a source with limits. Its authors asserted a right to self-government for the new states while describing resistance at the frontier in terms that dismissed Indigenous sovereignty.

The war did not put all people on one side. Enslaved African Americans considered offers of emancipation from British military authorities, served in various capacities on both sides, escaped to seek freedom, or remained under coercive control. Some Black Patriots also fought for the revolutionary cause. Native nations made divergent choices while attempting to defend their territories and political futures. Many colonists remained Loyalists. A revolution narrated only through the intentions of its best-known founders makes these decisions disappear.

After independence, the new states had to decide how authority would be divided. The United States Constitution, drafted in 1787 and effective in 1789, created a federal structure that balanced institutions and states while accommodating slavery. Neither independence nor the constitutional language of the people eliminated enslavement, Indigenous dispossession, or restrictions on political participation. Women organized political ideas, boycotts, households, and communities, yet formal rights remained sharply unequal.

This is not a reason to pretend the Declaration's universal language meant nothing. Universal claims can be taken up by people the original political settlement excluded. The point is to keep two questions apart: what a text promises, and what a government actually permits. The distance between those questions would become a site of struggle for generations.

## France changes the political vocabulary and reveals its limits

In France, fiscal crisis, social grievances, and conflict over reform pushed the monarchy toward the Estates-General in 1789. Representatives gathered in a political order that assigned formal places to clergy, nobility, and the rest. The Third Estate's claim to speak for the nation challenged the premise that political authority could be sorted so neatly into inherited orders. The National Assembly and the Declaration of the Rights of Man and of the Citizen carried arguments about liberty and equality into institutional politics.

Yet the vocabulary was unstable. Rights of man could mean a universal human claim while political citizenship remained narrower. The revolution transformed property relations, religious institutions, and state administration, but it did not instantly resolve the place of women in public life. Olympe de Gouges's 1791 Declaration of the Rights of Woman and of the Female Citizen exposed that gap by extending the logic of equality to women. Her intervention was not a minor correction to a completed consensus. It showed that the boundaries of the sovereign public were themselves contested.

Political conflict in France became increasingly violent. War with foreign powers, struggles among revolutionary factions, pressures from urban crowds, and fear of counterrevolution helped push the regime toward coercive emergency government. The execution of the king in 1793, the Terror, the Thermidorian reaction, the Directory, and Napoleon's rise are not one smooth line from absolute monarchy to democracy. They show successive attempts to answer who could govern, on what authority, and through which institutions.

The colonial question was inseparable from these arguments. France's overseas empire included Saint-Domingue, a plantation colony where enormous profits relied on the forced labor of enslaved Africans. Free people of color, some themselves property holders, demanded equal civil rights; enslaved workers had a far more fundamental claim to freedom. A declaration issued in Paris did not settle whether colonists were willing to lose their legal power over human beings. Conflict in the colony would make the supposedly domestic French Revolution into an Atlantic revolution.

In 1794, the French National Convention abolished slavery across French colonies, after uprisings and emancipation decisions within Saint-Domingue had changed the political situation. These steps were not merely gifts handed down from Paris. Armed struggle, international warfare, local politics, and formerly enslaved people's actions made emancipation both urgent and possible. Napoleon's subsequent colonial policies endangered these gains. The Haitian Revolution deserves its own sustained account, because it transformed the terms of the entire Atlantic debate.

## Not one revolution of the enslaved, one of the free

A careless comparison puts the American and French revolutions into a box labeled politics and the Haitian Revolution into a box labeled slavery. That separation is misleading. Slavery was political power: the legal right of one person to command another's labor and life. Emancipation was a change in sovereignty, citizenship, labor relations, land use, military authority, and the place of a new nation in the world.

Conversely, changes in government elsewhere did not necessarily break existing systems of bondage. An independent colony might continue slavery, adopt a new constitution, and permit only a narrow electorate to vote. The word republic does not, on its own, describe the lived range of freedom.

This makes the Haitian case a test of revolutionary universalism, but not simply a moral examination administered to France. People in Saint-Domingue had different political projects, and their disagreements mattered. White planters, free people of color, enslaved insurgents, French officials, British and Spanish forces, and leaders such as Toussaint Louverture and Jean-Jacques Dessalines did not all agree on the outcome or on the organization of labor after emancipation. The conflict produced a new state, but its internal debates did not end on independence day.

A historian who writes that one ideology naturally led to another misses contingency. Ideas circulate, but people interpret them inside wars, households, religious communities, and competing institutions. The same political slogan can become a demand for abolition in one setting, for property protections in another, and for restored monarchy in a third.

## When the king disappears but the empire remains

Now turn to Spanish America. The crisis that accelerated independence was not initially a colonial declaration modeled neatly on July 1776. In 1808 Napoleon's intervention in Spain forced abdications and placed his brother Joseph on the throne. Subjects across a far-flung monarchy asked a difficult question: if their legitimate king was absent or captive, where did authority go?

In many places, local governing councils, or juntas, claimed to exercise authority in the monarch's name. Their creators did not necessarily want permanent separation. Some hoped to defend Ferdinand the Seventh while reorganizing representation. Others saw opportunities for greater autonomy, constitutional change, or independence. Still others opposed the new political bodies. In the same decade, a constitutional experiment emerged through the Spanish Cortes at Cádiz, whose 1812 constitution proposed a political order for a monarchy spanning Europe and the Americas.

The Cádiz constitution was not simply an export from Europe to passive American subjects. Representatives and arguments from territories on both sides of the Atlantic shaped the debate. But representation itself was disputed: how should different territories be counted, and who counted within them? Distinctions of ancestry, caste, legal status, race, and property affected political membership. Enslaved people remained excluded, and free people of African descent faced particular restrictions in the system of representation. This made the promise of an empire-wide political nation deeply contested.

As war spread, independence movements emerged across Spanish America, but their trajectories differed. In regions of New Spain, the insurgencies associated with Miguel Hidalgo and José María Morelos involved powerful popular mobilization and questions of caste, land, and social hierarchy. In parts of South America, military campaigns associated with Simón Bolívar and José de San Martín intersected with local political conflict, Indigenous communities, enslaved and free Black populations, and rival visions of republics or monarchies.

It is tempting to tell this story through a handful of heroic liberators. They mattered. But the term liberation does not specify who received land, who could vote, or whether coerced labor disappeared. Some American leaders promised emancipation, recruited soldiers through appeals to freedom, or drew support from Haiti. Others feared social revolution. Governments after independence had to manage conflicts between provinces, regional elites, popular movements, and military commanders. The political order remained unsettled long after imperial capitals lost formal authority.

Brazil illustrates still another path. The Portuguese royal court relocated to Rio de Janeiro during the Napoleonic wars. Brazil declared independence in 1822 under a monarchy, not a republic; slavery persisted for decades. A single century's revolutionary map therefore includes republics, constitutional experiments, restored monarchies, and a new imperial monarchy. None can be understood simply as a late copy of the United States or France.

## A wider world, not an exclusively Atlantic origin of politics

This lecture concentrates on the Atlantic because its revolutions were entangled through oceanic warfare, empire, trade, migration, enslavement, and print. We should not confuse that geographic frame with the whole world.

Elsewhere, powerful states and communities debated authority in their own political vocabularies. Ottoman institutions faced reform struggles, fiscal pressures, and military challenges. The Qing empire governed diverse populations through layered administrative systems and confronted internal rebellion. South Asian political worlds changed as regional states and the East India Company competed for power. Political legitimacy was not discovered in eighteenth-century Europe and exported unchanged to the rest of humanity.

The comparison is useful precisely when it is limited. A European declaration of natural rights and a claim to dynastic legitimacy in East Asia might both ask who has lawful authority, but the answer cannot be extracted from a single universal schema. Institutions, languages, and histories matter. The analytical task is to compare problems without assuming that everyone was working toward the same constitution.

## The questions that survive a declaration

Return to the imagined town meeting. Representatives proclaim that sovereignty belongs to the people. Now take away the dramatic moment of proclamation. The assembly must decide who can vote in its next election. It must decide who may own land. It must decide whether to protect a plantation owner's claim over other people's labor. It must decide whether a nearby Indigenous nation has political rights of its own. It must decide who supplies the army and pays its debts. It must decide whether the assembly can be overruled by a general claiming to save the revolution.

Those decisions are the revolution too. Independence may remove one ruler while leaving older hierarchies in place; it may also open a space in which excluded people can insist that the stated principles be made more consistent. Rights can be a radical language even when institutions do not honor them. But the language must not be mistaken for its own implementation.

When historians call these events an Age of Revolutions, the phrase names connected conflicts, not a single historical personality. It encompasses emancipation and its enemies, democratic experiments and military dictatorships, anticolonial struggles and settler colonial expansion. It includes arguments made in formal declarations and decisions made by people whose words were never preserved in a library.

The best question to carry into the next history is therefore not whether a revolution was truly free or truly democratic according to a single modern test. It is more precise: who claimed the right to rule, who challenged the claim, what resources made the claim effective, and whose lives changed in ways that the declaration alone cannot explain?
