# One Currency, Many Budgets: The Eurozone Crisis, 2009–2015

## A shared currency and separate governments

Imagine a small business in Athens in two thousand and nine. Its rent, wages, and invoices are denominated in euros, the same currency used by firms in Dublin, Lisbon, and Berlin. That is useful: exchanging money across borders is no longer a routine obstacle. Yet if the Greek government cannot borrow at an affordable interest rate, it cannot simply instruct its own central bank to print a national currency to cover the gap. Greece belongs to a monetary union. It has national taxes, national elections, and national public services, while decisions about the euro's monetary policy belong to a common central bank. The crisis of the following years exposed how far these institutions had been integrated, and where they had not.

The euro had begun as an accounting currency in nineteen ninety-nine, with notes and coins introduced in two thousand and two. Member states agreed to common rules about deficits and debt, but taxation, most spending, and bank supervision remained substantially national. Banks lent across borders, and financial markets often treated government bonds from different euro-area countries as nearly comparable risks. The assumption rested partly on trust that euro membership itself made severe default or currency problems unlikely. A single interest-rate policy could not fit every local housing market or every national growth pattern. When credit was abundant, these contradictions were easier to ignore.

The global financial crisis of two thousand and seven through two thousand and nine brought private banking losses and recession. Tax revenues fell while unemployment support and rescue costs rose. Governments that had not all made the same fiscal choices nevertheless found themselves borrowing in markets that could suddenly demand far higher interest. The word sovereign means the state itself is the borrower. Sovereign debt becomes a crisis when investors doubt a government's ability or willingness to refinance or repay obligations at affordable rates. Yet a government bond is also an asset held by banks and pension funds. Fear about a state's finances can weaken its banks; fear about the banks can increase the potential burden on the state.

## Greece and the meaning of a rescue

In late two thousand and nine, a new Greek government announced that earlier official deficit figures had seriously understated the gap between public revenues and spending. This damaged confidence already strained by recession and long-standing weaknesses in tax collection, fiscal administration, and competitiveness. Greek bonds became more expensive to issue, and the government could not easily borrow enough on normal terms. In May two thousand and ten, euro-area governments and the International Monetary Fund agreed on the first major Greek assistance program. It was not a gift. It involved loans linked to fiscal adjustment and economic reforms, intended to restore the capacity to borrow and pay.

A rescue package contains several different promises. Creditors promise funding now, so the borrower does not have to repay every maturing debt in the middle of a panic. The government promises changes, often cuts in spending, increases in revenue, asset sales, or labor and administrative reforms. Advocates argue that without a credible plan no one will lend, and the eventual disruption will be worse. Critics ask whether cutting public expenditure and raising taxes during recession will shrink economic activity so much that debt becomes harder, not easier, to service. Both arguments involve the same arithmetic: debt is judged relative to the economy that supports it, and the economy itself responds to policy.

For Greek households, the arithmetic arrived as lost employment, lower incomes, higher taxes, reduced public services, and uncertainty about pensions and care. A family that had never traded government bonds might lose a job when spending contracted or a firm could no longer get credit. Young people faced especially difficult labor markets. Protesters opposing austerity were not simply refusing to understand public finances. They disputed the distribution of losses and the legitimacy of terms decided between elected governments and international lenders. Some people favored reforms but questioned their speed. Others argued that without funding and fiscal change the banking and payment system would face an even sharper collapse.

In two thousand and thirteen, the International Monetary Fund published an evaluation of its first Greek program. It acknowledged that important aims, including restored confidence and growth, had not been achieved, and identified delayed debt restructuring and inadequate attention to political economy among shortcomings. The evaluation is especially revealing because it comes from an institution that helped design the program. It does not prove that every alternative would have worked, nor does it erase the real financing problems confronting Greek governments. It shows why historical explanation must distinguish a policy's stated purpose, the assumptions behind it, and its observed consequences.

## Ireland was not Greece

In Ireland, the crisis traveled through a different starting point. Rapid credit expansion had supported a property boom. Banks became heavily exposed to construction and real estate and relied on international wholesale funding. When global markets froze and property prices fell, private banking losses threatened the national financial system. In September two thousand and eight, the government guaranteed broad categories of bank liabilities, seeking to prevent panic. The decision connected the state's fiscal capacity to banking debts. Ireland later committed enormous resources to bank support and sought international assistance in late two thousand and ten.

A state can become heavily indebted because of past public spending, because a recession reduces revenues, or because it takes on losses that began elsewhere. Those stories overlap but are not interchangeable. Ireland's property collapse differs from Greece's accumulated fiscal and administrative problems. In both places, however, households confronted austerity and unemployment while creditors debated who should absorb losses. The terms of an assistance program are therefore a decision about the future allocation of risk. Should bank bondholders suffer more? Can depositors be protected? How much public money should be spent before a bank is reorganized? The rules for answering these questions were being improvised during the crisis.

Portugal requested assistance in two thousand and eleven after years of weak growth, pressure on financing, and rising borrowing costs. Spain did not enter the same comprehensive sovereign adjustment programs as Greece, Ireland, or Portugal; in two thousand and twelve it sought European support to recapitalize troubled banks. Cyprus later needed a separate program. Grouping these cases together is useful for understanding a monetary union under stress, but careless grouping obscures the very mechanisms we need to see. It also hides the experience of countries that had different conditions and retained more access to ordinary borrowing.

## The loop between a bank and a government

Consider a bank whose balance sheet contains a large quantity of its own government's bonds. If those bonds fall in market value, the bank may appear less solvent. Depositors and lenders become nervous. The government may be expected to rescue the bank, but that rescue makes public debt larger and investors still more nervous about the government. This feedback is often called the sovereign-bank loop. It is not a mystical property of the euro. It follows from identifiable holdings, guarantees, and expectations. A monetary union without a shared bank resolution and supervision framework made the loop especially difficult to manage across borders.

The authorities created temporary rescue facilities in two thousand and ten and subsequently established the European Stability Mechanism, which began operating in two thousand and twelve. Governments and international lenders used these mechanisms to finance assistance programs. The European Central Bank also supplied liquidity to the financial system. These tools were designed to contain immediate panic, but they raised political questions about conditions and oversight. Assistance was negotiated among governments with sharply unequal borrowing costs, and the people affected did not vote for the program line by line. A euro-area parliament existed at the European Union level, but the crisis response still depended heavily on meetings of finance ministers, heads of government, central bankers, and lenders.

On July twenty-sixth, two thousand and twelve, European Central Bank president Mario Draghi publicly declared that, within its mandate, the bank was ready to do what was necessary to preserve the euro. This became famous as the promise to do whatever it takes. Markets paid attention because the central bank could potentially intervene at a scale unavailable to an individual small member state. In September, the bank announced the Outright Monetary Transactions framework for purchases of certain government bonds under conditions. The intervention's credibility did not require every proposed bond purchase to occur. The expectation that a powerful institution might stop a self-reinforcing panic could change investors' behavior. It did not eliminate the underlying disputes about debt and democracy.

The crisis also drove institutional reconstruction. A banking union began to centralize supervision, with the European Central Bank taking up direct supervision of significant banks in November two thousand and fourteen. New resolution rules sought ways of handling failing banks without automatically assigning all losses to taxpayers. Such changes were not minor technical repairs. They shifted decisions from national supervisors toward institutions operating across the currency area. Supporters argued that shared money needed shared safeguards. Critics asked how regulators and central bankers should be held accountable when their decisions affected employment and public budgets. The answer could not be only that experts would produce better forecasts.

## A referendum inside a monetary union

By two thousand and fifteen, Greece's conflict with its creditors had become an open confrontation over sovereignty. The Syriza-led government challenged existing bailout terms and sought debt relief and a different economic course. In a July referendum, voters rejected the proposal put before them. Some understood the vote as resistance to austerity, others as a decision about how to negotiate with lenders; it was not a simple legal vote to leave the euro. Bank closures and controls on withdrawals limited access to cash while negotiations continued. A referendum can express a democratic preference but cannot by itself make creditors provide funding or restore banking liquidity.

Greece subsequently agreed to a third assistance program with additional conditions. That sequence is a stark example of the distinction between electoral authority and control over financing. Supporters of the agreement argued that it preserved euro membership and prevented a more disorderly collapse. Opponents argued that it overrode democratic opposition and prolonged damaging policies. Historians need not choose one slogan as a replacement for analysis. We can trace who made decisions, what constraints they faced, which outcomes were observed, and which alternatives were contested. The referendum expressed political disagreement that did not disappear when a loan agreement was signed.

## Who bears the cost?

The euro-area crisis changed what people meant by solidarity. To creditors in stronger economies, solidarity might mean providing loans that their taxpayers could ultimately be exposed to. To borrowers in countries under adjustment, solidarity might mean not demanding that the weakest households bear the heaviest part of the response. Both were material claims, not merely emotions. Countries differed internally as well. A well-capitalized exporter, a public employee nearing retirement, and an unemployed recent graduate did not experience the crisis in the same way. A national average could conceal changes in access to medicine, housing, education, and the chance of leaving home to find work.

The crisis also exposed limits in the shorthand of responsible and irresponsible nations. Before the crash, global banks and European financial institutions helped finance property markets and state debt across borders. Investors earned returns from lending they later described as dangerous. Fiscal choices mattered, but they were not made in a vacuum outside the financial system. Nor was every spending cut imposed by outsiders; national governments had constituencies and domestic reasons for some measures. The challenge is to reconstruct the decisions without allowing the label of a country to stand in for the many institutions and people living there.

By the middle of the decade, panic had eased in much of the euro area and institutional mechanisms were stronger than they had been in two thousand and nine. Recovery remained uneven and debts were not magically erased. The lesson is not simply that the euro survived. A monetary union links people through a common unit of account but leaves them living under different labor markets, tax arrangements, political coalitions, and public services. In a crisis, the shared currency can turn these differences into bargaining conflicts. The hard historical question is who has the power to decide how the union responds, and who pays for preserving it.
