# When the Currency Falls: The Asian Financial Crisis, 1997–1999

## The money that suddenly left

On July second, nineteen ninety-seven, the Thai government stopped defending the baht at its accustomed exchange rate against the United States dollar. The currency fell. For a visitor exchanging holiday money, that might have meant that a dollar bought more baht. For a Thai company that had borrowed dollars to finance a building, it could mean disaster. The company might earn its revenue in baht while owing a fixed amount of dollars to its lender. When the baht lost value, the local-currency cost of paying the debt rose, even if the company had not bought another machine or borrowed another dollar. This mechanism helped turn a currency crisis into bankruptcies, banking troubles, unemployment, and political upheaval. Understanding the events requires following the balance sheets, and then following the people whose lives those sheets could not adequately record.

The crisis startled observers because much of East and Southeast Asia had experienced decades of rapid growth. Thailand, South Korea, Indonesia, and Malaysia were not identical economies, but their factories, cities, schools, and infrastructure had expanded during the preceding years. In some countries, policies that promoted exports and industrial investment produced substantial reductions in poverty. It would be wrong to describe all those gains as imaginary merely because a later collapse occurred. It would also be wrong to assume that fast growth proved their financial systems were safe. A factory can efficiently produce goods while financing its equipment with debts that must be renewed next month. Physical productivity and financial stability are related, but not the same thing.

Throughout the early nineteen nineties, international investors and banks sent substantial amounts of capital into the region. Some financed useful factories, ports, and communications networks. Other loans supported rapidly rising property values and investment projects unlikely to earn enough to repay them. Many borrowers owed money in foreign currency. Some local banks and firms borrowed at short maturities, planning to renew the loan when it came due. An exchange rate kept close to the dollar could encourage the belief that repayment in dollars would remain affordable. Yet a government promise to stabilize a currency is not an insurance policy against every reversal in international confidence. When creditors decide not to renew the debt, a company can face a crisis even if its building or factory has not physically changed.

## Why a falling currency can make debt grow

Take a simplified example. A business owes one million dollars. Suppose the exchange rate is twenty-five baht per dollar. Paying the principal would require twenty-five million baht. If the baht falls to fifty per dollar, the same dollar debt now costs fifty million baht. The amount of the loan in dollars has not doubled; its burden measured against the company's local revenue has. At the same time, a lender fearing default may refuse an extension, forcing immediate repayment or sale of assets. Other companies, seeing this, try to obtain dollars before the exchange rate falls further. Their purchases of foreign currency add pressure to the exchange rate. A movement that first looked like a change in price becomes a feedback loop involving fear, debt, and bank balance sheets.

Governments with a currency peg can spend reserves buying their own currency to support its price. But reserves are finite, and the market may believe the government will eventually have to let the exchange rate move. Raising interest rates can make holding the currency more attractive; it can also make business loans more expensive and push fragile firms toward failure. Letting the currency float can release pressure on reserves while making foreign debts harder to repay. There was no painless lever waiting in the finance ministry. Policy choices created winners and losers at different times. The crisis was therefore not merely an attack by outside speculators, nor simply the moral failure of local business leaders. It developed from interacting vulnerabilities, expectations, and the rapid movement of money across borders.

Thailand had seen a property boom and rapid expansion of finance companies. As concerns about weak lending and foreign obligations mounted, the government tried to defend its exchange-rate arrangement. The effort failed in July nineteen ninety-seven. The baht depreciated, and troubled financial institutions faced losses and closures. Property projects whose anticipated tenants or buyers could no longer obtain credit stood as a visible reminder of a broader interruption. Construction workers lost projects; small suppliers lost customers; and households faced debt they had incurred during a period when tomorrow's income had seemed likely to be larger than today's. The collapse of a finance company may occupy a line in an official chronology. Its effect on a neighborhood can last considerably longer.

## Several countries, several crises

Pressure spread to the Philippine peso, Malaysian ringgit, and Indonesian rupiah. South Korea became a major center of the crisis later in nineteen ninety-seven. The word contagion can suggest an illness moving automatically between populations, but financial contagion works through decisions: lenders reconsider exposures; foreign investors sell assets; companies scramble for dollars; and officials decide whether to defend exchange rates. Countries may be punished because they share real vulnerabilities, because investors cannot easily distinguish them, or because funds facing losses in one market sell positions in another. The mechanisms vary. A detailed chronology matters more than an assertion that the whole continent had made one mistake.

South Korea's difficulty involved heavily indebted businesses, financial institutions with short-term foreign obligations, and the sudden loss of access to international credit. The government turned to the International Monetary Fund for a large assistance arrangement in December nineteen ninety-seven. But an emergency loan is not the same as making private banks agree to renew their credit. That distinction became decisive. In late December, officials from the United States and other major economies encouraged international creditor banks to roll over short-term loans to Korean banks rather than demanding simultaneous repayment. Refinancing bought time for an economy that could produce goods but could not quickly obtain the dollars required to meet all its approaching obligations. The action showed that the crisis involved decisions by creditors as well as borrowers.

The social impact in Korea was severe. Businesses cut wages and jobs; smaller firms and parts of the financial sector bore heavy losses. In early nineteen ninety-eight, a tripartite commission involving government, business, and labor negotiated elements of the response. Labor law changed to allow dismissal in specified circumstances involving urgent managerial needs. What officials called flexibility could mean a firm surviving a collapse in orders; for employees it could mean the end of an expected career or a sudden fall in household income. Workers disagreed about the terms of reform and about who should carry the burden. The presence of labor representatives did not erase the unequal capacity of employers and employees to endure a long interruption of wages.

South Koreans also organized a highly publicized campaign to collect personal gold and jewelry to support the country's foreign-exchange position. It became an emblem of national solidarity. Yet a donated ring could not repair every failed loan, and patriotic sacrifice was not distributed according to who had taken the greatest financial risks. Economic history needs both the image and the institutional measures: debt extension, banking restructuring, emergency credit, labor negotiations, and the eventual rebound in exports. A narrative that praises national resilience while omitting dismissed workers would be incomplete. So would a narrative of permanent collapse: production and financial conditions did recover, though recovery did not restore every household to its earlier place.

## Indonesia and the fall of a president

Indonesia faced an even more sweeping breakdown. The rupiah lost much of its value; many businesses carried dollar obligations; and collapsing banks helped interrupt production and trade. Rising prices, especially for basic necessities, pressed on households whose wages did not rise in proportion. A drought compounded difficulties in food supply. The government's links with favored enterprises and powerful families weakened confidence that decisions would be made for the public good. When financial institutions could not function and everyday goods became more expensive, an economic crisis became a crisis of political legitimacy. Growth statistics from the preceding decades were real, but they could not protect President Suharto's system from the anger of people facing hunger, job losses, and long-suppressed demands for change.

Students and other protesters demanded reform, and events in May nineteen ninety-eight led to widespread unrest and deadly violence. Chinese Indonesians were targeted in attacks, including looting and sexual violence. Those crimes cannot be explained as a normal consequence of currency depreciation; attackers and political institutions made choices amid the collapse of authority. Suharto resigned on May twenty-first, nineteen ninety-eight, after more than three decades in power. The financial crisis accelerated the end of his rule, but it did not create Indonesia's entire history of authoritarianism, favoritism, or ethnic persecution. The political transition that followed involved activists, students, officials, and communities with different hopes about what reform should accomplish.

Indonesia entered several negotiations with the IMF and other lenders, while the government struggled over bank closures, monetary control, and subsidies. International assistance could supply funds and a framework for restructuring. It could also attach conditions to a government facing exceptionally weak bargaining circumstances. Decisions to remove or alter subsidies could save public resources while immediately raising the price of items people could scarcely afford. A bank closure intended to restore confidence could instead persuade depositors that other banks were unsafe. The fact that a policy has a plausible economic rationale does not mean its timing or sequencing works. As officials revised their programs, Indonesians faced the consequences before professional economists reached agreement about what should have been done first.

## An international lender under argument

The IMF described the crisis in terms of financial weaknesses, capital outflows, corporate debt, and policy failures. Its programs generally sought to restore confidence, stabilize currencies, and restructure vulnerable financial systems. Critics argued that demanding high interest rates and rapid fiscal restraint during collapsing output could deepen recession, and that some structural conditions reached beyond the immediate emergency. Defenders replied that unstable currencies and banks required credible action and that many reforms addressed long-standing weaknesses. Historians should not turn this into a debate in which one side knows that every policy must fail and the other knows that every prescription must work. The record contains changing diagnoses, revised programs, distinct national conditions, and disagreements that continued after recovery.

Malaysia took a notably different path. After pressure on the ringgit and financial system, its government introduced capital controls and fixed the exchange rate to the dollar in September nineteen ninety-eight. The measures were intended to reduce rapid capital flight and give policymakers greater freedom to lower interest rates and stimulate domestic activity. They were accompanied by measures to repair banks and corporations. Malaysia's recovery later became part of the debate about whether temporary restrictions on capital movements were compatible with economic stability. Evidence can show that controls helped create room for some policies without proving that controls alone caused every improvement. Other things were changing too, including international demand and conditions across regional markets.

The policy alternatives matter because they challenge the idea that integration into international finance must always have a single institutional design. A government may welcome foreign investment and still restrict a particular flow under emergency conditions. A country may rely on trade with the world while questioning whether banks should borrow enormous short-term sums in foreign currency. But capital controls are not a universal remedy: they can be poorly designed, circumvented, or used to protect politically connected firms. They also require administrative capacity and public trust. Malaysia's experience is evidence for asking which instruments were available to governments, not a promise that the same solution would deliver identical outcomes elsewhere.

## Work, schools, and the cost of adjustment

In official accounts, recovery can appear as the moment a currency stops falling or industrial production begins to rise. For a family, the recovery may arrive later. A parent who loses work may withdraw savings, borrow from relatives, sell property, or postpone medical care. A student may continue school but with less money for transport, fees, or books. A household with migrant relatives may depend more heavily on money sent from abroad, provided the relatives themselves have retained employment. None of these responses is an invented case history of a particular person; they are ways that falling earnings and rising prices transmit an aggregate crisis into family budgets. The distribution of costs depends on wealth, access to public services, informal support networks, and the capacity to move or find new work.

The crisis also changed politics. Some governments introduced or expanded social protection, others reduced certain subsidies, and labor organizations contested the terms of restructuring. In Indonesia, it helped bring down an authoritarian president. In Korea, it became an occasion for financial reform and contentious decisions about labor markets. These events are sometimes grouped under the tidy phrase structural adjustment. That phrase does not tell a listener whether the adjustment involved a bank, an employer, a household, or a ministry, or whether the person facing it had any say. The historian's job is to move between scales and identify the people and organizations that made the choices.

By the end of the decade, several affected economies had begun recovering, and South Korea in particular returned to strong growth. The experience encouraged changes in reserve management, banking oversight, and the way officials thought about foreign-currency borrowing and short-term debt. It also prompted continuing arguments about creditor responsibility and international financial institutions. The crisis did not demonstrate that markets were always unstable or that government intervention always solved problems. It demonstrated that prosperity financed through fragile obligations could unwind much faster than ordinary economic planning assumed. When the price of one currency moved, it changed the value of debts, the willingness of banks to lend, the security of jobs, and sometimes the survival of governments. That is what made the Asian financial crisis a central event in the history of globalization.
