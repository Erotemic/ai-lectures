# The Price of a Barrel: Oil, Money, and Environmental Politics in the 1970s

## A line for fuel and a question about power

In the winter of nineteen seventy-three and nineteen seventy-four, drivers in parts of the United States faced long lines at gasoline stations. Fuel shortages were visible in daily routines: commuting, deliveries, family budgets and the price of a journey. But the familiar photograph of a queue does not show the whole history. The crisis was connected to an oil market negotiated across continents, war in the Middle East, the rising influence of petroleum-producing states, and a monetary system already under strain. It was also experienced differently by an oil-importing household, a refinery worker, and a producing country seeking a greater share of revenue from its own ground. Those differences are the subject of this lecture.

Oil was deeply embedded in the postwar economy. It supplied energy for transport, factories and heating, and served as feedstock for plastics, chemicals and fertilizers. A country could be rich in industrial machinery while dependent on imported fuel. A producer might possess large reserves yet receive only a negotiated fraction of the value earned further down the supply chain. The companies that discovered, extracted, transported and sold oil operated across borders, often using agreements developed under colonial or unequal conditions. The question of who controlled prices was therefore linked to the question of who could make political choices in a formally sovereign state. This helps explain why petroleum policy became a language of independence.

A useful distinction is between possessing a resource and controlling the conditions of its extraction. A government might own the land beneath a field but depend on a foreign company's engineers, financing, tankers and access to consumers. Changing that arrangement could require negotiation, nationalization, technical training and the ability to bear economic risks. A higher price could fund public services or public construction, but it could also enrich a narrow set of decision-makers. We cannot infer popular prosperity from a national revenue statistic alone. The debates over oil brought together economists' questions about trade with anticolonial movements' questions about sovereignty and workers' questions about how the resulting wealth would be distributed.

Oil shocks were a dramatic expression of a longer change, not a bolt from nowhere. During the nineteen fifties and sixties, postwar industrial growth increased demand for energy. Producing states considered how to improve the terms on which international companies purchased and sold their resources. Meanwhile the United States, Europe and Japan built economic arrangements that assumed continued access to energy at prices their industries could afford. When a diplomatic crisis disrupted this system, it exposed dependencies created during earlier years of seemingly ordinary growth. The next part of the story begins before the embargo, among petroleum-producing governments attempting to organize their interests.

## The countries that organized to negotiate

In September nineteen sixty, representatives of Iran, Iraq, Kuwait, Saudi Arabia and Venezuela met in Baghdad and founded the Organization of the Petroleum Exporting Countries, OPEC. The organization's own history names five figures who took part in its founding, among them Juan Pablo Pérez Alfonzo of Venezuela and Abdullah al-Tariki of Saudi Arabia. They did not represent identical domestic political systems or development priorities. Their shared concern was that producer countries needed collective influence in a market dominated by major multinational oil companies. It was a new form of cooperation across regions: an oil producer in South America negotiating common interests with producers in the Middle East.

OPEC's establishment was part of a wider postcolonial argument about permanent sovereignty over natural resources. The United Nations General Assembly took up that principle in Resolution eighteen oh three, adopted in December nineteen sixty-two. Its language connected control of resources with national development and the well-being of people. Such a resolution expressed a claim about rights. It did not automatically alter every contract or resolve how states would compensate private owners, attract investment, and distribute revenue. For governments trying to change older concessions, however, it provided an international vocabulary through which they could present their actions as a matter of sovereignty rather than merely a dispute with one company.

Producer-country strategies were not confined to setting a price. Some governments sought larger ownership shares or national control over extraction, others more favorable taxes and contracts, and others expanded public development. These choices changed relationships with companies and importing countries. They could also generate disputes within producer societies: which region should benefit from a field, what environmental damage was acceptable, and how much revenue should fund public health or schools? OPEC's official founding story gives a clear account of its stated objectives, but it should be read as an organization's own explanation of itself. It cannot settle all of those domestic conflicts or tell us how a poor household experienced the resulting policies.

It is important to keep two names distinct. OPEC included Arab and non-Arab oil producers and was founded in nineteen sixty. The Organization of Arab Petroleum Exporting Countries, OAPEC, was a different group, whose members later organized measures during the nineteen seventy-three Arab–Israeli War. The distinction matters because people often attribute the October embargo simply to OPEC as a whole. That erases the specific diplomatic choices of Arab producers and the different positions of other oil-exporting states. It also obscures why the political targets of an embargo were not the same as the commercial and monetary effects of higher prices across the world.

## Money before the embargo

Another change took place before the fuel queues. After the Second World War, the Bretton Woods monetary system linked currencies to the U.S. dollar, and the dollar was convertible into gold at a set official price for foreign monetary authorities. By the late nineteen sixties, dollar claims abroad and pressures from inflation made the system increasingly difficult to maintain. In August nineteen seventy-one, President Richard Nixon suspended the conversion of dollars into gold and introduced domestic wage and price controls. The initial announcement was meant to defend the U.S. economy, but it had international consequences. A country holding dollars or pricing exports in dollars now faced a changing relationship between its revenue, exchange rates and purchasing power.

The gold-window closure did not instantaneously produce a stable new monetary order. Governments tried to restore exchange-rate arrangements, but by nineteen seventy-three major currencies had moved toward floating against one another. The transition mattered for trade and debt. A company making a long-term contract, a government borrowing abroad, and an oil producer receiving dollars could no longer assume that those dollars would have a fixed relationship to gold or other currencies. Monetary change is easy to narrate as an abstract problem among central bankers. In practice it altered the uncertainties under which businesses ordered equipment, governments financed imports, and households faced prices.

Economic tensions were already appearing in the United States before the major oil price increases. Inflation had risen through the late nineteen sixties, and policymakers disagreed about the proper response. Historian of monetary policy Lutz Kilian has pointed out that the surge in American inflation cannot be explained by oil alone, because the increase began well before the first embargo. This is not an argument that petroleum was irrelevant. Energy prices could sharply raise the cost of transport, manufacturing and heating. But a good causal explanation also examines monetary policy, wages, productivity, other commodity shortages and expectations. A single photograph of expensive gasoline cannot explain why many prices moved over several years.

The oil exporters faced their own monetary problem. International petroleum contracts were commonly denominated in dollars. When the dollar's external value changed, a producer could find that a given number of dollars bought fewer imported machines or other goods. Price negotiations therefore involved both the market for energy and the value of the money in which energy was priced. These were not identical causes, and states did not agree on a single remedy. But the coincidence of energy dependence and monetary instability meant that the world economy entered the nineteen seventies with vulnerabilities in more than one system.

## October 1973, and what the embargo did

In October nineteen seventy-three, Egypt and Syria launched attacks against Israeli-held territories, beginning another Arab–Israeli war. As the United States moved to resupply Israel, Arab oil producers sought to use petroleum supply as leverage. Contemporary U.S. intelligence documents record a decision by OAPEC on October seventeenth to cut production, while the targeted embargo against the United States followed days later. These measures were connected but not identical. Production cuts could affect overall supply; an embargo also selected particular importing countries. The governments involved had different interests and options, so the measures were neither a single uniform tap being turned off nor a decision by every oil producer in the world.

The effects traveled beyond the countries formally targeted. Prices rose dramatically in world markets, partly as producers exercised more control over pricing and partly because buyers and sellers responded to uncertain supply. The Federal Reserve's historical essay describes prices climbing from around three dollars a barrel before the embargo to more than eleven dollars in early nineteen seventy-four. Those figures represent a broad market development, not the price of every shipment in every contract. Producers gained revenue per barrel under new terms, and importers faced larger bills. For an industrial economy built around imported fuel, the same quantity of energy now cost more foreign exchange. For a lower-income importing country, this could squeeze already scarce resources for other imports.

The embargo ended in March nineteen seventy-four, but prices did not simply return to their earlier level. That persistence shows why the crisis should not be treated as a short interruption in an otherwise unchanged order. The relative bargaining power of producers and importers had shifted. Foreign-policy commitments, production strategies and company contracts had been renegotiated. The photographs of queues at fuel pumps are real evidence of a crisis, but they are not evidence that an embargo alone created every economic difficulty of the decade. Rising food prices, prior inflation and monetary decisions also influenced what households and governments experienced.

Economists often use the word stagflation for a painful combination of weak output or employment growth with continuing price inflation. In the nineteen seventies, policymakers confronted forms of this problem that challenged confident assumptions about how unemployment and inflation should relate. An oil shock can raise costs while reducing the real purchasing power of energy importers, creating pressure in both directions. But its consequences depend on wage bargaining, monetary responses, expectations and existing economic conditions. Even within one country, an energy producer, a bus driver, and a household heating a small apartment could experience the same price change very differently. Macro-level explanation becomes stronger when it distinguishes a national average from distribution among groups.

## The claim for a different world economy

The crisis strengthened a wider demand: countries that had emerged from colonial domination should have more influence over the rules of trade, finance, raw materials and development. Non-Aligned governments had been debating those issues for years. A major political declaration from their meeting in Algiers in nineteen seventy-three helped set the agenda for a special United Nations session. In May nineteen seventy-four, the General Assembly adopted the Declaration on the Establishment of a New International Economic Order. The name was ambitious because the claim was ambitious. Independence should include the capacity to negotiate economic terms rather than accept an international system constructed when many countries had still been colonies.

The declaration spoke about sovereign equality, resource rights, cooperation, development and disparities between richer and poorer countries. It was not a magic contract that redistributed income the following morning. It was a political document adopted by states with varied interests, and implementation required concrete agreements often fiercely disputed. Nevertheless, it records a moment when governments from Africa, Asia, Latin America and elsewhere insisted that the existing order itself was open to negotiation. This differed from treating development as a technical matter in which experts from wealthy countries simply advised others how to catch up. The declaration proposed that the rules of the relationship also mattered.

Oil exporters supplied a visible example of what collective action by producer states could achieve, but oil was not a universal model. A country exporting a crop that could be stored only briefly could not necessarily control supply in the same way. Minerals, food, industrial products and energy had different production structures, competitors and demand. Nor did the availability of higher public revenue settle domestic questions about democracy or inequality. The New International Economic Order united states around demands that did not guarantee agreement over taxation, investment, ownership or political participation within those states. The global conversation was important precisely because it exposed how development economics and political independence could no longer be kept in separate compartments.

From the perspective of an importing household, a sudden price increase could be damaging; from a producer's perspective, an earlier price might have been a sign of exploitation. Both experiences could be real. The task is not to decide whose needs vanish from the story, but to ask how bargaining power, institutions and distribution shaped outcomes. A fair historical comparison distinguishes the price of oil, the income of the state, the income of workers, and the public benefits that governments actually delivered. Those quantities may move in different directions. Sovereignty makes choices possible, but does not by itself ensure that the gains of those choices reach everyone.

## The environment becomes an international question

Meanwhile another debate was changing the meaning of economic progress. Rachel Carson's Silent Spring, published in nineteen sixty-two, brought a scientifically informed critique of indiscriminate pesticide use to a wide public in the United States. Pollution disasters, including oil spills and burning rivers, helped turn environmental protection into a political issue. The establishment of the U.S. Environmental Protection Agency in nineteen seventy was part of that history. But we should not tell the global environmental story as though it began with a single American book. Communities had long organized around forests, water, health and land. The particular institutions and languages of the nineteen seventies gave many of those concerns new channels of international recognition.

In June nineteen seventy-two, governments met in Stockholm for the United Nations Conference on the Human Environment. Delegates adopted a declaration of twenty-six principles and a plan of action. The United Nations account notes that the meeting brought into focus the connection between pollution, economic growth, and the well-being of populations with unequal levels of development. That formulation contained real tensions. States seeking industrial expansion worried that environmental restrictions might become new barriers imposed by countries that had already grown wealthy through resource use. Others argued that polluted air and water were immediate harms to workers and poor communities, not luxuries that could wait for prosperity. Environmental policy had become part of international economic politics.

An especially instructive example came from Kenya. In nineteen seventy-seven, Wangari Maathai founded what became the Green Belt Movement under the National Council of Women of Kenya. The movement's own history describes rural women reporting that streams were drying up, firewood was becoming harder to find, and the conditions for feeding families were deteriorating. Tree planting offered a practical response, but the organizing also linked environmental damage with the unequal distribution of power and resources. Here the issue was not a distant ideal of untouched nature. It was the distance a person walked for fuel, the reliability of water, the security of food, and whether local people had authority to improve the land on which they depended.

The contrast between an oil-price negotiation and a tree-planting effort is illuminating. Both concern natural resources, but they operate at different scales and with different immediate goals. A petroleum minister may seek a higher share of national export earnings. A rural organizer may seek trees that help retain soil and provide fuel. One demand can be assessed through contract terms and government accounts; the other through ecological change, household work, and community participation. Neither should be reduced to a symbol. Public policy requires more than praising producers for defending sovereignty or campaigners for protecting nature. It requires asking what actions changed, who controlled them and who benefited.

## The decade that unsettled assumptions

By the late nineteen seventies, the assumptions supporting early postwar economic optimism had been shaken. Energy could become politically scarce even when oil existed underground. A reserve currency could change value and alter international contracts. Industrial expansion could generate pollution and resource disputes, while development projects continued to promise better lives. The same decades also showed new organizing capacities: oil-producing states coordinating demands, nonaligned countries pressing for international economic reform, scientists and community groups identifying environmental harms. These were not all parts of a single political movement. Their strategies could collide. But they were responses to a common observation that economic systems were made through decisions, not delivered by nature.

What would count as a successful response? A government might celebrate higher oil revenues or lower inflation. A household might ask whether the rent, bus fare and food bill were manageable. A region dependent on mining or drilling might ask about employment and contamination. A farmer might want secure water, soil and access to markets. The evidence needed to answer those questions differs. We should be cautious with retrospective accounts that treat the nineteen seventies as the victory of a single economic theory, because the period combined multiple crises and multiple proposed solutions. A complete evaluation would need national histories, international data, and testimony from people living through the changes.

The lesson of these years is not that prices matter more than ideals, or that ideals can override economic constraints. It is that the price of fuel, the ownership of resources, the value of a currency and the quality of a local environment are all decisions about whose needs the economy is organized to serve. The queues at gasoline stations became an enduring image because they interrupted ordinary expectations. But the larger interruption was intellectual and political: people increasingly understood that the postwar economic order was neither permanent nor equally beneficial to everyone. It could be challenged by new states, by social movements and by material limits. What would replace it remained an open struggle.

## Source method and unresolved questions (display only)

Official state, military and organizational records establish what their makers stated, ordered and publicized, but must not be mistaken for comprehensive records of social outcomes. Participant accounts are situated voices rather than statistical samples. Where only an abstract, excerpt, or archive index was accessible, the limitation is explicit. Interpretations and contested causal accounts are distinguished from documentary chronology. No fixed length or duration rule applies to this lecture.
