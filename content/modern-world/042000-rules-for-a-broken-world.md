# Rules for a Broken World: The United Nations, Rights, and Postwar Economic Order

## An institution can be both an answer and a compromise

The people planning the world after 1945 were not starting from a blank sheet. They had seen the failure of the League of Nations to prevent another global war, the Depression’s pressure on trade and currencies, mass killing organized by states, and the displacement of entire populations. They also represented governments that wanted security, markets, prestige, and freedom to decide their own affairs. The resulting institutions were therefore built out of a genuine desire to prevent catastrophe and a series of political bargains among unequal powers. The question is not whether the postwar system was pure idealism or simply domination. It is how rules meant to bind states could be created by states that wanted some freedom from those rules.

The first distinction is between three things often grouped together under the phrase “the international order.” There are rules for the conduct of governments toward other governments. There are claims about what governments owe individual people, including people within their own borders. And there are mechanisms for managing money, investment, and trade among economies. Peace, rights, and prosperity connect, but a Security Council resolution is not a human-rights trial, and a balance-of-payments loan is not food relief. Each institution has its own authority, membership, scope, and limits. Confusing them makes international cooperation look either all-powerful or entirely meaningless.

## The United Nations and the permanent power bargain

Representatives of fifty countries met in San Francisco from April to June 1945 and signed the United Nations Charter on June 26. Poland joined them later among the original fifty-one member states. The organization officially came into being on October 24, after required ratifications. Those dates are important because the negotiations occurred while the war in Asia continued and before the world’s new borders had been settled. The United Nations was not a prize handed out at the end of peace. It was being constructed by wartime states to try to shape the peace they expected to follow.

The Charter sets out purposes that include maintaining peace, developing friendly relations among nations, and promoting cooperation on economic, social, and human-rights problems. It also articulates the sovereign equality of member states and restrictions on the threat or use of force. Yet the design made a sharp distinction between the General Assembly, where members could debate and vote, and the Security Council, where a small group would have special power over decisions about international security. The founders were not ignorant of that tension. They wanted an organization in which the major wartime powers would participate, rather than another institution that the strongest states might ignore when it mattered most.

Five permanent members—China, France, the Soviet Union, Britain, and the United States—received a veto over the Council’s substantive decisions. The precise voting rules and Council size have changed since 1945; the original Council had eleven members, whereas the present body has fifteen. The essential bargain remains recognizable: universal language of state equality coexists with privileged powers for a few states. The veto was defended as a practical condition for keeping the major powers inside the collective-security system. It also allowed a permanent member to block action even when many other governments disagreed. A reader need not endorse or condemn the entire United Nations to recognize that this institutional design creates both durability and paralysis.

Who had a seat? Many societies under colonial rule were not independent member states. British India participated in the founding framework before its own 1947 independence, but that did not mean every colonized people held sovereign control of its foreign policy. The Charter’s chapter on non-self-governing territories asked administering powers to promote inhabitants’ well-being and develop their self-government. It did not abolish colonial government at a stroke. Anticolonial movements could use the Charter’s language to press claims, but they also had to confront an organization whose membership and enforcement mechanisms were negotiated largely among existing governments. Universal vocabulary expanded more quickly than political equality.

## Can a government official be tried for a crime?

After the war, another principle was being tested: a senior official might bear criminal responsibility for acts undertaken under the cover of state authority. The International Military Tribunal at Nuremberg formally opened on November 20, 1945, with judges and prosecutors from the United States, Britain, the Soviet Union, and France. Its charges addressed crimes against peace, war crimes, crimes against humanity, and associated conspiracy allegations. The tribunal did not invent all the ideas behind international legal accountability, and it did not settle every problem in this field. It made a concrete claim that the violence of government and war could be judged under law rather than treated solely as an internal sovereign matter.

That claim immediately raised difficult questions. The tribunal was established by the victorious powers and judged leaders of defeated Germany; it was not a court applying identical scrutiny to every belligerent’s wartime conduct. The law also had to distinguish between participation in a government and individual criminal responsibility for specified acts. Critics argued about retroactivity and victor’s justice. Supporters argued that systematic aggression and mass murder could not simply become lawful because a state had organized them. The importance of Nuremberg lies partly in this disagreement: demanding legal accountability for power does not eliminate the need to scrutinize who writes the rules, who is tried, and whose suffering the proceedings include.

Raphael Lemkin, a Polish-Jewish jurist whose work was shaped by the destruction of communities, had campaigned to establish genocide as a category of international law. The Genocide Convention was adopted by the United Nations General Assembly on December 9, 1948, and entered into force in January 1951. Its legal definition is more specific than the everyday use of the word: it concerns certain acts committed with intent to destroy, in whole or in part, a national, ethnic, racial, or religious group as such. This is not a definition of every large massacre or every atrocity. Legal precision matters because it changes what must be proved, even when victims’ suffering remains equally real outside a particular legal category.

The convention declared genocide a crime under international law in peace as well as war and required contracting states to prevent and punish it. That was a statement of responsibility, not a mechanism guaranteed to stop the next crime. Its implementation depended on governments and subsequent legal institutions. Nor was the convention drafted by Lemkin alone: diplomats, legal experts, and delegates debated its text and boundaries. Crediting his formulation and advocacy helps identify an intellectual contribution, while recognizing a wider institutional process explains why the adopted definition differs from every aspiration its proponents might have held.

## Whose rights become universal?

On December 10, 1948, the United Nations General Assembly adopted the Universal Declaration of Human Rights in Paris. It was a declaration of principles rather than a treaty directly binding every state as a ratified contract. Yet its breadth was important. It addressed civil and political rights as well as social and economic claims: freedom from arbitrary detention, political participation, work, education, and an adequate standard of living. It did not ask a government to define who deserved human dignity before making the claim. Its basic assertion was that human beings possess rights that government should respect rather than grant as a reward for loyalty or citizenship.

The drafting history was more international than a story centered only on Eleanor Roosevelt, who chaired the Human Rights Commission. Hansa Mehta of India is widely credited by the United Nations with changing a formulation about “all men” to “all human beings.” Minerva Bernardino of the Dominican Republic advocated language making equality between women and men explicit. Begum Shaista Ikramullah of Pakistan pressed for equal rights in marriage and protection against forced and child marriage. These are not minor editorial footnotes. A supposedly universal statement has to make choices about whose experience counts, and women delegates from multiple regions challenged omissions that could have narrowed its meaning.

The Declaration also emerged under the shadow of decolonization. If all people had rights, why should colonized populations lack meaningful power to choose their governments? If sovereignty protected a state from interference, what happened when a state violated the rights of its own residents? Neither contradiction could be resolved in a single vote. The Declaration supplied language to reformers, activists, and opposition groups, but the practical ability to secure rights depended on institutions, collective action, and political struggle. This does not make the statement empty. It means that a document can articulate a standard against which people judge the state without having the capacity to enforce that standard by itself.

## The right to seek refuge and the right not to be returned

The problem of postwar refugees exposed the difference between announcing rights and arranging protection. Earlier international organizations had already helped some refugee groups; after 1945, relief and resettlement organizations handled the immediate crisis, and the United Nations created the High Commissioner for Refugees in 1950. In 1951, a convention defined responsibilities toward people who met its refugee criteria. One of its central principles is non-refoulement, meaning that qualifying refugees should not be sent back into serious threats to life or freedom. This is more precise than a broad promise that anyone who wishes to move will be accepted by any country.

The original convention had limits shaped by the immediate postwar world. Its definition referred to events occurring before January 1, 1951, and permitted states to choose a geographically European interpretation. The 1967 Protocol later removed the time restriction and the optional geographic limit for parties to that protocol. Thus the protections we may associate today with a global refugee system did not arrive complete and universal in the summer of 1945. They developed through treaties, enforcement decisions, and the pressure of new refugee crises, including those produced by decolonization. Legal rules do not repair displacement on their own, but they can change whether a person is simply subject to expulsion or has a claim recognized across borders.

## Money is also a peace settlement

Economic planning began before the war ended. In July 1944, delegates from forty-four countries met at Bretton Woods in New Hampshire. They were trying to avoid a repetition of the financial disorders that had followed the First World War and deepened the Depression: volatile exchange arrangements, interrupted payments, competitive restrictions, and fragile international lending. The conference designed the International Monetary Fund and the International Bank for Reconstruction and Development, the original World Bank. The proposals were negotiated amid wartime alliances and power imbalances; they were not laws of nature discovered by economists. They were institutional responses to failures of an earlier economic order.

The IMF’s central tasks included encouraging stable exchange arrangements, consultation about monetary problems, and financing to help members cope with short-term external payments strains under specified conditions. The reconstruction-and-development bank had a different purpose: mobilizing capital and financing productive investment, first with postwar reconstruction prominent and then with development as a continuing mission. The distinction matters. A country struggling to make international payments is not identical to a country needing a dam, a railway, or a reconstruction loan. Both institutions depend on membership, terms, and governance. Neither should be mistaken for a universal relief agency that simply hands money to every country damaged by war.

The Bretton Woods system placed the U.S. dollar at the center of a framework of adjustable but generally fixed exchange rates, with the dollar convertible into gold for official purposes under the arrangements of the period. Its architects included John Maynard Keynes on the British side and Harry Dexter White in the American negotiations, with other delegations also participating. The United States’ financial strength gave it immense influence, and the distribution of resources and political representation was unequal. Some of the world’s peoples were still colonized; even when delegations came from territories such as British India, their participation did not imply complete political independence. An economic institution can stabilize transactions while leaving deep questions about who sets priorities and who bears adjustment costs.

Trade was the third intended element. In 1947, twenty-three governments signed the General Agreement on Tariffs and Trade, or GATT, to establish tariff concessions and rules discouraging some restrictive practices. It was initially meant to operate alongside a fuller proposed International Trade Organization, whose Havana Charter never came into effect. GATT therefore survived as the principal multilateral trade framework until the World Trade Organization was established much later. The path was less tidy than the familiar picture of three perfectly planned postwar pillars. A temporary agreement became durable partly because the more ambitious organization failed. Institutions often endure in forms their designers considered provisional.

Why make trade part of peace? Policymakers remembered the interwar collapse of international economic cooperation and feared that closed markets and financial instability could intensify hostility. But the resulting rules did not ensure equal bargaining power, end colonial extraction, or prevent disputes about development strategies. Countries differed in their reliance on commodity exports, industrial protection, loans, and access to foreign currency. The stated pursuit of stability is distinct from proof that every group gained. As decolonized states entered these arrangements, some would insist that formal independence without control over trade and development policy left a great deal of economic power outside their borders.

## A world of imperfect rules

The postwar settlements created principles with real reach: governments should not wage aggressive war; officials can be accountable for specified crimes; persons have rights independent of the preferences of rulers; refugees can have legal claims against forcible return; economic cooperation deserves institutional support. They also gave exceptional powers to major states, preserved much of the world’s colonial structure in 1945, and relied on states to carry out standards that might constrain those very states. A contradiction within an institution does not prove every part of it worthless. It identifies where the design needs explanation and where later struggles will be fought.

Perhaps the most useful way to see this world is to imagine two documents lying on a desk. One says what a government has agreed it will do. The other is a petition, made by someone insisting that the government live up to that promise. The first document can make the petition possible, while the second reveals that the promise has not been kept. That relationship marks much of international politics after 1945. The meaning of the United Nations, human-rights law, refugee protection, and the economic institutions cannot be measured only by founding ceremonies or by later failures. It lies also in the ways people have used those rules, contested their exclusions, and forced governments to explain why universal words have not yet produced universal treatment.
