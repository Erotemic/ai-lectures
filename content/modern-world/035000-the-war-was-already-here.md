# The War Was Already Here: China and the Long East Asian Conflict

## The date on the other side of the globe

When people are asked when the Second World War began, a familiar answer is September 1939, the month Germany invaded Poland. That answer identifies the beginning of the European war that Britain and France entered. It does not identify the beginning of prolonged mass warfare in East Asia. China and Japan were fighting a major war by July 1937, and Japanese troops had taken Manchuria in 1931. Neither date should be made to disappear because it lies outside a European textbook calendar. We need several dates because there were several connected escalations. The world war acquired its global shape through them; it did not arrive everywhere at the same moment.

To understand the sequence, start with an aggressive military state and a Chinese republic already struggling with profound internal conflicts. Japanese officers based in Manchuria used a local incident as the pretext for expanding their control in 1931. Japan then sponsored Manchukuo, a nominally separate state directed under Japanese power. Calling it independent would conceal the military occupation that made its creation possible. Access to land, industry, transport, and resources mattered to Japanese expansionist planners, but their decisions were also political. They claimed that regional security justified overriding the sovereignty of people already living there. That claim was part of the aggression; it is not an explanation that absolves it.

For China, Manchuria was not a remote border detail. It raised questions about what territory a government could govern, what the nation meant, and whether the existing republican state could survive. Nationalist leaders under Chiang Kai-shek confronted military opponents, regional divisions, economic strains, and a Communist movement they had fought. Their effort to govern the country cannot be judged by pretending those challenges were imaginary. But the challenges also do not excuse policies that harmed civilians or the political suppression of critics. A war history that begins by awarding the future victory to one Chinese party will miss the choices made by millions of people before that future was known.

## The fighting becomes a general war

A clash near the Marco Polo Bridge outside Beijing in July 1937 became the starting point for large-scale hostilities. It was not the first act of aggression in the region, but it marked a new phase: Chinese forces and invading Japanese armies fought openly across major areas of the country. Fighting developed around Shanghai and the lower Yangtze. In a conventional account these names appear as stops on an advancing arrow. But a major city is not an arrow. It is a concentration of warehouses, schools, hospitals, railway lines, families, and people whose work depends on reaching one another. Losing a city changes the daily possibilities of survival even for those who never carry a weapon.

China's Nationalist forces engaged Japan in major conventional battles, taking losses that could not easily be replaced. The historian Richard B. Frank stresses that Chinese resistance during the early years was substantial, and he warns against later narratives that dismiss it by looking backward from the Nationalists' eventual defeat in China's civil war. A force's wartime performance must be assessed against the weapons, training, supplies, terrain, and choices available at that time. Japanese forces possessed major advantages in equipment and the ability to strike from the coast. The sustained Chinese refusal to accept conquest nevertheless prevented Japan from simply ending the war by occupying several important cities.

The Chinese Communist movement also fought Japanese forces, especially through forms of warfare organized around rural areas, although its military strength, methods, priorities, and relations with the Nationalists differed. Describing these organizations as one unified national army would obscure their conflicts. Describing them as only enemies would obscure the real Japanese invasion that each confronted. Their uneasy cooperation did not erase struggles over land, recruitment, local authority, taxation, and the legitimacy of a future Chinese government. That is why a war can be at once an international invasion, a contest over the social order inside a country, and a struggle whose political consequences will outlive the fighting.

## Nanjing: evidence and the duty to name the crime

Japanese forces entered Nanjing in December 1937. The weeks that followed saw mass violence against civilians and surrendered soldiers, including killings and sexual assault. The name Nanjing Massacre identifies the crimes, but it does not convert the people who experienced them into an abstract statistic. We should distinguish a carefully established description of atrocity from claims to have counted each victim with impossible precision. Wartime disruption, contested records, differing geographic boundaries, and the problem of what period is counted all complicate numerical estimates. None of those questions removes the evidence that grave crimes occurred. Responsible historical doubt concerns uncertain details; it is not permission to deny what the surviving record supports.

The Yale Divinity Library preserves letters, reports, diaries, and photographs gathered from American missionaries and other foreigners who remained in the city. Its Nanking Massacre Project explicitly says that this collection cannot provide a comprehensive understanding of events. That limitation narrows what we can infer from the surviving evidence. A letter from a foreign resident can document what that person saw or learned; it cannot describe every neighborhood, every surviving Chinese family, or every act that occurred elsewhere. The archive's finding aid also names individual collections, including the papers and diaries of Minnie Vautrin. A finding aid proves that records exist and where they belong; it should not be mistaken for having read every original diary page.

A listener might ask why historians bother to state the limits of evidence when the atrocity itself is established. Because precision is one of the obligations we owe to the people being studied. If we attribute a sentence to a witness who did not write it, we substitute our invention for their testimony. If we treat a foreign mission archive as the city's complete voice, we allow archival survival to reproduce a hierarchy of whose experience counts. And if we use uncertainty around totals as a pretext to set the crime aside, we replace historical method with an evasion. The correct response is to use the preserved evidence directly while seeking Chinese-authored accounts and scholarship that can correct its blind spots.

## A capital moves; a society moves with it

The Nationalist government relocated inland and made Chongqing its wartime center. This is more than a change of address for a ministry. Officials, factories, students, businesses, and families moved or tried to move into a country of strained transport and uneven resources. Rana Mitter's work emphasizes the immense scale of displacement and material damage that the war imposed. Numerical estimates of refugees vary, and the figures should remain attributed rather than presented as exact household registers. But the scale of movement changes the meaning of the war. A household could lose its livelihood without ever entering the field of a pitched battle. A school could be remade in temporary rooms while its original town remained under occupation.

The consequences of displacement were not evenly distributed. People with savings, official connections, transport, or a place to stay had options that others lacked. A factory transferred inland depended on workers, tools, power, food, and shipping; it did not become productive merely because someone announced that it had moved. A state under invasion had to support soldiers, refugees, administrators, and the supply networks linking them. A weakened tax base and damaged commercial routes made those demands harder. This is a point where economic history meets military history: money and goods do not automatically reach an army because the government can print a larger figure on a note.

Richard Frank argues that the military difficulties China experienced later in the war must be set beside the damage to its revenue-producing regions and the collapse of supply lines. This is a corrective to two easy judgments. One says that because Chinese troops continued fighting, the wartime state must have been adequate. The other says that because corruption and inflation developed, Chinese resistance cannot have been serious. Both are misleading. A government can continue a real struggle while employing harmful policies, mismanaging resources, and losing popular trust. A soldier can fight tenaciously while a village is exhausted by provisioning the same army. Those facts need to be held in view together.

## Occupation is a political system

Behind the military front lay large areas governed or influenced by Japanese occupation authorities and administrations that depended on them. Occupation required roads, supplies, police, taxes, censorship, and local intermediaries. It could exploit preexisting divisions within Chinese politics and turn the necessity of daily survival into pressure to cooperate. A clerk who continued an administrative job, a trader attempting to feed customers, and a political leader who joined a sponsored government did not necessarily possess the same motives or freedom. The historian's task is not to declare every person in occupied territory either a willing supporter or a uniform resister. It is to study the structures that narrowed their choices.

Occupation also changed what people later remembered and what they could safely say. Accounts written after 1945 were shaped by China's civil war, changes of government, public commemorations, and family memory. A veteran's record or a local account might be preserved because it fitted an official narrative, or neglected because it did not. Mitter's discussions of wartime memory caution against treating present-day national stories as transparent reports of the 1930s and 1940s. That does not mean memory is worthless. It means that the time and political circumstances in which testimony is recorded are part of the evidence that historians must examine.

Chinese rural communities experienced the war through requisition, flight, violence, guerrilla activity, and the needs of competing armed organizations. Whether local mobilization was experienced as liberation, exploitation, protection, or all three depended on circumstances that changed over time and place. No short national narrative can reconstruct every village. Yet this complexity is not a reason to remain silent. It is a reason to ask which local sources underlie a large claim. When historians debate the comparative military contribution of Nationalist and Communist forces, they are also debating how to weigh conventional battles, rural organizing, guerrilla actions, and the political effects of war. Those are different measures, not a single scoreboard.

## Supply lines and the widening of the war

China's resistance drew on outside support, but foreign aid did not arrive through a door that stayed permanently open. Japanese control of Chinese coastal regions sharply constrained trade. The Burma Road connected southwest China to another route for supplies until Japan's campaign in Burma cut it in 1942. Aircraft flying supplies over the mountains from India became important, but the quantities carried were small compared with what road and sea networks might move. The route's geography shaped both the movement of forces and the options available to civilians. Mountains, weather, fuel requirements, aircraft losses, and available airfields limited military promises. A cargo plane was not only a symbol of international cooperation; it was a very expensive answer to a broken transport system.

Japanese leaders widened the war in search of strategic security and resources, while the United States and European colonial powers grew increasingly involved. By December 1941, attacks on Pearl Harbor and other territories brought new opponents into open war with Japan. But calling this the moment the Pacific War began would leave Chinese people who had been resisting for years outside the definition. Historians use different labels because they may be describing different developments: the long Sino-Japanese War, the Asia-Pacific conflict, or the enlarged coalition war after 1941. The most informative chronology makes these layers visible instead of choosing one starting date and erasing the others.

The new alliances did not eliminate the severe constraints inside China. Military aid depended on other powers' priorities; those powers were fighting in Europe, Africa, the Atlantic, and across the Pacific. Chinese leaders had to negotiate foreign support while managing domestic demands and disagreements. Western military advisers could criticize Chinese decisions without necessarily appreciating what invasion had done to budgets, transport, and local political authority. Chinese actors could criticize their allies in turn. The question is not whether the war became international. It did. The question is how an international military coalition actually functioned when different members had very different exposure to occupation and very different abilities to control the resources on which survival depended.

## Why this history changes the story of the war

In 1945 Japan was defeated, but China's conflict did not end by returning households and political organizations to their positions in 1936. Refugees, shortages, destroyed infrastructure, wartime state policies, and the unresolved struggle between Nationalists and Communists would help shape the years that followed. Historians who begin with the civil war's outcome may read the war only as a path to 1949. Historians who begin with American entry in 1941 may read it only as a preface to the island campaigns. Both starting points miss something. Wartime China's political and social history is central to explaining why people fought, how they survived, and what kind of postwar world remained possible.

There is also a methodological lesson. The remains of a destroyed railway, a government financial record, a witness's letter, a military memoir, and a postwar political speech answer different questions. None automatically has the authority to interpret all the others. The surviving evidence becomes useful when historians explain who produced it, under what conditions, and which lives it could not observe. The gaps are sharpest for people whose testimony did not enter libraries with names attached. Keeping the limits of the archive in view is part of recovering their place in history, not a reason to give up on reconstruction.

The answer to the question with which we began is therefore not a corrected date in isolation. The war in East Asia demands its own history of imperial aggression, organized resistance, internal divisions, civilian flight, atrocity, and international entanglement. In 1931, in 1937, in 1939, and in 1941, new decisions enlarged the war and altered who faced it. To understand the Second World War as genuinely global, we must tell what had already happened before the best-known European and American dates, and we must leave room for the people whose lives did not wait for those dates to appear on a calendar.

## Source method and open research issues (display only)

- No personal testimony, speech, diary extract, dialogue, or private motive has been invented. Analytical comparisons are presented as interpretation, not disguised as documented episodes.
- Documented atrocities and state policies are described with direct institutional and scholarly references; uncertain totals are either attributed and qualified or omitted.
- Archival inventories establish the presence and provenance of documents, not the unexamined contents of those documents. Publisher abstracts establish an author’s stated thesis, not complete access to the work.
- Official statements and legal orders document what authorities said and demanded; testimony and independent inquiry are needed to establish how affected people experienced them.
- Narration and citations are separate so the lectures work in TTS. No word-count or lecture-duration rule has been used.
