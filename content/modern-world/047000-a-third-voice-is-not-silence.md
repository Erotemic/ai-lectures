# A Third Voice Is Not Silence: Bandung and Nonalignment, 1955-1961

## When the meeting itself mattered

In April 1955, delegates from twenty-nine Asian and African countries and territories gathered in Bandung, Indonesia. Some represented states that had only recently become independent. Others spoke for governments whose international standing, borders or domestic legitimacy remained in dispute. Still more people living under colonial rule could not send an independent government at all. To understand why the gathering mattered, imagine the ordinary pattern it challenged. For generations, decisions about Asian and African ports, taxes, borders and wars had been made in European capitals or in meetings dominated by the great powers. In Bandung, representatives of newly empowered governments could set an agenda for themselves. They did not agree about everything. They did agree that Asia and Africa should not be treated simply as the ground upon which other powers settled their disputes.

The conference did not materialize from one leader's invitation. Its five sponsors were Burma, now Myanmar; Ceylon, now Sri Lanka; India; Indonesia; and Pakistan. Their prime ministers had discussed cooperation at Colombo in 1954 and planned a wider meeting at Bogor later that year. Indonesian prime minister Ali Sastroamidjojo was a leading organizer. The Indonesian foreign ministry's museum history describes diplomatic approaches to prospective participants, arguments about whom to invite, and a substantial local effort to provide meeting rooms, hotels, communications and transportation. These prosaic details matter. Political solidarity requires institutions and people willing to perform organizational work. A conference is not the same as an international movement, but without the meeting, the movement has no shared place to make promises or test their meaning.

President Sukarno opened the conference on April eighteenth with a speech that reached backward and forward at once. He remembered earlier anticolonial gatherings, including the League Against Imperialism meeting in Brussels in 1927, and insisted that independent Asian and African governments no longer needed to meet on another continent just to be heard. He also described the responsibilities that followed independence: having defeated colonial rule, a government still had to decide what to build. The speech named racism, economic insecurity and the danger of another global war. Sukarno's point was not merely that Asia and Africa deserved sympathy. It was that their governments had political judgment to exercise, and that war and nuclear weapons were not questions for Washington and Moscow alone.

Bandung is often remembered through a photograph of leaders, but its roster reveals how uneven decolonization still was. The Gold Coast attended before it became independent Ghana in 1957. The People's Republic of China took part, as did both governments claiming to represent Vietnam. Japan was present, too, only a decade after imperial defeat. The conference included states with different religions, economies and alliances, and it did not represent every people in Africa or Asia. A delegation is not equivalent to an entire society. When participants spoke against colonialism, they were making claims that extended beyond those who held seats. That gave their diplomacy significance, but also raised a question for later decades: whose interpretation of liberation would count when people within a newly independent country disagreed?

## A position is not the absence of a position

In the Cold War, diplomats commonly asked whether a government was in the American camp or the Soviet camp. That question assumed that the central issue was alignment with a superpower. But an Indian, Indonesian or Egyptian government might instead ask how to obtain credit, defend its sovereignty, keep a military base from being imposed on its territory, support anticolonial movements, and avoid becoming a battlefield. Such a government could cooperate with one side on trade, negotiate with the other on diplomacy, and reject an alliance that endangered its own priorities. A policy of nonalignment was therefore not automatically neutrality about every conflict, nor indifference to repression. It could be an assertion of the right to judge issues independently. The participants did not all make that assertion in the same way.

This disagreement appeared inside the conference itself. Ceylon's prime minister, Sir John Kotelawala, criticized domination in Soviet-controlled eastern Europe and argued that opposition to colonialism should not be confined to western empires. China's premier, Zhou Enlai, was trying to ease fears among countries whose governments regarded communism with suspicion. The diplomatic record preserved by the British government describes Zhou changing the tone of his remarks after other leaders challenged communist policies. A British report cannot give us everyone's private intentions, but it establishes that Bandung was not a meeting where countries quietly agreed to overlook their differences. Participants debated the boundaries of the very word colonialism. Their common communiqué was an achievement of negotiation, not evidence of identical views.

The People's Republic of China makes a simple definition of nonalignment especially difficult. Beijing was allied with the Soviet Union in this period, yet China attended Bandung because it also sought relations with neighboring Asian states and recognition as a post-imperial power. Meanwhile some participants belonged to western security arrangements. The result was neither a third military bloc nor an oath that every participant would abandon its other relationships. It was a forum in which governments with opposing strategic positions attempted to define rules for coexistence. This was a different task from organizing an alliance: a coalition asks its members to confront the same adversary, while a conference on coexistence may ask adversaries to restrain themselves.

## A communiqué with material demands

The final communiqué, issued on April twenty-fourth, is often reduced to its ten principles of peaceful coexistence. But those principles were only one portion of the document. Its economic section addressed an urgent practical concern: political independence could coexist with dependence on exports of unprocessed commodities, foreign banks and shipping firms. Delegates wanted technical cooperation, more access to international development finance, regional research institutes, and greater opportunities for Asian and African governments to act together. The document allowed for outside capital rather than demanding complete economic separation. Its goal was to give poorer countries more choices over the terms on which foreign investment and assistance entered their economies.

Consider the problem of a farmer producing cocoa or a mine exporting ore. The producer might work harder and ship more while earning less if the world price collapsed. The Bandung declaration specifically called for cooperation to stabilize demand and prices for primary commodities. It advocated processing raw materials where feasible before export, improving trade among Asian and African states, and examining shipping rates that harmed their exporters. These are not decorative aspirations. They concern who captures the income between a field or mine and a distant consumer. Delegates were trying to shift bargaining power in the world economy. Yet a recommendation in a communiqué could not compel a shipping company or commodities market to behave differently. The difference between a common demand and an implemented institution would remain central.

The document also addressed scientific training and the peaceful uses of atomic energy. This was the decade when nuclear technology was associated with both the destructive capacity of weapons and ambitious proposals for power and medicine. The Asian-African countries wanted access to knowledge and representation in international bodies that might govern its use. The communiqué welcomed work toward an international atomic energy agency, while the peace section called for the prohibition and effective international control of nuclear weapons. There was a tension here, not necessarily a contradiction: countries wanted to use scientific advances without accepting that a handful of powerful governments would monopolize decisions about danger and development. It was an argument about technological sovereignty as much as diplomatic standing.

In its cultural section, the communiqué objected to the suppression of languages and educational opportunities under colonial rule. It urged renewed connections across a region whose scholars, merchants and religious communities had traded ideas long before the modern European empires. The language was expansive and sometimes idealized. Asia and Africa were never culturally unified civilizations, and some states represented at the conference had their own internal minorities facing official pressures. But the proposal carried a concrete implication: students, researchers and artists did not have to reach one another only through former imperial centers. Rebuilding direct intellectual exchange was part of the attempt to change global relationships after empire.

Human rights appeared alongside national sovereignty. The declaration endorsed the purposes of the United Nations Charter and referred to the Universal Declaration of Human Rights. It condemned racial discrimination, and it voiced support for dependent peoples pursuing self-determination. The communiqué discussed Palestine and the rights of the Arab people of Palestine, including implementation of United Nations resolutions. It also gave attention to the positions of Indonesia and North African movements confronting continuing colonial domination. These statements were diplomatic positions, not solutions to the conflicts named. They demonstrate that anticolonial internationalism was built from specific struggles and that participants disagreed about how principles of noninterference and solidarity should apply when governments themselves faced accusations of injustice.

## The ten principles, and what they could not do

The ten principles near the end of the communiqué offered a grammar for a less coercive international order. They recognized national equality, sovereignty, territorial integrity and human rights; opposed interference and aggression; endorsed peaceful settlement; and warned against using collective defense arrangements to serve the special interests of great powers. These ideas were not wholly new. Several echoed the United Nations Charter and earlier diplomatic proposals. Their importance lay in the group asserting them and the circumstances in which they were repeated. States historically treated as junior partners were claiming an equal right to pronounce rules of world politics. The principles also imposed a standard on those who signed: if sovereignty mattered, the sovereignty of one's neighbors mattered too.

A principle is easy to applaud while it favors your own country. The difficulty comes when another government invokes the same principle against you. Participants had territorial disputes, different views of communism, and different attitudes toward existing military treaties. Several remained economically reliant on powers whose military strategies they distrusted. The Bandung declaration did not create a court that could rule on every disagreement, nor a treasury that could fund every development promise. Its own text specified that consultation among its members was not intended to form a regional bloc. It is therefore misleading to call Bandung the formal establishment of the later Non-Aligned Movement. It helped make later cooperation imaginable; it was not that later organization.

The vocabulary of the Third World also requires care. Later, it was often used as if it referred simply to poor countries. In the Cold War setting it could carry a political meaning: countries refusing to have their futures determined solely by the contest between two armed camps. That did not mean they shared one economic system or had equal power. It also did not erase profound inequalities within them. We learn more by asking what particular leaders and organizers meant when they claimed independent judgment than by treating a geographic label as an explanation. Bandung's achievement was to make that claim audible across regions, even when the people making it did not agree about everything else.

## From Bandung to Belgrade

The first summit of governments formally identifying as nonaligned met at Belgrade in September 1961, with Yugoslavia joining the Asian and African founders of the earlier diplomatic conversation. Among the prominent leaders associated with the movement were Tito of Yugoslavia, Nehru of India, Nasser of Egypt, Sukarno of Indonesia and Nkrumah of Ghana. But lists of celebrated names can hide the significant work of other governments and officials. The Belgrade declaration shows participants worrying about nuclear tests, foreign military bases, disarmament, and the rights of peoples still under colonial rule. They did not promise to become militarily powerful enough to balance the superpowers. They claimed a role in reducing the dangers that the superpowers posed to everyone.

Belgrade also shows how the definition of nonalignment changed with the membership and the moment. Yugoslavia was a European socialist state whose relations with Moscow had ruptured. India had its own disputes with China and Pakistan. Egypt had confronted Britain, France and Israel over Suez. Ghana and Guinea sought to overcome economic dependence after independence. Their motives were neither identical nor reducible to a single theory of development. Governments could champion the movement while concentrating power at home. Some promoted international cooperation while restricting domestic political pluralism. To explain nonalignment is not to certify every government that used the word. It is to understand why foreign policy autonomy became a valuable claim for states with unequal economic and military resources.

An external power might interpret refusal to join its alliance as hostility. A newly independent government might interpret pressure to choose a side as an attempt to narrow its independence. Both perceptions could influence diplomacy even without a formal treaty. Bandung tried to create room for disagreement without immediate military confrontation; Belgrade tried to turn that room into a durable international voice. Their success was partial and contingent. Neither stopped wars by issuing a declaration. Both changed the way some governments argued for participation in global decisions. The development of voting blocs, trade cooperation, and demands for decolonization in international organizations would build on this language without being controlled by it.

## What Bandung teaches about independence

We can now see Bandung from three distances. At the nearest distance, it was an organized meeting: delegates, speeches, committees, negotiated lines and printed conclusions. At the middle distance, it was a debate about how governments freed from empire could use legal sovereignty to change trade, technology and diplomacy. At the widest distance, it challenged the habit of telling Cold War history only through American and Soviet decisions. These distances should not be collapsed. A resolution about commodity prices is not a stable price. A statement in support of self-determination does not end colonial rule. And a photograph of smiling delegates does not prove political agreement. Each belongs to a different level of historical evidence.

The lasting question is this: what would independence be worth if its new governments could only accept choices already made elsewhere? Sukarno placed that question at the center of his opening address. The final communiqué supplied particular, imperfect answers: better trade terms, access to knowledge, cooperation against racism and colonialism, and rules to restrain international force. Belgrade later carried some of these demands into a more organized movement. It was not a story about countries stepping out of world politics. It was a story about entering world politics on their own terms, arguing with one another about what those terms should be, and discovering how difficult it was to turn a shared claim into shared power.

## Source method and unresolved questions (display only)

Each source is attributed to its author or institution. Diplomatic, archival, and legal texts document what officials recorded or enacted, not universal consent or direct proof of implementation. Publisher abstracts and catalog descriptions are identified as such and are not misrepresented as books or archival files read in full. An institutional description of an oral-history collection is not used to invent, quote, or merge individual testimonies. Historical debates are distinguished from settled document chronology. All source locators and known limits are retained in the ledger and metadata. No arbitrary lecture length requirement is used.

**Provenance note (display only):** The digitized Sukarno speech transcription labels the date April 18, 2015 on its individual page. The archive conference index, the Bandung conference record and other histories date this opening speech to April 18, 1955. This is a metadata inconsistency in the hosting archive, not a new date for the historical event.
