# A Country Divided, a War Without Peace: Korea, 1945-1953

## Liberation is not the same as control

In August 1945, Japan's defeat ended thirty-five years of colonial rule over Korea. For Korean people who had worked to preserve their language, organize independence movements, and resist imperial authority, liberation carried promises that no foreign army could confer on its own. Yet at the moment the Japanese empire collapsed, United States and Soviet forces were preparing to occupy different parts of the peninsula. Korea was not a blank space on which two superpowers invented politics. It was a society of farmers, factory workers, students, religious communities, landowners, and organizers with arguments about independence already well underway. The problem was that the military arrangements of victory and the political wishes of Koreans did not arrive at the same destination.

Imagine a clerk sorting school forms in late 1945. Which language should children study? Who would appoint their teachers? Should the property of former Japanese employers be confiscated, returned, or reorganized? These questions sound local, but they were also questions about who could govern. The Korean independence movement had operated across decades and borders, with a provisional government proclaimed in Shanghai in 1919 and other organizations developing in Korea itself. At liberation, Koreans formed committees, proposed reforms, and tried to reclaim their public institutions. Many different Koreans could agree that colonial rule should end while disagreeing over land ownership, class conflict, national elections, and relations with outside powers. Independence was a project with competing possible forms.

The thirty-eighth parallel was initially a line for accepting the Japanese surrender, not a border that emerged from a Korean referendum. Soviet forces occupied the north and American forces the south. Efforts to negotiate a unified postcolonial government ran aground amid disputes among Korean political groups and the occupying states. To describe what followed only as a clash between Washington and Moscow would repeat a mistake of the occupation itself: treating Korean political preferences as something that could be arranged by governments elsewhere. The north and south also developed distinctive institutions, security systems, and reorganizations of property. Those changes made the line increasingly consequential long before full-scale war in 1950.

## Competing claims to independence

In the southern zone, the first post-liberation years included political mobilization, ideological conflict, repression and attempts to create representative institutions. Elections for a National Assembly were held in May 1948 in the south; the Republic of Korea was established that August. A separate Democratic People's Republic of Korea was proclaimed in the north in September under Kim Il-sung. Both governments claimed to represent Korea, not merely a half-country. For a family whose relatives lived across the dividing line, this meant that each new government could describe the others' town as territory yet to be recovered. A division originally described as temporary was becoming a political and military reality.

The elections and founding ceremonies must not erase people who opposed the direction of state building. On Jeju Island, shootings at a protest in March 1947 helped widen conflict, and an uprising began in April 1948 amid resistance to separate southern elections. Security forces, armed insurgents and other local actors became involved; the subsequent campaign of suppression killed many island residents, including civilians with no demonstrated connection to armed groups. Jeju's own public memorial account describes the violence as extending well beyond April 1948. That history matters because the Korean War did not introduce political killing into an otherwise peaceful division. Long before armies crossed the thirty-eighth parallel, people already faced lethal struggles over what sort of Korea would be allowed to exist.

For decades many victims' families could not speak freely about state violence without consequences. Later South Korean institutions established investigations, memorial practices, and legal recognition for the events on Jeju. The existence of a truth-finding law should not make us imagine that the law has solved every disagreement. It shows that historical knowledge changes when a state permits formerly suppressed evidence to be collected, graves to be investigated, and families to demand recognition. A police list, an official report and a survivor's testimony each answer different questions. An argument about ideology cannot substitute for asking who was killed, by whom, and under what authority.

Land policy was another dividing point. In northern Korea, agrarian reform redistributed land under communist authority. In the south, reform and new constitutional institutions developed under different political and economic circumstances. A landless cultivator might care about title and rent more urgently than diplomatic alignment; a landlord might experience the same policy as dispossession. To evaluate these programs one must distinguish what they promised, how they were enforced, and what happened locally. The governments' rivalry reached into farms and villages, where a family's standing could become entangled with accusations of political loyalty. The Cold War was experienced through property, policing, and neighbors as much as through slogans about capitalism and communism.

## June 1950 and the movement of the front

On June 25, 1950, North Korean forces launched a major offensive south across the dividing line. Seoul fell within days. The United Nations Security Council adopted Resolution 82 calling for North Korean withdrawal. The Soviet Union was then boycotting Council meetings over the question of who should occupy China's seat, an absence that helped permit the Council's early action. The United States and allied countries organized military support for South Korea under a United Nations command. None of these legal or diplomatic steps by themselves conveyed consent from Korean civilians, but they transformed the scale of the war. A military invasion of the south became an international conflict that involved troops from several continents.

By late summer, North Korean forces held most of the peninsula, while South Korean and UN-command troops defended a southeastern area around Busan. In September, an amphibious landing at Incheon changed the military situation. UN-command forces recaptured Seoul, crossed the thirty-eighth parallel and moved northward. The stated objective had expanded from repelling an invasion to unifying the peninsula by force. The movement of the front explains why a fixed map is misleading. A locality could experience one army's advance, a second army's return, the arrest of supposed collaborators, shortages, and the loss of relatives within a single season. A border on a map did not predict how many times a community might be conquered.

Chinese forces entered the war in large numbers in the autumn of 1950, as UN-command armies approached China's northeastern frontier. The front reversed again, and fighting eventually settled around the central peninsula. To call this simply a war between two Korean states is insufficient; to call it only a proxy war is insufficient too. Korean leaders made political and military choices, Chinese leaders weighed regional security and ideology, and the United States and its allies made decisions about intervention and escalation. Each government also had internal differences. The war became global not because Korea lacked an independent history, but because several states tied their strategic futures to the outcome of a Korean struggle.

## Civilians beneath military categories

A person leaving a village did not necessarily know whether the road ahead was controlled by soldiers, refugees, police, or an advancing army. The National Archives of Korea preserves images of families on the move alongside government plans for evacuation and relief. The archive lets us see that flight was not an incidental episode that took place after the serious military history. The destruction of housing, interrupted harvests, overcrowded cities and family separation were part of the war's causal structure. An evacuation order might protect people in one district and fail elsewhere; a photograph can show a line of travelers without proving what every traveler feared.

Governments on both sides repeatedly portrayed civilians as possible enemies. Communist security forces killed or imprisoned people classified as hostile, while South Korean authorities and allied forces committed serious abuses against civilians suspected of leftist sympathies or treated as threats during military operations. Dong-Choon Kim's research on South Korea's Truth and Reconciliation Commission examines such killings through state files and witness testimony. The commission's work does not invite the flattening statement that every side behaved identically. It asks us to distinguish specific incidents, chains of command, places and dates, and to recognize why families spent decades pursuing records that had been hidden or denied.

At the start of the war, South Korean security forces carried out mass executions of people associated, sometimes only administratively, with the National Guidance League and other alleged leftist networks. In other episodes, North Korean units and collaborators killed prisoners and civilians characterized as rightists. Later reprisals could reverse the pattern again when control of a town changed. Political membership might be inferred from a list, an old association, or a neighbor's accusation. Such violence cannot be reconstructed responsibly from a single side's memorial language. Kim's study also identifies the Guchang killings of civilians by South Korean forces in February 1951. It matters to the history of the war that these events were investigated long after the armistice.

The phrase civilian casualty can conceal several different experiences. A child killed in a bombing, a prisoner executed in custody, a farmer whose crops were destroyed, and a family that could not return across the new military line did not suffer the same event. Even the word refugee can describe a person fleeing one particular battle or a person whom repeated displacement prevented from establishing a home. We should resist telling an invented composite victim's story to make the lecture seem vivid. The surviving archives already supply concrete questions about routes, rationing, missing relatives, and the practical ability to survive when the front moved. The absence of a named witness in a summary is a reason to preserve the limits of that summary, not to manufacture one.

There is a second kind of incompleteness in the evidence. Official Korean records available in English often come from the Republic of Korea and institutions linked to UN-command forces. They are valuable, but they do not provide equal access to ordinary residents of North Korea, whose wartime experiences included bombing, displacement, coercion and the rebuilding of destroyed cities. A careful historian can describe that structural gap without pretending to fill it through conjecture. For Korean families, the division of archives reflects the division of the country: even the ability to establish what happened to relatives can be shaped by the states that emerged from the war.

## Fighting stops without a settlement

By 1951 large-scale movement of the front had slowed, though artillery, air war, raids, and attempts to gain advantageous terrain continued. Armistice discussions opened while the killing went on. One of the most difficult questions was what to do with prisoners of war who resisted returning to the state from which they had fought. Should all prisoners be repatriated automatically, or should some have a choice? This was not a technical dispute about railway schedules. It raised problems of political coercion, personal agency and the fear that people might be punished upon return. The negotiating history of 1951 to 1953 reveals how a disagreement about the status of people could keep a war going.

On July 27, 1953, the military commanders of the UN Command and the Korean People's Army and Chinese People's Volunteers signed an armistice at Panmunjom. The agreement ordered a cessation of hostilities and established a demilitarized zone, with opposing forces withdrawn from a line of military control. It also created mechanisms for supervising the ceasefire and addressed prisoner repatriation. The words on the document matter: this was a military agreement intended to halt fighting while a future political settlement could be negotiated. It was not a peace treaty that resolved the rival claims to sovereignty. The Republic of Korea's president, Syngman Rhee, opposed the terms; the southern government was not a separate signatory to the armistice.

Read the armistice not simply as a date but as a structure. A demilitarized zone separates armed forces. It does not automatically reunite a separated mother and son, restore a destroyed village, or decide the title to land across the line. The agreement itself recommended a higher-level political conference on foreign forces and a peaceful settlement. Those recommendations record recognition that the central problem remained unsolved. An archive can tell us when the guns were ordered silent, but it cannot make a military boundary identical to a legitimate constitutional settlement in the eyes of all the people it divided.

What did people gain? An end to open, large-scale hostilities meant a possibility of reconstruction, the return of some prisoners, and fewer immediate threats from moving armies. What did they lose? Many had already lost homes and relatives, and a lasting frontier restricted the possibilities for recovering them. The Korean peninsula remained divided, with two sharply opposed political systems and a ceasefire capable of being violated. The term forgotten war, often used in American memory, is not a description of Korean memory. For people whose families, cities and political institutions were shaped by the conflict, the war was not a minor interruption between world wars.

## What a map cannot tell us

The most misleading way to narrate this period is to start with a world map colored red and blue, then place Korea where the colors meet. That map might reveal the reach of superpower interests. It would hide the Korean independence struggle, the different paths of land reform, the deaths on Jeju, the local organization of political violence, and the people who crossed the peninsula seeking safety. The division of Korea was both an international settlement imposed at a moment of imperial collapse and a history of Korean actors making choices within narrowing circumstances. We need both levels if we want explanation rather than a contest of slogans.

A more useful map would include not only the thirty-eighth parallel and the armistice line, but also former Japanese institutions, farmers' fields, election districts, refugee routes, massacre sites, bombed cities, and homes whose residents were separated by war. It would change as the front moved. It would show that two years of negotiations could coexist with continuing destruction. And it would distinguish an agreement between commanders from peace among the people they governed. That is the larger lesson of Korea between 1945 and 1953: the end of a colonial empire did not guarantee an agreed nation, and a ceasefire did not complete the work of liberation.

## Source method and unresolved questions (display only)

Each source is attributed to its author or institution. Diplomatic, archival, and legal texts document what officials recorded or enacted, not universal consent or direct proof of implementation. Publisher abstracts and catalog descriptions are identified as such and are not misrepresented as books or archival files read in full. An institutional description of an oral-history collection is not used to invent, quote, or merge individual testimonies. Historical debates are distinguished from settled document chronology. All source locators and known limits are retained in the ledger and metadata. No arbitrary lecture length requirement is used.
