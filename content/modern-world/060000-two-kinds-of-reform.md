# Two Kinds of Reform: China After Mao, 1976–1989

## A change that had no single author

Imagine a Chinese farming household in the late nineteen seventies. For years, agricultural work had been organized through collective institutions with instructions about production and delivery. A household might work hard without being able to decide how much of the harvest it could sell or what to plant next. Now imagine that some neighbors begin discussing another arrangement: the land will remain collectively owned, but the household will take responsibility for a contracted plot and keep more of what it produces after meeting its obligations. To an official, the proposal may look like a challenge to established policy. To a family, it can look like a way to make labor count. China's transformation after Mao began with choices of this kind as well as decisions made by leaders in Beijing. It was not written in full by one person and then implemented unchanged.

Mao Zedong died in September nineteen seventy-six. His death came after the upheavals of the Cultural Revolution, which had damaged educational institutions, professional lives, public trust, and the party itself. The arrest of the political group later known as the Gang of Four was one sign that the leadership struggle had changed. Hua Guofeng initially succeeded Mao in central positions. Deng Xiaoping, who had previously been purged, returned to influence and became associated with a program of reform and opening. It is useful to know Deng's name, but it is misleading to imagine an uncomplicated transfer of absolute control from Mao to Deng. Policy emerged through internal party struggles, local experiments, administrative negotiation, and arguments about what could be changed without ending Communist Party rule. The state was trying to recover authority while changing some of its instruments.

The Third Plenum of the Eleventh Communist Party Central Committee, held in December nineteen seventy-eight, became a political landmark. Its significance lay in a reorientation toward economic modernization and institutional change. The four modernizations—agriculture, industry, national defense, and science and technology—were not invented that week, but the gathering helped establish a new direction. Later official histories understandably make the meeting a clear turning point. The actual sequence was messier. Rural arrangements had already been tested in particular areas; the leadership did not begin with a complete economic blueprint; and subsequent changes were neither inevitable nor universally welcomed. Historical turning points are often named after the fact, when people can see which competing possibilities became dominant. In nineteen seventy-eight the destination was still being contested.

## Land, harvest, and the price of effort

The household responsibility system became the emblem of agricultural reform. It did not mean that rural land suddenly became individually owned property in the ordinary private-law sense. Instead, collective land was contracted to families under changing rules, and households acquired greater control over production decisions and above-quota output. Governments also adjusted agricultural procurement prices and allowed greater market exchange. These changes altered incentives: a farmer might receive more of the benefit of an extra harvest rather than see it absorbed into a collective accounting system. The familiar village of Xiaogang in Anhui became an important symbol because farmers there had experimented with household contracting before national endorsement. Yet a national transition cannot be explained entirely by a single village legend. Similar pressures and experiments existed elsewhere, and central decisions helped determine their spread.

By the early nineteen eighties the new rural system was widespread. Studies cited by the World Bank attribute a substantial part of the sharp increase in agricultural output to changes in production arrangements, while also identifying the role of prices, inputs, and longer-term conditions. This is a useful place to distinguish a mechanism from a miracle. When one group receives more freedom to decide how to work, production can rise because effort, information, and rewards are connected differently. But harvests depend on weather, irrigation, land quality, knowledge, and access to equipment. A region near roads and markets may benefit more quickly than an isolated settlement. A numerical increase in output does not automatically describe the living standard of every household. We need to ask both how much was grown and how gains were distributed.

The new arrangements also unsettled older forms of rural life. Commune structures were dismantled or reorganized. Rural government offices had to administer a different relationship between households and the state. Local cadres sometimes became entrepreneurs, brokers, or gatekeepers for emerging businesses. A family that could sell more produce might invest in tools or send a member into nonfarm work. Another might struggle because of illness, limited land, or poor access to transport. These are not side effects to be mentioned after the exciting growth figures. They are what a change in an economic institution means: people gain some options and lose some protections, while local officials acquire new ways to help, obstruct, or profit. Rural reform altered social power as well as agricultural techniques.

Rural industry expanded through township and village enterprises. Their ownership and management arrangements varied, but collectively they created employment beyond the fields and supplied goods to a changing domestic economy. An enterprise producing furniture or machine parts was not necessarily a privately owned corporation in the same way as a Western firm. Some were tied to local governments, others operated through changing arrangements of collective and individual initiative. This ambiguity was part of the transition. Leaders could encourage experiments that produced jobs and revenue without immediately settling every ideological question about markets and socialism. The phrase reform and opening can sound like a gate being flung open. In practice, different doors moved at different speeds, and some remained locked.

## Experiments at the coast

At the coast, the government tested another kind of arrangement. In nineteen eighty, Shenzhen, Zhuhai, Shantou, and Xiamen were designated special economic zones. Their purpose was to attract investment, trade, technologies, and managerial practices under rules different from those elsewhere. Shenzhen's position near Hong Kong was especially important: proximity to business networks and transport made the experiment more than a line drawn on a map. But calling a place a special zone did not build its factories. Roads, electricity, labor, housing, and local administrative decisions were required. As investment arrived, workers came from other regions, sometimes under conditions that offered new earnings but insecure housing or limited social rights. The zone was a legal and political experiment with material consequences for people moving through it.

China did not simply replace all planned prices with market prices overnight. Under dual-track arrangements, some goods continued to move through required quotas and official prices while additional output could be sold at market-influenced prices. A factory might have to deliver a specified amount under state rules and be allowed to bargain over production above that amount. This reduced the immediate shock of abolishing the plan while creating incentives for expansion. It also created opportunities for those with privileged access to buy cheaply through one channel and sell dearly through another. The same dual arrangement that made incremental reform politically possible could open a path to corruption. Understanding the design explains why economic liberalization and demands for clean government became entangled rather than belonging to unrelated debates.

Urban reform was harder in several respects. State-owned factories supported jobs and services, and workers depended on institutions that provided housing, health care, and other forms of security. Managers were asked to respond to profits and prices while still meeting administrative requirements. Reform could create new products and higher wages, but price changes also threatened household budgets. By nineteen eighty-eight inflation had become a major political concern. Leaders worried about social stability and retreated from some proposed price reforms. For a worker watching food and consumer prices rise, statistics about future national efficiency might be little comfort. A lesson of the Chinese transition is that changing an economy is not a single variable called growth. It involves price systems, employment guarantees, opportunities, and fears that people experience at different times.

Nor was China's growing engagement with international trade an immediate surrender of state direction. Foreign investment could be welcomed in particular locations while political authority remained concentrated in the Communist Party. Officials selected sectors, negotiated rules, and retained power over land and major financial institutions. Foreign businesses looked for access to labor and markets; Chinese administrators sought capital, technology, and revenues. These interests could overlap without being identical. The larger results are familiar in retrospect: rising output, rapid industrialization, and an important change in China's place in the world economy. But a retrospective success story can flatten the uncertainty of the decade. In the early nineteen eighties nobody had a complete map of how much could change economically while the political system retained its monopoly over organized opposition.

## The argument over political futures

Economic experimentation raised another question: who had a right to question the people directing it? Students, intellectuals, journalists, and workers had different ideas about what reform should mean. Some wanted reliable rules and limits on official corruption. Some wanted freedom of expression, public accountability, or a more democratic political structure. Others placed their hopes in higher living standards and greater professional opportunity. These positions were not mutually exclusive, and neither did they form one uniform opposition. The memory of political campaigns, purges, and the Cultural Revolution could make open argument both urgent and frightening. Within the leadership, support for economic change did not imply a willingness to surrender party authority. The boundary between permitted criticism and forbidden collective action remained contested and unstable.

In April nineteen eighty-nine, the death of former party general secretary Hu Yaobang prompted mourning that became a wider movement of public protest. Students gathered in Beijing, and demands expanded to include concerns about corruption, censorship, and political accountability. Workers and other city residents joined; demonstrations occurred in other Chinese cities as well. It is misleading to imagine the protests as a small group of students with one carefully drafted constitutional program. Participants were responding to differing anxieties and hopes, including the uneven experience of economic reform. Some argued for dialogue with the government; others adopted more confrontational tactics. The Tiananmen demonstrations became a focal point, but the movement's geography and social composition were wider than the square.

In May, authorities declared martial law. On the night of June third and into June fourth, soldiers and armored vehicles moved into central Beijing and used lethal force against civilians. Many people were killed and injured in streets approaching the square and elsewhere. The exact death toll remains disputed, and the state's restrictions on investigation make certainty difficult. We should not use uncertainty about an exact figure to make the violence disappear; nor should we invent a precise number that evidence cannot justify. The crackdown was followed by arrests, imprisonment, censorship, and punishment of participants. For families searching for relatives, the event was not just a moment in international television coverage. It was a continuing struggle over knowledge: the names of those killed, what happened to them, and whether a government would acknowledge responsibility.

One way to misunderstand nineteen eighty-nine is to assume that the protesters rejected every economic reform because they challenged political authority. In fact, the experience of inflation and corruption could give people reasons to demand more accountable government while still valuing new opportunities for work and learning. Another mistake is to interpret the later growth of the Chinese economy as a verdict on whether the use of force had been justified. Economic outcomes do not answer the moral and political question of state violence against civilians. Nor does the repression settle the economic question of which reforms had improved lives and which had produced new inequalities. These issues are historically connected, but they require distinct forms of evidence and judgement.

## What changed, and what did not

By the end of the nineteen eighties China had undergone changes that would have been difficult to imagine a decade earlier. Households held different responsibilities for production; rural enterprises employed millions; coastal zones were expanding; managers and workers learned to operate amid a mixture of planned and market transactions. The changes laid foundations for decades of growth and poverty reduction. They were also built on earlier investments in literacy, public health, industry, and infrastructure, not on an empty landscape. A careful explanation avoids both extremes: reform was neither a magic switch that created all subsequent prosperity nor a minor adjustment in a system otherwise untouched. Its consequences depended on institutional design, local ingenuity, labor, and the world's changing markets.

Some inequalities and pressures were intensified by the new arrangements. Access to land, contracts, foreign investment, and local government protection was not evenly distributed. Families who moved toward cities could find themselves essential to new industries while remaining disadvantaged by registration and social-service rules. Entrepreneurs and cadres could prosper, but a worker employed in an old state factory might wonder whether a promised lifetime position would survive. At the same time, a government capable of making large economic policy changes retained the power to prevent independent political organization. The decade showed that markets and political rights are not a single package. They can expand or contract on different schedules, sometimes in ways that reinforce one another and sometimes in sharp conflict.

The most revealing comparison may be between two kinds of initiative. In one setting, a village household took risks by trying a new way to organize production. In another, students and workers took risks by trying to make political institutions answerable to the public. The authorities eventually incorporated one sort of experimentation into national policy and crushed the other. That contrast does not make the participants identical, or imply that all of them wanted the same society. It tells us that the state was willing to revise some rules and not others. The economic story of China after Mao and the political story of the late nineteen eighties should therefore be taught together, not because one explains away the other, but because they reveal the different meanings that the word reform could carry.

A historian returning to this period should keep several questions alive. Who first tested a proposal, and who later received credit for it? When an institution creates new wealth, which people can claim a share? When official reports celebrate growth, what independent evidence describes conditions at work, at home, and in the countryside? When a government insists that political silence is the price of stability, whose testimony can be heard? China's transformation is neither reducible to Deng Xiaoping's biography nor exhausted by images from Tiananmen Square. It is the history of millions of people responding to a changing set of possibilities within a state whose leaders would not allow all possible futures to be pursued. That tension is central to understanding modern China.

## Source method and remaining gaps (display only)

Records from state departments and international agencies are evidence about institutional policies and how they are represented, not independent confirmation of motives or social outcomes. Web excerpts and bibliographic abstracts are labeled and not passed off as fully read archival works. Where counts or causal explanations are uncertain, the spoken narrative preserves that uncertainty. Expand this pack with Afghan and Soviet participant oral histories, Chinese local and worker archives, African independent policy evaluations, and non-U.S. histories of technology before claiming comprehensive regional coverage. Lecture length is dictated by the material, not a word or time limit.
