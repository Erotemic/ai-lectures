# The Rules Behind the Price: Trade and the World Trade Organization, 1986–2001

## A meeting room and a street

In November nineteen ninety-nine, a trade conference opened in Seattle. Delegates arrived to negotiate rules that governed products, services, and economic relationships around the world. Outside, dockworkers, students, farmers, environmental campaigners, union members, and activists from many countries gathered in the streets. Police blocked intersections; protesters blocked access to the meetings. By the end of the week, the conference had failed to agree on the next round of negotiations. The images invited a simple explanation: protesters had stopped world trade. That explanation confuses a dramatic scene with an institutional history. The negotiations had already been divided by disagreements among governments, including between wealthy and poorer member states. In Seattle, a debate previously concentrated in ministries and negotiating rooms became visible to a much wider public.

For the people of Washington State, international commerce was not an abstraction. Boeing sold aircraft abroad; software companies marketed products on several continents; agricultural exporters depended on foreign buyers; port workers depended on ships arriving with goods to unload and leaving with goods to sell. For other workers, foreign competition and the ability of firms to move production threatened wages and bargaining power. Those interests did not divide neatly into people who liked trade and people who opposed it. A port worker could value export markets and still object to weak labor protections. A farmer could want buyers abroad while questioning whether subsidized foreign crops would undercut local producers. A treaty set the conditions under which these conflicts would be negotiated, but it could not make the conflicts disappear.

## From a postwar agreement to a global institution

The organization at the center of Seattle had been created only four years earlier. After the Second World War, governments negotiated the General Agreement on Tariffs and Trade, commonly called GATT. It became a forum for rules and successive rounds of bargaining about restrictions on trade. The central purpose was to make trade less vulnerable to sudden, discriminatory barriers, which policymakers associated with the destructive economic rivalries of the interwar years. Yet the arrangement was not a comprehensive economic government. States retained authority over many parts of their economies, and large agricultural and textile sectors were subject to persistent exceptions and political bargaining. The postwar trading order had to coexist with development planning, national industrial policy, and the social compromises explored in other areas of modern history.

In September nineteen eighty-six, ministers meeting in Punta del Este, Uruguay, launched negotiations later called the Uruguay Round. The talks took years, crossed changes of government, and became a dispute over more than tariffs on imported manufactured goods. Negotiators tackled agricultural support and trade in textiles, but also services, intellectual property rights, and ways of resolving disagreements between states. These categories require explanation. A service might mean banking, insurance, telecommunications, or the operation of a foreign business. Intellectual property includes patents and copyrighted works. When a trade treaty reaches these subjects, it reaches domestic choices about how corporations, professionals, consumers, and governments should behave. The rules shape what may be sold, who may enter a market, and who holds the legal right to an invention or a creative work.

The agreements were signed in Marrakesh in April nineteen ninety-four, and the World Trade Organization began operating on January first, nineteen ninety-five. The new organization did not abolish GATT; the goods agreement remained part of its legal structure. Its members also accepted agreements covering services and trade-related intellectual property, and a more systematic procedure for settling disputes. The shift was constitutional in a practical sense. A tariff reduction can sometimes be described as a number on a schedule. A rules-based organization needs procedures for asking whether a state has followed the commitments it negotiated, and for determining what other states may do in response. Governments gained a venue for challenging one another, though unequal bargaining resources and negotiating priorities did not vanish with the new legal structure.

Consider the term most-favored nation. In ordinary speech it sounds like a promise to give a special friend the best deal. In the core trade rules it generally means that if a member grants a covered trade advantage to one partner, it must extend the same treatment to other members, subject to established exceptions. Another principle, national treatment, limits certain discriminatory treatment of imported goods once they have entered the market. These rules are meant to reduce bargaining by threat and personal favor. They do not require that every country charge the same tariff or that every product cross a border without any conditions. Agricultural regulations, health measures, anti-dumping duties, customs unions, and development provisions occupy their own contested parts of the system. Understanding the exceptions is as important as memorizing the slogans.

## A container, a field, and a patent

Imagine a container unloaded from a ship. It may hold clothing assembled in one country using fabric made in another, packaged and sold under a brand controlled elsewhere. Lower tariffs can make such supply chains cheaper and expand sales. They can also give large buyers an opportunity to move orders among factories and play producers against each other. A worker's wage depends on local labor law, collective bargaining, competition among employers, and productivity, not on the customs rate alone. Gains from trade can be real without being evenly distributed. The historical question is who captures the savings and who has enough bargaining power to demand a share. Trade negotiations rarely write the answer directly into a household's pay envelope.

Agriculture exposed another difficulty. Farmers in a wealthy country might receive subsidies and sell into world markets; farmers in a poorer country might be expected to compete without similar financial support. Food importers could benefit from cheaper grain, while small producers might lose markets or livelihoods. Some governments wanted stronger disciplines on subsidies. Others defended support as necessary for food security, rural employment, or political stability. The word liberalization was therefore not a single policy with a single effect. Reducing an import tariff, limiting a subsidy, and opening a market to a foreign bank can change different parts of an economy through different mechanisms. Countries negotiated these changes in packages, trading concessions in one sector for opportunities in another.

Patents made the stakes more difficult to see in a shipping container. The agreement on Trade-Related Aspects of Intellectual Property Rights, or TRIPS, established common minimum standards for protection and enforcement in international trade. Advocates argued that protection rewarded research, invention, and creative work, and reduced uncertainty for firms operating abroad. Critics asked whether rules suited to wealthy, research-intensive economies would raise costs for poorer states and make technology and medicines harder to obtain. The conflict was not a choice between caring about science and caring about patients. Both drug discovery and access to treatment mattered. The question was how the legal distribution of exclusive rights affected investment, prices, manufacturing, and the capacity of public health systems to act.

During the nineteen nineties, HIV treatment made the question immediate. Combination therapies transformed the prospects of many patients who could obtain them, while prices and health-system capacity restricted access in many poorer countries. Governments, activists, pharmaceutical companies, and health organizations disagreed about patents, compulsory licensing, and generic manufacture. A later declaration at Doha in November two thousand and one affirmed that the TRIPS agreement could and should be interpreted to support public health and access to medicines. That declaration was a political response to disputes that had sharpened through the nineteen nineties. It did not retroactively erase the earlier conflicts or solve the practical problem of supplying a medicine to every patient who needed it.

## Who writes the rules?

Representatives of developing countries did not share a single position. Some sought wider access for agricultural or manufactured exports. Some wanted time to implement costly new legal obligations. Some feared that wealthy countries could use environmental or labor standards selectively as disguised trade barriers. Some believed that a rules-based organization offered small states at least a more predictable basis for challenging powerful countries than bilateral pressure would. The ability to win a legal dispute, however, was not identical to the ability to hire lawyers, assemble technical evidence, withstand retaliation, or turn a ruling into changes in a distant market. Institutional equality and economic equality were separate questions.

The procedure of negotiation raised its own political questions. Large meetings might proclaim the equal status of members, but complicated decisions were sometimes prepared in smaller groups where a few governments dominated. Ministers from smaller delegations could lack the specialists needed to attend simultaneous sessions on agriculture, services, patents, and labor concerns. In Seattle, representatives of poorer countries objected to opaque consultations and the feeling that decisions were being prepared without them. That criticism did not mean they all favored suspending trade negotiations. It meant they wanted meaningful influence over the rules they would be asked to follow. Representation is not accomplished simply by placing a flag at the door of the conference hall.

Outside the hall, an equally mixed set of interests assembled. The streets included unions worried about jobs, environmentalists concerned about enforcement, students criticizing corporate power, campaigners from the global South, and groups with broader opposition to capitalism. Some wanted a different trade regime, some wanted to reform the existing one, and some wanted public accountability before new agreements were made. They did not possess one platform. Organizers used marches, blockades, teaching events, and coordination across previously separate networks. A large labor demonstration was one part of the week. Other groups took direct action at entrances. Police made arrests and used force, and some people damaged property. These actions belong in the history, but a television montage of conflict cannot replace a map of what the participants demanded.

Government investigators later concluded that Seattle's failure resulted from several difficulties. Major trading powers remained divided about the scope of new talks; developing-country delegations objected to the process; the negotiating text was full of incompatible proposals; and the organization had struggled through a leadership transition. Demonstrations disrupted access and intensified the difficulties, but the protests were not the sole reason no new round was launched. The failure of negotiations and the impact of protests have to be explained separately. Governments can fail to reach agreement even without protesters. Protest can nevertheless change what issues a wider public notices and how much legitimacy a negotiation enjoys. Both statements can be true without pretending that one caused the other.

## Winning and losing were not permanent identities

Trade can enlarge the market available to a producer, give consumers lower prices, and make machinery and ideas easier to obtain. It can also produce concentrated losses when a factory closes or a rural district cannot compete with a cheaper imported crop. Those outcomes are mediated by transport, exchange rates, education, infrastructure, finance, labor law, industrial organization, and social insurance. A model showing aggregate national gains cannot tell a dismissed employee that the new job will appear in the same town, at the same wage, or before the next mortgage payment. Equally, a story of one closed factory cannot measure all the opportunities created for exporters, service workers, or consumers. Serious historical analysis must preserve both levels.

The legal commitments were also not a straight road to a borderless world. States retained customs offices, inspection agencies, political tools, and the ability to negotiate changes. Disputes continued, and decisions did not always deliver prompt compliance. National governments remained accountable to citizens who could reasonably want the same government to protect workers, keep food affordable, encourage industrial growth, and obey international commitments. Sometimes those goals supported one another; sometimes they conflicted. The trade regime did not eliminate the politics of distribution. It moved a part of that politics into rules that required specialized knowledge to understand and organized pressure to change.

When Seattle ended in December nineteen ninety-nine, there was no agreement to begin the proposed new round. Yet the WTO continued to exist and its members continued to trade and litigate under existing agreements. In November two thousand and one they launched negotiations at Doha, where public health and the concerns of developing countries occupied an important part of the agenda. The change of location did not settle all disagreements. What Seattle had shown was that the world economy was governed, not merely interconnected. Every rule about a tariff, a patent, a subsidy, or a hearing procedure reflected choices. Those choices could expand opportunities, distribute costs, and be challenged by the people living with their consequences.

## The question behind a price

A price tag on imported clothing looks as though it tells a simple story: the shopper pays, the seller earns, and the garment moves. Behind it stand several states, a series of contracts, customs rules, factory decisions, wage negotiations, and perhaps a patent or trademark. During the nineteen nineties, international institutions sought to make some parts of this chain more predictable. People arguing in streets and ministries asked whether predictability for firms also meant security for workers, farmers, and patients. History cannot answer that question by assigning each group a permanent role as a winner or loser. It can show the mechanisms through which rules create options and constraints, and explain why people who all depended on world trade disagreed about what its rules should be.
