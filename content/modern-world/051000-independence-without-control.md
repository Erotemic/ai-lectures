# Independence Without Control: Congo and the International Crisis, 1960–1965

## The promise at the ceremony

On June thirtieth, nineteen sixty, a new state stood before the world in Léopoldville, the city now called Kinshasa. Belgium's King Baudouin spoke at the formal independence ceremony, presenting Belgian rule as a civilizing achievement. Patrice Lumumba, the new Congolese prime minister, spoke with a different sense of where the country had come from. He described the labor, humiliation and organized struggle that had made freedom necessary and possible. His words were not a request for history to begin under European supervision. They were an insistence that Congolese people had already made history, and intended to govern what came next. Both speeches were delivered at one ceremony. They described profoundly different relationships between past and future.

Independence usually appears on a map as a single date: the color of a territory changes, and one flag replaces another. In Congo the date was also an argument about who had authority over an army, mines, courts, provinces, money, and the foreign advisers still occupying important posts. These were not secondary questions to be dealt with after the independence celebration. They were the institutions through which independence would be lived. A family looking for a school, a soldier expecting promotion, or a worker demanding wages had reason to ask whether a transfer of sovereignty would change their actual access to power. We can use the events of Congo's first years to test the distance between declaring independence and possessing it.

The Belgian Congo was enormous and economically central to multiple industries abroad. Mining companies and colonial officials had developed extraction routes and selected infrastructure to move copper, cobalt and other materials, while deliberately restricting political participation. Colonial education and administration had not prepared a broad class of Congolese officers to run all parts of the state. The result was a structural vulnerability created by colonial policy, not evidence that Congolese society was incapable of self-government. Congolese historians, among them Georges Nzongola-Ntalaja, remind us that racial separation had been visible in everyday settlements as well as in the formal machinery of rule. When independence arrived, that inherited inequality shaped the choices available to the new government.

There was no single Congolese independence movement with a single plan. Joseph Kasa-Vubu and the Alliance des BaKongo, usually known as ABAKO, had organized forcefully around Kongo cultural identity, territorial claims, and the demand for self-government. Patrice Lumumba and his Mouvement National Congolais sought a different kind of national coalition, though the party itself experienced divisions. Historian Yolanda Covington-Ward has argued that ABAKO's political performances and organizing deserve much more weight in the independence story than a Lumumba-centered narrative gives them. Her argument challenges us to look beyond famous speeches. How did associations, neighborhood demonstrations, and competing visions of federalism make the colony politically ungovernable for its rulers? That question begins before nineteen sixty.

## A government with many constituencies

In May nineteen sixty, Congolese voters chose representatives in elections held only weeks before independence. No single party could simply command the entire new state, so leaders assembled a coalition government. Lumumba became prime minister and Kasa-Vubu president. The two positions were not interchangeable: the prime minister had a governing mandate, while the president held a separate constitutional office. Rival parties also commanded local and provincial loyalties. The disagreements concerned real issues: how much authority provinces should have, whether mineral wealth should remain where it was extracted, and who should appoint officials. To call those disagreements mere tribal confusion repeats a colonial habit of reducing political choices to fixed ethnic labels.

There were women organizers as well as male party leaders. Julienne Mbengi helped establish a women's association associated with ABAKO in nineteen fifty-eight. Lumumba's movement also sought women's support, even while top decision-making remained dominated by men. Andrée Blouin, whose anticolonial organizing crossed several African countries, became a prominent ally in the Congolese independence struggle. Her memorial organization preserves the memory of that work, while historical research cautions that the institutional influence of women's groups still needs closer archival study. We should neither invent an egalitarian revolution that had already arrived nor erase those who mobilized outside cabinet rooms. Political history is also made in meetings, petitions, networks, and the difficult work of persuading others to act together.

The new government faced a terrible contradiction in the security forces. The Force Publique had been an instrument of colonial authority, with white Belgian officers commanding African soldiers. On July fifth, only days after independence, soldiers mutinied. Demands for promotion, pay, respect, and the end of a colonial chain of command were part of the upheaval. The mutiny spread, frightened residents, and produced violence. Belgium then sent troops without approval from the Congolese national government, describing the operation as protection for its citizens. From Brussels, this could be presented as restoring order. From the new government's perspective, a foreign army had reentered sovereign territory. Both perspectives had consequences, and neither dissolves the rights and harms of civilians caught in between.

On July eleventh, Moïse Tshombe declared the secession of Katanga, a mineral-rich province in the southeast. Belgium and foreign commercial interests had influence there, but Katangan leaders also pursued political ambitions of their own. Soon Albert Kalonji's movement challenged the central state in South Kasai. The names Katanga and South Kasai are important because the crisis was not only an argument about communist influence. It was a struggle over the territorial and fiscal boundaries of a state formed from colonial administration. Could Katanga keep more mining revenue? Could a government in Léopoldville command armed forces in regions whose leaders denied its authority? Those questions now became urgent enough that people were fighting, fleeing, and dying over them.

## The United Nations enters the crisis

Lumumba and Kasa-Vubu appealed for international assistance and the withdrawal of Belgian forces. The United Nations Security Council adopted Resolution one forty-three on July fourteenth, nineteen sixty. It authorized the secretary-general to provide military assistance to the Congolese government while its own forces developed capacity, and called for Belgian withdrawal. The resulting operation was known by its French initials, ONUC. Troops arrived from a range of countries, including African and Asian contributors. A United Nations force was neither simply the army of the United States nor a blank page with no politics. It had a mandate, a command structure, and member governments whose views about intervention differed.

The first mandate emphasized restoring security, replacing foreign soldiers, and helping essential services function. But its central dilemma appeared almost immediately. A government that had requested help expected assistance in reasserting its authority. United Nations officials sought to avoid being used as one Congolese faction's army. What counted as impartiality when a province announced secession and received foreign assistance? What counted as respecting sovereignty if the national government itself was divided? United Nations accounts show that the mission's authority expanded over time, eventually including stronger action against outside mercenaries and the defense of territorial integrity. The changing mandate records not only decisions in New York but also the failure of early rules to resolve conditions on the ground.

Lumumba grew frustrated with the limits on the United Nations response to Katanga. His government sought Soviet aircraft and assistance to move forces and press its claims. In Washington and Brussels, officials increasingly viewed him through a Cold War security lens. The United States feared that Soviet involvement would gain influence over central Africa. Soviet leaders in turn could describe themselves as helping an anticolonial government that Western powers had failed. Neither superpower's framing exhausted the question facing Congolese leaders: whether external help could be obtained without sacrificing political choice. The same aid that addressed an immediate need could give outsiders leverage over policy, intelligence, and appointments.

Military operations by Congolese forces also inflicted devastating civilian harm. Violence in South Kasai, including killings of civilians during central-government campaigns, makes a simple division between righteous national unity and illegitimate secession impossible to sustain. Civilians could support independence and still fear the armed agents claiming to act for the new nation. The principle that Congo should remain territorially united did not excuse massacres. Nor did evidence of atrocities establish that Belgian troops or foreign companies had a rightful claim to decide Congo's political future. Any history that celebrates or condemns whole political movements without examining what their armed agents did to particular communities will miss the human stakes of state formation.

## The removal of a prime minister

In September nineteen sixty, President Kasa-Vubu announced Lumumba's dismissal. Lumumba rejected it and challenged the president's own authority. Their confrontation was also a constitutional dispute over what a recently written system allowed and who could speak for the state. On September fourteenth, army officer Joseph-Désiré Mobutu intervened, saying he was neutralizing politicians. Military power now disrupted a government that had just acquired electoral legitimacy. U.S. records trace the administration's political efforts to marginalize Lumumba, and later historians examine Western support for his opponents. Yet to describe Mobutu only as a foreign instrument is to erase a consequential Congolese actor whose later rule would reshape the country. Foreign pressure mattered because people inside Congo made decisions within and against it.

Lumumba attempted to reach political supporters elsewhere in the country and was captured by forces loyal to Mobutu. He was imprisoned and, on January seventeenth, nineteen sixty-one, transported with two associates, Maurice Mpolo and Joseph Okito, to Katanga. A contemporary United Nations investigation, summarized in the published American diplomatic record, established that all three were killed there on that date. The Katangan government's initial public story claimed they had escaped, then announced a later killing; the investigation did not accept that chronology. Here source criticism changes the narrative in a concrete way. A government proclamation is evidence of what it wanted people to believe. It is not automatically evidence of what happened.

Responsibility for Lumumba's murder remains part of a long political and judicial reckoning. A Belgian parliamentary inquiry concluded in two thousand and one that Belgium bore a moral responsibility for circumstances leading to his death. When Lumumba's remains were formally returned to his family in twenty twenty-two, Belgium's prime minister acknowledged that Belgian officials could have recognized the danger of the transfer and failed to prevent it. Such acknowledgments matter, but they are not substitutes for examining which individuals ordered, enabled, witnessed or concealed particular acts. The role of U.S. covert efforts to remove Lumumba is also part of the historical record; claims about direct responsibility for the actual killing demand careful distinctions among plans, decisions, and execution.

Across Africa, Lumumba's death became a symbol of the threat that independence might be formal while powerful external actors retained influence. But Congolese people did not all agree on his leadership while he was alive. His opponents had political constituencies and fears of their own. The historian Georges Nzongola-Ntalaja has written and spoken about the importance of Lumumba's vision while warning against the long erasure of other dimensions of Congolese political experience. We can hold two propositions together: Lumumba was a major elected leader whose overthrow and death involved grave injustice, and his government was part of a complex landscape of disagreement rather than the only imaginable Congolese future. Recalling that complexity makes the injury of violence more intelligible, not less.

## Katanga, a peacekeeping army, and a changing world

After Lumumba's death, the crisis did not end. Antoine Gizenga and other Lumumbist leaders claimed political authority from Stanleyville, now Kisangani. Parliament eventually reconvened while rival factions continued to contest control. The United Nations was expected to assist the central government, withdraw foreign personnel, prevent civil war, and eventually confront secessionist armed forces. The Security Council's February nineteen sixty-one Resolution one sixty-one urged measures to prevent violence; a later resolution strengthened the mission's ability to remove foreign mercenaries. Each change raised hard questions. If a peacekeeping force uses force to end a secession, is it maintaining the peace or deciding a war? Who authorizes those decisions when there is no settled domestic consensus?

Dag Hammarskjöld, the United Nations secretary-general, died in September nineteen sixty-one when his aircraft crashed near Ndola while traveling to seek a ceasefire. The death intensified scrutiny of the operation and is itself the subject of continuing historical inquiry. What we can say from the United Nations mission history is that the negotiations were taking place amid fighting between UN forces and Katangan troops. ONUC eventually acted more forcefully and Katanga's secession ended in early nineteen sixty-three. By June nineteen sixty-four the military mission had withdrawn. Reunification of the territory was a major result, but it did not mean Congo had become peaceful, democratic, or immune to further intervention.

During the crisis, the United Nations fielded a force numbering nearly twenty thousand at peak strength, along with civilian personnel providing technical assistance. The size of the operation shows how the organization had moved far beyond the modest peace-observation model that earlier postwar hopes imagined. But a count of soldiers does not tell us how people in a particular village assessed their presence. Some could see protection; others could experience foreign military authority with little accountability to them. Contemporaries disagreed over whether the organization had reacted too slowly, exceeded its mandate, or been constrained by competing states. Evaluating these criticisms requires separating the instructions issued by the Security Council from what contingents actually did.

In nineteen sixty-four, insurgencies and counter-insurgency brought new violence. Rebel forces captured Stanleyville, and U.S. and Belgian interventions became entangled in efforts to support the central state and rescue foreign hostages. The published State Department history gives an account of Operation Dragon Rouge in November, which is useful for understanding how Washington explained its decisions. But hostages were not the only people whose lives were at risk, and the diplomatic record is a thin source for the experiences of Congolese civilians in rebel-held areas or during attacks. A serious history has to follow both the diplomatic negotiations and the people who did not hold diplomatic passports.

## A second coup and an unfinished independence

On November twenty-fifth, nineteen sixty-five, Mobutu seized power again, this time establishing the political basis for a long dictatorship. The military intervention settled the immediate contest for the presidency by removing the ability of elected rivals to govern. Western governments continued to support him in part because they judged his rule useful in the Cold War. Yet power was also consolidated through Congolese institutions, patronage, repression, and choices made by people around Mobutu. To describe his regime as the inevitable outcome of nineteen sixty would deny the possibility of other trajectories that people struggled for. To describe it as purely a domestic affair would hide important external resources and incentives. Both levels remain necessary.

Consider the phrase territorial integrity. It was used to justify efforts to bring Katanga back into Congo, and it had a powerful anticolonial logic: a foreign-backed province could not easily be allowed to take valuable resources away from a newly independent people. But integrity alone could not guarantee justice to communities harmed by central armies, or representation for provincial opponents. Consider, too, the word neutrality. The United Nations sought to avoid becoming a participant in local politics, but a decision to defend a state's borders already had effects on the balance of power. These words were not meaningless. They were indispensable tools that still required arguments about whom their use would protect.

Why did Congo attract such intense outside intervention? Its mineral resources and geographic scale made it strategically important. The Cold War gave governments ready-made reasons to fear an opponent's influence. Belgium retained institutional and commercial connections created by colonial rule. But the combination was combustible because decolonization had been hurried and structurally incomplete. Political organizations were trying to settle basic questions of representation while an army, foreign troops, and provincial secessions made those arguments violent. None of these causes by itself explains everything. The historian's task is to trace how they interacted, and to resist making either Congolese agency or foreign intervention disappear from the picture.

At the end of this lecture, return to the independence ceremony. Lumumba told his listeners that they would pass the story of struggle on to their children. That demand was not only a rhetorical flourish; it was a demand to decide what counted as the country's history. Later official documents describe resolutions, troop deployments, parliamentary offices and coups. Those records are indispensable, but they do not replace the memories of people who organized, lost family members, resisted military orders, or remained on land whose legal owner suddenly changed. Congo's first five years teach a difficult lesson about sovereignty: recognition by other states is vital, but political self-rule must also survive struggles over armed force, public resources, competing rights, and the power to tell what happened.

## Source method and unresolved questions (display only)

Official state, military and organizational records establish what their makers stated, ordered and publicized, but must not be mistaken for comprehensive records of social outcomes. Participant accounts are situated voices rather than statistical samples. Where only an abstract, excerpt, or archive index was accessible, the limitation is explicit. Interpretations and contested causal accounts are distinguished from documentary chronology. No fixed length or duration rule applies to this lecture.
