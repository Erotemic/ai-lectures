# The Right to Remain: Rohingya Persecution, Flight, and Evidence, 2016–2020

## Before the crossing

To tell the history of the Rohingya refugees who reached Bangladesh in two thousand and seventeen, we must begin before the boats and the long walks toward the border. The Rohingya are a predominantly Muslim community with deep histories in the region now known as Rakhine State, in western Myanmar. They are not a single family or a uniform set of political opinions. Generations have lived, farmed, traded, married, and raised children there. Yet many came to be treated by state institutions as if they did not belong. The history is not adequately explained by saying that two religious communities simply hated one another. Laws, administrative categories, security forces, propaganda, land arrangements, and political decisions helped determine whose home and citizenship would be recognized.

Rakhine State borders Bangladesh and the Bay of Bengal. Its population includes ethnic Rakhine Buddhists, Rohingya Muslims, and other communities, with histories of cooperation and conflict under different rulers and colonial administrations. Disputes over migration, state boundaries, and belonging sharpened in the twentieth century. Myanmar's military and many officials rejected Rohingya identification and often labeled the group as immigrants from Bengal. That designation was politically consequential: it made citizenship claims appear foreign rather than historical. But a government's description of a population cannot settle the population's ancestry, rights, or identity. A careful account distinguishes what people call themselves, what officials call them, and the policies attached to those names.

Myanmar's nineteen eighty-two Citizenship Law provided an ethnic and documentary framework in which many Rohingya were denied recognized citizenship. Citizenship rules and their administration became entangled with restrictions on movement, schooling, marriage, healthcare, and livelihood. The details were not identical for everyone, but the accumulated effects were severe. If a person cannot obtain accepted documents, even a short journey to hospital or school may require permission or expose them to arrest. A child can inherit not only a family's language and traditions, but also a state-created vulnerability. Statelessness is not the absence of a life or a community. It is the failure of states to acknowledge a person as a national under the operation of their laws.

Violence and flight preceded the crisis of two thousand and seventeen. Communal violence in two thousand and twelve uprooted many people and led to displacement camps and restrictions in Rakhine State. Earlier waves had already sent Rohingya to Bangladesh and other countries. Rohingya civilians and ethnic Rakhine civilians experienced distinct harms and insecurities in a region with poverty, political exclusion, and armed conflict. These histories matter because they make the later exodus neither an inexplicable explosion nor an inevitable ancient quarrel. Policy choices structured the setting long before the largest attacks, and state authorities had opportunities to change course.

## The warning and the operation

In two thousand and sixteen, following attacks on border security posts attributed to Rohingya militants, Myanmar security forces launched operations in northern Rakhine State. Human rights investigators documented allegations of killings, sexual violence, arbitrary detention, and village destruction. In September of that year, the Myanmar government and the Kofi Annan Foundation established an Advisory Commission on Rakhine State to examine longstanding problems. Its membership included Myanmar and international commissioners. It was asked to consider citizenship, rights, development, movement, and relations among communities. This was an institutional acknowledgment that the situation demanded more than a police response.

On August twenty-fourth, two thousand and seventeen, the commission's final recommendations were published. They called for action on citizenship verification, equality before the law, freedom of movement, and the situation of displaced people, as well as economic and political reforms. A day later, on August twenty-fifth, the Arakan Rohingya Salvation Army attacked Myanmar security posts. Those attacks were part of the sequence of events and require direct acknowledgment. They did not justify indiscriminate violence against civilians. The Myanmar armed forces, known as the Tatmadaw, began what authorities called clearance operations. The naming of the operation reveals the official framing, not its legitimacy.

Survivors described assaults on villages, killings, rape and other sexual violence, homes burned, and families forced to flee. United Nations investigators later found corroborated patterns across multiple locations in northern Rakhine State. The independent fact-finding mission concluded on reasonable grounds that soldiers and other forces had committed war crimes and crimes against humanity, and identified acts that could support findings of genocidal intent. Myanmar's military and civilian authorities disputed accusations and frequently defended the operations as a response to militant attacks. Those official denials must be reported as positions taken by authorities, not treated as equal substitutes for a body of documented eyewitness, satellite, and forensic evidence. The task of history is to explain what can be established and how.

More than seven hundred thousand Rohingya fled toward Bangladesh after the August attacks, joining people displaced in earlier years. The exact cumulative numbers depend on dates and population-counting methods; it is important not to turn different estimates into competing certainties. They crossed rivers or traveled on foot, carrying children and whatever possessions could be moved. Many arrived injured or exhausted. The movement was so large that a numerical description is necessary, but the number alone cannot convey the loss of relatives, homes, possessions, burial places, and the everyday ability to decide where to go. Displacement is both a journey and the enforced interruption of a social world.

## How an investigator knows

A report can contain a conclusion without adequately showing how it was reached. The United Nations fact-finding mission described its methods in its published report. For its two thousand and eighteen findings, investigators conducted hundreds of in-depth interviews with victims and eyewitnesses, examined satellite imagery, checked photographs and video, and compared accounts against other evidence. They used a standard called reasonable grounds to believe. That is a serious evidentiary threshold, but it is not the same as a final criminal conviction after trial. Investigators identified what the material supported and urged further legal inquiry. Knowing this distinction does not weaken the findings; it explains their proper weight.

Why compare a refugee's account with images of a village? Because every form of evidence has limits. A witness may remember a particular attack but not know what occurred fifty kilometers away. Satellite images can show that buildings burned or disappeared but cannot by themselves establish who ordered an attack, what a soldier intended, or what someone said. Hospital records can document an injury without explaining the entire history of violence. Corroboration connects partial views. When several accounts describe similar conduct across separated villages, and physical or geospatial evidence supports the chronology, an investigator can test whether an event was isolated or part of a broader pattern.

Sexual violence required particular care. The fact-finding mission and later United Nations reporting documented widespread and systematic assaults against Rohingya women and girls, as well as harm to men and boys. Survivors could face stigma or fear retaliation, which means an absence of a formal complaint cannot be taken as evidence that no assault occurred. Interviews had to consider trauma, consent, confidentiality, translation, and the possible danger of making someone identifiable. The historian's obligation is to make the scale and political significance of this violence intelligible without converting another person's suffering into invented dialogue or spectacle. The people who testified were not merely sources. They were people deciding whether, and under what conditions, to tell what happened.

Questions of legal language are also important. Persecution, deportation, crimes against humanity, and genocide are not synonyms. Genocide under the nineteen forty-eight convention involves certain prohibited acts together with a specific intent to destroy, in whole or in part, a protected group as such. A fact-finding mission can say evidence supports an inference of that intent; a court may later examine legal responsibility under its own procedure. Calling the violence ethnic cleansing describes a pattern of forced removal but does not itself substitute for the elements of a specific criminal charge. Precision protects the account from both denial and exaggerated claims of what a legal body has already decided.

## The other side of the border

Bangladesh received enormous numbers of people in a short period. Communities and authorities around Cox's Bazar, together with local and international organizations, provided emergency shelter, food, water, and medical services. Over time, an emergency response became the management of immense, crowded settlements. Monsoon rains, landslides, fire, infectious disease, insecurity, and shortages of education and livelihood opportunities brought further risks. Refugees themselves organized aid, taught children, shared information, and tried to preserve social life. They were recipients of assistance, but they were also actors with knowledge, labor, political views, and plans of their own.

This created competing obligations and real tensions for Bangladesh. The government faced demands on land, infrastructure, security, and public services in a region that was not wealthy before the arrivals. Host communities also experienced costs and provided support. International funding could provide basic relief but did not offer a durable political status. Most refugees could not simply return home without credible guarantees of safety, rights, and citizenship. Repatriation requires more than arranging transportation across a river. A return becomes voluntary and sustainable only when the conditions people fled have changed enough that they can exercise meaningful choice.

A community's desire for safety is not the same as a willingness to abandon its claim to home. Some Rohingya wanted to return to Myanmar with recognized citizenship and protection; others feared that promises would not be kept. Those positions need to remain distinct to explain the conflict without attributing one group's claims to another. Asking whether a refugee wants to return, without asking under what conditions, turns a political demand for rights into a misleading yes-or-no survey. Bangladesh, Myanmar, aid organizations, and foreign governments could negotiate schedules and memoranda. Refugees still had to face the question of whether their neighbors, homes, and legal status would be secure.

## What the courts could and could not do

International accountability proceeded along more than one legal path. The International Criminal Court, which prosecutes individuals, examined whether it could investigate alleged deportation from Myanmar into Bangladesh. Myanmar was not a party to the Court's founding treaty, but Bangladesh was. In two thousand and eighteen the Court ruled that crimes completed in part on Bangladeshi territory could come within its jurisdiction. In November two thousand and nineteen judges authorized an investigation. This was a jurisdictional decision and an investigation, not a judgment convicting any accused person.

The International Court of Justice had a different role. It hears disputes between states. In November two thousand and nineteen, The Gambia brought a case against Myanmar under the Genocide Convention. In January two thousand and twenty the Court ordered provisional measures requiring Myanmar to prevent acts covered by the Convention, protect evidence, and report on implementation. Provisional measures are binding directions issued while a case proceeds; they are not a final ruling that genocide has been proved on the merits. Keeping the International Criminal Court and International Court of Justice distinct is essential. Their jurisdictions, parties, standards, and available remedies differ.

The legal proceedings took place amid a much slower struggle for safety and belonging. Preserving evidence, naming responsibility, and limiting the risk of further harm can matter even before a final judgment. But a court cannot by itself provide a school, rebuild a destroyed village, or restore trust between communities. Nor should the search for criminal responsibility obscure the political and administrative systems that made persecution possible before the dramatic violence of two thousand and seventeen. Denial of citizenship, restrictions on movement, and failures to protect civilians were historical causes as well as legal questions.

The Rohingya crisis asks us to rethink what a border does. Crossing into Bangladesh could mean physical survival, but did not automatically mean a secure future. Remaining in Myanmar could mean attachment to home, but not recognized citizenship or protection. The evidence gathered by survivors, journalists, human rights workers, and investigators preserves a record of crimes and of choices by institutions. That record cannot restore the years lost to a child growing up displaced. It can, however, prevent a story of hundreds of thousands of people from being reduced to an anonymous migration statistic. The central question is not simply why so many left. It is how people were deprived of the right to live safely where they belonged, and what would be required for that right to be real.
