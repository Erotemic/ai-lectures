# Who Holds Knowledge? Schools, Manuscripts, Translation, and Living Traditions

An examination candidate in late Ming China, a scholar copying an Arabic manuscript in Timbuktu, a Sanskrit specialist working with a Persian-language translator at a Mughal court, and a Pacific navigator teaching an apprentice at sea are not participants in one school. Their training, languages, sponsors, and purposes differ profoundly. Yet all four pose questions that any history of knowledge must answer. How does one person learn what another knows? Who decides that the learner is competent? How does knowledge travel without becoming unrecognizable? And what happens when the authority to answer these questions is concentrated in a state, a court, a household, or a profession?

We often tell the history of the modern world as though knowledge first emerged from darkness with the European printing press and then spread outward. That account confuses a vital development in one region with a universal beginning. Printing in East Asia has a much longer history. Scholarly institutions across Africa and Asia had their own trajectories. Expertise could travel through seafaring apprenticeships, craft shops, women’s household work, itinerant scholars, traders, healers, religious teachers, and bureaucratic examinations. Many of those practices left different kinds of records, which is one reason they appear so differently in our textbooks.

The fact that a source survives is not proof that its knowledge was more sophisticated than knowledge transmitted orally. The fact that something was committed to memory is not proof that it was immune to criticism. We need to study institutions without confusing one institution's preferred evidence with the entire world's intellectual life.

## Printing and examinations in Ming China

The Ming state relied on systems for selecting officials through examinations grounded in classical learning. This institution was much older than the sixteenth century, and it changed over time. Official rank and social standing were bound to success in examinations, but preparation was demanding and access sharply unequal. The system could reward literary accomplishment while also narrowing the questions a candidate was expected to answer. It could support mobility for some families without creating anything like equal educational opportunity.

Now notice what a commercial publisher sees. An examination system creates a large, repeatable demand for books. Candidates need editions of the classics, commentaries, collections of model essays, and advice on how to write. Kai-wing Chow's research into late Ming print culture shows that publishers advertised directly to this market. An advertisement from the 1590s might promise books specifically suited to examination success. The book's seller was not merely passing on timeless learning; the seller was shaping what could be bought as useful knowledge.

This interaction produces a paradox. Examinations make scholarship a matter of public standards, at least within the governing elite, yet they can encourage conformity to a recognizable format. Expanding print markets make more texts available to more readers, yet not everyone receives equal access to education. Some critics at the time complained about rote imitation. The existence of commercial study guides does not prove that every candidate was intellectually passive. Chow cautions that actual reading practices were more varied than that caricature suggests.

Think about the people beyond the candidate. Someone manufactures paper; another cuts woodblocks; someone edits text; a merchant extends credit; a family sacrifices resources to support years of study. Some women received literary education and made intellectual contributions, while the formal civil service examinations overwhelmingly excluded them. The social life of a book is larger than its author's name. An institutional history that stops at the brilliant examinee misses the economy and household effort making scholarship possible.

## Timbuktu and the life of a manuscript

Travel west to Timbuktu, a city connected by trans-Saharan exchange and the Niger River. From at least the later medieval period it developed a significant manuscript and scholarly culture within networks of Islamic learning. Scholars authored, copied, taught, collected, and circulated works. The Library of Congress exhibits manuscripts from families and libraries in the region that treat law, astronomy, mathematics, religious commentary, commerce, and other subjects. These were not accidental scraps from a supposedly illiterate place. They were products of a sustained book culture.

A handwritten book is neither a failed printing press nor an exact opposite of print. It is an object produced through skills and relationships. A scribe needs materials and training. A teacher authorizes particular readings. A household may preserve a collection over generations. Copies travel with traders and scholars. Texts can acquire marginal notes, commentaries, corrections, and new uses. Their authority may be negotiated through recognized teachers, chains of transmission, and the standing of a local scholarly community.

Consider a manuscript of astronomical calculations. The existence of a mathematical text does not tell us precisely who studied it or which calculations were used in daily practice. We can study its contents and material form, compare copies, investigate ownership, and look for independent evidence of teaching. That is what turns a surviving artifact into a defensible history of knowledge. The Library of Congress holdings include a manuscript explaining numerical calculation in astronomy; the exact composition date for this example is uncertain. Evidence has limits, even when the page is right in front of us.

Timbuktu also exposes a problem in our use of the word institution. When scholarship depends partly on households, private libraries, and networks of teachers, it may not resemble a modern university governed by a single campus and set of offices. Calling every scholarly setting a university in the modern sense risks anachronism. Denying that learning was institutionally organized because it looked different is equally mistaken. What matters is the actual form of transmission and recognition.

## Translation as intellectual work at the Mughal court

In the late sixteenth century, the Mughal emperor Akbar sponsored ambitious projects to translate Sanskrit works into Persian. Among them was the Mahabharata, rendered in a Persian version known as the Razmnama. Scholars proficient in different languages cooperated; the result was not a transparent transfer from one container to another. It involved decisions about the meaning of passages, literary style, political language, and how a court could use a translated text.

Akbar's senior minister Abu al-Fazl framed translation as part of an imperial vision of intellectual and religious inquiry. Contemporary research by Christian Blake Pye examines the way this vision appears in Abu al-Fazl's preface. But we should not mistake a minister's praise of enlightened sovereignty for an impartial report of every translator's motives. The emperor was a patron with political interests. The translations served an intellectual project and a vision of empire simultaneously.

Translation also exposed productive friction. A term in Sanskrit might not have an exact Persian counterpart. A narrative understood one way by a specialist might be read differently by a court patron. A translator might simplify prose to make it accessible while another favored a high literary register. Recent scholarship on Mughal retranslations shows that competing expectations could make even an impressive effort fail by the literary standards of its intended audience.

What, then, counts as a faithful translation? Literal wording, recognizable style, philosophical sense, political usefulness, or the intention of a particular patron? There is no single answer independent of the project's purpose. The important point for this course is that knowledge was actively made in a contact zone by collaborators whose skills and authority were unequal. The result cannot be described adequately as either an original revelation of one civilization or a passive borrowing by another.

## People who knew the ocean

Let us move from books to open water. Pacific wayfinding traditions developed complex knowledge of star paths, waves, winds, birds, currents, and the behavior of ocean environments. Such expertise could be taught through apprenticeship, practice, memory, and social obligation. Its credibility was not settled by a written examination in the Ming style or an imperial translation bureau. A navigator demonstrated competence in relation to a real sea and a community whose safety depended on the voyage.

UNESCO's documentation of Carolinian wayfinding emphasizes that canoe construction and navigation embed measurement, environmental observation, and inherited expertise. The tradition is not a museum specimen frozen in 1500. It has been sustained, changed, threatened, and revitalized by living communities. One must avoid pretending that a modern documented technique is necessarily an unchanged record of a precise sixteenth-century voyage. Continuities are real, but their historical specifics need evidence.

The word traditional is often used to suggest that knowledge is opposed to innovation. Yet an effective navigator continually interprets changing conditions. Apprentices learn both remembered routes and how to respond when the sea differs from expectation. That is not an argument that Indigenous navigation and European astronomy used identical methods. It is an argument against grading every intellectual tradition by whether it resembles a particular European instrument or written genre.

The same caution applies to agriculture, land management, medicine, and ecological knowledge in the Americas and Africa. Practices developed through observation and communal transmission may have been extremely consequential even where colonial documents mention them only as incidental services. Some colonizers actively appropriated such expertise while devaluing its holders. Reconstructing who knew what, and how, requires more than treating European publication as the moment a fact entered history.

## The archive favors its own creators

Imagine a research library today that preserves examination essays, a royal translation, and a physician's printed compendium, but few conversations with farmers or sailors. It would be easy to conclude that knowledge was created only by literate elites. That conclusion would be a property of the archive as much as of the world. The people paying for education, growing medicinal plants, navigating ships, and teaching children might be largely invisible in the official record.

This is why the historian must combine evidence. Material objects reveal techniques that formal texts omit. Oral traditions can preserve history and interpretation across generations, but require careful attention to context, performance, and change. Government records show where authorities claimed power, not necessarily how smoothly their orders worked. A book may credit an elite compiler while its content depends upon unnamed contributors. Archaeology can reveal a system of production even where no worker wrote a memoir.

There is a further moral question. Some forms of knowledge were restricted. A family might protect specialized skills from outsiders; a sacred practice might have rules governing disclosure; a ruler might classify a report; a colonial collector might acquire knowledge without consent. The mere ability to extract information is not the same thing as a right to publish it. Historians need to understand who considered knowledge shareable, with whom, and on what terms.

## The politics of what counts as learning

Ming examinations, Timbuktu libraries, Mughal translations, and Pacific apprenticeships show us four different solutions to the problem of maintaining knowledge across people and time. Every one of them combines standards with power. The examination selects and excludes. The manuscript binds intellectual authority to teachers and collections. The court sponsors exchange while making translation serve imperial aims. An apprenticeship grounds expertise in practiced relationships and responsibility to others.

No single example can stand for its whole civilization. Within China there were healers and artisans outside the examination system. Within West Africa there were many intellectual traditions beyond Arabic manuscript scholarship. The Mughal court was not all of South Asian learning. Pacific voyaging was not a solitary Indigenous tradition detached from all others. Those obvious statements are worth saying because a global overview can flatten differences even while celebrating diversity.

If we ask how the modern world acquired knowledge, a catalog of brilliant discoveries is not enough. We must ask how an observation became credible, how it was taught, translated, stored, challenged, and put to work. The crucial actors include teachers, book producers, interpreters, navigators, families, and people whose labor made a celebrated institution function. Modernity did not arrive when the world's different knowledge systems were finally replaced by one. It emerged partly through encounters among them, some collaborative and some violently unequal.
