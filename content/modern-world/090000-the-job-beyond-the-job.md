# The Job Beyond the Job: Wages, Platforms, and Inequality in the 2010s

## A product has more than one workplace

Consider a garment hanging in a shop window in two thousand and fifteen. A customer can see its brand and price, and perhaps the country named on its label. Less visible are the people who spun the fiber, cut the fabric, sewed the seams, packaged the shipment, unloaded containers, arranged the advertising, and cleaned the store before opening. They do not all work for the same employer or under the same law. Their wages are not necessarily a fixed fraction of the final price. This is what a global supply chain makes possible: companies can coordinate the sale of a product while the production risks and responsibilities are distributed among firms, countries, and workers.

The first decade after the global financial crisis of two thousand and eight brought economic recovery to many countries, but recovery in national output was not the same as recovery for every household. A country could produce more while wages grew slowly, unemployment remained high in some regions, or the gains went disproportionately to owners of assets and workers in the most profitable occupations. Meanwhile millions of people in rapidly growing Asian economies experienced meaningful income improvements. The relevant question is therefore not whether the world became richer or poorer in one simple sense. It is who earned more, who bore risks, and which public institutions could turn increased production into security.

To answer, we must separate several measures often called inequality. Inequality among households in one country is not the same as inequality among countries. Wage inequality is not the same as inequality in wealth, which includes property and financial assets. A worker may receive a higher wage but face higher rent, irregular hours, or the loss of health coverage. Even the average wage can rise while the typical worker sees little improvement if high earners receive most of the increase. Economists use different statistics because the questions are different. A single headline about the richest one percent cannot describe every change in living standards without the rest of the distribution.

## The building that collapsed

On April twenty-fourth, two thousand and thirteen, the Rana Plaza building collapsed in Bangladesh, killing more than eleven hundred people, most of them garment workers. The disaster exposed the human cost of producing goods in workplaces where building safety, management accountability, and workers' power were inadequate. Cheap clothing did not require a building to collapse; it required a commercial arrangement in which suppliers were pushed to deliver at a price and deadline, while safety risks could be hidden from distant buyers. But the exact choices of owners, inspectors, managers, governments, brands, and buyers mattered. Calling the disaster the fault of globalization alone would be too vague to identify what could be changed.

After the collapse, international and Bangladeshi organizations helped create factory inspection and safety initiatives. Unions, employers, governments, and brands negotiated programs to address structural hazards, fire safety, compensation, and labor rights. One prominent arrangement, the Accord on Fire and Building Safety in Bangladesh, was signed by global unions and participating brands in May two thousand and thirteen. Other initiatives took different institutional forms. These efforts did not mean every factory immediately became safe or every injury was repaired. They did show how pressure could connect a distant buyer with enforceable responsibilities in production sites that the buyer did not directly own.

Compensation raised another question. A payment made after someone is killed or permanently injured cannot restore the life or health that was lost. Yet compensation may determine whether dependents can pay rent or continue schooling. By two thousand and fifteen, an internationally coordinated fund had obtained the money estimated as necessary for payments under the Rana Plaza arrangement. The process required identifying claimants, assessing harm, and resolving the responsibilities of many participating institutions. It was both a limited remedy and a practical acknowledgment that the commercial benefits of garment production were connected to the workplace risks borne by workers.

## When the workplace becomes an application

During the same decade, digital platforms offered another way to organize labor. A driver could accept rides through a phone. A courier could receive delivery assignments without entering a traditional office. Someone working from home could label images, review text, translate small documents, or complete tasks through an online labor marketplace. These services often made it easier to find customers and gave some workers control over when to log in. But the platform could also set prices, rank workers, decide who saw which assignments, and suspend accounts. A company did not need to own a factory floor to exercise power over the terms of work.

Platform labor took several forms. A ride-hailing driver used a vehicle and interacted physically with customers; an online microtask worker might perform hundreds of tiny tasks for requesters in other countries. Both depended on digital matching, but their hazards and legal relationships were not identical. The International Labour Organization's later survey of online microtask workers found issues involving pay, time spent searching for work, access to social protection, and uncertainty about task availability. When a task takes two minutes but finding it and securing payment takes twenty, pay per completed task does not reveal pay per hour of labor. The denominator matters as much as the advertised rate.

The word flexible could describe genuine convenience, but it could also conceal a transfer of risk. An employer paying a regular wage generally carries some of the cost when business is slow. A platform that pays only for completed tasks may leave waiting time, equipment depreciation, injuries, or sudden loss of access with the worker. In some settings, workers chose such arrangements as supplements to other employment; in others they relied on them for basic income. Regulation had difficulty keeping up because older legal distinctions between employee and independent contractor did not fit every digital arrangement cleanly. A new method of assigning work did not remove the old question of who had the power to set its terms.

## The work that is counted and the work that is not

Not all economically necessary work appears as a wage. Raising children, caring for older relatives, cooking, cleaning, and supporting a sick household member occupy time and require skill even when no one receives a paycheck. In many societies, women performed a disproportionate share of this unpaid care work. The International Labour Organization's analysis of women's work in two thousand and sixteen stressed that access to education had not automatically eliminated inequality in employment or earnings. If one parent can take a paid shift only because another person provides unpaid childcare, the paid job depends on work excluded from the wage record.

Paid domestic workers occupied a related but distinct position. They cared for children and older people, cleaned, cooked, and maintained homes, often as migrants or workers without the protections available in more visible workplaces. International labor research in the early twenty-tens counted tens of millions of domestic workers, many excluded from ordinary rules about rest days, working hours, and social protection. When a household employs a worker in a private home, the workplace can be difficult for inspectors to enter and for the worker to leave or challenge. Making care a paid job does not automatically make it secure, while leaving it unpaid can hide its contribution altogether.

These are not merely accounting disputes. A government deciding whether to invest in childcare, elder care, public transport, and healthcare shapes the range of jobs people can accept. Reliable buses can reduce commuting time; childcare can allow a parent to work more predictable hours; health coverage can make it less dangerous to change employers. A high nominal wage may be less valuable where essential services must be purchased at high cost. Economic security is therefore made partly at the workplace and partly through institutions outside it. The person who earns a salary is never independent of public infrastructure and the unpaid labor of other people.

## The puzzle of global gains

The evidence of the two thousand and tens does not support a story in which all workers lost ground. World Bank research on shared prosperity found positive income growth for the poorest forty percent in a majority of countries in its study, with especially strong average growth in parts of Asia and Latin America. Yet some industrialized countries saw income declines among that lower segment during the measured period. These were averages across selected countries and years, not a direct measurement of every village or family. They matter because global progress and local hardship can coexist. A truthful account needs both the growth that helped people escape poverty and the insecurity experienced by those who did not share in it.

Researchers associated with the World Inequality Report estimated that, over a longer period ending in two thousand and sixteen, the global top one percent captured a much larger share of income growth than the bottom half of the world's population. That finding concerns the distribution of income growth, not the proposition that the bottom half received no gains. It also combines countries with different tax systems, housing markets, wage institutions, and growth histories. Within-country inequality could widen even as the economic gap between some large countries narrowed. Thinking clearly about inequality requires asking whether the comparison is global, national, occupational, or within a single company.

Firms were not homogeneous either. The International Labour Organization's wage research emphasized inequality between workplaces as well as among employees within them. A successful multinational corporation might pay skilled employees high salaries while subcontracting cleaning, security, or transport to companies with different wage scales. A small firm could depend on the orders of a powerful buyer while having little capacity to negotiate prices. This fragmentation complicates simple advice to workers to improve their skills: education can matter tremendously, but it does not decide how bargaining power is distributed across an entire production system.

## How workers tried to change the rules

Workers responded in different ways. Factory employees organized unions and pressed for inspections; domestic workers demanded coverage under labor law; platform workers shared information about pay and challenged arbitrary decisions; and public-sector employees contested spending cuts and the quality of public services. Some campaigns succeeded in changing laws or company practices; others encountered employer resistance, political repression, or low membership. Collective bargaining was particularly difficult where workers had different employers on paper, were spread across cities, or feared that complaining would lead to dismissal. Yet their actions challenged the idea that a labor market was only a set of individual contracts.

There was no universal solution in a single minimum wage, training program, or technology. A minimum wage could raise pay for covered workers, while weak enforcement limited its reach. A new app could improve matching between buyers and sellers while concentrating control over access. Supply-chain audits could detect structural hazards yet miss harassment or the suppression of unions. Better national statistics could make inequality visible without deciding the political bargain needed to change it. Each tool addressed a real part of the problem, but outcomes depended on implementation and who had the authority to enforce promises.

## What a paycheck cannot explain

The history of work in the two thousand and tens was not simply a contest between humans and machines. It was a history of organizations deciding where jobs were located, how income was divided, what risks were insured, and which kinds of labor counted as work. Digital networks changed how assignments could be made, while global supply chains changed who could control production without directly employing those who made a product. Neither arrangement abolished human dependence. Goods still needed to be produced and transported, homes still needed care, and injuries still had consequences that could not be reduced to a line in a balance sheet.

A worker's paycheck is evidence about one relationship. It does not fully explain the worker's hours, bargaining power, housing costs, family obligations, safety, or future security. Measuring those things is harder than counting jobs or announcing a growth rate, but the difficulty does not make them optional. To understand the decade, we need to follow the garment beyond the shop window, the digital task beyond the phone screen, and the paid shift into the network of households and public services that makes it possible. The history of inequality begins when we ask not only what an economy produced, but how people lived while producing it.
