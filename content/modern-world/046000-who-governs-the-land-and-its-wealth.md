# Who Governs the Land and Its Wealth? The Middle East, 1947-1956

## A region after empire

In the years after 1945, political arguments across the Middle East concerned borders, sovereignty, refugees, trade routes, and control of resources. These arguments were related, but they were not one single conflict. In Palestine, British rule was ending and Jewish and Palestinian Arab national movements made incompatible claims about the country's political future. In Iran, control of oil income became a central test of elected government and foreign influence. In Egypt, the operation of the Suez Canal became a question about the remaining power of British and French imperial interests. Calling everything a Cold War dispute would obscure what people in the region had been demanding for decades. Calling everything decolonization would conceal the particular histories of each society.

The problem of Palestine cannot be introduced by imagining an empty land awaiting a political decision. Palestinian Arab communities had villages, towns, agriculture, schools, religious institutions and political organizations. Jewish communities also had long histories in the region, and Jewish immigration and Zionist nation-building had expanded under conditions that included European antisemitism, imperial regulation and the catastrophe of the Holocaust. The British Mandate was an international framework through which Britain governed a territory where these movements came into sharp conflict. As the British prepared to leave, the question was not simply which government would receive a set of offices. It was how two peoples with different national claims would live, or fail to live, within the same territory.

## A proposed partition is not yet a settlement

On November 29, 1947, the United Nations General Assembly adopted Resolution 181. It recommended dividing Palestine into a Jewish state and an Arab state, linked by an economic union, with a special international arrangement proposed for Jerusalem. The vote was 33 in favor, 13 against and 10 abstentions. Those details help expose a common confusion. The Assembly adopted a recommendation and a plan; it did not administer a peaceful partition on the ground. The plan was made by international delegates at a time when local national movements disagreed deeply about legitimacy and territorial distribution. A diplomatic map describes a proposed division of power, not a census of consent by the people living in the proposed territories.

The principal Zionist leadership accepted partition as a basis for creating a Jewish state, while Palestinian Arab leaders and neighboring Arab governments rejected it as unjust. For Jewish national leaders, statehood promised self-determination and refuge after persecution and mass murder. For Palestinians, the allocation of territory and power appeared to deny self-determination to a population already living in the land, particularly where the proposed Jewish state included large Arab communities. These positions were not symmetrical in every respect, and neither political leadership should be mistaken for the voice of every resident. The plan's adoption made the decision more urgent, but it did not resolve the conflict. Violence escalated after the vote, months before Israel declared independence.

After the partition vote, fighting spread among Jewish and Palestinian Arab forces during the final months of British rule. Road access, mixed cities, villages and routes to Jerusalem became strategically important. The conflict was already changing civilian life before the armies of neighboring states entered. In the weeks and months that followed, people fled attacks and fear, and others were expelled. The Palestinian refugee crisis unfolded differently from town to town and campaign to campaign. No single order explains every departure, and leaving under threat did not mean surrendering claims to homes and property.

On May 14, 1948, David Ben-Gurion announced the establishment of the State of Israel as British rule ended. The declaration presented Jewish history and persecution as reasons for statehood and promised immigration, religious freedom and equality of civil and political rights for citizens. It also addressed the Arab inhabitants of the new state with an appeal for peace and citizenship. This text should be read as a primary political document: it tells us what the state's founders declared and the principles to which they publicly committed themselves. It does not establish by itself how those principles operated under wartime conditions, or how people who rejected the partition experienced the new state's creation.

Armies from neighboring Arab states then entered the conflict. The war crossed a new threshold, involving states with different military capacities, interests and domestic political situations. Israeli forces fought for the survival and territorial position of the new state. Palestinian Arab communities faced the destruction of local institutions and a widening refugee crisis, while political and military organizations within and beyond Palestine pursued competing strategies. The historian's task is not to reduce this sequence to a single uninterrupted battle between two homogeneous populations. The violence had a phase before May 14 and a phase after it; it included internal fighting, the actions of outside armies, and changing control of towns and transport routes.

## The Nakba and the refugee question

Palestinians call the mass displacement and destruction associated with 1948 the Nakba, the catastrophe. More than seven hundred thousand Palestinians fled or were displaced from homes during the war, according to the United Nations relief agency's historical account. People reached the West Bank, Gaza, Lebanon, Jordan, Syria and elsewhere; others remained inside the new Israeli state, sometimes separated from homes and property. The total is a retrospective estimate based on different registers and categories, not a name-by-name count that every historian defines in exactly the same way. Still, the displacement on a mass scale is a historical fact. Disagreements over detailed counts do not make those people disappear from the record.

A statistical figure tells us something essential about scale, but not what an individual place meant to its residents. The Palestinian Oral History Archive at the American University of Beirut preserves first-generation refugee accounts of pre-1948 life, flight, work, family and the loss of home. Its purpose is not only to collect descriptions of military events. It preserves the social geography of towns and villages whose histories cannot be recovered from diplomatic maps. The archive itself is evidence that people have struggled to keep those histories legible across generations. We should not invent a particular refugee's words from a catalog description; to tell a personal story faithfully we would need to listen to and cite that person's recorded account.

Historians have argued over the degree to which expulsions, direct military assaults, fear of violence, political collapse and instructions from different authorities explain particular departures. Some claims once repeated in official national histories have been challenged through the opening of archives and the collection of Palestinian oral histories. A review published by Sciences Po describes how the historiography of 1948 became entangled with competing national narratives, then changed as different researchers asked new questions. This does not mean that truth is merely a matter of choosing a side. It means that each specific historical assertion needs evidence appropriate to its scale. A documented expulsion from one locality cannot automatically explain every departure, while an example of flight cannot negate documented expulsions elsewhere.

The United Nations tried to address the consequences while fighting continued. Its mediator, Folke Bernadotte, reported in July 1948 on the urgent need for a ceasefire and a political settlement. A later General Assembly resolution, 194 of December 11, stated that refugees wishing to return to their homes and live at peace with their neighbors should be permitted to do so at the earliest practicable date, and that compensation should be provided in specified circumstances. The wording and legal significance of this resolution have remained subjects of dispute. What we can say directly is that the Assembly addressed return and compensation as unresolved problems and that a recommendation did not itself arrange transport, restore property or settle the claims of residents and states.

In December 1949, the General Assembly established the United Nations Relief and Works Agency for Palestine Refugees in the Near East, later known as UNRWA, to provide relief and works assistance. Its creation is a direct measure of the persistence of the crisis after the major battles. The existence of a dedicated agency did not decide the conflict's constitutional questions. It organized humanitarian response to people whose home, political membership and prospects for return remained contested. Refugee relief and a political settlement are related but different tasks. The former can provide vital services without giving an answer to the latter.

## Jewish refuge and other histories of displacement

Jewish statehood also had to be understood in the aftermath of the Holocaust. Hundreds of thousands of Jewish survivors remained displaced in Europe after 1945, facing uncertainty, immigration restrictions and difficulty returning home. For many, migration to Israel promised safety and the possibility of a Jewish political future. The United States Holocaust Memorial Museum describes how the new state removed restrictions on Jewish immigration and received survivors. This history is part of the explanation for the urgency felt by Jewish communities, while the history of Palestinian displacement is essential to understanding the consequences of the state's foundation. Neither should be used to erase the other. They are different sets of events whose relationships require explanation rather than rhetorical cancellation.

Another often neglected history concerns long-established Jewish communities in Arab countries, whose experiences cannot be reduced to European immigration. In Iraq, Jewish life had roots stretching many centuries into the past. Anti-Jewish violence in Baghdad in 1941, followed by growing insecurity and discriminatory pressures around the 1948 war, contributed to a major departure. The Iraqi Jewish Archive at the U.S. National Archives records that most members of the community left during 1950 and 1951. Historians such as Esther Meir-Glitzenstein show that the migrations of Iraqi, Yemeni, Libyan and other Jewish communities occurred in different political circumstances. The dates, mechanisms and motives should be studied separately, not flattened into a universal regional story.

These histories of refuge are not interchangeable. A Holocaust survivor who emigrated from a European camp, an Iraqi Jew who left a centuries-old community, and a Palestinian family driven from its village in 1948 could each experience the rupture of home under distinct institutions and pressures. The existence of one group does not resolve the property claims or political rights of another. A historian can take multiple communities' histories seriously without pretending that all experiences had the same causes or legal status. Keeping those distinctions visible is part of treating people as more than demographic counters in an international argument.

## Oil: the question of who owns an industry

The struggle over resources offered another path into postwar sovereignty. In Iran, the Anglo-Iranian Oil Company had long operated within an unequal political and economic relationship. Iranian critics challenged the division of revenues and the influence of a foreign-backed enterprise on the country's future. The Majlis, Iran's parliament, moved to nationalize the oil industry in 1951, and Prime Minister Mohammad Mosaddeq became the internationally prominent leader of this effort. Nationalization meant a government asserting control of an asset and revenues previously governed by a concessionary relationship. It did not settle how to operate the industry, obtain buyers, or meet the resistance of the British government. Those practical problems became part of a constitutional crisis in Iran.

Mosaddeq's government faced opposition from several directions: international economic pressure, the Iranian monarchy and its supporters, rival domestic factions, and disagreement about how to exercise constitutional authority. One error would be to imagine Iran as a passive stage on which Britain and the United States alone acted. Iranian political movements were central to the crisis. Another error would be to describe foreign intervention as an unsupported rumor. The U.S. State Department's later published diplomatic record includes a section on the planning and implementation of Operation TPAJAX, a covert American effort to remove Mosaddeq. It is important that some relevant original operational cables were lost or destroyed; the surviving record is strong evidence of intervention without being a complete transcript of every event in Tehran.

An internal CIA memorandum dated March 1954 described a program to install a pro-Western Iranian government and stated that the operation succeeded in removing Mosaddeq in August 1953. That is unusually direct evidence that state officials later recognized a covert role in the overthrow. It does not mean that every street demonstration was invented abroad, or that Iranian opponents of Mosaddeq had no separate aims. The coup's significance lies in the intersection of domestic constitutional conflict and foreign action directed at removing a prime minister who had gained parliamentary backing for a contested resource policy. A government could claim sovereignty over oil while powerful foreign states acted to influence who would govern it.

## Suez: a canal and the limits of empire

Egypt's Suez Canal presented a related but different problem. The canal connected Mediterranean and Red Sea shipping, making it an artery of global commerce. Its company had been shaped by nineteenth-century concessions and European financial control. Egyptian nationalists had long challenged British military and political influence in the country. On July 26, 1956, Egyptian president Gamal Abdel Nasser announced the nationalization of the Suez Canal Company. The Egyptian decree transferred the company's assets and rights to the state and included provisions about compensation and continued operation. The central claim was that a facility located in Egypt would be controlled by Egyptian authorities, rather than remaining under a corporate arrangement tied to foreign influence.

British and French leaders treated the nationalization as a political and strategic threat. Israel had its own security disputes with Egypt. Representatives of Britain, France and Israel met secretly at Sèvres, agreeing on arrangements under which Israeli forces would attack across Sinai and Britain and France would intervene around the canal. The U.S. diplomatic record reconstructs the consultations while noting that participants' later accounts differed in their descriptions of the agreement. The attack began in late October 1956, followed by British and French military action. This was not an impartial peacekeeping intervention. It was a coordinated attempt to impose a military solution to a dispute in which all three states had distinct interests.

International reaction helped bring the intervention to a halt. The United States opposed the British-French-Israeli operation, partly from strategic concerns about regional instability and Soviet involvement, even as it maintained its own powerful interests in the region. United Nations diplomacy and pressure contributed to a ceasefire and withdrawal. The crisis exposed how far Britain and France's capacity to impose their preferred solution had diminished. Yet the outcome should not be narrated as the disappearance of external influence. Financial pressure, military alliances, oil markets and the politics of aid continued to shape what choices regional governments could make. Suez changed the form of foreign power more quickly than it ended foreign power.

## The common problem, the different histories

Palestine, Iran and Egypt were not three versions of the same event. Palestine involved competing national projects and war, the establishment of Israel, and the mass displacement of Palestinians. Iran involved a dispute over oil nationalization, constitutional authority and covert intervention. Egypt's Suez confrontation involved state control of a strategic canal and coordinated foreign military attack. All three demonstrate why formal sovereignty is not the same as control over the institutions, resources and security on which independence depends. But making that comparison should preserve the particular people, laws, dates and choices in each place.

The United Nations appears repeatedly in this story, but in several different roles. It recommended partition without being able to impose a peaceful settlement. It appointed mediators and later created institutions for refugee relief. It took part in crisis diplomacy around Suez. Those actions should not be collapsed into a judgment that the organization either solved or caused every problem. The first question is what authority an institution had. The second is what its members and affected populations wanted. The third is what actually happened after a resolution, decree or ceasefire was announced. Keeping those questions distinct lets us see where international rules mattered and where they left conflicts unfinished.

The most important documents from these years are not all the same kind. The Israeli declaration states its founders' commitments. Palestinian oral histories preserve the worlds disrupted by war. A United Nations resolution makes a proposal or recommendation. An Iranian nationalization law asserts control over a resource. A CIA memorandum documents a foreign power's intervention. An Egyptian decree and the diplomatic records of Suez reveal competing claims to authority. A serious history does not force them into one simple storyline. It brings each source to the question it can actually answer, then asks how people living with these decisions experienced their consequences.

## Source method and unresolved questions (display only)

Each source is attributed to its author or institution. Diplomatic, archival, and legal texts document what officials recorded or enacted, not universal consent or direct proof of implementation. Publisher abstracts and catalog descriptions are identified as such and are not misrepresented as books or archival files read in full. An institutional description of an oral-history collection is not used to invent, quote, or merge individual testimonies. Historical debates are distinguished from settled document chronology. All source locators and known limits are retained in the ledger and metadata. No arbitrary lecture length requirement is used.
