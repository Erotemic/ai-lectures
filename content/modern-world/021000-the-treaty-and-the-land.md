# The Treaty and the Land: Indigenous Sovereignty in Settler Expansions

## What the line on a map leaves out

Take a nineteenth-century map with a thick line separating a state from a region labeled frontier. The line implies that the land on one side has a government and the land beyond it has yet to be politically organized. Now replace the map with a record of treaties, local communities, cultivated fields, ceremonial places, family responsibilities, trade routes, and rivers with names already known to those who live beside them. Nothing about the second record is empty. The difference is not between government and no government, or civilization and wilderness. It is between rival ways of describing who has authority and what land means. That is the starting point for understanding settler expansion and Indigenous resistance.

Settler colonialism is a useful analytic term when an expanding political community seeks durable settlement and jurisdiction over land already inhabited and governed by others. But it is a framework, not a substitute for finding out what happened in particular places. Aotearoa New Zealand, the United States, and Australia have distinct legal histories and relationships among Indigenous peoples and settler governments. A treaty in one place must not be used as though it described a land conflict in another. We can compare mechanisms—acquisition, coercion, settlement, law, and cultural transformation—without pretending that all participants lived through one generic event.

One principle will recur throughout the lecture. Indigenous sovereignty was not invented by a colonial treaty. The National Museum of the American Indian explains that the United States negotiated with Native nations precisely because they were independent political communities. Māori rangatira entered their own diplomatic negotiations before and during British colonization. Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander peoples defended their countries under their own traditions of authority. If a record mentions a treaty, it is evidence of a political relationship; it is not proof that sovereignty previously belonged to no one.

## Two versions of a foundational agreement

In 1840 representatives of the British Crown and many Māori rangatira entered an agreement known as Te Tiriti o Waitangi, or the Treaty of Waitangi. Two texts exist, one in te reo Māori and one in English. The Waitangi Tribunal publishes both, including an English translation of the Māori text. It is essential to read them together. They are not interchangeable versions of a document that everyone understood in precisely the same way. In the English first article, the chiefs cede sovereignty. In the Māori text, the term is kāwanatanga, a form of governance. That difference is not a small spelling issue. It bears directly on what each side believed it was authorizing.

The second article deepens the distinction. The Māori text guarantees tino rangatiratanga, an expression of chiefly authority, concerning lands, homes, and taonga—treasured things. The English text emphasizes possession of lands, forests, fisheries, and other properties. Archives New Zealand and the Waitangi Tribunal explain these terms and the disputes that have followed from them. We should not pretend a brief English paraphrase captures every Māori understanding, nor say that the two texts can be resolved merely by choosing the word that appears in the English version. A translation can define the terms through which an imperial government claims power while failing to preserve the political concept used by its treaty partners.

To study this responsibly, distinguish an agreement from its interpretation and its subsequent enforcement. We have a treaty with two texts, accounts of the negotiations, later land purchases, legislation, conflict, and decisions by bodies established to examine claims. Those are separate forms of evidence. The Waitangi Tribunal's work is important precisely because present-day inquiries must examine questions that cannot be settled by reading a document in isolation. The treaty did not eliminate Māori governments, local interests, political choices, or disputes; nor does its signature prove that every later action taken in the Crown's name was consistent with its promises.

## Land, war, and confiscation in Aotearoa

Conflict increased as colonial settlement pressed into areas governed by Māori communities. New Zealand's historical record identifies wars beginning in Taranaki in 1860 and fighting in Waikato in 1863 and 1864. The Kīngitanga, or Māori King movement, formed in part around the effort to preserve Māori authority and land. It is misleading to describe Māori resistance as a reflex against change. Leaders confronted a concrete political question: could a colonial government expand its jurisdiction and settlement while Māori maintained meaningful authority over their own land and communities? Different iwi and hapū answered the pressures differently, just as European settlers and imperial officials disagreed among themselves.

The New Zealand Settlements Act of 1863 permitted confiscation of land from Māori labeled rebels. NZ History's chronology records confiscations and shows that their effects did not fall neatly along lines of military participation; communities regarded as neutral or loyal also suffered losses. The British state's legal description of rebellion did not erase the grievances of those whose land was taken. Nor does a statute authorizing confiscation establish the justice of a particular confiscation. It shows how a government converted a political and military conflict into a mechanism for transferring land to settlers. This is a recurring feature of territorial expansion: violence establishes a new balance of power, and law then works to make that balance durable.

But it is equally important not to flatten Māori experiences into a single story of defeat. Māori communities negotiated, fought, sustained political movements, maintained institutions and language, and contested land losses across generations. The survival of a claim is itself evidence of continuing political life. If a later tribunal investigates an earlier act, it is asking how institutions and obligations carried forward, not treating Indigenous peoples as remnants who vanished when a boundary moved. A historian can recognize dispossession without making dispossession the only event that happened to the people involved.

## The United States: treaties and breaking treaties

Across North America, the National Museum of the American Indian's Nation to Nation exhibition places treaties at the center of United States–Native nation relations. It emphasizes that the United States negotiated because Native nations possessed political independence. Treaties committed parties to boundaries, obligations and protections. But as settler population and state power expanded, many treaties were coerced or broken, and the federal government increasingly imposed removal, reservations and other policies. The history is not explained by calling treaty-making either completely honorable or wholly meaningless. The existence of the agreement matters; the failure to observe it matters too.

Take removal in the first half of the nineteenth century. The National Museum's educational account of Indian Removal traces the federal law of 1830, contested state claims, the Cherokee Nation's struggles, and the displacement of Indigenous communities. This material belongs in our larger story even though our main focus is later decades. It illustrates an important mechanism: a government can use negotiations, statutes, judicial decisions and force together, while people affected by those actions continue to petition, litigate, organize and resist. The law is not simply a peaceful alternative to violence when it creates the machinery by which a community is removed.

The Dawes Act of 1887 marked a further transformation in federal policy. Rather than negotiate exclusively through tribal land arrangements, the law provided for allotting portions of reservation land to individuals. The National Archives publishes the act and explains its official assimilationist ambitions and its contribution to major losses of tribal land. Here again legal terms matter. Allotment was presented as a means of making Indigenous people individual property holders and farmers; it also undermined collective landholding and created opportunities for non-Native acquisition. The law had exceptions and was extended through further acts. It would be inaccurate to describe every Native nation as governed by one identical allotment measure from 1887 onward.

The legal categories themselves tell us how policymakers imagined a desirable future. The state wanted to replace relationships of collective territorial authority with registries of individual property holders, standardized paperwork and an agricultural ideal shaped by white American expectations. But a legal reform in property ownership is also a reform in political power. Who can speak for the community? Who receives an allotment? What happens to land considered surplus? What about someone whose identity does not fit the official roll? A detailed enrollment record can be invaluable to later genealogical research and still have originated in a system that constrained self-determination.

Another dimension of that policy was education. The U.S. Department of the Interior's Federal Indian Boarding School Initiative documents a school system intended to assimilate Native children and suppress Indigenous languages and practices. Its investigation relies on many archival sources and identifies enduring harm. The point is not to imply that every school or child's experience was identical. It is to recognize the relationship between land policy and efforts to remake families and communities. A state that seeks jurisdiction over Indigenous territory may also seek control over how the next generation learns its history, language and obligations. Education becomes a field in which sovereignty is contested.

## Australia and the problem of a vanishing record

In Australia, settlement expanded through forms of dispossession and violence not organized around a single nationwide treaty like Te Tiriti. Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander peoples possessed many different relationships to Country, and resistance took different forms across the continent. The Australian Institute of Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander Studies teaches the frontier wars as conflicts in which Indigenous people defended their homelands. That matters for historical framing. If a school narrative describes only explorers and pastoral expansion, it leaves out the people whose land was entered and who fought to protect it.

The University of Newcastle's Colonial Frontier Massacres research project, documented by AIATSIS, reconstructs sites of mass killing through multiple types of historical evidence. Such work is demanding because official and settler records can conceal violence, report it euphemistically, or preserve testimony unevenly. The resulting map is an evidence-based research product, not a perfect enumeration of all deaths or a complete account of every community. We should resist both the temptation to turn a partial archive into a total number without qualification and the opposite temptation to treat missing records as evidence that an event did not occur.

Indigenous accounts and research partnerships are crucial here. A colonial official may record a conflict as a policing incident. A community may remember it as an attack on a place of obligation, kinship and ceremony. The historian must assess the provenance of each account rather than granting automatic superiority to the record most closely resembling a government form. AIATSIS's research resources emphasize that the cultural and ethical context of materials matters as well as their informational content. Some knowledge is not public simply because a researcher wishes to fill a silence. Respect for continuing communities is compatible with rigorous historical inquiry.

## Three regimes, several common questions

Set the cases side by side without making them identical. In Aotearoa, a treaty whose two texts diverged became central to later arguments over governance and land. In the United States, treaty relations were followed by federal efforts to convert collective lands into individual holdings and by boarding-school policies. In Australia, conquest and settlement produced a different documentary landscape, including frontier-war evidence that researchers still reconstruct. In each case state officials could describe their actions as lawful or civilizing. The historian needs to ask whether those descriptions explain the interests and authority of people who did not consent to the changes.

An exercise can make this concrete. Imagine an archive containing a treaty page, a property survey, a school attendance roll, and a map of a military campaign. None is a neutral summary of sovereignty. A treaty records negotiation and claims; a survey identifies land within a particular administrative framework; a school roll records institutional control over children; a military map gives the movements of forces. The documents might overlap in geography but answer different questions. Before accepting the historian's story, ask which voices each document includes and which it structurally tends to omit. The archive is not a box of ready-made conclusions.

The central conclusion is not that the modern nation-state was inevitable, nor that Indigenous political life ended with colonial expansion. It is that nineteenth-century states acquired and exercised power through settlement, treaties, wars, property law and schooling, while Indigenous peoples negotiated, resisted, adapted and continued their claims. Their political histories are not preliminary chapters to someone else's national history. They belong at the center of any account of how the modern world was made. A map can show where a government says its borders lie. It cannot, by itself, answer who has the right to rule, or whose promises remain binding.
