# The World Around 1500: A Map with More Than One Center

Where would you place the center of the world in the year 1500?

That is a trick question, but not because there was no such thing as a center. Many people lived at the center of worlds that were real to them: a capital and its dependent towns, a sacred landscape, an ocean where ships connected familiar ports, a chain of cultivated valleys, or a river whose flooding ordered the agricultural year. The trouble begins when we take one of those centers and insist that it must be the center of everybody's history.

Imagine spreading a large map across a table and placing a marker at Lisbon. You could tell a history of Atlantic sailing, royal patronage, ships, and the competition for African gold and Asian spices. That would be a valuable history. But move the marker to Gujarat on India's western coast, and the story changes. Merchants there dealt with the enormous circulation of people and goods through the Indian Ocean, a trade system that did not wait for Portuguese ships to exist. Move it again to Tenochtitlan, the great city in the Basin of Mexico. It was the center of a tributary empire with its own conflicts, ambitions, markets, religious institutions, and opponents. Move it to Timbuktu or Gao, cities in a West African political and commercial world linked through the Sahara to North Africa and beyond. Or move it to the Peruvian highlands, where the Inka state governed a growing empire through roads, labor obligations, administrative arrangements, and negotiation with subject communities.

No single position lets us see all of these societies at their own scale. That is the difficulty, and also the attraction, of world history.

The year 1500 is useful as a vantage point because important connections were changing. But it is not the moment at which a previously disconnected humanity suddenly discovered that it shared a planet. Most human communities had long histories before this date, and many parts of Afro-Eurasia were tied together by dense networks of trade, warfare, scholarship, and migration. The Americas had their own extensive political and exchange systems. Pacific navigators had traversed distances that would challenge sailors in other traditions. The histories were not identical, and contact between them was very uneven.

Our task is to understand both truths at once: the world contained many developed, interacting centers, and the sixteenth century began transformations that connected some of them in new and often coercive ways. The most useful first question is not, 'Who was ahead?' It is, 'What structures were already in motion, and what changed when particular people attempted to connect, conquer, or reorganize them?'

## A world with networks, not one single network

Start with the Indian Ocean, because it corrects a surprisingly persistent mistake: the assumption that Europeans invented the long-distance maritime commerce of the early modern period.

Picture several voyages rather than one straight line across a map. A ship might move from ports on the Arabian Peninsula toward western India. Another might bring textiles from Gujarat to markets farther east. Merchants, pilots, brokers, craftsmen, and port authorities at the Strait of Malacca made it possible to pass between the Indian Ocean and the South China Sea. Along the coast of East Africa, Swahili-speaking trading towns had relationships with inland producers and with merchants reaching from Arabia to India and farther away. A porcelain fragment excavated at an East African site may help document these connections. It does not prove that a Chinese merchant personally handed the vessel to the person who used it. Objects could pass through many hands. That is precisely what a trading network means.

The routes were shaped by geography and knowledge. Monsoon wind patterns made long-distance journeys possible in particular seasons, and sailors accumulated the expertise to work with them. Local rulers taxed trade, protected or attacked ports, and sought alliances. Religious affiliation could provide common institutions across distances, but no religious or ethnic group owned the whole network. Arabic, Persian, Gujarati, Malay, and many other languages were part of its life.

Kilwa Kisiwani, on the coast of present-day Tanzania, offers a concrete example. Its urban remains include substantial stone buildings and a history of prosperity tied to Indian Ocean commerce. UNESCO describes its connections with Arabia, India, and China, with gold and ivory moving outward and imported goods arriving from far beyond the coast. None of that makes Kilwa merely a stopping point on the way to somewhere more important. Its political leaders, merchants, builders, and surrounding communities made choices that shaped the system.

It is equally important not to mistake the Indian Ocean for the whole world. The overland routes of Central Asia, the trading cities of the Sahara, the Mediterranean, the Baltic, and regional circuits within the Americas all had their own logics. A merchant in northern India could be richly connected to one part of Eurasia and know very little about political life in Mexico. 'Connected' is always a claim that needs a route, a period, and an explanation of what traveled.

## China, and the difference between official policy and economic life

The Ming dynasty had ruled China since 1368. Its court administered a large realm with elaborate institutions, an educated bureaucratic elite, regional economies, craft production, and long-standing commercial relationships. European countries around 1500 could not simply dictate its foreign policy. China was neither an untouched civilization waiting to be opened nor a static empire outside historical change.

During the early fifteenth century, the Ming court sponsored the famous voyages associated with Zheng He, whose fleets reached across parts of the Indian Ocean between 1405 and 1433. Scholars still debate the mixture of diplomacy, prestige, political power, and commercial interests behind them, as well as the causes of their cessation. It would be wrong to tell this history as a simple competition in which China chose to stop exploring and Europe chose to begin. States funded voyages for different purposes; merchant shipping did not follow exactly the same timetable as state-sponsored expeditions; and maritime communities continued to operate amid shifts in government policy.

The distinction between a dynasty's regulations and the lives of its subjects will matter repeatedly in this course. A law restricting trade is not proof that nobody traded. A court's declaration does not describe the whole countryside. And an imperial map claiming territory says something about political aspirations, not always about the reach of everyday administration.

Think of a history textbook that gives a ruler a full page and a river delta one sentence. The ruler's decree may have survived in an official archive; the delta's farmers may be harder to recover by name. Yet it is their labor, negotiation, transport, and resistance that often determine how far policy travels. A global history has to read the decree while also asking what happened beyond the archive's preferred field of vision.

## African histories cannot be reduced to the Atlantic slave trade

Now turn westward to the great curve of the Niger River. By the late fifteenth century the Songhai empire had grown under Sonni Ali, and Askia Muhammad came to power in 1493. Its territories, armies, markets, and scholarly centers formed a major West African political world. Timbuktu's importance involved trade and Islamic learning, but the region contained many other communities and institutions that did not fit into the story of a single court.

Salt, gold, agricultural goods, manuscripts, textiles, and people moved through the wider networks connecting the Sahel to North Africa. Some movement was voluntary exchange; some was compelled. Slavery existed in forms within African and other societies before Atlantic plantation slavery expanded, but these histories cannot be flattened into one unchanging institution. Who was enslaved, who claimed ownership, what labor was demanded, what legal distinctions were made, and where people were moved all require a specific context.

Looking only backward from the later Atlantic slave trade creates a second distortion: it turns African history into preparation for a European catastrophe. But Songhai's conflicts, West African scholarly traditions, the politics of coastal and inland states, and the histories of artisans, farmers, religious leaders, and merchants deserve to be understood in their own terms. They did not exist merely to enter someone else's narrative in the sixteenth century.

Elsewhere on the continent, the Kongo kingdom, the Ethiopian highlands, the Swahili coast, and numerous other states and communities followed distinct trajectories. Some encountered Portuguese traders or ambassadors around this period; others had little direct contact with them. The continent was not a political unit, just as Europe was not one. Even the labels 'Africa' and 'Asia' are too large to explain local decisions without further work.

This suggests a useful discipline for world history: when a chapter introduces a region as a source of a commodity—gold, pepper, ivory, labor—ask immediately who lived there, who controlled production, who negotiated terms, and whose experience the commodity label conceals.

## Multiple American worlds before conquest

On the other side of the Atlantic, the island societies of the Caribbean, the cities and kingdoms of Mesoamerica, the varied communities of North America, and the Andean states were parts of histories not organized around Iberian voyages.

In central Mexico, Tenochtitlan was the capital of a powerful empire that demanded tribute from many subject polities. The city had large marketplaces, religious and political institutions, craft specialists, and carefully organized urban life. Tributary power was not the same as universal consent. Rival states and communities had reasons to resist the Mexica-led alliance, and those rivalries would matter profoundly in the Spanish invasion and the alliances that followed. The later conquest cannot be explained simply by comparing a handful of weapons. It involved Indigenous political choices, armed conflict, epidemic disease, divisions among rivals, and brutal colonial action.

In the Andes, the Inka state, called Tawantinsuyu, connected diverse environments and populations along a remarkable system of roads. The Qhapaq Ñan was not an ornamental highway. Roads, bridges, stations, and administrative practices helped move people, resources, orders, and obligations across difficult terrain. The Smithsonian's account of this infrastructure emphasizes the scale and planning of a system created before the Spanish conquest. But the word 'Inka' should not erase the many peoples living within and around the empire, including those who opposed its demands.

Neither the Mexica nor the Inka were representative of every Indigenous American society. They were particular imperial formations among many. Smaller settlements could have sophisticated agricultural and ecological knowledge without belonging to a large conquest state. Peoples maintained trading relations, diplomatic obligations, and cultural practices that did not require a centralized empire to be significant.

For a listener encountering these histories for the first time, one habit is especially helpful. Resist describing people in the Americas only as the subjects of what happened after Europeans arrived. A person living in a city in 1490 did not wake up believing their world was the 'pre-contact era.' That label belongs to a later historical account. Their immediate concerns included harvests, kinship, ritual, politics, conflict, disease, and the future as they understood it.

## The Pacific was also a world of skilled navigation

There is another risk in drawing the world around 1500. If the story is only about the Mediterranean, the Indian Ocean, and the Atlantic, the Pacific becomes a large blank space on the page. But the vast ocean was not empty of human history or navigational expertise.

Pacific communities developed sophisticated ways of sailing and wayfinding, using observations of stars, winds, swells, birds, and other environmental patterns. Their migration and voyaging traditions had already connected widely separated island worlds. Navigation knowledge was taught and preserved in social traditions, not necessarily in the kinds of written records favored by European historians. UNESCO's work with Pacific wayfinding communities makes clear that this is living expertise as well as a subject of historical study.

Here a historian has to hold two ideas apart. A region can have a sophisticated system of maritime movement without being part of the same trading network as Gujarat or Lisbon. Contact does not have to be global to be historically significant. And the fact that one archive preserves more maps or logbooks than another tells us something about surviving evidence, not automatically about the relative skill of the people who crossed the ocean.

## Empires were changing, not standing in a queue to become modern

The turn of the sixteenth century also sits between major political changes in Afro-Eurasia. Ottoman rulers had already built a powerful state in southeastern Europe and western Asia, and the conquest of Constantinople in 1453 reshaped regional politics. The Safavid dynasty established its rule in Iran beginning in 1501. The Mughal state in northern India was founded in 1526. So a map precisely dated 1500 should not present the Safavid and Mughal empires as already established in their later form. That may seem like a small chronological correction, but it protects our understanding of history as a process rather than a static arrangement of color-coded territories.

Religion was integral to these transformations, and not merely as a banner carried by an army. Islamic scholarly networks, Sufi traditions, legal institutions, and merchant communities connected places across and beyond imperial boundaries. Christianity also had multiple centers and institutions, as did Buddhist, Hindu, and other religious traditions. Religious ideas were argued about, translated, institutionalized, reformed, and adapted. Communities rarely experienced them in one way. When rulers announced religious authority, believers could assent, reinterpret, negotiate, or resist.

Likewise, political authority depended on more than gunpowder. Artillery and firearms mattered in several expanding empires, but so did taxation, land tenure, supply, fortifications, alliances, language, and administration. The convenient term 'gunpowder empires' can direct our attention to one technology while obscuring how regimes endured or why they came apart.

This is where a historical date can become deceptive. If we say, 'The world in 1500 was becoming modern,' we risk inventing a finish line whose shape is known only to the narrator. The communities we have described were not taking turns in a race toward Europe's later institutions. They were solving problems, making conflicts, and altering their own environments under constraints they could not fully foresee.

## What became different after 1500?

There were real changes, and some were immense. Sustained Atlantic contact connected biological and political systems whose long histories had been largely separate. New transoceanic movements of plants, animals, pathogens, and people transformed diets and environments. European imperial conquests brought violence, dispossession, and colonial institutions to Indigenous lands. Coerced labor systems expanded, including plantation economies that demanded the enslavement and transport of Africans across the Atlantic. Silver from American mines later moved into commercial circuits reaching Asia; the consequences involved mine laborers, officials, sailors, merchants, and people who might never see a piece of American silver directly.

But the mechanism matters more than a triumphant slogan about 'globalization.' Some connections were created by coercion rather than trade between equals. Some produced opportunities for people who exercised ingenuity within new constraints; others carried epidemics and destruction. The same ship route could distribute a useful crop, deliver armed settlers, and transport people against their will. Networks amplify choices made under unequal power.

Neither must we say that the older networks simply disappeared. Indian merchants continued to trade; West African states continued to pursue local strategies; Indigenous peoples maintained and reconstructed social worlds through devastating change; Pacific communities did not stop being maritime societies. Historical transformations combine rupture and continuity. To understand their human consequences we have to ask: what persisted, what was redirected, who benefited, who was harmed, and which claims can the evidence actually support?

The next history is not therefore a story of Europeans entering a vacant world. It is a story of Europeans, Africans, Asians, Americans, and Pacific peoples encountering shifting systems—sometimes directly, sometimes through the movement of products and forces, often under radically unequal conditions. At any given moment different people had different capacities to decide what would happen next.

## How we know, and where the evidence is uneven

One last point changes how the entire course should be heard. When we say 'world history,' we are claiming to reconstruct past worlds from evidence that has survived selectively.

An imperial archive may record what rulers wished their governors to do. It may say less about what governors actually did or how ordinary people understood their actions. A European travel account may accurately describe a harbor's buildings and misunderstand the relationships among its inhabitants. A conquered community's testimony may survive only through a hostile court record, while living oral traditions preserve memories and identities that a colonial clerk did not value. Archaeology recovers cities, ceramics, plants, roads, and changes to landscapes. None of these is transparent on its own. The work lies in comparing them and asking what each kind of source is capable of showing.

Suppose a pottery fragment from one region appears in an excavation in another. We can reasonably infer movement of an object, given a sound archaeological context and identification. To infer the language of the person who carried it, we need more. To infer that the two regions belonged to one unified market, we need much more. Evidence supports claims at different scales. Good world history moves between scales deliberately rather than letting an attractive big picture swallow the details.

Or suppose a chronicle describes a ruler's magnificent pilgrimage. The account may tell us about wealth, religious authority, political reputation, and the conventions of praise. It does not, by itself, tell us what every subject of the ruler thought. A historian has to understand the chronicler's purposes as well as the event being recorded.

This care is not a barrier to telling a compelling story. It makes the story more convincing. The great question of the early modern world is how people with existing histories were pulled into larger, changing systems while continuing to act within their own social worlds. Our opening map is crowded with centers because the past really was crowded with people making history.

Return to that map now. Lisbon is still on it. So are Gujarat, Kilwa, Timbuktu, Tenochtitlan, Cusco, the Ming coast, and the Pacific islands. We need not erase any of them to explain the changes of the sixteenth century. We need to learn how to keep several locations in view, draw the real lines between them, and resist drawing connections where the evidence does not yet warrant one. That is the beginning of a global history worthy of its name.

### Interpretive cautions

- 'Around 1500' is an analytical frame, not a synchronized global event; political periods vary regionally.
- A distribution of archaeological objects does not establish direct personal contact between the sites where those objects originated and ended up.
- The Ottoman state predates 1500; Safavid rule begins in 1501 and the Mughal Empire is founded in 1526.
- Terms such as 'pre-contact,' 'discovery,' 'globalization,' and 'gunpowder empires' have useful but limited explanatory scopes. Ask what they obscure as well as what they emphasize.
