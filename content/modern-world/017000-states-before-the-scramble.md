# States Before the Scramble: African Sovereignty, Colonial Conquest, and Historical Memory

## The problem with a map drawn after conquest

Open an atlas of Africa around 1914, and vast regions may be colored with the names of European empires. The shapes are familiar enough to persuade the eye that colonial possession was the central fact of the nineteenth century. Now imagine turning back a few decades. There were courts, markets, pilgrimage routes, farming settlements, armies, schools, trading alliances, and conflicts that European boundary lines did not create. The question is not only how foreigners divided territory. It is how African societies governed themselves, pursued ambitions, fought one another, and responded to a sudden acceleration of conquest.

UNESCO’s General History of Africa separates the nineteenth century before the 1880s from the period of intensified colonial occupation. That editorial choice is itself a warning. If the story starts at the Berlin conference, many African political developments appear only as prefaces to European arrival. If it starts with African institutions, the same events become a history in which Europeans intervened in existing rivalries, economies, and territories. It also becomes possible to study resistance without making resistance to Europe the only form of African political activity worth remembering.

The sources for this history require special care. A British or French administrative report may record tax collection or a military expedition in great detail while offering a hostile account of the people it attempted to rule. A court object may preserve status symbols but not the names of all the artisans who made it. Oral traditions can tell us about legitimacy and memory, while the politics of later generations shape the stories that survive. No method is automatically self-sufficient. We will place institutional records, material objects, African state histories, and modern historiography beside one another, and say which kind of question each can answer.

## Sokoto: government did not begin with colonial administration

Across much of the northern Nigerian region in the early nineteenth century, religious and political movements reshaped the landscape. Usman dan Fodio’s reform movement led to the emergence of the Sokoto Caliphate and a network of emirates. Historians Toyin Falola and Matthew Heaton describe the new political scale of these states in their History of Nigeria. Their chapter summary gives a reliable entry point but not the full detail of local experience. The important point is that a powerful state, with its own religious arguments and fiscal institutions, existed long before British conquest made the area part of a colonial administrative map.

A later study by Paul Naylor describes governance during Sultan Muhammad Bello’s era: officials and courts, tax and tribute collection, markets, city architecture, and agricultural production. These institutions rested on human effort and coercive capacities, not just a ruling title. The emirates were neither all identical nor free of internal competition. To speak only of a single great leader would erase the clerics, farmers, traders, craftspeople, officials, and dependent laborers who made state power possible. We can compare Sokoto with another empire, but first we must see its own administrative logic.

Not every political project reached its own people as liberation. A movement that claimed religious or moral reform could also create new hierarchies or intensify forms of servitude. A centralized court might protect some traders while taxing or conquering others. These are not contradictions we need to hide in order to demonstrate African statecraft. They are features of states and political struggles that deserve the same scrutiny we apply to Ottoman, British, Qing, or Japanese rulers. An Africa-centered history does not ask the audience to admire every African government. It asks the audience to take African governments seriously as historical actors.

## Asante: gold is also a language of politics

In the Asante kingdom, centered around Kumasi in what is now Ghana, political authority could be read in objects as well as documents. The Metropolitan Museum of Art’s study of gold in Asante courtly arts explains how gold regalia and staffs associated with office communicated rank, spiritual force and the sovereign’s relation to chiefs. A staff carried by a court linguist was not simply a decorative object. It made counsel and political speech visible. A goldweight used in trade also tells us something about systems of value and standardized transactions. These are ways of knowing a polity that a later colonial census cannot replace.

Asante political history involved military conflict, alliances, tribute and commerce. Gold and other goods linked the kingdom to regional and long-distance networks, but we should not imagine an economy consisting only of exports to Europeans. Courts and merchants had their own interests and rivalries. When British expansion pushed into the region, it did not encounter an empty frontier but a political system able to mobilize wealth, diplomacy, and war. European officials might see objects of regalia as treasures to collect; within the Asante system those objects also expressed obligations and legitimacy. The same item belongs simultaneously to economic, political and cultural history.

Material evidence is particularly helpful because official European narratives often present African politics only when a colonial commander reported them. Yet material collections have their own bias: which objects were seized, sold, preserved, photographed, or given museum labels? What survived from ordinary homes compared with royal courts? We cannot reconstruct all social positions from gold regalia. That limitation is part of the lesson. Rather than treating a museum as neutral proof, ask the date of the object, who made it, what collection path it followed, and whose interpretation is represented by the label.

## The conference and the conquest

European governments met in Berlin between November 1884 and February 1885. The German Federal Foreign Office’s history of the conference is especially useful because it corrects a common misconception: the participants did not sit down and draw all of Africa’s present borders. Fourteen states were represented, and the resulting General Act concerned navigation and trade in important river basins as well as rules governing claims to territory. No African delegation participated as an equal decision-making party. The agreement provided a diplomatic framework for imperial rivalry; actual conquest and boundary making continued through later treaties, campaigns, and negotiations.

Why insist on that distinction? Because declaring an entitlement in a European capital was not the same as controlling a village, road, river crossing or kingdom. Colonial agents made agreements under unequal pressure, brought troops, attacked cities, installed administrators, and sought access to revenue and labor. African rulers and communities chose among negotiation, alliance, relocation, armed resistance, or strategies that changed as circumstances changed. Even the words “collaboration” and “resistance” may make a decision look simpler than it was to someone facing multiple rivals at once. A treaty with one foreign power might be meant to deter another.

The coercion should not be softened by saying colonization was merely administrative change. To claim control over land is to claim the power to set rules for cultivation, extraction, policing, movement, courts and taxation. It can also change whose records survive. A new provincial report may make a community legible to outsiders, while breaking the institutions that had once recorded authority differently. But even under colonial rule people continued to debate, evade, resist and innovate. The fact that conquest transformed political possibilities is not the same as the claim that history ended when a European flag was raised.

## Samori: whose resistance, whose empire?

Samori Touré’s political and military career in West Africa makes the limits of heroic narrative especially clear. He fought French expansion while building his own empire. Anti-colonial history remembers his military resistance, but historian Brian J. Peterson asks what the people conquered or coerced by Samori remembered. Peterson’s study of southern Malian oral traditions argues that local accounts complicate celebrations of resistance, preserving memories of violence and dissent inside Samori’s own domain. Those voices do not invalidate opposition to French conquest. They make the history more exact: someone can resist one empire while exercising coercive power over others.

This creates a difficult but necessary question about political labels. Was Samori an emperor, an anti-colonial fighter, a state builder, a conqueror? Depending on the episode, more than one description may apply. A school narrative that permits only one description hides the people whose experiences do not fit. It also treats later nationalist memory as though it were identical to contemporary testimony. Peterson’s work reminds us to track who told a story, when they told it, and which local communities were inside or outside the power of the celebrated figure. Oral history has a date of telling as well as a date of remembered events.

A useful discipline is to ask the same questions of European and African rulers. Which communities were asked to supply food or soldiers? Who could challenge a tax demand? What religious arguments justified power? What happened to the conquered? Who gained new routes to trade or schooling? What sources let us hear people without titles? If we only ask whether a ruler was a hero in an eventual national story, we cannot answer those questions. The broader purpose of comparison is to restore the complexity of local politics without losing sight of the unequal imperial force threatening them.

## Ethiopia and the danger of a single victory story

Ethiopia did not enter the nineteenth century as one timeless, uniform state. Rulers struggled over the scale of imperial authority, regional alliances, and expansion. Menelik II’s consolidation of power had consequences both for communities incorporated into his state and for relations with European powers. In 1896 Ethiopian forces defeated an invading Italian army at Adwa, preserving independence from that attempted conquest. A Cambridge scholarly account links the victory to political centralization, alliances among nobles, and Italian strategic mistakes. It also notes that Ethiopia’s own territorial expansion involved conflicts with southern communities, including Oromo-speaking populations.

The victory at Adwa mattered profoundly, but calling it proof of a continent-wide military miracle would be misleading. It was a particular campaign involving leadership, planning, regional politics, supplies and decisions by the opponent. Ethiopia’s political structure and opportunities differed from those of neighboring states. Other African rulers also fought European armies, negotiated treaties, or sought alliances, sometimes with very different outcomes. We should celebrate neither defeat nor victory without investigating its causes. The important comparative insight is that African political capacity took many forms, and military outcomes cannot be explained by a single cultural label.

A regional history of Ethiopia also needs to avoid turning the experiences of peoples incorporated under imperial rule into a footnote to foreign policy. A government can successfully defend sovereignty against Italy while imposing new authority inside its claimed borders. This is not a reason to dismiss the significance of Adwa. It is a reason to separate two questions that political commemoration sometimes fuses: independence from external conquest, and consent to internal structures of power. The same analytical distinction applies elsewhere in the world, including nineteenth-century European and Asian empires.

## Benin: what conquest did to the archive

In 1897 British forces attacked Benin City in what is now southern Nigeria, removed its ruler, and plundered works from the royal palace. The Metropolitan Museum of Art’s collection records both the invasion and the later distribution of Benin artworks through sales, museums and private collections. Its curatorial account also discusses rivalries within the kingdom’s court and conflict over trade. These facts do not justify the invasion. They remind us that the kingdom had internal politics before British troops arrived. The violent removal of objects also destroyed or scrambled their relation to royal altars, locations and generations of makers.

This is a case in which the archive was physically rearranged by conquest. An object displayed today in an overseas museum might preserve extraordinary evidence of an Edo guild’s skill, but it may have lost information about which particular shrine or palace setting held it. A historian must therefore ask not only what the work shows but how it came to be available for inspection. The Met’s accounts make the loss of context a central issue. That is why questions of restitution and documentation are not separate from the history being taught; they concern what evidence and political relationships can be reconstructed.

Now compare two maps: the states that African peoples governed and argued over in the early nineteenth century, and the colonial jurisdictions shown by 1914. Neither captures everything. Courts, trade networks, seasonal land use and religious institutions do not all fit into neat colored polygons. But the distance between those maps is not a blank space labeled “Scramble.” It is full of African choices, competing ambitions, catastrophic violence, and memories shaped by survivors and later governments. The best way to make the past visible is to link each assertion to its proper kind of record—and to say plainly when one record cannot answer the question we have asked of it.
